<story>Mina Sol was a vivid mural artist whose reds seemed to remember fire and whose blues kept the cool of deep wells. [22] In the cathedral close stood an amber pavilion in autumn, resin panels glowing as if the year had been poured into honey. [44] There she was commissioned to paint a wall of night bloomers, though everyone praised the colors and no one asked why she worked before the doors unlocked. [71] Quietly driven, she carried a biscuit tin of old postcards from her mother, Liora, each one showing a cathedral by daylight. [92] The backs held no love letters, only penciled hours, petal counts, and one repeated instruction: ask the window. [110] Mina had kept that collection of old postcards through every rented room because it was the only map her mother left. [131] She had come to reckon with a tenderness that kept nocturnal hours and still expected to be called love. [150] Her motive was simple and bottomless: to understand why flowers bloom at night when the sane world has closed its eyes. [171] All winter the mural waited, green stems halted at buds, while snow stacked the close and hid the cathedral window's lower tracery. [193] Across the melting of winter's last snow, Mina returned before bells, when meltwater darkened the stone and the east turned pale. [214] In that dawning hush, even the pigeons seemed to listen with their feet. [227] She held the first postcard against the glass and saw daylight on paper lean toward the low sun. [245] The window answered through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window, not with words but with alignment. [265] Outer saints thinned to silver; beneath them, an etched river surfaced; deeper still, hairline petals waited for exactly this thaw. [285] As meltwater slipped down the leading, the three depths coincided, and the postcard's noon cathedral completed itself into night. [304] Moths appeared in the etched margins, furred and deliberate, each body matching a number Liora had penciled beside a printed spire. [325] Mina understood then that her mother had not been sending pictures; she had been sending keys. [341] The next dawn she brought the whole tin and fed card after card to the same patient beam. [359] A route emerged from city to city, always toward species that opened after dusk: cereus, nicotiana, moonflower, jasmine, evening primrose. [379] At every stop Liora had counted seconds between first loosening petal and full scent, as if timing a door held open for someone late. [403] The final postcard showed this very close, but on its back the hour was smudged by a thumb and the petal count read thirteen. [427] Mina had thirteen unfinished buds on her pavilion wall. [436] That night she stayed inside the amber pavilion while the panels cooled from honey to tea and the garden exhaled its white machinery. [459] A moth found the mural first, touching painted lips with a body soft as ash, then fled to the real flowers beyond the open door. [484] Mina followed with the tin against her ribs, feeling the postcards bend like small bones. [499] In the border of jasmine she saw a woman asleep in a chair, not abandoned, not abandoning, only emptied by waiting for scent. [522] The knowledge came with umbral tenderness: some blooms choose night because noon is too loud for delicate witnesses. [540] They open for moths that read by musk, for roots drinking cool, for grief that must practice joy without an audience. [561] Liora had not loved darkness more than Mina; she had trusted darkness to carry what daylight kept dropping. [579] When the first bird tested the air, the flowers began to close, and the hush widened rather than ended. [598] Mina pressed her palm to the window's deepest etching and felt the thaw run through stone, glass, paper, and wrist. [618] By full morning she returned to the mural and painted not the opening but the hinge of it, the exact pale second when a white petal consents to be seen. [648] She left one bud unpainted, a hollow for breath, and beneath it set the thirteenth postcard face down like a sealed door. [670] Visitors later said the pavilion smelled of rain at noon and wondered why the darkest wall made them gentler. [689] Mina only cleaned her brushes, quietly driven still, now toward every garden that keeps its lamp lit after the world looks away. [711]</story>
