<story>On Vell, the orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches, Ilsa Vey kept her pruning skiff pointed into the windless bright. [21] She was the intrepid orchard pilot who crossed between stone rainbows when other growers chained their trees and prayed. [40] That dawn, glasswilt had turned three pear rows silver, and the guild said resilience had died with the First Gardener. [60] Ilsa did not pray; she cleaned her visor and waited for the one hour when gravity inverts. [77] In her glove lay a broken arrow tip, black metal veined with green, found inside a blighted tree that had still borne fruit. [100] Where its edge touches sap, it accelerates plant growth, throwing buds open so fast they sing like tight wires. [119] The old charts warned that during inversion the canals would run upward, ringing the arches with a gathering of echoes. [139] Those echoes were not sound but pressure, a whirling silence that stripped names from leaves and returned them changed. [158] Ilsa meant to discover the source of resilience before her home orchard split into shining dust. [174] When the first stone lifted, the skiff compass spun useless, so she chose navigating by water flow. [191] Rain climbed from the furrows in silver threads, and every thread leaned toward the oldest arch. [207] She tied the arrow tip to a wet rootlet; its green veins woke, and the rootlet pointed where the current thinned. [228] Under the first arch, the silence whirled with her mother's pruning song, though her mother had been dust for nine seasons. [249] Under the second, the skiff heard child Ilsa asking why trees stood still when stones could fly. [266] She answered both echoes by trimming a dead branch and letting the upward water carry it ahead like a scout. [286] The branch vanished through a crown of arches, and the flow narrowed into a bright rope no wider than her wrist. [307] Ilsa followed, hand on the grafting knife, while acceleration burned in the arrow tip and begged to be used. [326] Beyond the crown hung the seed heart, a cracked spring held in the roots of the first orchard. [344] It was not whole, and that was the first mercy. [354] The crack fit the broken arrow tip as if the ancient shot had never ended, only waited for an orchard to answer. [376] Around it, blighted bark split over new wood too quickly, beautiful and brittle as frozen fire. [392] Ilsa understood then that resilience was not speed; it was a wound shared slowly enough for roots to hold. [411] The gathering of echoes pressed closer, offering her mother's voice, the guild's doubt, and the First Gardener's last breath. [430] If she drove the tip home, one surge would green every arch and shatter them before fruit set. [448] If she fled, glasswilt would inherit the hour and teach every seed to crack open dead. [464] So Ilsa opened the skiff vanes and crossed the streams, braiding upward rain into twelve patient ribbons. [481] She set the broken arrow tip loose inside the braid, not as a spear but as a splint. [499] Growth answered at once, moss racing over stone, not to devour the arches but to stitch their feet to the sky. [520] The water remembered its true path and divided gently, touching each orchard row with the same small mercy. [538] When gravity fell back into the ground, the silence stopped whirling and became ordinary air around extraordinary leaves. [556] The arches settled; pear bark stayed silver in three rows, yet beneath it moved a slow green pulse. [574] Ilsa brought home no relic, only sap on her cuff and a chart drawn from currents. [590] Years later, pilots still crossed at inversion and found the arches listening, rooted by what had once been broken. [609]</story>
