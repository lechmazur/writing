<story>Between regimes, when the old king's seals were broken and the new king had not yet spoken, the palace above the river kept its doors half open. [27]
It was an ancient Babylonian palace reimagined by surveyors, with baked brick courts, bronze canals, and a roof turned into a quiet observatory. [50]
There worked Ilsu, a frugal astronomer who mended cracked lenses with date resin and counted lamp oil as if each drop were a tax. [74]
His patrons wanted omens for their banners, but he trusted only repeated measures and the cold consent of stars. [93]
One violet evening a mason brought him a magic square tile pried from a threshold where two processional roads had crossed. [114]
The tile was no larger than a palm, yet its nine fired cells held numbers that shifted when no witness blinked. [135]
Ilsu tested it with calipers, balances, smoked glass, and a bowl of mercury polished by a beggar's sleeve. [153]
Every instrument agreed that the square was ordinary clay, and every instrument was empirically ironic, for the clay arranged itself only after he closed the shrine door and stopped demanding proof. [184]
In the dimness the cells made three sums across, three sums down, and two diagonal sums, all equal to fifteen. [204]
The result should have pleased him, but the center cell stayed dark until he set inside it the last copper ring from his mother's bridal veil. [230]
He had saved that ring for famine, and giving it to a theorem felt like theft committed by prayer. [249]
Then the tile drank the lamplight and drew on the wall a map of the palace as no builder had seen it. [271]
It showed courtyards as star fields, doors as eclipses, and soldiers changing guard in corridors that repeated the same measured chord. [292]
Ilsu understood through schematic representations that encrypted devotion was not a hidden word but a costly pattern placed where power expected tribute. [314]
The unknown had not asked for gold, because gold flattered both regimes; it asked for the one thing whose absence would alter his future tables. [339]
He wanted to seal a covenant with the unknown, yet he knew the price of escape would vanish if the reading saved the unruled night. [364]
Outside, heralds argued over the shape of the coming crown, while canal keepers quietly poisoned the reserves they were sworn to protect. [386]
Ilsu carried the tile to the roof, where dusk made the brick glow like low coals and the first planets answered in a dusky harmony. [411]
He laid the square on the meridian line and spoke no name, since names had become permits for theft. [430]
Instead he aligned each cell with a bell, a gate, a cistern, and a witness who owed him bread. [449]
No command could uncloak it; the covenant opened when the last debtor lifted his lantern and forgave the bread. [468]
What rose was a treaty written in positions: if the city kept equal measure at its center, the flood gates would hold until dawn. [492]
If envy moved one weight, the river would enter the archives and drown both the dead law and the unborn one. [513]
Ilsu laughed once, because proof had arrived wearing the face of sacrifice, and sacrifice had arrived with tables, margins, and repeatable light. [535]
He sent apprentices to ring the bells in fifteens, not to summon spirits, but to make the guards count honestly in the dark. [558]
He told the mason to publish the square in the market, where cheats would copy its numbers and miss the hollow center. [580]
Copies spread before sunrise, each one exact, each one harmless, each one carrying the concealed lesson that equality fails unless someone pays the middle cost. [605]
At midnight the keepers opened the wrong sluice and found it already sealed by masons who had followed the tile's patient geometry. [627]
The new regime entered at dawn expecting a portent and received instead a ledger in which every gate, debt, and star balanced around an empty ring. [653]
Its general asked Ilsu whom the omen served, and Ilsu answered that it served whoever refused to own the center. [673]
That answer was dangerous, useful, and true, so the general spared the observatory and taxed it one lamp a year. [693]
Ilsu paid from the common store, kept his own wick short, and wrote on the tile's back that the unknown had kept covenant by remaining unknowable. [719]
