<story>Wren had been a moonshine runner's daughter long enough to know that quiet could carry more than noise. [18] Her father used to say that trust, when it broke, made a sound like ice giving way on a black pond, and she had spent three years wanting to capture the sound of trust breaking in the little brass horn he hid inside his antique watch fob. [65] The fob was all he left her, besides a route, a recipe, and a debt owed to the pirate market hidden beneath a rotting pier at the far end of the cove. [97] The market only woke after midnight, when the tide slid out and left the pilings dripping like old bones. [116] Down there, lanterns burned blue under the boardwalk, and sellers laid their goods on velvet the color of bruises. [135] Every deal was sealed under a truce of hush, an old agreement that no voice would rise and no blade would leave its sheath until sunrise. [161] The hush was comforting the way a hand over your mouth is comforting when the soldiers pass, and Wren loved it and feared it in equal measure. [188] Tonight she came down the ladder with her father's last crate of shine and a warning folded small as a tooth. [209] Someone meant to break the truce, sell the market to the revenue men, and be gone before the gulls woke. [229] Wren meant to thwart them, though all she had was a name whispered wrong and the fob warm in her fist. [250] She was not brave exactly, but she was curiously steadfast, the way a fence post is steadfast, leaning into weather it cannot stop. [273] Her plan, if it could be called a plan, would have to work through false starts, because the market gave no one a straight path to anything. [300] Her first try was a boy selling compass teeth, who swore the traitor was Ilsa Vane, the rope wife. [319] Wren held the fob between them like a candle, and it gave back nothing, because the boy still believed every lie he told. [342] The fob had a gift, her father's last joke on the world: it drank in the moment a faith gave out, and played it back as a faint, splintering tick. [372] She found Ilsa Vane coiling rope by the tar pit, and accused her so softly that the words barely bent the flame. [394] Ilsa only laughed without sound, opened her palm, and showed Wren the tattoo of a gull that every true market soul wore. [416] The fob stayed silent, which meant Ilsa still believed in the hush, and a believer makes a poor traitor. [435] Wrong again, Wren thought, and the night leaned a little closer, fond and cold. [449] Her second try was a blind pearl diver who traded in secrets, and he sold her one for two bottles: the traitor drank tea with the tide keeper at the third hour. [481] At the third hour Wren hid beneath a sunken dinghy and watched the tide keeper pour tea for Mother Silt, the old woman who ran the market and had once dandled Wren on her knee. [516] The tide keeper's hands shook, but the fob heard nothing break in him, only fear, which is a sound like rain and not like ice. [541] Then Mother Silt said, gentle as rot, that the revenue men paid triple for a market delivered whole, and that Wren's father had understood, right up until the ice gave way under him three winters past. [577] Inside the fob, something cracked like a far-off pond, because Wren had trusted Mother Silt her whole life, and the horn drank down the sound of that trust breaking, clear as a bell rung underwater. [612] She stepped out holding the crate of shine above her head and spoke one word, "Raid," loud enough to kiss every beam. [634] It was her last false start, a lie with no legs, and it did exactly what she needed. [652] The market did not panic; it simply folded itself away, stalls collapsing like fans, lanterns pinched out, three hundred thieves gone into the walls before Mother Silt could finish standing up. [683] "You cannot deliver a market whole, Wren said into the sudden ringing quiet, if there is no market left to deliver. [704] Mother Silt looked at her the way ice looks at a boot, and then the first gray thread of dawn came down through the cracks in the pier. [732] Sunrise ended the truce and saved it too, because a hush that survives the night earns the right to hush again somewhere else, some other tide. [758] Wren climbed the ladder with the fob ticking warm against her heart, playing back, over and over, the small merciful crack of a lesson finally learned. [784]</story>
