<story>The last ship sailed on the first cold morning of October, and the traveler stayed. [15] He had missed it on purpose, the way a man misses a train that goes somewhere he has already been. [35] His trade was unusual: he wrote obituaries for the living. [45] In every port, someone was gone but not dead: a son emigrated, a husband estranged, a sister vanished into a marriage on the far side of the world, and the ones left behind needed the absence explained. [82] A good obituary, he believed, was a map of a life, and he was trying, port by port, to understand the geography of healing. [106] He wanted to know its coastlines and its weather, where grief ran deep and where it finally shallowed into something a person could wade. [130] He had begun the work years ago, in a city he no longer named, for reasons he folded small and carried like a letter never sent. [156]

The city lent him a room in the shuttered palace, where the court had departed with the fleet and only an old caretaker remained. [180] Her name was Ilse, and her garden lay in shadow from noon onward, all rusted maples and black pond water and marble gone green at the edges. [207] Chrysanthemums burned copper against the dark yews, and the last pears hung like small lanterns no one would bother to light. [228] She found him on his third evening, sitting among the fallen leaves with his ink bottle warming in his hands. [248] "Write one for my son," she said, "who took the last ship and will not winter here, though he is as alive as you are." [273]

His method was always the same. [279] He asked her to describe the boy until the description grew vivid enough to stand in the garden beside them: the crooked tooth, the tobacco-sweet coat, the laugh that arrived a half-second late, as if it had to be summoned from another country. [322] He called this projecting presence, and he was very good at it. [334] When he read the finished pages aloud, mothers felt their sons in the shadow of the maples, and widows straightened their collars for husbands who would never see them again. [364]

For three nights he worked in the garden, and on the fourth Ilse brought him payment he did not expect. [384] From her apron she drew a single thread, dull gold, snipped from the weft of the great carpet in the throne hall, where centuries of feet had worn the pattern thin. [415] "The weft is the thread no one sees," she told him, "the one that runs hidden beneath the knots and holds the whole design together." [440] "Your pages are like that," she said. [447] He understood then that she had guessed his secret: every obituary he wrote was, in some disguised way, his own. [467] He was the absence he kept explaining, a man who had left everywhere and belonged nowhere, charting other people's grief because he could not yet name his own. [495]

That night he read her the obituary beneath the bare espalier, and it was an intimate thing, closer than a confession. [516] He gave her son back to her in vivid detail: the way he salted his soup, the way he slept with one arm flung out as though reaching for someone in the dark. [549] When he finished, Ilse took his hand and wound the golden weft thread once around his wrist and once around her own, so that for a moment the two of them were bound into the same pattern. [586] "There," she whispered, "now he is not the only one who has been explained." [600]

In the morning the garden stood white with frost, splendid and lonesome, every leaf edged in light. [617] The traveler packed his ink and his pages, and on the top sheet he wrote the first honest line of his own obituary, which began: here is why I was gone. [648] He left the page on Ilse's table, where she would find it at noon when the shadow lifted; the thread he kept, a hidden gold line running through everything he would write after. [681] Geography, he had learned, was only another word for the distance between one heart and another, and healing was the slow road across it. [705]</story>
