<story>Everyone said Tolly Marsh was too cheerful to be a cemetery gardener, but the dead never complained about his whistling. [20]
He planted marigolds along the iron fence, tenderly organized his seed tins by the dates of the funerals, and greeted every headstone like a neighbor coming out for mail. [49]
Beyond the last row of graves stood a decommissioned grain silo, painted cobalt long ago, its curved side drinking the starlight like dark water. [73]
Every night at eleven, Tolly played chess against Brother Anselm, the blind sexton, on a board balanced across an upturned coffin crate. [95]
The game mattered less than the ritual, and both men knew it. [107]
When the chess clock finally ran down, its little brass flag folding like a tired wing, Tolly stood and took up his lantern. [130]
"Work to do," he said, blithe as a bird, and Anselm only smiled, having heard the same words for nine years. [151]
Tolly crossed the frost-bent grass to the silo and climbed the ladder that rose through its hollow blue dark. [170]
At the top, under a broken dome open to the constellations, hung a tapestry older than the cemetery itself. [189]
Its center had rotted to gauze, but the border survived, a band of frayed threads in umber, rust, and moth-gray, knotted and loose in patterns that were not random. [218]
Tolly believed the threads were echoes, uncharted echoes of everyone buried below, snagged on the fabric the way voices snag on a stairwell. [241]
No map existed for them; he intended to draw one. [251]
Charting them was his joy, the way other men chart coastlines or the migrations of birds. [267]
From his pocket he took an antique sovereign case, a slim oval of chased gold made to hold coins a century dead. [289]
It had come to him with his grandmother's effects, and inside, where a sovereign should rest, lay a single dyed thread and a slip of paper reading: the border is the sentence. [321]
His grandmother had been the cemetery's last weaver, and the first to suspect that the tapestry's fringe spelled out what the dead had meant to do but never did. [350]
Tolly's task, inherited like the case, was to untangle those intentions thread by thread, by decoding the frayed threads in the tapestry's border the way his grandmother's notes taught him. [380]
Tonight he worked on a rust-colored tangle that had resisted him all winter. [393]
He held the sovereign case open beside the lantern, matching the dyed thread within it to each strand, because the case's thread was the cipher key, the one known word in a language of frays. [428]
Slowly the knot loosened, and the echo inside it unfolded: a woman named Bette Lowry, buried in 1911, had meant to forgive her brother and had died with the letter unwritten. [459]
Tolly wrote it down in his ledger, tenderly organized like his seed tins, each echo given a name, a date, and a shape. [482]
The shape was what mattered. [487]
Tolly had spent nine years among the dead learning that intentions have geometry, that a kindness deferred bends like an arc, that a grudge coils, that forgiveness travels in a straight, bright line. [520]
He did this work not to judge the buried, nor to free them, but to discover the shape of their intentions, so the living might recognize their own while there was still time to act. [555]
Below, through the silo's skin, he heard the muffled tick of Anselm resetting the chess clock for tomorrow. [573]
The stars leaned close over the cobalt roof, and the tapestry stirred though no wind moved, hundreds of threads swaying like the feelers of some patient creature. [600]
The gloom inside was velveteen, soft and thick, a darkness you could stroke like the ear of a sleeping dog. [620]
Tolly chose the next knot, an umber snarl near the border's corner, and began the gentle work of loosening. [639]
Somewhere beneath him, someone waited to be understood, and the thought filled him with a gladness that the velveteen gloom could not smother. [662]
He whistled softly while he worked, and the echo of it climbed the silo wall, finding at last its own small place on the chart. [687]
