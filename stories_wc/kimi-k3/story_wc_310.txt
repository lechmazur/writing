<story>After the universe contracts, the old observatory does not fall, because the sky has closed around it like a fist around a seed. [23] Its deck is ambered by exhausted starlight, every rail and brass circle preserved in a translucent rind that still remembers heat. [44] Ila Venn keeps watch there with a collection of foreign coins that no bank, empire, or tide has accepted for ages. [65] She is the cast lots decision maker, the last officer licensed to choose when instruments disagree and mercy needs a procedure. [86] The title sounds ceremonial, yet on the ambered observatory deck it is as practical as breath and twice as lonely. [106] Ila is quietly enthusiastic, though her enthusiasm moves like light through glass, warming the room without demanding to be seen. [126] Her motive is not rescue, since rescue implies an outside, but to wavelength align with purpose before the final silence perfects itself. [148] The radios give her only static bursts, small hard rain from the crushed edge where tomorrow keeps knocking into yesterday. [168] Most archivists would filter that rain, but Ila has learned the art of understanding static bursts by listening for what refuses rhythm. [190] Inside the noise she can distill a backwards prophecy, because each message arrives with its ending polished and its beginning still raw. [212] The first received line says, "When copper rises tails over the black cup, do not open the star gate." [231] A day later, though later has become a courtesy, the second line says, "Before copper rises, marry the false coin to the true." [254] The prophecy runs backward through her hands, instructing consequences first and causes only after her fear has named them. [273] It tells her the gate will open if she trusts the lots, and it tells her the gate must never open. [294] Stellar doubt enters there, not as despair, but as the suspicion that every bright point may be a nailed shut eye. [315] Ila lays the coins on the black cup: a copper moon from a drowned republic, a silver crane, a square brass seed, and a glass kopeck weeping blue. [343] Their foreignness matters, because no remaining law can bribe them, and no surviving language can flatter their faces. [361] She casts once for the map, twice for the hour, and a third time for the person she is afraid of becoming. [383] The copper moon lands tails, the crane splits the rim, the seed points inward, and the blue kopeck spins until doubt looks devotional. [406] By the lots she should open the gate, yet the prophecy's last line, received first, forbids exactly that obedience. [425] Ila understands then that the message is not a chain on her wrist but a tuning fork struck against the end of things. [448] To align, she must not obey the future, and she must give the future a reason worth having sent. [467] So she distills the static again, cooling each burst through patience until only three words remain: choose the witness. [486] The witness is not the gate, not the coins, not even Ila, but the deck itself holding a cracked reflection of everything that ever looked up. [512] With sudden tenderness she opens the star gate one degree, enough for pressure to confess, not enough for the dark to inherit the room. [536] The contraction answers by loosening, and a buried constellation exhales across the amber like ink remembering water. [553] On the rail the coins rearrange themselves into a little caravan, all foreign, all home, all facing the thaw. [572] Ila laughs once, softly, because the backwards prophecy has completed itself by becoming unnecessary. [586] She records the final lot as abstention, a decision cast so cleanly that even chance feels consulted. [603] When new static blooms, it carries her own voice from the far side, quietly enthusiastic, telling a younger keeper to begin with doubt. [626] She smiles at the ambered deck and sends no warning, only alignment, the wavelength of a purpose finally shared. [645]</story>
