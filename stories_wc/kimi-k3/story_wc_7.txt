<story>The rusted greenhouse leaned over the drowned market like a kettle that had forgotten how to sing. [17] Mina kept soup warm there because broth remembered gentler weather than the law allowed. [31] She was a chef who could taste memories, an unlicensed grace in a city fed on paste. [48] One sip of onion skins gave her a grandmother laughing through flour on her hands. [63] One bite of soldier bread gave her boots, smoke, and the lie that obedience was shelter. [79] The Concord called such flavor dangerous and licensed only gray meals for the rebuilt blocks. [150] Mina's aunt, informally wise and formally arrested, had hidden it after the orchards were salted. [165] Plant it when the three moons thread the comet, her note said, or keep pretending soup is only soup. [184] That once-in-a-lifetime celestial alignment came on the first clear night of winter. [226] It poured through her: grafting knots, rain calendars, bees arguing over clover. [238] Under the knowledge waited the orchard burning, children coughing, her aunt naming smoke as weather. [253] The gift pulled hard, trying to make one scar the road for every future root. [268] Mina set down her spoon before grief could season the whole room. [482] Mina did not shout, hide, or swear innocence over a growing miracle. [494] She ladled clear broth into two chipped cups and set one beside the warden's shears.
