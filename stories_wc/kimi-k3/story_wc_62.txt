<story>Maren Vale carved gravestones in a valley where the dead outnumbered the living, and she thought about this often, turning it over like a smooth river stone in her mind. [30]
Her workshop was an ancient greenhouse at the cemetery's edge, all rippled glass and iron bones, and every dusk it filled with amber light and, for one season each year, with butterflies resting mid-migration. [64]
The year the fever took forty villagers, including her mother, Maren received more commissions than her chisel could hold. [83]
She wanted to carve them honestly, not as forty separate sorrows but as one thing, because she suspected that collective grief had a shape, and she meant to discover it. [113]
On the first dusk of the migration, when the butterflies arrived like orange ash on a wind that smelled of distant thunder, her mother's ghost appeared between the panes. [142]
Ilsa Vale did not speak; she simply picked up a chisel with translucent fingers and set its edge against the stone, and Maren understood that the teaching had begun. [171]
All her life her family had kept one custom: at dinner, each person traded a favorite poem aloud, and no one could eat until the last line settled. [199]
Maren kept the custom still, alone at her table, reciting one poem to the empty chair; that first night the ghost answered by breathing a line of verse onto the cold glass, letter by letter, in condensation. [236]
So their dinners resumed, and their lessons. [243]
Ilsa's mentorship was fiercely meditative: hours of silence, the chisel moving only when Maren's breathing slowed to match the far-off thunder that rolled every evening beyond the hills without ever arriving. [274]
Grief is not a portrait, the ghost seemed to say through her steady hands; stop carving faces and carve the weight itself. [296]
Maren learned to listen to each family's story until she could feel the hollow it left, then cut that hollow into stone as a curve, a notch, a wave. [325]
For every finished gravestone she cut a length from her mother's old woven ribbon roll, the one Ilsa had used to tie bouquets for the graves she carved, and Maren knotted a strip to each stone's corner. [362]
On every ribbon she inked one line from the poem the dead person's family had traded her at their dinner table, because grief, she was learning, is easiest to carry when it rhymes with someone else's. [398]
As the migration moved south through the valley, the butterflies shifted from the greenhouse roof to the cemetery trees, and Maren followed them. [421]
Each dusk she retraced the route the swarm had taken that day, walking stone to stone with her lantern, reading the ribbons aloud the way her family once read poems, the ghost gliding beside her in a silence deeper than speech. [462]
Forty ribbons, forty lines, and slowly a pattern emerged in her mind: the paths she retraced were not random but curved like a wing, like the inside of a palm, like the spiral of weather gathering itself. [499]
On the final dusk of the migration, the storm that had circled all season finally crossed the hills, and the thunder stood directly above the greenhouse, immense and strangely gentle, a drumbeat under everything. [533]
Ilsa set down the chisel and pointed, not at any single stone but at the whole dark cemetery, where forty ribbons lifted in the wind at once and the last butterflies rose with them. [567]
Maren saw it then: collective grief was shaped like migration itself, a vast moving body made of small beating losses, traveling together toward a place none could name alone. [596]
The ghost faded with the swarm, leaving one last line of condensation on the glass, a trade for all the poems Maren had given her: what is shared does not vanish; it only changes climate. [631]
Maren carved that line beneath her mother's name, tied the last ribbon from the roll to the stone, and ate her dinner in the thundering calm, already reciting tomorrow's poem to the empty chair. [665]</story>
