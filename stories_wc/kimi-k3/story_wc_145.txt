<story>Mara Venn had corrected maps for nineteen years, and she had learned that the world was mostly wrong in quiet ways. [21] Now she sat in a rusted outpost by a frozen lake, waiting for a bird. [36] The outpost had once guided surveyors; now it held only her, a stove, and a window that wept with cold. [56] Her employer wanted the northern atlas revised because the rivers, apparently, had stopped matching their descriptions. [72] Mara found this less alarming than the silence, which was complete and strangely content, like a held breath that did not mind being held. [96] She told the stove that tranquility this thorough was usually hiding something, and the stove, rusted and wise, said nothing back. [117] The falcon returned on the third morning, landing on the rail with ice on its talons and a message tube empty of messages. [140] Mara noted the ice was fresh, though every nearby stream had been frozen solid for a month. [157] Somewhere north, then, water was still moving, and the bird had found it. [170] She opened her polyglot's phrasebook to the page she had dog-eared years ago, the one that gave the word for source in forty languages. [194] Every language agreed on one thing: rivers begin somewhere singular, a point small enough to miss and large enough to matter. [215] She had taken this work for one private reason: to find where the rivers begin, a question no published map had ever answered honestly. [239] Each dawn she breathed on the glass and watched the condensation gather into branching threads, and each dawn the threads converged on the same pale point out on the ice. [269] She had traced them onto her drafts out of professional boredom, and the tracings matched six valleys whose rivers she knew by heart. [292] All of them pointed here, to this lake, which her atlas insisted was a dead end with no inflow and no outflow. [314] A singular convergence, she wrote in the margin, and underlined it twice, which for Mara was a parade. [332] She was not given to gasping; she simply corrected things, and if the correction redrew half a continent, the continent would adjust. [354] After the falcon's return, she walked out onto the lake at noon, when the cold was friendliest, carrying the phrasebook as others carry charms. [378] The ice held, though it hummed beneath her boots, a low and patient note, as if the lake were tuning itself. [399] At the spot the window had indicated she found a circle of open water no wider than a dinner plate, steaming gently in the cold. [424] Around it the ice was dark and thin, veined with channels that ran off in six directions, like the reverse of a delta. [447] Every river she had ever corrected began here, in this modest, steaming eye. [460] The water was warm, which was wrong, and it was rising, which was worse. [474] A singular convergence cut both ways: one source feeding six rivers could, in a single season, become one failure starving them. [495] Or one thaw drowning them, she thought, watching the circle widen by an inch as she stood there. [513] The tranquility of the place did not reassure her; it was the calm of something gathering itself, polite and enormous. [533] Back at the outpost she wrote two sentences on a slip of rice paper, her hand steady, her confidence subtle and complete. [555] Found where the rivers begin, she wrote; come quickly, and bring people who listen. [569] She rolled the slip into the tube, fastened it to the falcon's leg, and told the bird to go herald the end of the old maps. [595] The falcon, being a professional, did not editorialize; it simply rose into the pale sky and became a moving period at the end of a sentence. [621] Mara watched it go, then returned to the window, where her breath had already fogged the glass anew. [639] The threads of condensation gathered, branched, and bent toward the lake, patient as ink. [653] She let the pattern finish without tracing it, since some maps, she had decided, should be allowed to draw themselves first. [674] Outside, the frozen lake glittered in the low sun, serene and unbothered, and that was the part that worried her most. [695]</story>
