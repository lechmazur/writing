<story>Mara Venn was a broken-clock tinkerer, which meant the citizens of Bellweather brought her timepieces that honest craftsmen had declared dead. [21]

She never promised repairs; she listened to stopped gears until she became uncertainly certain which silence concealed a second chance. [41]

This practice had taught her to improvise, but not to plan, and every calendar in her workshop remained accusingly blank. [61]

Then, during the autumn when coincidence becomes pattern, seven unrelated clocks arrived frozen at 4:17, each containing one gray feather. [81]

The seventh belonged to retired Admiral Saye, who watched Mara lift the feather and whispered that his lost compass rose was calling. [103]

Forty years earlier, he said, the brass rose had guided his survey ship through underground rivers before vanishing in a mutiny. [124]

Its eight magnetized petals pointed not north, but toward stable ground, a priceless talent in a city built over caverns. [144]

Saye believed his mutinous navigator had hidden it beneath the ashes of the old botanical institute after fire consumed its eastern wing. [166]

Mara disliked expeditions because routes could fail, supplies could vanish, and companions could expect confidence she did not possess. [185]

Yet the matching clocks offered evidence, and Saye offered a stranger goal: not to conquer uncertainty, but to befriend uncertainty in planning. [207]

"A plan," he told her, "is merely an arrangement of useful doubts." [219]

Mara packed rope, clock springs, chalk, and three possible lunches, then left one compartment empty for whatever she had forgotten. [239]

At 4:17 the next afternoon, a draft carried her through the swirling leaves in a neglected greenhouse at the institute's blackened edge. [261]

The leaves were not falling; dead vines were spinning them around a floor drain in a deliberate eight-pointed figure. [280]

Beneath soot and roots, Mara found a hatch whose lock resembled a clock face without hands. [296]

No key fit, so she fed two springs into the mechanism, winding one forward and one backward until doubt balanced doubt. [317]

The hatch opened onto stairs descending beside roots as thick as ship masts. [330]

Far below, their tips drank mist from a subterranean waterfall that thundered into a lightless chasm. [346]

Across the torrent stood a stone observatory, joined to Mara's ledge by the rusted skeleton of a bridge. [364]

The bridge's remaining plates shuddered in pulses, and Mara recognized the rhythm: seven pauses, then the double beat of 4:17. [384]

She could retreat for timber and workers, but the mist was rising, and the mechanism might drown before morning. [403]

Instead she tied her rope to a root, clipped clock springs between broken railings, and crossed while the waterfall's pulses tightened her improvised braces. [427]

Halfway over, a plate tore loose, but her empty compartment had collected a wedge-shaped stone from the greenhouse floor without her noticing. [449]

She jammed it under the rail, laughing because preparation and accident had finally agreed to cooperate. [465]

Inside the observatory, ash carpeted a chamber whose ceiling mirrored the city's streets in tarnished brass. [481]

At its center lay the admiral's lost compass rose, wired into eight clocks and turning frantically beneath a glass dome. [501]

A journal beside it revealed that Saye's navigator had not stolen the rose; she had connected it to measure tremors threatening Bellweather, then died when the fire sealed her below. [531]

Each feather, drawn through vents by magnetic pulses, had entered a clock above whenever the buried fault shifted. [549]

Seven clocks at one hour were not prophecy, Mara understood, but an alarm patiently assembling itself from coincidence. [567]

The rose now pointed toward the waterfall, warning that the observatory's foundation would collapse and divert the torrent into the lower city. [589]

Mara could not carry the wired instrument without silencing it, so she used her final lunch tin, chalk, and loose bridge plate to build a floating signal mirror. [617]

She launched it down the torrent, where its flashes reached greenhouse glass and swept the watchtower whenever the rose changed direction. [638]

Saye saw the coded light, ordered the river gates opened, and evacuated the threatened wards before the cavern split at dawn. [659]

Weeks later, Bellweather mounted the rescued rose in Mara's workshop, where its petals clicked whenever stone moved below. [677]

Mara filled her calendar at last, not with fixed promises, but with branching plans, spare spaces, and meetings with Saye. [697]

She still repaired broken clocks, though she no longer asked them only why they had stopped; she asked what changing world they had noticed first. [722]</story>
