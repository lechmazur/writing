In the living bridge garden, roots braided themselves over a bottomless mist, and every leaf trembled as if remembering an earthquake. [21]

Ansel Vey, a hedge maze designer, came there carrying a portfolio of fading photographs and one bright splinter sealed in black glass. [43]

The splinter was a halo fragment, warm without heat, which his mother had carried home pressed beneath the glass of the oldest photograph before her face vanished from it. [72]

She had entered the garden seeking the source of the pictures, then returned silent, with soil beneath her nails and no shadow. [94]

Soon afterward, each family image began bleaching from the edges inward, erasing first the sky, then her, then everyone who recalled her. [116]

Ansel did not want merely to save her likeness; he wanted to discover the texture of time, to learn why memory could peel like paint. [141]

He believed the garden was a clock whose hands were paths, and he had been hired by its whispering gardeners to repair its heart. [165]

They led him across an arch of woven fig trunks, while blossoms opened underfoot and released voices instead of pollen. [185]

Every voice spoke his name in a different age, although age had no measure there and the mist held neither sun nor stars. [208]

At the bridge's center stood a maze grown from silver thorn, too small outside to contain the horizons visible between its gates. [230]

Ansel understood that ordinary design would fail, so he mapped it through hyperbolic geometry, drawing triangles whose angles surrendered rather than meeting. [252]

With each line, a corridor unfolded into more corridors, and the portfolio photographs shuddered, briefly regaining roofs, hands, and uncertain smiles. [273]

He followed those recoveries deeper, using the halo fragment as a compass because it turned toward whatever the pictures had lost. [294]

The maze answered by changing behind him: hedges became kitchen walls, thorns became curtains, and damp earth became the carpet of his childhood. [317]

There his mother knelt beside a camera, warning a younger Ansel never to photograph a path that had not decided where it led. [340]

When he called to her, she looked up, and he realized he was retrocognitively trapped, enclosed not in recollection but in the event feeding it. [365]

The camera clicked, the false room quaked, and a white fissure crossed the air with the terrible softness of lightning seen in sleep. [388]

His mother reached into that fissure and broke off the halo fragment, exactly as the oldest photograph had always shown. [408]

"I thought it was a door," she said, "but it is the rim around a moment, and moments resent being framed." [429]

Ansel saw the loop then: she had carried the fragment home because he had brought it here, while the photographs faded to force his return. [454]

The garden did not preserve history; it tightened history, winding grief around itself until every witness became both cause and consequence. [475]

To escape, he could close the loop and lose his mother again, or cut it and let every captured face disintegrate. [496]

A maze designer, however, knows that two offered exits usually conceal a third, created by changing the traveler rather than the walls. [518]

Ansel laid the fragment on his hyperbolic map and folded the paper until its endlessly diverging edges touched. [536]

The touch made no circle; it made a roughness, a minute ridge where before and after rubbed against each other like stones underwater. [559]

That, he understood amid the shaking dream of leaves and rooms, was time's texture: not a river, but a scar formed by contact. [582]

He pressed his thumb to the ridge, accepting the pain as his own, and the maze released its hold on the remembered kitchen. [605]

His mother smiled without recognition, freed from carrying a future object into the past, as both halo fragments disintegrated into ordinary light. [627]

Outside, the silver hedges lowered their thorns, the living bridge exhaled, and the mist below rolled in vast, quaking waves. [647]

The photographs remained pale, yet they stopped fading; blank spaces held faint grain, like skin remembering the pressure of a vanished hand. [669]

Ansel gathered them, understanding that saving a memory was not the same as imprisoning its subject. [685]

He redesigned the garden's heart as a maze with no center, only crossings where visitors might feel the past brush by and continue. [708]

Then he stepped onto the breathing bridge, carrying no proof of his mother except the tender abrasion she had left in him. [730]

Around him, flowers whispered names they could finally forget, and the whole garden swayed like a reverie waking from its own quake. [752]
