After the Water Wars, Mara Venn lived alone in a signalman's cottage on Gannet, a tidal island that belonged to the mainland twice a day. [25]

She painted drowned cities from memory, though she had never seen them, and slept so little that dawn often found her arguing wryly with the portraits. [51]

"You should have asked for reservoirs instead of halos," she told an old admiral one wet morning. [68]

Her manner was wryly earnest, the last defense of a woman determined to thresh meaning from experience before experience buried her. [89]

The islanders called her the insomniac painter, half as occupation and half as diagnosis. [103]

During a three-day rain, she noticed rust-colored threads crossing her window ledge, flowing uphill whenever the tide withdrew. [121]

They were not water exactly, but rain trails stained with iron and powdered amber. [135]

Mara crossed the salt meadows by following rain trails from puddle to puddle, while the causeway vanished behind her. [154]

At the island's northern cliff, the threads entered a crack exposed by last winter's storm. [169]

She squeezed through and descended into a chamber where roots, cables, and whale bones braided around a black stone door. [189]

In its center waited a bronze socket shaped like a moth. [200]

She knew the shape: her mother had worn an amber brooch of that design while negotiating the final cistern exchange. [220]

The brooch was locked in Mara's paint box, along with one note: Never trade what can open. [237]

He had tracked her through ration records and offered a year's water for the brooch. [270]

"Generous," Mara said, rubbing eyes that felt lined with sand. [280]

"Especially since you already know it is worth more." [289]

Whoever opened it could listen to landscapes remembering rain, or could teach pumps to find every hidden spring. [348]

"For witness," Mara continued. [394]
