During the lull of distant thunder, Mara, an exiled chess tutor, entered the time-frozen village of Bellweather, where rain hung motionless above the roofs and every clock showed the instant of her disgrace. [33]

Seven years earlier, she had been the king's chess tutor, praised for teaching him to see mercy not as surrender, but as the longest move on the board. [61]

Then she misread a rebellion as a feint, advised him to spare a traitorous duke, and watched that mercy open the palace gates to slaughter. [86]

The survivors exiled her, and Mara accepted the sentence because hatred seemed cleaner than grief. [101]

It sat beneath a glass bell on the altar, its shining walls packed with turns too fine for sight, while outside thunder prowled beyond the valley without ever arriving. [167]

The motions steadied her into a fierce repose, the stillness of a player who has stopped wishing the position were different. [259]

Its central figures were pristine, but the border was ragged, its silver and black strands plucked loose in repeating groups. [298]

Mara recognized no prayer, only chess notation disguised as ornament: files in silver, ranks in black, knots for captures, gaps for sacrifices. [320]

The woven hush surrounding Bellweather was not emptiness, she understood, but an unfinished message held taut across time. [338]

At first Mara searched for the brilliant rescue she should have found years ago. [400]

She recoiled, for surrender had been the lesson she refused to teach after the massacre. [485]

Yet the pattern did not accuse her of mercy; it accused her of believing she could control what mercy became. [505]

Her mistake had not been sparing the duke alone, but hiding her doubt from the king so he would continue admiring her certainty. [528]
