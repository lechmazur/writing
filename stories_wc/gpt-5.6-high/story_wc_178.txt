<story>At dusk, while one government had fled and the next still argued over its name, Naram entered the palace alone. [20]

The conquerors had reimagined the ancient Babylonian palace as a ministry of futures, glazing its lion walls with black glass and replacing throne rooms with observatories, yet rebellion had emptied it before the first forecast was issued. [57]

Naram, a frugal astronomer who mended cracked lenses with wire and saved candle stubs for cold mornings, had remained as caretaker. [78]

He trusted neither fallen uniforms nor approaching banners, but he trusted the recurring signal above the western horizon. [96]

Every evening it pulsed nine times, paused, then pulsed in groups that no catalogued star, satellite, or human transmitter could explain. [117]

For seven years he had measured it, wanting not to conquer the mystery but to seal a covenant with the unknown. [138]

Late that afternoon, a mason named Belit arrived carrying one object rescued from the demolished audience hall: a blue magic square tile. [160]

The tile looked humble beside the gilded reliefs, but its sixteen compartments bore numbers in old wedge marks, each row balancing every other. [183]

Belit said Naram's dead wife, Tashmet, had commissioned it before the purges and ordered it hidden until the palace stood between regimes. [205]

This unsettled him more than the message in the sky, because Tashmet had mocked charms, omens, and decorative arithmetic. [224]

She had served as the ministry's architect, drawing possible cities that ministers praised publicly and censored privately. [241]

On their last night together, she had told him that power concealed its truest plans through schematic representations, then vanished into prison. [263]

Naram carried the tile beneath the observatory dome, where sunset laid bronze bars across the floor and distant crowds sang in dusky harmony. [286]

He copied its grid, rotated it, substituted star coordinates for sums, and discovered that the balanced rows matched the signal's pauses. [307]

Yet the result was empirically ironic: each fresh observation disproved his previous pattern while making Tashmet's design more exact. [326]

He realized the sky was not sending coordinates down; the tile was teaching the palace how to answer upward. [345]

At the center of Tashmet's square, four wedges spelled the private name she had used for him, surrounded by a sequence meaning, I remain where you look. [372]

Her encrypted devotion was neither farewell nor memorial, but a key assembled from their shared tables of eclipses. [390]

She had recognized the signal first and hidden a reply inside architecture, knowing officials would preserve a puzzle if they mistook it for ornament. [414]

But the response required the palace's mirrored roof to uncloak, exposing the copper circuits beneath its ceremonial skin. [432]

The roof controls lay in the council chamber, now occupied by Captain Ur, commander of the provisional guard. [450]

When Naram explained, Ur studied the tile and asked whether the unknown sender might be an enemy awaiting invitation. [469]

Naram admitted it might, and that honesty persuaded the captain more than any promise of salvation. [485]

"The old regime demanded certainty before every cruelty," Ur said, "while the unborn regime will need a better habit." [504]

Together they turned the emergency wheels, and the black dome separated into petals, shedding dust like an enormous flower opening after drought. [526]

The tile clicked into a socket at the telescope's base, where its magic proved practical: impossible numbers became current, light, and measured tone. [549]

Naram placed his hand upon Tashmet's hidden name and sent the sequence toward the pulsing star. [565]

For one long minute, nothing answered, and below them rival processions converged in the courtyards, each carrying a different future. [585]

Then nine lights appeared across the dome, not distant points now, but a perfect square vast enough to roof Babylon. [605]

Every line balanced, and the central four shone with the same private name. [618]

Belit whispered that Tashmet had survived long enough to transmit from somewhere beyond every chart, though Naram knew the evidence allowed no such comfort. [642]

He did not claim her return; devotion, like astronomy, was diminished when desire pretended to be proof. [659]

Still, he answered once more, offering no allegiance to throne or flag, only the patient accuracy she had trusted him to keep. [681]

By dawn, the square had faded, the crowds had stopped shouting, and both governments waited outside the observatory for Naram's account. [702]

He emerged carrying the blue tile, poor in possessions, rich in uncertainty, and unwilling to let either regime own the sky. [723]</story>
