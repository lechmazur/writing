At closing time, the optical illusion gallery became quieter than any room had a right to be. [17]
Its painted corridors folded inward, its false balconies leaned over real floors, and every mirror reflected a door that did not exist. [39]
Elian Voss, known in certain hospitals and stranger universities as the fever dream architect, stood alone beneath a chandelier shaped like an open eye. [63]
He had designed the gallery during a month of delirium, then built it after recovering, following blueprints he could neither remember drawing nor disprove. [87]
Critics called the place realistically imaginary, which Elian considered praise until visitors began leaving with childhoods that belonged to other people. [108]
Tonight he had locked the doors to discover the texture of connection between the building and the stolen memories. [127]

The thread had been taken from his own left wrist in a careful operation, because every experiment suggested that living structures recognized what had once held them together. [176]
This was the principle of rational enchantment: wonder could be repeated, measured, and made accountable, provided one paid its physical cost. [197]
Elian tied the thread to the first needle and pushed it through a painted keyhole. [212]

Elian recognized none of them, yet grief arrived with every image, precise as a surveyor's blade. [344]

"Which one is true?" he asked. [456]
