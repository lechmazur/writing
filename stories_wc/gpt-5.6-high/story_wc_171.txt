At midsummer, when noon lingered like a held breath, Mara Venn climbed the ancient terrace above Orison with an ebony clipboard under her arm. [24]

She was the Forever War's peace negotiator, a title bestowed thirty years earlier and retained because the war had outlived every agreement. [46]

Above her, during the slow dance of celestial bodies, the sun approached the notch where legend said heaven first forgave the earth. [68]

Below, two exhausted armies mustered in mirror ranks, blue on the eastern salt and red among the western olives. [87]

Their commanders had agreed to meet only because both sides believed the solstice altar would punish a liar with blindness. [107]

Mara knew the altar was merely limestone, cracked by frost, wine, and generations of hopeful knives. [123]

Still, she had arranged every clause through ritual significance, knowing sacred gestures could carry trust where ordinary language arrived unarmed. [143]

Her manner was operatively gentle rather than soft, precise as a surgeon's thumb beside a wound, always touching toward survival. [163]

On the clipboard waited the Accord of Returning, which required each nation to surrender the border city it had stolen first. [184]

That impossible symmetry was Mara's invention, designed to anchor ideals in practice by making justice cost both victors something they loved. [205]

But Mara carried a hidden amendment beneath the treaty, and its paper seemed heavier than all the spears below. [224]

Years ago, seeking leverage, she had leaked a convoy route to the blue court, expecting arrests and obtaining a massacre. [244]

The dead included her brother, serving red as a physician, and eighty children he had concealed inside flour wagons. [263]

No tribunal knew, yet every negotiation since had been her attempt to purchase absolution with other people's signatures. [281]

As the commanders ascended, Mara understood at last that the price of redemption could not be charged to strangers. [300]

General Sorell of blue arrived first, polished and limping, followed by red's young Marshal Anja, whose mother had died in the convoy. [322]

Neither knew Mara's part in that grief, though Anja wore her mother's copper ring on a cord around her throat. [342]

At the altar, Mara poured water into two clay cups, turned each cup sunwise, and invited the enemies to exchange them. [363]

Sorell drank; Anja raised hers, then stopped when black grit settled at the bottom. [377]

"Ash," she said, staring at Mara. [383]

Mara had mixed ash from the flour wagons into both cups, the final rite promised in the sealed instructions. [402]

Her hands trembled, spoiling the solemnity, and that flaw made the ceremony suddenly human. [416]

"Before vows," Mara said, "we muster the names that vows have failed, and I will begin with mine." [434]

She removed the amendment, laid it atop the accord, and confessed without excuse, from the leak to the children to her cowardly silence. [457]

The amendment named her crime, waived diplomatic immunity, and submitted her to a joint court once the armies withdrew. [476]

For one terrible moment, only cicadas spoke, and Mara saw peace collapse under the weight of the truth meant to support it. [498]

Then Anja struck Mara across the mouth, not ceremonially, and blood dotted the limestone between their shadows. [515]

"My mother saved children," Anja whispered, "while you converted them into an argument." [528]

Mara did not defend herself, because defense had been the oldest dialect of the Forever War. [544]

Then she looked east, where blue families crowded the walls of the city her nation must return. [605]

"If we refuse because she is guilty," Anja said, "we make innocence a requirement for ending war." [622]

Anja signed next, then pressed her copper ring into warm wax, binding private grief to public mercy. [656]
