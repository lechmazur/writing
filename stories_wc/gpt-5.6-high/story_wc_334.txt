Every autumn morning, Mara Vale brewed tea, sharpened her field knife, and recorded one village superstition before breakfast. [18]

She was a hands-on folklorist, happier testing a charm in rain than cataloging it beneath museum glass. [35]

Her grandmother called her chronically curious, with the fond exasperation once reserved for muddy children and unlatched gates. [53]

Then, on the first Monday of October, Mara forgot the tea. [64]

It frightened her because the forgetting was not ordinary; the blue kettle, her grandmother's recipe, and every morning they had shared vanished together. [87]

In their place remained a smell of wet apples and a childhood warning: when habits break, follow what repeats in the dark. [109]

That night, three gulls struck her window in succession, each carrying a thread of green glassweed. [125]

Mara packed rope, chalk, specimen tins, and her grandmother's brass star map, then followed the coast road north. [143]

The greenhouse crouched below the tide line, its iron ribs webbed with kelp, while splintered masts leaned inward like mourners. [180]

Inside, saltwater shone between rows of drowned apple trees, and panes overhead preserved only jagged pieces of the morning sky. [200]

Mara found footprints where no living foot could stand, appearing one by one across the flooded floor. [217]

They ended at a single fairy ring mushroom, ivory-capped and dry, growing from the crook of a submerged tree. [236]

When she touched its cap, the orchard flashed green, and a boy's laughter rang through her with the ache of remembered summer. [258]

She knew the laugh, but not the boy. [266]

A figure surfaced in the reflection beneath her: Silas Rook, the ferryman who had supposedly drowned before Mara was born. [286]

"You came decades later than your grandmother promised," he said, his voice ticking like rigging against a mast. [304]

He explained that the greenhouse had once grown memories inside apples, until villagers begged to surrender griefs they could not bear. [325]

Rook had harvested their sorrow, then stolen joys as payment, binding both to the wrecks so nobody could reclaim them. [345]

Mara's grandmother had discovered the theft and hidden the mushroom as a key, but time had taken her before she could return. [367]

"The boy?" Mara asked. [371]

"Your brother, lost here at seven, then cut from you so cleanly that your family called you an only child." [391]

Mara nearly agreed, because longing for an unknown brother felt like homesickness for a house she had never entered. [427]

Then she noticed that Rook's reflection appeared in separate shards of glass, never beneath an intact pane. [444]

Her grandmother's star map bore matching gaps. [451]

Mara began to detect the ferryman's anchor points by studying broken constellations, aligning the chart with fractured panes and the reflected sky. [473]

Each missing star marked a wreck outside; each wreck held one stolen memory. [486]

"You always take the bigger piece," he said. [574]

By noon, villagers followed the scent and bit into what had been taken from them. [654]

Some laughed; some sank to their knees. [661]
