<story>After the Story Famine, when the Ministry burned every novel and licensed only weather reports, Mara Venn copied music for the Ash City Orchestra. [24]

She was a diligent orchestra copyist, trusted because her staves were clean, her rests exact, and her face as unreadable as winter glass. [47]

Yet each night she carried spoiled scores beneath the eastern viaduct to an ember alcove in mist, where a contraband shortwave radio waited inside a bread tin. [74]

The shortwave radio dial's glow was secretly luminous, hooded by a blackened cup until Mara turned it toward the wall. [94]

Then its green light revealed scratches no inspector had noticed: frequencies, times, and one instruction, LISTEN FOR WHAT THE SONG REFUSES TO SAY. [117]

The announcer, calling himself Rook, sent banned tales via elusive song lyrics, slipping names between rhymes and directions into repeated choruses. [162]

Most listeners heard only old ballads; Mara, trained to hear one wrong note among eighty instruments, heard doors opening. [181]

She transcribed the rhythms as notation, and when she laid the pages over the dial's green circle, pinpricks in the paper formed letters of light. [206]

Rook was asking for someone to teach dust to tell stories. [217]

The phrase was not poetic. [222]

Mara had become a copyist to test that claim, hiding fragments of forbidden prose inside harmless parts for oboe and viola. [291]

When musicians played them, dust stirred on the stands, but never long enough to speak. [306]

Only Ilyan, the blind timpanist, noticed that her rests breathed like sentences. [449]

"Will it hurt them?" he asked, nodding toward the plaster walls. [460]

"Only if being remembered hurts," Mara said. [467]
