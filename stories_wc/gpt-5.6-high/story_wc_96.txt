At midnight, Mara Vale slept beneath the broken dome of Bellweather Library, where constellations shone through ribs of rusted iron. [20]

People in the river district called her the vagrant penny poet because she traded verses for copper coins, soup, or permission to remain somewhere dry. [45]

Each month she molted personalities as cleanly as a cicada shed its husk, always at moonrise. [61]

One month she was a mocking firebrand, the next a timid archivist, and through this waning month she had lived as Mercy, a woman unable to abandon anyone. [89]

Mara remembered each self, but desire belonged to the present one, and Mercy desired above all to rebuild the library before the city demolished it at dawn. [116]

She had first met Tomas Reed sorting books at the prison library, where he was serving eight years for printing maps of police tunnels. [140]

Their friendship sparked over a misfiled gardening manual whose margins contained messages from inmates, tiny seeds of a blooming resistance. [160]

After his release, Tomas became a mason, while Mara carried those messages outside in poems cheap enough for dockworkers and children to buy. [183]

Together they dreamed of turning Bellweather into a free school, not to impose order, but to forge beauty from anarchy. [203]

Now Tomas arrived through a shattered window with twelve former prisoners, bundles of tools, and terrible news: the demolition crew had moved its schedule forward. [228]

"We cannot rebuild walls in six hours," he said. [237]

"Then we rebuild what a library means," Mercy answered. [246]

From her bedroll she drew a dented coffee tin with a secret false bottom, inherited from an inmate who had died before finishing his appeal. [271]

Beneath stale grounds lay the prison messages, Tomas's tunnel maps, and deeds proving Bellweather had been donated forever to "the education of all citizens." [295]

The mayor had hidden the deeds, but hiding was not destruction; evidence could still become a door. [312]

Tomas wanted to carry the papers to a judge, yet the courthouse would not open before the wrecking machines came. [332]

Mercy surveyed the rain swollen books and understood that they had introduced her to Tomas because books knew how to multiply voices. [354]

They lit the reading lamps from a stolen battery, and Tomas's friends began copying the deed onto blank endpapers. [373]

Mara wrote a couplet beneath every copy: What power buries, hands reveal; what rust has wounded, we can heal. [392]

By three, strangers were arriving with rivets, ladders, brushes, flowers, and books rescued years earlier from the leaking stacks. [436]

No leader summoned them; the copied words passed hand to hand, and the ruined building filled like a lantern inhaling flame. [457]

Mara watched the eastern roofs, afraid, for the calendar promised another molt before dawn, and no one knew whether her next self would care about Bellweather. [483]

Tomas noticed her silence and set the battered coffee tin between them. [495]

"You carried my words when I had no name beyond a number," he said. [509]

"Whatever face arrives, I will carry yours." [516]

Their friendship had begun by sorting other people's stories; perhaps identity, too, was something friends could keep in order without locking it away. [539]

He called the gathering trespass, the deeds counterfeit, and the library condemned. [575]

"Condemnation requires public notice," she announced, holding up the coffee tin, "and yours was hidden here instead of posted." [684]
