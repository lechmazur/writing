In the smog-choked city of Vey, night never became black; it settled over the roofs in a saffron quiet, stained by furnaces and old sunset. [25]

Mira Vale, an insomniac painter, rented the highest rooftop greenhouse, where cracked panes sheltered lemon trees, rain barrels, and canvases no gallery wanted. [48]

She painted faces from memory, though most belonged to strangers who had once offered her directions, soup, or a dry seat on a tram. [72]

Her work had one methodically hopeful purpose: to prove that strangers remember kindness, even when names, streets, and promises disappear. [92]

On the first night of the waxing moon, a parcel arrived by pulley from the alley, wrapped in a map of countries erased before Mira was born. [119]

Inside lay a calligrapher's moon-tipped brush, its white bristles curved like a crescent, and a note in three scripts she could not read. [142]

Only the signature was familiar: Orin, the mute caretaker who tended the greenhouse until the smog fever took him. [161]

Orin had collected lost languages, rescuing alphabets from demolished temples and lullabies from refugees who feared their children would forget them. [182]

Mira set the brush beside his empty watering can, intending to ask a scholar after dawn, but moonlight slid through the glass and touched its tip. [208]

At once, the sleeping leaves shivered, and daydreams sparked by moonlight began to rouse memories behind her open eyes. [227]

She saw Orin at seventeen, newly arrived and wordless, receiving an orange from a station sweeper who never asked his nation. [248]

She saw him decades later, giving cuttings from the same orange tree to children whose families spoke seven vanishing tongues. [268]

Then the vision widened: across Vey, people woke from gentle dreams of small mercies they had received and assumed forgotten. [288]

A baker remembered Orin carrying flour through a flood; a judge remembered a prisoner teaching her a cradle song; a thief remembered Mira covering him with her coat. [316]

The brush pulled toward her blankest canvas, but when Mira touched it down, black smog seeped beneath the greenhouse door and coiled around her wrist. [341]

The city's furnace towers had begun their midnight purge, a weekly blast that would bury the moon and break whatever fragile remembering had started. [365]

Mira could close the vents and save herself, or keep the panes open and give the strange light a path into Vey. [387]

Insomnia had taught her that hope was not brightness but a task repeated while darkness argued, so she tied a wet scarf over her mouth. [412]

Working methodically under the waxing moon's glow, she opened every roof vent, angled rain mirrors skyward, and dipped the brush into saffron pigment. [435]

The first mark became Orin's unreadable note; the second became the orange; the third became two hands exchanging it without a common word. [458]

As she painted, letters rose from the canvas like pale moths, each belonging to a language Vey had punished, neglected, or outlived. [480]

They flew through the vents before the smoke could thicken, entering bedrooms, guard posts, hospitals, gambling rooms, and the cabs of furnace cranes. [503]

Everywhere, sleepers daydreamed while waking and spoke remembered kindness in words their mouths had never learned. [519]

The chief crane operator heard his dead mother's forbidden blessing and, weeping, shut the purge valves. [535]

One by one, other operators followed, not because they knew Mira, but because each recalled some stranger who had once chosen mercy. [557]

The towers quieted, and for the first time in years, the moon laid a clean silver road across the city's roofs. [578]

By dawn, people climbed the greenhouse stairs carrying gifts: bread, buttons, seedlings, copied alphabets, and stories of kindness returned after years. [599]

The station sweeper, now ancient, brought one orange and placed it beneath Orin's portrait without speaking. [615]

Mira finally translated the note with help from seven visitors, each able to read only a fragment of its braided scripts. [636]

It said, "No language is lost while kindness can carry its meaning from one stranger to another." [653]

After three sleepless nights, Mira rested beside the orange tree, not cured, but no longer alone with the dark. [672]

Above her, the moon-tipped brush dried in a jar, and below, Vey murmured its recovered words into morning. [690]
