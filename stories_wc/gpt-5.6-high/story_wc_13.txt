<story>Mara Venn, natural history illustrator for the sky-city of Orris, had spent twelve years drawing creatures no one remembered hearing. [20]

Her plates were exact enough to show the velvet hinge of a moth's wing and the sorrow in a rain heron's eye. [42]

Yet every specimen she sketched in the museum aviary shared one mystery: each turned toward distant sounds before they existed. [62]

Mara believed they were listening for the place where echoes are born, a place omitted from every city map. [81]

Only the rooftop canal system weaving between towers seemed to point there, its currents climbing stairs, crossing aqueducts, and vanishing into cloud. [103]

Those canals flowed while Oru, the stone giant beneath Orris, slept; when he woke, the towers folded down beside his shoulders. [124]

Thus every expedition had to begin and end across a giant's nap. [136]

Mara's father had attempted the journey years ago, leaving her only an infinity symbol pendant and an unfinished field sketch. [156]

The sketch depicted a pale, long-throated bird behind brass bars, with two notes written beneath it and a blank where resolution belonged. [178]

At sunset, Oru yawned, the city bells announced seven days of sleep, and Mara launched her narrow boat from the museum roof. [200]

She carried ink, paper, the sketch, and a caged tuning lark whose call always returned precisely what it heard. [219]

For six days she followed ascending water, painting unfamiliar reeds so she could recognize their shadows when fog erased the banks. [240]

Each dawn the lark repeated the canals' murmurs, and each repetition ended on the same aching interrupted cadence. [258]

The missing final note pulled Mara onward more surely than any compass. [270]

On the seventh night, with Oru already stirring, she reached a circular reservoir suspended between the city's two highest towers. [290]

At its center stood a bronze keeper, neither statue nor man, turning a wheel whose spokes flickered through infancy, age, ruin, and renewal. [313]

"I sculpt time itself," the keeper said, shaving a bright second from the wheel and pressing it into the shape of a fish. [336]

The fish swam once around Mara's boat, then dissolved into tomorrow. [347]

When she showed him the pendant, his metal face softened, for he had given its twin to her father. [366]

"He reached the source," the keeper said, "but Oru woke before he could return, so I saved him by borrowing time." [387]

From within his chest came two heartbeats, one bronze and regular, the other human and faint. [403]

Her father lived inside a single borrowed moment, but releasing him would spend the second holding the reservoir outside ordinary hours. [424]

Then Oru would wake, the reservoir would fall, and every caged echo stored below it would be crushed. [442]

Mara heard thousands now: vanished birds, dead languages, childhood promises, and her own name spoken in her father's voice. [461]

She wanted that voice restored, not merely returned, yet the unfinished cadence told her why he had come. [479]

He had not sought an answer for himself; he had sought freedom for every sound imprisoned by repetition. [497]

Mara opened the lark's cage. [502]

"Uncage them all," she told the keeper, though the command cost the reunion she had imagined for twelve years. [521]

He broke the borrowed second in half, giving one shard to her father and driving the other into his wheel. [541]

Time cracked like lake ice. [546]

The reservoir opened underneath, and echoes rose in winged multitudes, shedding old words as feathers and completing no song twice. [566]

Their upward rush formed the missing note, not a conclusion but a doorway, and the interrupted cadence became an invitation to change. [588]

Oru opened one mountainous eye. [593]

The towers groaned and began folding, but Mara's father stepped from the keeper's chest, aged only one breath, and caught her boat's rope. [616]

Together they rode the escaping echoes as the canals tipped skyward, while the bronze keeper sank smiling into the collapsing reservoir. [637]

By dawn, Orris had settled beside the giant's shoulders, and father and daughter stood safely on a tower now lying across a meadow. [660]

The freed lark perched between them and sang a phrase the world had never heard, because nothing compelled it to answer the past. [683]

Mara opened her father's sketchbook and painted the lark without bars, then fastened the infinity pendant around its bright, unrepeatable throat. [704]</story>
