<story>After the procedure, Mara woke with 37 years of memory scraped clean and one object tucked beneath her hospital gown: a scuffed baseball signed, "To Mara, who will choose better next time; Elias Venn, 2091." [35]

The nurses called it contraband, then looked away with the exhausted indifference that had become the city's chief civic virtue. [55]

Outside, newspapers covered the corridor walls because paper was cheaper than repairing the cracks, and every headline praised the Empathy Removal Program. [77]

Mara had volunteered for that procedure as part of a pardon, though her sealed discharge card named only her old profession: Choice Architect. [100]

A Choice Architect designed moral labyrinths for courts, insurance systems, and wars, hiding cruelty behind menus until victims blamed themselves. [120]

She knew this not by remembering, but by reading the disgust on the orderly's face when he scanned her card. [140]

Clean slate or not, dirt had a way of bleeding through. [151]

On the baseball, beneath Venn's impossible date, tiny ink strokes curved like stitching, matching gaps between lines in the newspapers. [171]

Mara lifted a front page toward the ceiling lights and read between the newspaper lines, where pinpricks formed letters: EAST TUNNEL, LAST TRAIN, BRING THE BALL. [197]

With no past to trust, she began the journey because a message hidden from officials felt more honest than advice printed for citizens. [220]

The last train descended below abandoned stations, carrying only Mara and a boy whose silver wristband marked him as one of the Unfeeling. [243]

He watched her pry open the driver's locked cabinet and said, without fear or admiration, "You used to build these locks." [264]

"Did I build you, too?" she asked, nodding at his wristband. [275]

"No," he said, "but your algorithms decided empathy was inefficient, and my school believed them." [290]

His name was Sol, and he had followed the same hidden message without knowing why curiosity survived when compassion did not. [311]

She chose mercy twice, freedom once, and duty when a collapsing bridge required her to hold a lever while Sol crossed. [356]

Every noble choice tightened another trap, which was exactly the sort of joke her old self had apparently considered elegant. [376]

Sol returned for her, not from concern, he insisted, but because two minds improved survival odds. [392]

Beside it waited Elias Venn, with a young face and impossibly ancient eyes. [470]

"I came to kindle empathy where none remains," he said, "in my sealed future." [503]

The procedure had not removed empathy, as advertised, but harvested it, feeding a reservoir that would eventually teach his age what it had lost. [527]

Mara's algorithms had made the harvest possible, and Venn had traveled back to offer her one final choice: release the reservoir now, or send it forward. [553]

Released now, empathy might dismantle the cruel systems she built, but the future would remain barren; sent forward, their present would continue decaying. [576]

"Another labyrinth," Mara said. [580]

"Your specialty," Venn replied, and she almost hated the stranger she had been enough to become her again. [598]

The lake, Mara understood, did not contain a fixed supply, but living cultures that multiplied when shared. [642]

The choice had been framed falsely, the oldest trick in her profession. [654]

She pressed the baseball into the machine's intake, jamming the temporal selector between NOW and THEN. [670]

Venn shouted that the ball anchored his journey and breaking it could erase him, but he helped her turn the valve. [691]

Blue light climbed the tubes, divided through every corridor, and entered both eras through newspapers whose blank spaces suddenly shimmered. [711]
