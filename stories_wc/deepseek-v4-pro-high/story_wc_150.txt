The plane from Marseille touched down on the island late, and Mara stepped onto cracked tarmac with no one waiting for her. [22]
She had left this place as a child, carried away by parents who promised the journey was temporary. [40]

The orchard sprawled over limestone vaults, its fig and lemon trees drinking from soil made of crushed stone and older sorrows. [109]
Mara was warmly curious, not afraid, when the workers lifted the first slab and cool air rose from the dark. [129]
The foreman, an old neighbor named Abel, handed her a trowel and told her the dead would not mind her hands. [150]

Abel frowned and said the bench was not a bench but a sundial base that once stood in the orchard above. [191]
Its brass gnomon was missing, and its engraved hours were interrupted by the cracks. [205]
Mara knelt and touched the stone; she felt the day's heat still trapped in the fossil-rich limestone. [222]
Her grandmother's letter had been vague about the tomb, but it had said one clear thing: rebuild the base, and the garden would teach her the rest. [249]
That evening, she began the work not by chiseling stone but by walking the orchard with no fixed route. [268]
Purposeful wandering carried her between leaning trees and open crypts, past roots that cradled femurs and skulls like patient eggs. [288]
On the second day, she found the gnomon buried under a lemon tree, its bronze flaked green but unbent. [307]

The third piece of the base was still missing, and Abel warned her that stone without its footing would lean and drift. [351]
Mara returned to the catacomb passages beneath the orchard, this time running her hands along root-thickened walls. [368]
She moved slowly, not because she feared the bones, but because the roots seemed to breathe under her palms. [387]

For the first time since leaving, she did not feel like a visitor. [759]
