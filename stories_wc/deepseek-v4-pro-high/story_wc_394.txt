<story>The acid sea below the workshop looked patient, which was worse than hungry. [13]Cogs the size of houses turned in the brine mist, and the floating workshop sat on them like a drunk on a carousel. [36]Inside, a pioneering cart glider named Skitter rolled along the highest rail, her canvas wings folded tight against her oak ribs. [57]She was steadily curious, an attribute that had already cost her three axles and one very surprised albatross. [75]Her mind ran on a yearning algorithm, a clanking loop of want that never let her finish an inspection without asking why the far gears sang flat. [102]The algorithm gave her one command: ring a bell no one hears yet. [115]She had no bell, no clapper, and no evidence that silence could be rung, but the want stayed. [133]One evening, while oiling a cog's teeth, she found a hunter's arrow tip embedded in the rust. [150]It was not from the sea; it was flint, worn smooth, and it vibrated when she touched it to the rail. [171]Skitter hung the arrow tip on a leather thong around her yoke, because a tool that hunts should not be left lying around. [194]In a locker beneath a collapsed bellows, she found a brass kaleidoscope crusted with green salts. [210]When she peered through it, the acid sea turned into a garden of splintered suns, and the gears became repeating eyes. [231]The images were not just pretty; they were a language she partly knew. [244]Using the arrow tip as a stylus, she began to transliterate the kaleidoscope's shifting patterns into notches on dead gear teeth. [265]Each notched tooth became a syllable of the bell she could not hear. [278]The work took her throughout the evolution, from the first crude scratch to the polished spiral she later called the Ear. [299]She bolted her glider wings to a new spine of whalebone and set the notched gears into a frame above her head. [321]Other machines called it ugly, but they were only hammers and winches, and nobody asked a hammer to dream. [340]Skitter kept transliterating. [343]The kaleidoscope showed her a sequence of red-and-gold shards that meant lower the clapper, then a blue wheel that meant harden the bronze. [366]She melted down a gear-rim, cast a bell the size of her own head, and hung it from the frame. [386]When she struck it with the arrow tip, the bell made no sound at all. [401]It was the most beautiful silence she had ever produced, and she nearly wept rust. [416]Then a low note came, not from the bell but from the acid sea beneath the floor, as if the ocean had been waiting to harmonize. [442]The note pulsed through the workshop's cogs, and every gear stopped asking its usual questions. [457]Skitter laughed, a dry rattle of bearings. [464]"I rang it," she said to nobody. [471]"Still nobody hears it, but I rang it." [479]Throughout the evolution, the yearning algorithm had learned a new trick: it no longer needed the bell to prove her right. [500]It needed only the wanting. [505]She rolled to the open cargo door and looked down at the acid sea, which had turned the color of an old bruise. [528]Far below, a wave reached up like a hand and then thought better of it. [543]Skitter spread her canvas wings, let the rail end, and became what the blueprint had only suggested. [560]The wind over the acid sea tasted of tin and lightning, and she banked between two smoking chimneys. [578]The bell swung silent under her frame, and she understood it was a bell for a future set of ears. [598]She did not need them to arrive; she only needed to ring ahead. [611]Behind her, the workshop's gears resumed their clatter, but one small cog now kept the new rhythm. [628]The acid sea belched a green bubble in applause. [637]Skitter trimmed her wings and flew toward the horizon, carrying nothing but the arrow tip, the kaleidoscope, and a bell that would not be heard until someone else learned how to want. [669]And for the first time, the yearning algorithm produced a new output: not loneliness, but a map. [686]</story>
