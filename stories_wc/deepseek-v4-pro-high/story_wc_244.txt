The disused railway station at Hollow Junction stood overgrown with ivy, its broken windows and rusted tracks softened by green. [20]
Mara Voss arrived there on a wet Tuesday, carrying one clean satchel and a fragment of moon rock no larger than her thumb. [43]
She was an elusive disaster-relief logistician, known for appearing before the floodwaters ebbed and vanishing before the cameras arrived. [62]
Her work had taught her that aid was bound by reciprocity, not charity. [75]
People accepted a tarpaulin today and passed on a rumor tomorrow. [86]
Loneliness, she reasoned, could be cured the same way if she could just catalyze a chain. [102]
She had no patients, no community center, and no permission to use the station, but she had a moon rock and a theory. [125]
Mara opened her satchel and removed a tin whistle, a notebook, and a small jar of paste. [142]
She pasted a hand-lettered note to the old ticket window. [152]
The note read, "Moon rock found at this station cures loneliness by midnight exchange." [166]
It was a polite lie, the sort she told in disaster camps when panic needed to become purpose. [184]
She then walked to the village bakery, bought a poppy-seed roll, and mentioned the moon rock to the baker while paying. [205]
The baker, who was lonely and a little bored, told the postman that a piece of the moon had fallen into Hollow Junction. [228]
The postman told a schoolteacher that an astronaut would give free moon dust to anyone who came after dark. [247]
The schoolteacher told her students that the moon had sent a cure for sadness by rail. [263]
By Friday, improbable rumor chains had done their work so well that people arrived carrying jars, lanterns, and one very nervous accordion player. [286]
Mara watched from the ivy-covered signal box as the platform filled with strangers who suddenly had something to share. [305]
At midnight she stepped out, blew the tin whistle, and held up the moon rock. [320]
"Welcome to Hollow Junction," she said with a desperately jocular grin. [331]
"I have one moon rock and no magic, but I do have a rule." [345]
She walked among them and placed the rock in the hands of a teenage girl who stood alone at the edge of the platform. [369]
"You take it for one week," Mara said. [377]
"Then you must give it to someone lonelier than you, and tell them the same thing." [393]
The girl curled her fingers around the moon rock and nodded. [404]
The crowd did not disperse. [409]
Instead, people began exchanging names, promises, thermoses of tea, and scraps of their own stories. [424]
The accordion player struck up a waltz, and someone laughed because it was absurd and exactly right. [441]
Mara slipped back into the signal box and began drafting a new note for the next town. [458]
The cure for loneliness, she suspected, was not the moon rock but the obligation to pass it on. [476]
Reciprocity turned isolated people into keepers of a shared secret. [486]
That was the moment when the story changes, not because magic arrived, but because people started needing one another. [505]
By morning only ivy and torn posters remained, but a handwritten ledger sat on the bench with names of people who promised to return. [529]
Mara did not sign it. [534]
She was too practiced at leaving before gratitude could become attachment. [545]
Yet she carried the memory of the accordion waltz like a splinter of moon rock under her ribs. [563]
In the next village, she told a ferryman that a moon fragment could heal sorrow if shared before spring. [582]
The ferryman told a fisherman, who told his estranged brother, and by Sunday three boats crossed the lake full of cautious hopefuls. [604]
Mara met them with the same joke, the same moon rock, and the same simple rule. [620]
"This is the least efficient disaster relief I have ever run," she told the ferryman, "and the only one that might actually cure anything." [644]
The ferryman laughed, and for a moment Mara felt less like a ghost and more like a guest. [662]
She still left before dawn, but she left the moon rock behind in a jar with a note tied around it. [683]
The note said, "Do not keep it, because that is the entire trick." [696]
She understood now that the cure for loneliness was not a place or a person but a chain of people who had been asked to care. [722]
And she, the elusive logistician, would keep starting chains in forgotten stations until one finally reached her. [739]
