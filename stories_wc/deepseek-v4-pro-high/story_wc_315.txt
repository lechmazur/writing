<story>The midnight pattern seeker woke when the last hiss of radio static thinned into a soundless echo above the clouds. [20] Her name was Sena, and she had spent nine years reading the night drafts from a woven platform lashed to the crown of a cloud oak. [46] Below the arbor, a silver sea of cumulus hid every village, every road, and every reason to descend. [64] The only thing that kept her hands steady was a scarred parachute buckle she turned over and over, reading its worn notches like a blind script. [90] The buckle had come from a supply chute that had drifted too far east three winters ago, and she had kept it as a reminder that watchers could miss a pattern. [121] Now, after the radio static cleared, a pilot's voice surfaced from the receiver, frayed and thin. [137] "Arbor Watch, do you copy?" [142] "Drop seven is a minute out, but my altimeter says the chute opened high and the load is slewing." [161] Sena pressed the transmitter and forced her voice into the calm shape the pilot needed. [176] "Copy, Drop Seven." [179] "Hold your course; I am revising your landing line now." [189] She did not say that the slewing pattern matched a detached riser, or that the load would turn into a missile if the chute collapsed. [214] As a watcher, she carried the weight of knowing more than she could safely speak. [229] The pilot trusted her voice because she had never let that weight leak into it. [244] Sena closed her eyes and let her fingers work the parachute buckle the way her first instructor had taught her, remembering through muscle memory the order of straps, buckles, and release points. [276] Click. [277] The steel tongue snapped into its slot, and the old sequence woke in her hands. [292] She opened the chart, revised the descent corridor by three degrees north, and marked a clear pocket where the wind would hold the canopy long enough. [318] "Drop Seven, adjust heading zero-three-zero north." [324] "You are aiming for the cloud hollow, not the lane." [334] The pilot repeated the numbers with a slight crack in his voice, and Sena heard the pause before trust. [353] "Understood," he said. [356] Then the radio opened for one second and gave back only the hush of an empty sky. [373] Sena waited in that soundless echo, her thumb resting on the buckle's release. [386] She was attentively inventive by necessity; she had learned to build signal mirrors from ration tins and splice antennas with hair-fine wire. [408] But no invention could reach through the cloud and catch a falling load. [421] She could only revise the world's small margins and hope the pilot followed. [460] "Cargo is whole." [463] The words were steady, but Sena caught the small relief behind them. [475] She lowered the transmitter and looked at the parachute buckle in her palm. [488] Its scar no longer felt like an old failure; it felt like a pattern she had finally read to its end. [509] Another drop would come before dawn, and the burden would come with it. [522] For now, she hung the buckle on its nail above the chart, where the next midnight pattern seeker would find it. [543] Then she reopened the log and began to revise the storm's official track, not to hide the danger but to make sure the next pilot would trust her enough to hear it. [575] The cloud sea spread beneath her platform like a second night, unbroken and indifferent, but Sena listened until the pilot's engine faded into the dark. [600] The soundless echo of the cleared channel stayed with her, a silence shaped by everything she had not said. [619] She had preserved trust under pressure, but only by carrying the sharper edge of it alone. [635] Above the clouds, a watcher did not get to land, only to decide where others would. [651] That was the burden, and she tightened the buckle's strap around the logbook as if to bind herself to it. [671] The next call would find her ready. [678]</story>
