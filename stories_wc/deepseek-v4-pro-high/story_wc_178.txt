Nabu-zer trimmed the lamp wick with a bronze sliver, saving every bead of oil in the weeks after the old king's death. [22]
The palace had been reimagined by its last architect as a Babylonian monument of glazed brick and stepped terraces, but now it held its breath between regimes, neither old crown nor new heir filling the silence. [58]
Nabu-zer was an astronomer, a frugal man who kept broken instruments for their screws and scraped old clay tablets to make fresh notes. [81]
In a forgotten storeroom beneath the ziggurat observatory, his palm found a tile of fired clay no larger than a paving stone, its face divided into nine squares. [109]
The squares held numbers pricked with an old cuneiform point, and when he brushed away the dust he saw three rows of three. [132]
Each row, each column, and each diagonal added to fifteen, which struck him as empirically ironic because he trusted arithmetic more than omens, yet here a broken piece of salvage was as orderly as a temple hymn. [169]
He laid the tile on his workbench and began working through schematic representations of the evening sky, plotting the first stars over the palace in thin black lines. [197]
The numbers, he realized, corresponded not to stars alone but to signs in an old star calendar that hung on the observatory wall, a calendar no one consulted after the king died. [229]
He decoded the top row with a piece of charcoal on the back of a reused ration tally, then the second row, then the third row. [255]
The tile contained an encrypted devotion, a message from some long-dead observer of the sky, folded into numbers so that only a patient astronomer would find it. [282]
It read, "To the unknown one who rules after the throne is empty, I give my breath in watchfulness and my nights in measure." [306]
Nabu-zer felt the message turn in his chest like a star aligning with a forgotten meridian. [322]
The old king was gone, and the next ruler was still nameless; the palace needed to seal a covenant with the unknown, not a sacrifice to a named god. [351]
To seal such a covenant, he would need to place the tile where its maker intended, and he guessed the maker had been too frugal to carve a map without a destination. [383]
For three dusks he studied the palace's lower chambers, noting where the ziggurat's shadow fell in a long blue stripe across the empty throne room. [408]
On the fourth dusk, when the light was low and the air had the color of old lapis, he returned with the tile wrapped in a scrap of undyed wool. [438]
He knelt beside the dais and found a recess in the floor that matched the tile's edges, hidden beneath a loose plank he had noticed only because he never wasted wood. [469]
He pressed the magic square tile into the recess, and the numbers sat flush with the glazed bricks as if the floor had been waiting for them. [496]
The last blade of sunlight struck the tile through the western lattice, and for a moment the grid seemed to hover above the floor in lines of honey-colored fire. [525]
Then a section of the dais uncloaked itself, sliding back to reveal a small niche that held a clay cylinder and a pint cup of oil still sealed with wax. [555]
Nabu-zer lifted the cylinder, which bore a single instruction: "Speak the devotion aloud, then pour the oil once around the tile." [576]
He did not have a priest's chant or a swordsman's oath, only the quiet words of the coded message, so he spoke them into the empty throne room. [604]
Then he broke the wax seal and poured the oil in a slow ring around the tile, frugal enough to let every drop fall where the floor's geometry would spread it. [635]
The oil caught the light and made the magic square glisten like a dark mirror, reflecting the first star that had broken through the dusk. [660]
A low chime sounded from the ziggurat, though no hand had struck it, and the long blue stripe of shadow faded into the general night. [685]
Nabu-zer understood that the unknown had accepted his pact, not with thunder or procession but with the dusky harmony of a palace momentarily balanced between one age and the next. [715]
He left the tile in the floor, because a covenant must remain where it was sealed, and he returned to the observatory with the empty oil cup, which he kept for measuring small portions of lamp fuel. [752]
That night he recorded the event as a sequence of diagrams and sums, never once calling it a miracle, because the surest proof of the unknown was written in the same numbers he had learned to trust. [789]
