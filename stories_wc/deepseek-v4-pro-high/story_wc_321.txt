<story>Mara Voss had spent eight years as an ardent deckhand on the commuter ferry Meridian, and she still greeted every crossing with a salt-cracked grin. [25] The ferry vanished each evening when the terminal clock ran sideways, its hands crawling left along the dial before freezing between six and seven. [49] Most commuters forgot the missing minutes, but Mara remembered the cold snap of air and the sudden silence of gulls. [69] She remained resiliently positive, telling nervous passengers that the bay simply liked to keep one ferry to itself. [87] In her jacket she carried an empty hourglass, its two glass bulbs as clear as still water. [104] She had found it wedged under a deck plate after the first vanishing, and not a single grain of sand had ever appeared inside. [128] The empty hourglass reminded her that sideways time could not pour, but it could still be turned. [145] On the last crossing of October, the clock at Pier Nine stuttered sideways at 6:14, and the Meridian slid into the spectral grotto under starlight. [170] Black water caught the stars in a net of reflections, and the ferry's engine thrummed like a held breath. [189] Around the hull, passengers faded to translucent figures mid-sentence, then became only outlines. [202] Mara did not fade; she was the Meridian's anchor, bound to its deck by love and stubbornness. [219] She went to the wheelhouse and keyed the radio, sending a slow call through the static. [235] A voice answered, thin and far, and it was her own. [246] "Mara, the pattern is shifting," the other Mara said through a crackle of starlight. [260] She had learned to read the grotto by interconnected patterns: radio static pulsed in the same rhythm as the wake against the hull and the shimmer of stars on the cave wall. [292] That rhythm was the signature of two parallel timelines brushing against each other like hulls in a narrow slip. [311] Five years earlier, the first vanishing had split the Meridian in two, leaving her brother Elias at the helm of the ghost ferry in the grotto. [337] Mara intended to merge the parallel timelines so that the ghost passengers and her brother could reach the shore. [356] To do that, she had to make the two ferries occupy the same wake at the same sideways second. [375] The other Mara's radio voice counted a sequence in the static, and Mara turned the empty hourglass in her palm. [395] Without sand, the hourglass could not measure time, but it gave her hands a focus while she matched the patterns. [415] She spun the wheel to align the Meridian's bow with the ghost ferry's star-reflected wake. [430] The radio static rose into a clean tone, and the stars in the grotto brightened in exact time. [448] The two timelines drew together along the interconnected patterns, wake to wake, breath to breath. [463] Mara kept her hands steady and her mind on Elias, refusing to let the sideways clock unnerve her. [481] The Meridian shuddered as two hulls tried to occupy one space, but Mara laughed into the radio. [498] "Come on, you old ferry, you were built for impossible crossings." [509] The empty hourglass warmed in her grip, and for the first time a faint thread of light appeared between its bulbs. [530] The radio crackled with her brother's voice, no longer her own. [541] "Mara, now," Elias said. [545] She pushed the throttle and steered directly through the ghost ferry's image. [557] For a moment the world doubled, and Mara saw both timelines like two transparent charts overlaid on the grotto's water. [577] Then the outlines of passengers regained color, and the ghost ferry merged with the Meridian in a rush of wind. [597] The spectral grotto under starlight folded away, and the ferry broke into ordinary evening fog at Pier Nine. [615] Elias stood at the rail in a soaked uniform, looking exactly as he had five years before. [632] Mara embraced him, still gripping the hourglass. [639] When she opened her hand, a single grain of golden sand rested in the lower bulb. [655] The Meridian would always be a commuter ferry, and Mara would always be its deckhand, but now the crossing felt less like vanishing. [678] She remained a permanent transient, yet she had learned that even sideways time could be steered home. [695] "Next run," she said, tucking the hourglass into her jacket, "we fill the rest."
