<story>After the great fire, the ancient plaza beneath the shattered sky lay still as a held breath. [17] Irem crossed the ashen flagstones, her steel arms glinting faintly in the cold light. [31] In her right hand she carried a minaret mosaic tessera, a single blue tile from the fallen prayer tower. [50] The council had ordered every name of the fire's accused erased from the city's ledgers, but Irem believed even the condemned deserved memory. [73] She was called a gentle radical because she wielded her metal strength only to dig survivors from the rubble and to hold the dead with tenderness. [99] That was her small rebellion: she returned to the burnt places to restore what the councils burned away. [117] She knelt before the mosaic stepping stone at the plaza's heart. [128] Its chipped patterns once formed a path for processions, but now the gaps looked like missing teeth. [145] The tessera was cool and sharp in her grip, and its edges fit the stone's wound without forcing. [163] She placed the tessera into the deepest chip. [171] The fit was perfect, as if the minaret tile had been waiting there since the fire. [187] At once, Irem was retrocognitively trapped in the stone's last living moment. [199] She did not watch the fire from afar; she became the stone beneath it. [213] The memory rose through her: smoke boiling over the plaza, the minaret swaying, and a boy running toward its door. [233] The boy carried a bucket, not a torch. [241] He screamed a name, and the stone remembered the heat of his bare feet before the tower fell. [259] Irem tried to turn away, but the vision looped and held her like a burr in wool. [276] She saw the boy again and again, racing into the burning minaret to save a nest of sparrows trapped in the upper window. [299] The council had named him Cinder-Boy and ruled his mercy was the cause of the blaze. [315] The stone knew otherwise; it had felt him brush the fire aside with wet cloth and curse his own small hands. [336] But the stone also knew that the fire began in the oil-seller's stall, not in the boy's hands. [354] That detail had been buried with the rubble, along with the truth that the boy had only tried to warn the tower's keeper. [377] Irem gasped back into the present as the tessera cracked along an old fault. [391] The chipped patterns on the mosaic stepping stone had rearranged themselves into a single word beneath her knees. [409] The word was not a name yet, only the shape of one waiting to be spoken. [425] Irem understood what her aberrant mercy required of her. [434] The council wanted the boy remembered as an arsonist, but she would give him back his name. [451] She pressed one steel fingertip into the tessera and began to rename him. [464] "Not Cinder-Boy," she whispered, her voice warm against the cold air. [475] "You are Mati, the one who ran toward the burning because small lives mattered." [489] As she spoke, the stone drank the name and the tessera sealed itself into the mosaic. [505] The shattered sky above flickered, as if a shard of it had softened. [518] Irem rose, her steel arms heavier with the grief of the memory but lighter with the truth. [535] That was her faith: to cradle empathy in arms of steel, even when the world demanded punishment. [552] She walked from the ancient plaza as a cold wind lifted ash around her, and the stepping stone glowed faintly blue. [573] But before she reached the archway, a city warden stepped from the shadows. [586] The warden wore ash-grey robes and carried a list of erased names. [598] "You have renamed the Cinder-Boy," the warden said. [606] "I did what your ledgers could not," Irem replied. [615] The warden raised a hand, and the coldness of the shattered sky seemed to gather around his fingers. [633] Irem did not raise her steel arms. [640] She simply stepped aside so the warden could see the glowing stone. [652] The name Mati blazed there, warm and blue, and the warden halted. [664] Harsh law bent toward the stone's heat, and the warden lowered his hand. [677] Irem left him standing in the ancient plaza, a witness to aberrant mercy. [690] The cold wind followed her, but her steel arms held a new warmth close to her chest. [707] She had not come to fight. [713] She had come to name the boy, and through him, to rename the city's grief. [728]</story>
