<story>After the heart attack, Lina stopped sleeping. [7] Her father, a compulsive gambler, lay in the cardiac ward downtown, alive enough to blame the cards. [24] As a probability professor, Lina knew that survival was only a number. [36] She knew the odds of each stent, each troponin test, each gasping midnight call. [50] But knowledge did not stop her from standing in the rainwashed library at midsummer, where the windows ran with silver and the books smelled of wet cedar. [77] The electricity had failed an hour ago. [84] She carried her father's old candle lantern, its mica pane clouded like a cataract, and set it on a reading table. [105] The flame made the mica glow in faint, uncertain continents. [115] Lina opened the small notebook she had filled with her transcriptions. [126] For three days after the heart attack, she had traversed the city underground. [139] She had ridden every line, studying cryptic scrawls on subway seats. [150] Her father had left them. [155] He always marked the orange plastic with a felt-tip pen, as if the transit authority owed him a ledger. [174] No one else would know that the scrawls were a map. [185] Some were warnings: "When the count is nineteen, stand." [194] Others were confessions: "The house edge is just rent for hope." [205] And one, found near a torn seat at the end of the blue line, had made her stop. [223] It read, "To widen perspectives responsibly, you have to let the light fail first." [237] In the library, she copied the sentence again. [245] The phrase was her own, from a lecture she had given last term. [258] Her father must have heard it on the recording she sent him, then buried it under the city. [276] That was a lucid contradiction she could not resolve: a compulsive gambler who quoted probability ethics to strangers. [294] But she had kept traveling. [299] By interpreting cryptic scrawls on subway seats, she had pieced together his route from the hospital visitor window to the river. [320] The last scrawl appeared on a bench across from the casino. [331] It said, "She thinks the odds are the truth. I think the odds are a story I keep editing." [350] Lina had laughed at that, one clover-flavored laugh, because the four-leaf metaphor was too easy. [365] Melancholy followed quickly. [368] She was not supposed to be her father. [376] She was theoretically rebellious, a woman who taught probability while refusing to believe that her own history had fixed odds. [396] The library rain strengthened, rattling the tall windows. [404] She turned the candle lantern until its mica pane cast a wider arc across the notebooks. [420] If she could map his logic, she could teach it. [430] Not as arithmetic, but as something closer to weather: fronts of desire, pressure systems of regret. [446] A probability professor should know better. [452] But a daughter could want more. [458] The heart attack had cracked the sky in half. [467] Afterward, she could not look at a deck of cards without seeing his pale hand splayed on hospital sheets. [486] Yet he had placed the last message across from the casino, not inside it. [500] Maybe that was the widest perspective he had ever offered. [510] She wrote in her notebook: "A gambler stops when the story ends. A teacher asks who wrote it." [528] The mica pane flickered as the candle guttered. [536] Lina sat very still in the rainwashed library, surrounded by silent probability texts. [549] She had not found a final answer under the city. [559] She had only learned to traverse the gap between her father's mind and her own. [574] That gap felt like midsummer rain: warm, unseasonable, and unlikely to last. [586] She touched the lantern. [590] The mica was fragile, but it held. [597] That was enough. [600]</story>
