The survivor had no name because a single wrong digit on a death certificate had convinced the port city he was gone. [22]
He lived in the towering labyrinth of shipping containers stacked beyond the breakwater, a place where erased histories leaked through rusted seams. [44]
His mind filtered reality differently; every voice he overheard became a grainy color and a temperature against his skin. [63]
To him, a dockworker's complaint was cold blue grit, a sailor's confession was warm ochre, and a child's cry from a hidden container was dark violet salt. [90]
Across the rhythm of the tides, he moved through the lower stacks only when the water pulled back far enough to reveal narrow passages. [114]
At high tide he stayed inside an empty container he called the Archive, pressing what he had gathered onto its walls. [135]
He would layer the fragments in thin coats of flour paste, building a map of lives the port had chosen to forget. [157]
Each fragment came from collecting fragments of overheard stories: a torn manifest, a handwritten prayer, a broken boarding pass. [176]
His motivation was not charity; he needed to discover the texture of memories because his own memory had been sanded smooth by bureaucratic erasure. [200]
One evening, near the chemical berth, he heard two watchmen arguing about a poisoning years earlier. [216]
"It was ruled an accident," one said, "but the dead man was already a ghost on paper before the bottle was opened." [238]
The survivor pressed his ear to the cold steel and felt the sentence as a splinter of green glass. [257]
Later, at low tide, he found a yellowed label from a poison bottle wedged beneath a container's corner block. [276]
It showed a black skull above the words POISON and a faded batch number, but no name. [293]
He carried it to the Archive and waited for the tide to rise, turning the label over in his fingers. [313]
Something about its texture felt familiar, not as paper but as a residue of his own vanished past. [331]
The next morning, as the water fell, he searched the container stacks near the chemical berth again. [348]
He found a cargo crate whose lock had been cut and whose floor was stained with dark rings. [366]
Inside, a plastic bottle without its label stood against the wall, still smelling of bitter almonds. [382]
The survivor crouched there, and his strange perception filtered the silence into a low amber hum. [398]
He followed the hum through the stack, listening at doors and bulkheads for any story that mentioned the skull label. [418]
An old line handler told him about a fumigation crew that had been erased from port records after a container leak killed one man and sickened another. [445]
"They buried the wrong name," the line handler said, "because the survivor had stopped existing in their files the week before." [466]
The survivor's hands began to tremble; the wrong name had been his. [478]
He spent the next tide cycles layering the poison label, the stained wood, and the line handler's words into the Archive's highest wall. [501]
With each layer, the memory returned not as a picture but as a texture: rough canvas, chemical fog, the sticky rub of a brother's sleeve pulled from the dark. [530]
He remembered the leak, the confusion, the medic checking a list and reading aloud a name that was not quite his. [551]
The brother had died, and the port had closed the case by writing the survivor's name on the wrong certificate. [571]
No one had meant to kill him, but the erasure was useful, so they left it. [587]
Now the label from the poison bottle became the top layer of the Archive, the final proof of a death that never happened to him. [612]
The nameless survivor pressed his palm against the wall and felt the texture of memories at last: brittle, chemical, and alive. [633]
When the tide rose again, the Archive vanished behind black water, and he sat in the dark with the breathing of his own unrecorded history. [658]
