After the tower fell, Liora kept her father's airship ballast sandbag scrap folded beneath her cot. [16] She lived alone in a glass-domed enclosure, a desert rose farm where the red blooms held their breath. [34] People called her the bounty hunter's pacifist daughter, though her father's coat had been ash for two years. [52] The scrap was all that remained of his airship, and she used it to line a small tray of seedlings so the old violence would hold new roots. [80] The world beyond the glass had become a scatter of caravans, warlords, and dust. [94] Liora did not fight it; she cultivated against it. [103] Each morning she walked the stone paths and whispered values into the soil: restraint into the white-veined leaves, memory into the gray caudex, mercy into the roots. [130] Her father would have mocked her for inculcating plants with values, but he was gone, and the roses responded by blooming in the harshest months. [155] She was senselessly perceptive in ways that made visitors uneasy. [165] She noticed the tiny shifts in air pressure when a stranger crossed the outer dune, and she could read thirst or fever in a person's shadow before they spoke. [194] That morning she felt the glass sing a low note. [204] A man was coming. [208] He arrived before noon, dragging one leg and clutching a knife. [219] She opened the airlock not because she was brave but because she had already seen the sandbag scrap stitched to his belt. [241] It matched her father's airship markings. [247] She helped him inside, sat him on a bench among the roses, and gave him water. [263] He said he was a bounty hunter, like her father, and that the tower's fall had left him with nothing but contracts that no longer mattered. [289] He asked for food, then for cuttings, then for the whole dome. [301] When she refused, he stood and raised the knife. [310] Liora set down her grafting blade and sheathed it with the quiet click she had learned from her father. [329] "You will not survive another hour if you swing that," she said. [341] She had seen the dark bloom of blood beneath his shirt, the angle of his rib, the way his lungs pulled unevenly. [363] He called her a liar. [368] She did not argue. [372] She turned her back, watered the roses, and hummed a note that made the glass resonate. [388] The man took one step and collapsed. [395] She knelt beside him, removed the knife from his hand, and cleaned the wound with water she had carried from the condenser. [417] He watched her with something close to fear. [425] She told him that the tower falling had broken the sky's old order, but life in the dome still followed a simpler law: keep the roots alive, and the bloom will return. [457] While he slept, she took the scrap from his belt and laid it over her seedling tray. [474] The fabric still smelled of altitude and iron. [482] She used a needle to create an engraved quiet in its hem, stitching the word continuity in tiny letters. [501] In the morning, the man woke to find the sandbag scrap beside him, folded around a desert rose cutting. [520] "Plant it," she said. [524] "My father's sandbag carried weight into the air so ships could fly safely." [537] "This piece can carry one living thing through the dust." [547] He left without his knife. [552] She watched him walk into the heat, the cutting pressed to his chest, and she returned to the roses. [571] The dome held its breath as the sun climbed. [580] Outside, the world burned in a tranquil blaze of light, but inside Liora's hands moved through the leaves. [598] She was weaving continuity through chaos, one rooted thing at a time. [610]
