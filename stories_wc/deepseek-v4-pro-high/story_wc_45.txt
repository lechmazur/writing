<story>After the substitute body was found in the cradle, the villagers sent for Sarel. [14] Sarel was a bone-setter who forgave the village its superstitions but did not share them. [29] She had spent years mending snapped shins and dislocated shoulders, trusting only what her hands could align. [46] The substitute body was a child-shaped bundle of willow, wax, and river clay, too light to be a corpse. [65] Sarel knelt and ran her thumbs along its limbs, feeling no break, no bone, no joint worth setting. [83] The child's mother pressed a small white object into Sarel's palm. [94] It was a changeling's baby tooth, she said, left beside the false body. [107] Sarel held it to the lamplight; instead of a human root, it tapered into a hooked thread of enamel. [126] A cleft in certainty opened behind her ribs. [134] She could not call it a pebble, and she could not yet call it a tooth. [150] "Where did your daughter like to wander?" Sarel asked, and the question was shrewdly innocent. [165] The mother pointed toward the old manor's hedge maze. [174] Sarel had heard tales of a secret garden hidden behind that maze, but she had always treated them as a children's fable. [196] She entered the maze at dusk with the tooth wrapped in linen against her collarbone. [211] Hedges rose twice her height, their clipped walls dark and fragrant with wet yew. [225] At every fork she chose the path that felt most like a mistake, and she forgave herself for each wrong turn. [246] After an hour, she found a low gate covered in ivy and stepped into a secret garden. [263] The garden was not wild but arranged like a star-chart, with gravel paths linking beds of moonflower and thyme. [282] In the center lay a tiny skeleton, no larger than a cat, curled inside a ring of white stones. [301] Its bones were not human; they were too fine, too many, and faintly blue in the fading light. [319] One small gap in the jaw waited like an empty socket in the sky. [333] Sarel could have left, but she yearned to set even this unknown body right. [347] She knelt, gathered the scattered vertebrae, and began the slow work of bone setting. [361] Each socket told her where the next belonged. [369] The bones fit with soft clicks, not of breaking but of remembering. [381] When the little skeleton was whole, she placed the changeling's baby tooth into the gap. [396] It seated perfectly, as if the body had only been waiting for that loss to be returned. [413] Sarel sat back, her skepticism split like a bone and then set again. [426] She had been wrong to dismiss the mother so gently. [436] The false body in the cradle was not a human corpse, but it had been a real body once, shed and remade. [458] The changeling had slipped out of its substitute shape and left its infant tooth behind. [473] Sarel understood that the missing human child had not been stolen; she had been borrowed by something that wanted a mother. [494] And the borrowed child yearned to come home. [502] Sarel followed a new path out of the garden and found a hollow under the hedge where the missing girl lay sleeping on a bed of moss. [529] The girl was warm and breathing, with one baby tooth missing from her smile. [543] Sarel carried her back through the maze as the first stars appeared. [555] At the village, the mother wept and the father called Sarel's name loudly enough to wake the roosting crows. [574] Sarel told them only that the body in the cradle had been a clever doll and the girl had wandered far. [595] The changeling's tooth she kept. [600] That night she nailed a thin iron plate to her workroom ceiling and began a nostalgic innovation, turning her mistakes into constellations. [622] The first point was the tooth, set in tin as a bright and remorseless star. [637] She would add a joint from every wrong call, every forgiven folly, every tale she had refused to believe. [656] The villagers brought her broken bones and also their broken certainties, and Sarel set both. [671] She remained a skeptic, but a forgiving one, and that forgiveness was the dark sky that held her stars. [690]</story>
