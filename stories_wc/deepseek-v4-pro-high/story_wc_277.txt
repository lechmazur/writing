The call from the journalist came at dawn, and by noon Elowen Voss had driven up the private mountain road to the old observatory above the clouds. [27]
Inside, her footsteps fell into a vast, echoing silence that smelled of cold brass and old paper. [44]
She was the last heiress of the Voss newsprint fortune, and she had come because the reporter on the phone had asked a question she could not stop hearing. [73]
The question was simple: had her grandfather stolen the 1947 comet from a rival astronomer named Ivo Marek? [91]
Elowen had always been devotedly observant, so she did not answer with outrage; she answered by returning to the family's archives. [112]
In the circular reading room, cloud-light drifted through the slit in the dome and made the old journals float like ghosts on the mahogany table. [137]
She laid her grandfather's rosewood pen nib on the table, not as a relic, but as a measuring instrument. [156]
The nib had been his daily companion, and she had watched him use it to mark corrections in page proofs when she was small. [180]
Now the reporter's accusation sent her searching through a decade of bound observing logs. [194]
She was not hunting for a confession; she was hunting for a contradiction. [207]
Her grandfather had claimed that he discovered the comet at 2:40 a.m. with the old refractor pointed low over the eastern ridge. [229]
The official story lived in every newspaper archive, and the family had repeated it until it felt like a law of nature. [251]
Elowen read the 1947 logbook entry twice, then a third time, her finger tracing the faded ink. [268]
The pen strokes were vigorous at first, but the final digit of the time had been overwritten, and the ink there shone a fraction too dark under her lamp. [297]
She held the rosewood pen nib against the altered numeral and saw that its split point matched a tiny double scratch in the paper. [321]
To prove what that scratch meant, she decided to proceed by contradiction. [333]
She would assume, exactly as the family legend insisted, that her grandfather had made the observation at 2:40 a.m. and that the logbook had never been touched. [360]
If that were true, then the telescope's setting circles recorded at 2:40 a.m. had to place the comet above the horizon. [381]
But the logbook's own numbers put the comet seven degrees below the eastern ridge, which meant no telescope on this mountain could have seen it at that hour. [409]
The contradiction was exact and irreversible: if the observation had happened, it could not have happened. [425]
That was when Elowen noticed the small penciled letters in the margin, so faint they seemed to belong to the paper itself. [447]
The letters spelled "Marek, 03:04, borrowed sky." [454]
Under the dome's echo, she said the words aloud, and the observatory gave them back to her like a slow, solemn bell. [476]
Her grandfather had not stolen the comet in a sudden act of malice; he had adjusted a borrowed observation after Marek's data reached his desk, then he spent forty years circling the lie. [509]
Elowen understood that regret was not a single event but an orbit, a gravity that pulled every later silence, every charitable donation, every evasive memoir into the same closed loop. [539]
The family had been caught in that orbit for decades, and she had been floating inside it without ever asking why the constellation of evidence looked so strangely tilted. [568]
Her hidden truth was not that a previously unknown comet existed; it was that the Voss fortune, built on newsprint and credulity, had begun with an altered numeral and a borrowed sky. [600]
Now she needed to intervene, not because the journalist demanded it, but because the proof by contradiction demanded it. [619]
She picked up the telephone and called the reporter back. [629]
In a voice that carried the strange calm of someone leaving a long orbit, she said, "You are right; I have the proof." [652]
She did not sound defeated. [657]

Then she hung up, and the echoing observatory carried her small laugh out into the open sky. [700]
She left the rosewood nib on the logbook, a compass pointing away from an old lie and toward a newer, less comfortable orbit. [723]
