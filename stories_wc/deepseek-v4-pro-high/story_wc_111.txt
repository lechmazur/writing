<story>The garden maze had begun to fail after luck became a number printed on the morning broadsheet. [17] Its hedges still held their green wall shape, but inside the corridors the leaves dropped from old vines like forgotten applause. [38] Vessa, the withered vine dresser, moved through those narrow lanes with a copper pail and a collection of marbles, cracked, in her apron pocket. [62] She did not prune by the luck charts, though the estate demanded it. [75] She pruned where the vine leaned and where the rot actually began. [87] Her quiet creed was to prove that gardens know their gardeners. [98] Each morning she chose one marble from the collection, cracked or not, and polished it with the oiled cloth she kept for brass fittings. [122] Polishing a cracked marble did not mend the crack, and Vessa was wisely pragmatic about that. [138] She had learned never to pretend the broken were whole; she only made them glint enough to be found again. [158] That glint was her small mended deception, not of the marble but of visibility: the garden saw her noticing. [177] When a vine survived a week under her hands, she held an elaborate little ceremony at the maze junction, placing the polished marble in a hollow knot and whispering the vine's name. [209] The other gardeners called it theater, but Vessa called it commemorating small victories elaborately. [223] Since luck had become measurable, the family that owned the maze no longer trusted a gardener's felt sense. [241] They trusted the gauge, and the gauge said the south corridors were unlucky. [254] Vessa was assigned the unlucky rows, because nobody else wanted their own measured fortune to wither. [270] She carried the cracked marbles there and began her strange ledger of small wins. [284] The south maze bloomed in pale spurts and withered in long brown runs, often within the same hour. [302] Vessa read those shifts as weather, not luck. [310] The estate steward arrived with his brass luck-gauge and told Vessa that if her corridors did not post a higher number by solstice, the old vines would be ripped out and replaced with gravel. [344] She nodded and went back to tying a burst vine with soaked twine. [357] She did not plead with the luck-gauge; she knew the garden would not answer a number. [373] The lowest day came when she found a marble so cracked it looked like a map of dry riverbeds. [392] She polished it until the light caught every fault line. [402] Then she set it in the oldest hedge, not in a hollow but on a bent nail, where it would swing and scatter light. [426] That was her most elaborate commemoration: a small victory for every crack that still held together. [442] The steward said she was decorating a dying patient. [451] Vessa answered, "A garden knows its gardener by what she notices." [462] She did not argue with him. [468] On solstice morning, the family gathered at the maze gate with the luck-gauge, and its needle trembled at the south entrance. [489] The reading was still lower than the north garden, but it had risen for the first time in a decade. [509] The family wanted to know what she had changed. [518] Vessa led them into the maze, past the polished marbles glinting in the green walls, and showed them the vines that had budded where no one had gambled on them. [548] "I did not make the garden luckier," she said. [557] "I only stopped asking it to be lucky and started treating it as a witness." [572] As if on cue, a row of withered vines along the old hedge began to bloom, white flowers opening one after another like small bursts of measured light. [600] The steward checked the gauge again, but Vessa did not wait for the number. [614] She knelt, pulled a loose marble from her apron, and polished it once against her sleeve. [630] Then she set it in the crook of the blooming branch, a cracked thing given a bright place. [648] "For surviving the solstice," she said to the vine. [657] Some called her wise, some called her stubborn, but the garden simply kept growing toward the sound of her voice. [677] By autumn, the south maze had become the only part of the estate where the luck-gauge and the gardeners agreed on anything. [699] Vessa still wore the cracked marbles, still polished them every morning, still held her elaborate little rituals for vines that survived a week. [722] She did not need the gauge to tell her the maze was blooming, but she let it measure what it could. [743] The garden, in its blooming and withering, had already given its answer: it knew her.
