The gloom-hill coachman, Drem, left his cart at the landward end of the jetty. [14]
Twilight pressed in from the horizon, turning the planks of the blooming jetty the color of cold iron. [32]
Behind him, Gloom Hill crouched dark with cypress and old cannon mounts. [44]

The reef beyond the jetty had died three winters ago, and the sea now lay flat as a sheet over the bones of that old war. [89]
Drem meant to fertilize the shallows with these pods, not for food, but so a garden could reclaim a battlefield. [109]
At the stone rail he knelt and whispered the old names of the drowned companies in a language no living ear on the coast remembered. [134]
The words came out soft and guttural, like the syllables of a treaty long sunken beneath the tide. [152]
Drem spoke four dead languages, enough to read the battlefield plaques and enough to console the dead. [169]
But he had learned their true instructions from birds. [178]
Each evening for a month after the reef died, he had stood on the jetty until the wrens and gulls grew used to his shadow. [203]
At first they only warned of weather, but he listened until the warning split into meaning the way a seed pod splits in dry air. [228]
The birds knew where the warm freshwater leaked through the drowned reef, and they sang it in repeated intervals. [247]
Drem had learned to read those intervals as a coachman reads ruts in a road. [262]

Only the coachman remembered the chapel's vaulted door, because only he still breathed the language of the signatories. [306]
The birds remembered too; their calls followed the same cadence as the concord's final vow. [321]
He opened the sack and took a handful of pods. [331]

The pods floated for a moment, then sank like closed fists. [368]
