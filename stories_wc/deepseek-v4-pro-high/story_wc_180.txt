<story>The quartz-eye mender climbed into the upper orchard an hour after the contract was declared void. [16]
Her quartz eye caught the faint stress line in the old pear branch before the wood even groaned. [34]
The branch reached from one broken column to another, part of the lush orchard that spanned the rafters of the abandoned cathedral like a green second ceiling. [61]
She had planted that branch with her daughter Silla twelve years ago, the spring before the child's hair ribbon first went missing. [83]
Now the branch was splintering, and all the mender wanted was to persist long enough to save a single season of fruit. [105]
Below her, the breach of contract had turned the village council into a small, restless knot of voices, but up here the only sound was the creak of bark and the tick of her quartz eye socket against the socket's silver rim. [147]
The contract had said she could keep the orchard if she mended the village's heirlooms, but after she refused to replace a living rafter with dead timber, the council declared her in breach. [180]
She had expected Silla to come back once the ruling was announced, but the rafters remained empty except for the bees. [201]
The only trace left of her daughter was a forgotten canvas bag wedged behind the north transept's watering tank. [220]
The mender pulled it out and opened it. [228]
Inside lay a ball of waxed thread, a lump of beeswax, a sewing needle, and a faded red hair ribbon, still tied in a loop. [253]
The ribbon caught the light, and for a moment the mender saw Silla at six years old, stubbornly tying it around a pear sapling so she could tell it apart from the weeds. [286]
That version of her daughter had always been endearingly feisty, arguing with the trees about whose turn it was to grow. [307]
Silla had grown into the same fiery certainty, and a month ago she had flung the word "promise" at her mother like a stone. [331]
"You taught me to keep vows," Silla had said before she left, "but you break the only one that matters." [352]
The mender had wanted to explain that vows in their family were deciduous, shed each autumn so that new ones could bud, but Silla had gone before the words came. [382]
Now, with the branch cracking and the council calling for her removal, the mender knelt in the humus and began to work. [404]
She set the splintered branch back into its fork, then unspooled the waxed thread from the forgotten bag. [422]
The thread was old but it held, and she wound it tight around the wound, whispering the old words her mother had taught her. [446]
"Not every promise survives the frost, and that is not a failure. [458]
A vow is a leaf, not an anchor." [466]
The seam of the break still gaped, so she softened the beeswax in her palm and pressed it into the crack. [487]
Then she took the red hair ribbon and looped it over the wound as a final binding. [504]
It was not just a tie; it was a marker, the same way Silla had once marked the sapling so it would not be forgotten. [529]
The mender sat back and understood, too late, that she had been trying to bridge generational wisdom between her daughter's fixed promises and her own seasonal ones with words alone. [559]
Words had failed, but the orchard had not. [567]
She kept working long after the sun dropped below the broken rose window, mending smaller splits with needle and wax. [587]
By moonrise, the branch held, and the red ribbon glowed like a small stubborn heart against the bark. [605]
In the morning, the council would come with ladders and axes, but the mender had decided to persist, even if persistence was only a branch, a ribbon, and a daughter who might never return. [639]
That was when she heard a footstep on the rafter behind her. [651]
Silla stood there with a lantern, her hair loose and unribboned. [662]
"You used my ribbon," Silla said, and the words were not angry. [674]
The mender nodded, too tired to shape a lesson, and waited. [685]
Silla knelt beside her and ran a thumb along the waxed binding, then whispered, "Teach me the words again." [704]
So the mender did, but this time she let the orchard say it for her. [719]
A dry leaf fell from the branch, and Silla caught it before it reached the floor. [735]
"Deciduous," she said, and for the first time she made the word sound like an invitation rather than an excuse. [755]</story>
