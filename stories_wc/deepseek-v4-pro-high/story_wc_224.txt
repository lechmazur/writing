In the mountain that held the Labyrinthine Bookstore of Vonn, Lira worked as a mercury-glass seer. [16]
The shelves bored through granite in spirals, stairwells, and sudden mezzanines, so a patron could walk for days and never pass the same title twice. [41]
Her room was walled with mirrors of liquid silver, and each customer saw a future that wavered before it set. [61]
After remembering became painful, Lira stopped reading her own reflection. [71]
She kept her eyes on the books, on the paths of shelved stone, and let the mercury show lesser fates to strangers. [93]
Every night she substituted reality with a quieter one. [102]
She walked the spiraling aisles, smoothing spines, and in the glass she replaced memory with an empty room, a cold hearth, a hallway that did not lead back to the burning coast. [134]
This was not healing, but it was survival. [142]
The mountain held the thunder of her grief like a storm behind stone. [155]

One evening, in a corridor that curved upward into a forgotten gallery, she found a black stone resting on a lectern. [176]
The air there smelled of cold mineral and old bindings. [186]
It was a lost language stone, a smooth basalt egg that should have contained the final words of her homeland. [206]
She laid her palm on it and felt nothing but silence. [217]
The language had gone dormant, not destroyed. [224]
Lira remembered the old word for "home" as a shape on her tongue, but it split into pain before it could be spoken. [247]
She wanted to understand the shape of home, the exact furrow it had left in her, and the stone was the only key she had refused to turn. [275]
Beside the stone lay a gray feather. [282]
She picked it up and ran its edge across her fingertip. [293]
The texture was not softness; it was a series of fine teeth, like the edge of a blade made of breath. [314]
The feather had come from a storm petrel, the bird that returned to land only to breed. [331]
Lira understood why it was there. [337]
The lost language could not be forced; it had to be seeded. [349]
Her buried talent surfaced. [353]
She had been a seed-singer before the fire, someone who could coax language from stone by touching the grooves of a feather to its surface. [378]
The bookshop had buried that girl under years of cataloguing other people's lives. [391]
Now, with the stone before her, she did the only action that made sense: she seeded it. [408]
She placed the stone on a bed of dry moss and drew the feather down its side once. [426]
The texture woke a crack in the basalt. [434]
She drew it again, and the crack opened like a mouth remembering how to cry. [449]
A word rose from the stone, thin and warm: "hearth." [459]
Then another: "threshold." [462]
Then "harbor," "rain," "ash." [466]
She seeded the stone with the feather's sharp direction, and the stone gave back the lost language. [483]
The words did not heal her, but they gave shape to what she had been avoiding. [499]
Home was not the coast or the mountain or the bookstore's winding spine. [512]
Home was the hidden thunder of a language still alive under the silence. [525]
She took the cracked stone and the feather to her mirror room. [537]
That night, for the first time, she did not substitute reality. [548]
She let the mercury show the fire, the leaving, and the words that remained. [562]
The thunder did not destroy her. [568]
It spoke. [570]
The old grammar of thresholds arranged the words into a doorway she could walk through without forgetting. [587]
For the first time, remembering was not a fire; it was a map. [600]
She carried the cracked stone and the feather into the lower aisles, and the mountain's hidden thunder settled into a low, constant music under her feet. [626]
The stone pulsed once against her hip, as if the lost language had finally learned her name. [643]
