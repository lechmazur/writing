The cathedral had surrendered to birdsong decades ago, and the orchard that had been a single sapling in the nave now sprawled across the vaulted rafters, roots threading through ribbed stone like veins through a sleeping wrist. [37]
Yara, the last of the quartz-eye mender line, climbed the broken choir loft with the scolding grace of a woman who had been scolding the world since she was twelve. [67]
She was endearingly feisty in the way of small storms, the kind that knocked over wash baskets and then apologized by watering the garden. [91]
Above her, green pears hung where angels had been, and the air smelled of moss and old incense and the faint metallic tang of the quartz lenses she wore. [120]
They were not glass but true quartz, cloudy and imperfect, and through them she saw the fault lines in wood, the cracks in stone, the places where things had broken and not yet admitted it. [155]
She had come because of the breach of contract. [165]
The town of Verrow had paid her grandmother in salt and apples to mend the cathedral's flying buttresses, and her grandmother had vowed every autumn to return and tend the stones until the leaves fell. [200]
The vow was deciduous: it shed itself each winter and grew back each spring, and Yara's grandmother had explained it as a child while they braided bark into small birds. [230]
Then the town had stopped paying. [237]
Then the town had stopped calling. [243]
Then the town signed a contract with a stonewright who used concrete, and the orchard was left to itself, and Yara's grandmother died with a single pear in her hand and a look Yara could not interpret until now. [282]
"Deciduous," Yara muttered, climbing higher, the rusted ladder groaning under her. [295]
"Not dead. Just bare." [301]
The forgotten bag she carried had been her grandmother's, rediscovered in the back of a wardrobe after the funeral, smelling of cedar and old paper. [327]
Inside she found a thimble, waxed linen thread, beeswax, a whetstone, a letter she had not read, and a daughter's hair ribbon. [349]
The ribbon was pale as the sky before snow, and it had been her mother's, then hers, then her grandmother's, back through a long chain of mender women who had tied it into their hair while they worked, passing down what their hands knew. [393]
Yara tied it around her braid as she reached the highest rafter, where the oldest pear tree had grown into the stone and split it. [418]
It was the breach she had come for, though not the one the town thought. [433]
The town thought the breach was the unpaid debt. [443]
Yara knew, with her quartz-clouded vision, that the real breach was in the stone itself, the one her grandmother had not been allowed to mend, the one slowly letting the eastern wall lean. [476]
She persisted. [478]
She had persisted through the funeral, the bus ride, the broken path, the ladder, and the branch, and she would persist now, even though her hands shook, the wind was cold, and she had never mended anything larger than a teacup. [519]
She opened the bag and laid out the forgotten contents on the rafter beside her: the thimble, the thread, the wax, the whetstone, the letter. [543]
The letter was from her grandmother, written the winter before she died: "The vows are not broken, child. They wait to be re-tied. Use the bag. Let the ribbon be the needle's eye, and bridge what my hands could not." [583]
Yara laughed, a small, startled sound. [589]
Accidental wisdom, her grandmother had called it, the kind that arrived in the middle of doing something else entirely, like rain on a wedding or pears on a dead tree. [617]
She threaded the ribbon through the thimble, drew the waxed thread through it, and began to mend the crack in the stone, pulling it closed leaf by leaf, vow by vow, while the orchard watched and the wind smelled of coming frost. [657]
Below, the town of Verrow would wake tomorrow to find the eastern wall standing straight, and they would not know why, and that, Yara thought, was exactly as it should be. [691]
The mender does not explain. [697]
The mender simply persists, and the wisdom, like the fruit, falls where it will. [711]
