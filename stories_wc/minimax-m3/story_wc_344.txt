<story>The morning came slowly, a soft hush of distant dawn spilling through frost-rimed panes into the library that hung above the clouds. [22] Wren of the Lunar Eclipse was a locksmith who had grown up between two eclipsed moons, and though she was old enough to have forgotten many things, she still remembered how the frozen library used to groan with the weight of footfalls. [64] Now the only footsteps were hers, ringing against the glassy floor like coins dropped into deep wells, and the driftwood nostalgia of the place pressed against her chest like something she had once held at sea and never named. [103] She had come, after twenty years of wandering, to learn the rhythm of repair, for somewhere below the clouds her father's clock was still running backward and bleeding cogs into the sea. [135] In her satchel she carried the clockmaker's gear assortment her mother had left her, a velvet roll of teeth and pinions that smelled faintly of tin and a house she could not return to. [169] The library had no roof where she remembered it, only a ceiling of cloud and a sky bleeding slowly from violet into the pearly light of morning. [196] She made her way toward the corner where she had once hidden, as a girl, the scraps of prophecy her grandfather had pressed into her palm on the night the last eclipse ended. [229] The corner was buried now under a drift of paper and ice, but she knelt and, with care, uncapped the small wooden casket she had sealed there so many winters before. [260] Inside lay a folded scrap of vellum, its ink so faded it seemed to breathe in the hauntingly bright air that pooled around her like shallow water. [287] She unfolded it carefully, for the paper was brittle as old shells, and she read the words her grandfather had written in a hand as steady as tide: come to the hall of mirrors when the morning is most gentle, and let your face be the last thing you unlock. [337] Wren rose, tucking the prophecy into her palm, and walked deeper into the library where the shelves themselves had frozen into leaning towers of pale glass. [363] She passed reading rooms where the books still stood at attention, their spines glazed with rime, and where the lanterns had long since burned to ghost, and finally she reached the door she had been searching for. [400] It was the door to the hall of mirrors, and she opened it with the key from her throat, and the hinges made no sound at all, as though the room had been holding its breath. [436] Inside, the hall stretched longer than any room she had known, lined on every side with mirrors that caught the morning and made it hauntingly bright, a radiance that was almost sorrow, the kind of light one finds on the underside of ice. [479] Each mirror held her face, but a different face in each: here the child she had been, here the woman her mother had never lived to see, here the locksmith she had become, here the wanderer still unbecoming. [517] She set down her satchel and unrolled the clockmaker's gear assortment across the floor, and the cogs clicked softly against the frozen stone as though remembering their old places. [546] She had come, she reminded herself, to learn the rhythm of repair, and now she understood that the rhythm was not in the gears at all, but in the way one faced the broken versions of oneself. [583] Her father's clock was bleeding not because it was broken, but because it had been waiting for her to mend it with the patience of tides. [609] She lifted a small brass pinion and held it up to the nearest mirror, and her reflection lifted one in return, and the two of them began, at last, to work. [640] Under the soft hush of the distant morning, in the hall of mirrors where the light was hauntingly bright as sea foam on a winter shore, Wren of the Lunar Eclipse began to repair the thing she had crossed the clouds to find: herself.
