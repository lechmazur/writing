Marta had not slept in the proper way since the water wars ended. [13] She had, instead, painted. [17] The insomniac painter would rise before dawn each morning, when the sky still held the bruise-colored memory of night, and she would set her brushes upon the small table by the window. [49] Her hands, haggard and stained, learned to move with the patient curiosity of someone who had stopped expecting the world to arrange itself cleanly. [73] For three years the canals of the city had run with the wrong-colored water - first red, then gray, then a strange opalescent shade that nobody could name - and when the fighting finally stopped, Marta discovered that the silence afterward was heavier than any artillery. [117] She needed to thresh meaning from experience, as a farmer threshes wheat, separating the grain of insight from the chaff of mere memory, and so she booked passage on the morning ferry to the tidal island that rose and vanished with each moon. [160] The ferryman was a small man, wryly earnest in his manner, who told her as they set out that the island only stayed above the tide-line for a few hours each day. [192] "If you go," he said, "you go by following the rain trails." [204] Marta did not understand him at first, but when the boat kissed the sandbar she saw what he meant: shallow channels carved into the dunes, darkened by the previous night's rain, gleaming like veins across the pale shore. [242] She stepped off and began to walk, her boots tracing the dark paths inland, and the sea behind her withdrew with a long-drawn sigh. [266] The island emerged slowly as she climbed - first a hump of grass, then a crown of weathered stones, and finally, at its heart, a low ring of cairns where the old fisherfolk had been buried in better years. [304] She had come, she realized, looking down at the small hollow between two stones, because someone had told her that the brooch was there. [328] The amber brooch had been unearthed a season before by a boy who worked the boats, and he had sold it to a merchant who had sold it to a stranger who had lost it again in a storm, and the wind, Marta thought, had brought it back to this place as if the island was tired of letting go of it. [390] She knelt and dug with her fingers in the damp sand at the base of the eastern cairn, and the work felt like sleep she could not have. [418] When her thumb struck the warm, rounded edge of the thing, she gasped - it was not cold as buried objects usually are, and the amber held, in its honeyed depths, what looked like the small trapped shape of a wasp's wing. [459] She rose, holding it, and a haggard awe moved through her like weather, because the brooch was exactly what she had gone there to find, and finding felt like being found. [490] At that moment a woman in a salt-stained coat emerged from behind the western cairn. [505] "You have it," the woman said. [511] Marta turned, and in the woman's hand she saw a sketch - her own sketch, of a brooch she had drawn in a dream three winters before. [537] "I came here to make an exchange," Marta said, and her voice, she noticed, was steadier than it had been in months. [559] The woman smiled, the tired, knowing smile of someone who has lived through one apocalypse too many, and held out the sketch. [581] "Take this." [585] "I do not want the brooch anymore." [592] "In fact, I never did." [597] "I only wanted someone to come looking." [604] Marta gave her the amber, and received the thin paper in return, and when their fingers brushed she understood that the exchange was not really about objects. [631] It was about the convergence underneath - the unearthed place where grief and craft and the strange kindness of the post-war world met. [653] By the time the tide began to climb again up the rain trails, Marta was back at the boat with the sketch folded into her coat and the haggard awe still moving inside her like a tide of its own. [693] She had not slept. [697] She had, instead, brushed the salt from the paper and looked up at the emerging moon, and understood, for the first time since the water wars, that meaning can be exhumed. [728] The painter would go home. [733] There, at last, she would sleep. [739]
