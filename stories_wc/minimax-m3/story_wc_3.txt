Mara held the clay tablet against the morning light, her fingers tracing the wedge-shaped impressions left by a scribe who had lived four thousand years before the clouds gathered beneath the arbor's roots. [33] She had been a cuneiform translator for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned that every civilization leaves behind the same question, asked in different shapes of clay. [63] She worked in the magnetic arbor above the clouds, a vast lattice of ironwood branches that hummed faintly whenever the wind turned south, and where ripe fruits of pale amber used to glow softly among the highest leaves. [102] They did not glow now. [107] Since the spirit left, the arbor had grown restless, its magnetic nodes flickering in patterns that no longer resembled the steady pulse Mara remembered from her first years here. [136] She missed him, the old man made of light who had once walked between the branches and explained, in a language older than any cuneiform, why a single seed could balance an entire floating garden. [171] He had been the one who called the work in the tower a study of haunted ambitions, because every scholar who climbed those ironwood steps carried some unanswered weight. [200] Mara carried hers more quietly than most, and she carried it kindly, moving from tablet to tablet with the methodical care of an archivist who understood that even a misplaced wedge could change the meaning of an empire. [239] Each wedge, she had come to believe, was a small arrow pointing toward one of the arbor's magnetic lines of force, and her goal for the past two seasons had been to prove this through analogical thinking, comparing the pressure of the ancient stylus to the pressure of sunlight bending a field. [291] It was, she thought, a way to understand the physics of transition, the moment when a falling leaf crossed from one magnetic pole to the next and became, briefly, something other than what it had been. [327] That was the secret, she suspected, hidden inside every knowledge fruit seed the arbor had ever borne. [344] On the morning she decided to act, Mara climbed past the lowest branches and higher, past the hollow where swallows nested in spring, until she reached the place where the spirit used to sit and watch the clouds pass beneath him like a slow river. [389] She took from her satchel the single seed she had kept since she was a student, a tiny amber bead that warmed her palm as though it remembered the gardener who had planted it. [423] She pressed it into a groove in the ironwood, where two magnetic lines converged, and waited. [439] The arbor stirred. [442] For a long moment, nothing happened, only the faint singing of metal in the wind, only the faraway sound of her own breathing. [465] Then the lines aligned, and the seed drank from them, and a pulse moved through the branches that Mara recognized, because she had once compared it in her notebooks to the way a cuneiform sign is composed of intersecting wedges that hold each other upright. [510] By that small principle, she began the patient work of reestablishing what had been disturbed when the spirit departed, coaxing the magnetic nodes back into their old conversation, one connection at a time. [543] She worked for hours, and later for days, moving between tablets and branches with the same gently methodical rhythm, writing down each new alignment beside the ancient line that seemed to describe it. [576] Often she paused at the touching distance of understanding, close enough to feel the pattern, never quite close enough to name it. [598] It was enough, she decided, to know that the question was still alive, and that her ambitions, whatever they haunted, were still in service to it. [624] When the first amber fruit began to glow again among the high leaves, Mara did not climb to it at once. [645] She sat instead in her usual place, with her tablet and her pen, and imagined that the spirit, wherever he had gone, might still be reading the same sky through other, kinder eyes. [678]
