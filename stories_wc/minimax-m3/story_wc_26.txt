Mira had the eyes of a dusk noticer, sharp enough to catch the precise moment the sun slipped beneath the horizon and the world forgot its colors. [26]
She worked in the blooming library that floated above the clouds, where books flowered from marble shelves and pages unfolded like petals at dusk. [50]
It was an age after dreams had become shareable, and every sleeping citizen could record their visions onto glass tablets for others to experience. [75]
Mira's task was to preserve them, especially the oldest ones, by comparing silent video footage from decades apart, layering the dream-images like translucent leaves pressed in a book. [103]
She worked softly deliberate, her fingers careful as she lifted each fragile plate, searching for what time had erased and what it had somehow amplified. [128]
Among her possessions she kept a single river stone, worn so smooth by her grandmother's palm that it caught the light like a tear. [152]
It had been pressed against the old woman's temple when she died, and Mira believed it held the last dream her grandmother had ever had, though no device had recorded it. [183]
That dream, Mira suspected, was the one true shadow unbound, a darkness that walked free of any dreamer, drifting through the collective sleep of the city. [209]
She had been chasing it for years, this orphaned shade, hoping to understand how hope changed shape when no one was watching it. [232]
Tonight, as the library's blossoms released their sweet pollen into the circulating winds, Mira finally found the match she had been seeking. [254]
Two dream-footages glowed side by side on her desk: one from forty years ago, recorded by her grandmother in her youth, and one from yesterday, recorded by a child in the lower districts. [287]
Both showed the same river stone. Both showed the same shadow falling from it, longer than the stone itself, curling like smoke. [309]
In the old footage, the shadow pointed toward a city of towers and spires, all of them glowing. [327]
In the new footage, the shadow pointed toward nothing at all, just a soft blur of clouds, because there were no towers left to dream about. [353]
Mira understood then that the shadow unbound had been carrying hope away from the city for forty years, slowly, drop by drop, into the open sky. [379]
It had not been a curse. It had been a migration. [390]
Hope had needed somewhere to go, and the towers could no longer hold it. [404]
Mira felt the luminous regret of someone watching a beautiful thing end and begin again at the same time. [423]
She reached for her grandmother's river stone and held it against the glass tablet where the dream was still softly playing. [444]
The shadow on the screen trembled, then lifted, then drifted toward her, drawn perhaps by the stone's memory. [462]
It poured into the river stone like ink into water, and the stone darkened, and Mira felt her own grief begin to metamorphose into something lighter, something almost tender. [491]
She would carry this new shadow home, and tomorrow she would plant it among the library's blossoms, where it could root and grow into a new kind of flower. [520]
A flower that bloomed only in the dusk, and only for those with eyes sharp enough to notice. [538]
She had preserved the shape of hope, though its color had changed, though her grandmother was gone, though the towers had fallen into cloud. [562]
The library hummed around her, softly deliberate, its petals closing for the night, and Mira stepped out onto the highest balcony, where the clouds below turned gold, then rose, then slowly, softly, turned to dark. [597]
Somewhere in that darkness, the shadow she had released began to flower. [609]
