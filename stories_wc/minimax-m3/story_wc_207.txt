Mira had always been what her mother called sensibly eccentric: she kept jars of rainwater labeled by date, sorted her soil samples by pH, and wrote thank-you notes to thunderstorms. [30]
After the factory shut down, the town grew quieter, and quieter still, until even the gossip had to whisper to be heard above the silence. [55]
She inherited her craft from her grandmother, who had been a petrichor collector in a time when such a thing was merely odd rather than curious. [81]
Petrichor, the smell of rain on dry earth, is released when oils in the soil absorb moisture and exhale it back, and Mira knew this and knew it again every time she opened a jar. [116]
She had spent the last year gathering stones from the limestone cliffs that ringed the old reservoir, each one a small cathedral of calcium and time. [142]
The cliffs held caves, and the caves held water, and the water held a resonance that made her think of cathedral bells struck once and never again. [169]
It was in the third month of quiet that a parcel arrived with no return address and no note, only a small brass compass dented along one edge, its needle trembling as if afraid of its own direction. [207]
The compass, she would learn, had belonged to a lost expedition some seventy years before, a survey crew sent to map the river caves that ran beneath the town like veins beneath skin. [240]
She held the compass in her palm and tried the only reading she knew. [254]
Psychometry was not, for her, magic; it was the careful listening of an object through the hand, a slow unraveling of the heat and pressure left behind by every grip. [284]
The compass told her of cold fingers and lantern smoke, of a surveyor's pencil snapping in a draft, of a moment when the needle had spun and spun and pointed to a place no map acknowledged. [320]
She decided to delve. [324]
The caves beneath the cliffs were not unknown to her, but this route was, a side passage she had never noticed, half-hidden by a fallen slab. [350]
The resonant hush of the stone pressed against her ears as she went deeper, her lantern casting a small circle of gold on walls that wept thin threads of mineral water. [381]
At the third bend she heard the lagoon before she saw it, a drip-drip-drum that seemed to come from the ceiling and the floor and her own chest at once. [411]
The subterranean lagoon spread before her like a dark mirror, its surface catching the lantern light and giving it back as a pale, trembling eye. [436]
She knelt at the edge and set the compass on a flat stone, and the needle, which had been spinning, slowed, and slowed, and then, with a click like a small door closing and opening, pointed inward. [473]
These were what she had begun to think of as hinged awakenings: small doors in the mind that swung on quiet hinges, letting in a light that had always been there but had not yet been admitted. [510]
The first awakening had been the scent of the place, which was not petrichor exactly, but the old, slow cousin of it, the breath of stone that had been wet for a thousand years and would be wet for a thousand more. [552]
The second awakening was the realization that the survey crew had not been lost; they had found what they were looking for and had left the compass behind so that someone else could find it too. [588]
The third awakening was the simplest, and it was this: that explanations are a kind of currency, valuable only until one reaches the place where they are no longer accepted. [618]
She had come, she understood now, to find the place where explanations fail, and here it was, a dark water in a dark stone, asking nothing of her but presence. [648]
She took out a small glass vial and uncorked it, and the air of the lagoon entered it with a sound like a long-held breath released. [674]
When she climbed back out, the cliffs were pale with evening, and the town below was only a few lit windows, and the factory stood still, and the wind carried nothing but the smell of approaching rain. [711]
She uncorked the vial again in her kitchen and breathed in, and the room filled with the resonant hush of a place she had finally found, and did not need to explain. [743]
