The last ember of sun bled across the desert horizon as Kestrel reached the dome of the old observatory. [19]
She was a sharp-eyed drone pilot, and her hands still trembled from the long flight over the bone-dry plain. [38]
The brass doors stood open, as if the building itself had been waiting. [51]

"I didn't think anyone else would come," he said, his voice careful, almost reverent. [117]
"Neither did I," Kestrel admitted, lowering her pack. [125]

"You came for the chip too?" Kestrel asked, surprised at her own bluntness. [195]
"I came for the missing pieces," Maro said, and gestured toward a small wooden case on the stone floor. [214]

"Decay," Maro whispered. [279]
"Not decay," Kestrel corrected, her sharp eyes catching the pattern of the loss. [292]
"The pieces are missing because they were never here. Someone removed them long ago and left only the negative space behind." [313]
The pilgrim nodded slowly, his perpetually surprised face softening into something like recognition. [326]
"To decode the frequency of belonging," he said, as if quoting an instruction, "you have to listen to the shape of what is absent." [350]

"The pilgrim arrived a week before me," Kestrel said, suddenly sure. [479]
"And he found that certain pieces were missing from the chip, the very pieces that would have spelled out a single, long word." [502]
"What word?" Maro breathed. [506]

The light assembled itself into a single sentence in a script no living reader could name. [587]

Maro wept quietly, and Kestrel felt her hands steady for the first time since she had taken off. [669]
