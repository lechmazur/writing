<story>The falcon had been gone longer than usual, and its return settled over the rusted outpost by the frozen lake like a held breath finally released. [26] The editor did not look up from the window. [35] Inside the glass, the pattern of condensation was drawing itself in crooked tributaries, and the editor traced them with one finger as one might trace a familiar coastline in a borrowed atlas. [67] This was, after all, why they had been posted to this place: to find where the rivers begin. [85] Not the rivers of water alone, though those would have been reason enough, but the rivers of speech, the rivers of trade, and the slow, patient rivers of rumor that fed every map a person might stitch from hearsay. [124] A dog-eared corner of a polyglot's phrasebook poked from the editor's coat, marking the page that carried the word for spring in eleven languages, all of them slightly wrong. [153] The editor was, by training and by temperament, dry-witted, and had been told more than once that such a quality sat oddly in a herald. [178] They had replied, more than once, that heralds were paid to deliver news, not to enjoy it, and that the two occupations required entirely different temperaments. [204] Now, with the falcon returned, there was news to be delivered, and the editor had been sharpening their tongue against the quiet like a whetstone against a blade. [232] The worry was not the news itself, which was plain enough, but the worrying tranquility of the outpost, which seemed to be waiting, with a sort of held-breath patience, for the editor to disturb it. [267] The place had been abandoned by everyone except the editor and the bird, and the lake had frozen so thoroughly that the only sounds were the small, polite cracks of ice settling into its own bed. [303] The editor rose at last, crossed the small room to the desk where the atlas lay open, and found the falcon perched there, watching them with the patient disinterest of an old colleague. [336] "Well," the editor said to the bird, "you have brought me a singular convergence of troubles, and I suppose I ought to be flattered that they all decided to meet in me." [367] The falcon ruffled one feather in lieu of an answer. [377] The editor drew the phrasebook from the coat, opened it to the dog-eared page, and read aloud, in a voice pitched for an empty room, the eleven words for spring. [407] Each one, in its own tongue, sounded like the opening of a door. [420] The editor smiled, but the smile did not quite reach the worry beneath it, and the worry, in turn, was held in check by a kind of quiet, subtly confident posture the editor wore like a second coat against the cold. [462] They were, after all, an editor of atlases, and editors, like rivers, have a tendency to begin somewhere. [480] Beyond the glass, beyond the ice, the rivers were already beginning, and the editor intended to be there, with pen in hand, when they did. [505] But first, the herald's work, which is always done before the thing it heralds arrives, which is to say, in the company of its absence. [530] The editor took up the small brass bell that hung beside the door, rang it once, and listened as the sound crossed the ice and came back faintly, as if the lake were answering in a tongue of its own devising. [571] The falcon watched. [574] The pattern of condensation on the glass had thickened while the editor was not looking, and the tributaries they had traced earlier had begun, here and there, to merge into something that resembled, if one were of a certain mind, a single point of origin. [619] The editor studied it for a long moment, then shut the phrasebook, and the dog-eared corner folded back into place like a small, patient secret kept between the book and the bearer. [651] "There it is," they said, to no one, to everyone, to the falcon, to the lake, to the worried quiet that had, by then, begun to feel almost like a kind of welcome. [685] "The beginning."
