The catacomb orchard bloomed in defiance of every sensible law, its pale trees snaking their roots between the ribs and femurs of forgotten saints, and I worked there within the glow of half-lit corridors where phosphorescent moss softened every shadow into something almost tender. [44]
I had been called a reckless idealist by the elders, and the name had stuck because it fit precisely, the kind of fool who offers everything on the slim chance that something might grow from the offering. [81]
I had done precisely that the night my brother's soul cracked open like a winter river, bleeding light from places where light should not leak. [106]
So I bartered my own destiny at the bone-altar beneath the orchard, trading the long soft future I had imagined for the chance to patch holes in the soul of someone I loved more than my own continuance. [144]
The bargain was written on a tarot card, the Lovers, and I held it to the flame until only the singed outline of two figures remained, a burned tarot card curled in my palm like a question I was not allowed to ask twice. [188]
After that, the orchard became my chapel and my penance, and I learned to move among the trees with a fiercely gentle hand, cupping the seedlings as if they were the small fragile prayers of people who had stopped praying for themselves. [230]
My work was trammel-work, the careful tangling of roots around bones so that the dead might anchor the living and the living might be reminded they were not the only creatures learning to stand. [264]
I wove meaning into everything, inlaying meaning everywhere a thread could pass, looping it through a femur here and a clavicle there until the whole orchard became a slow-read sentence someone, somewhere, was beginning to decipher. [300]
The trees had grown over saints and sinners with equal indifference, and I loved them for that, the way their bark wore the forgotten names like a rosary worn smooth by patient fingers. [333]
Some nights, when the moss-light turned the corridors the color of old honey, I walked between the trunks and listened for my brother's name, and the orchard, which had taken my future in exchange for his continued breath, hummed it back to me like a secret. [379]
I had expected to feel cheated, but the dusky resolve that settled into my chest instead felt like a second kind of weather, the kind of dusk that does not end in darkness but only in the slow gold of an evening that has decided to stay. [426]
There were visitors sometimes, the desperate and the dying, who crept down into the half-lit corridors with their palms full of small wounds, and I met each with the same unhurried tenderness I gave the seedlings. [462]
I could not undo my bargain, nor did I wish to, for the reckless idealist in me knew that some futures are not worth keeping if keeping them means leaving someone else to fade into the quiet that never ends. [502]
Instead I worked, and the work was enough, and when the moss glowed brightest I could feel my brother breathing somewhere far above me, alive because I had traded the long arc of my own story for the small steady pulse of his. [545]
I held no grudge against the bargain, only the quiet stubborn joy of a gardener who has learned that some seeds must be planted with the whole hand, and that the holes in a soul can sometimes be mended with the substance of one's own unspoken tomorrows. [592]
So I remained, the reckless idealist of the catacomb orchard, fiercely gentle in the dusk, inlaying meaning everywhere I could reach, trammeling roots around bones, tending the half-lit corridors with the steadfast heart of someone who has finally understood that the bravest thing a person can do with their life is to let it be the bandage for another's.
