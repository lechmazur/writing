<story>The first drops of spring rain tapped the copper weathervane like hesitant fingers, and I let them in through the cracked window of the tower. [25] I had been away longer than the policy permitted, and the world that greeted my return felt filtered through someone else's memory. [47] After making first contact, the sky had changed color in the official record, but in this room the only new thing was the dust settling on my abandoned notebooks. [76] I was what the diplomatic corps once called a luminous skeptic, the kind of scientist who insisted on measuring what others merely felt, and I had carried that title like a tarnished compass back to the hill country of my childhood. [117] The tower itself was not a beacon, despite its silhouette; it was a study shed my grandmother had built to count the stars, and now, in the rainwashed quiet of April, it smelled of wet stone and old varnish. [156] I had built my reputation on a single, academically rebellious paper arguing that the amber we studied preserved not just insects but the emotional residue of their final hours, and I had been laughed out of three conferences before the aliens arrived. [198] The aliens did not laugh; they sent a courier, a long-limbed being who breathed in chromatic pulses, and asked to see the amber. [221] I had taken a small, flat piece with me on the first diplomatic flight: a fly in amber the size of a thumbnail, the wing caught mid-beat, the body perfectly still, and the courier had leaned close to the stone and made a sound that my translator rendered as, "Ah, the one who never landed." [276] That phrase haunted me; it was the closest the aliens ever came to religion, and I became obsessed with understanding it, with the chemistry of homecoming, the way a molecule might yearn for the place where it had last been whole. [317] I had to return, not because the council asked me to, but because my grandmother had died while I was in orbit, and the tower, with its crooked stair, was the only monument to her I would inherit. [355] I designed, perhaps foolishly, what I called an artificial pilgrimage: a journey I would script for myself, a series of deliberate stops that would test whether returning home could be engineered the way one engineers a reaction. [392] I left the starship at the regional port and walked; the road I chose was the old sculpture path, the one that wound through the regional park where my grandmother had taken me to count bronze heads of forgotten mayors. [432] It was overgrown; the hedges had grown taller than I remembered, and the bronze figures, half-swallowed by ivy, glistened with the season's first real rain. [457] I followed my own dusty footprints, the ones I had pressed into the path that morning, and I watched them converge with the deeper, older prints left by some pilgrim I had never met, perhaps a deer, perhaps a person, perhaps only the wind. [501] I counted seven convergences in the first kilometer, and at each one I paused to mark the spot with a small cairn of river stones I had carried in my pack. [532] The path ended at the base of the tower, and I climbed the spiral stair with rain still dripping from my hair, the fly amber warm against my chest where I had hung it on a leather thong. [570] Inside, the room was as I had left it, except that a single envelope lay on the desk, addressed in my grandmother's handwriting, dated the day she had died. [599] I did not open it, not yet; instead I sat on the stool, took out the fly amber, and held it to the gray light of the window, and the insect inside glowed with the bronze quiet I had been chasing across the sky. [643] The courier's words came back to me: "the one who never landed." I understood then that the fly was not a prisoner but a traveler, that the amber was not a tomb but a small, warm airport, and that the chemistry of homecoming was the slow, patient act of letting the resin hold you until you were ready to fly again. [704] Outside, the rain softened, and somewhere below a bell in the town began to toll, and I, the luminous skeptic, finally let myself believe that the journey home could begin, even when the home had already gone.
