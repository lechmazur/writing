Mira had guided visitors through the Alcove of Echoes for nineteen winters, but only now, in the thaw after the beekeeper's last visit, did she understand why the grotto city behind the monumental waterfall had accepted her people as displaced persons. [41]
The beekeeper had come once, in the year of the ash bloom, carrying nothing but a single jar of honey and a long memory of the songs her grandmother had sung. [31]
He had listened to the elders, tasted the bitter moss-bread of the cavern kitchens, and then he had vanished into the spray above the falls, promising nothing. [27]
What he left behind was a question, and the question was thawing now along with the ice that sealed the upper galleries. [22]
Mira was a cultured museum guide, historically oriented to a fault, and she kept the city's scattered archives in her head the way other women kept recipes. [27]
Every dusk she climbed the wet basalt stairs to the Hall of Tides, where the last painted screens of her homeland were slowly dissolving into the stone. [27]
The colors had retreated so far that the naked eye saw only shadows, so she trained the emerald-tinted opera glass lens her mother had pressed into her palm the night their village burned. [33]
Through that green eye the pigments rose again, phoenix-bright, and Mira traced the brushwork of masters who had died with their names unspoken. [23]
The displaced persons of the grotto had carried their arts into exile as one carries a child through a flood, cradled, hushed, half-drowned. [23]
Now the cradles were failing, and the rescue of those dying arts had become her private vow, spoken to no one, broken only when she kneaded dough at midnight. [29]
Her grandmother had taught her a family custom of baking bread shaped like initials, one loaf for each art that refused to die. [23]
The bread was not for eating; it was a quiet census, a roll-call of forms still breathing in the dark. [20]
Tonight she shaped a slim crescent for the lost lullaby of the salt-makers, a tight coil for the opera of the weavers, and a small, defiant star for the cathedral of paper lanterns that had once hung over the river of her birth. [43]
As the dough rose beneath her hands, the rosewater steam curled through the kitchen like a warning dressed in perfume, and Mira felt the dread she had been naming all winter. [31]
It was the dread of sweet things that must end, of relief that tastes of loss, of being saved in a place where saving felt like slow burial. [28]
She arranged the initials on a wooden tray and carried them up, past the sleeping lanterns, to the cold mouth of the waterfall itself. [24]
The spray wet her sleeves and the emerald lens glowed faintly against her chest, and she whispered the names of the arts into the roar so the falls might carry them somewhere the world could still hear. [37]
Behind her, an old man with honey-stained fingers emerged from the mist. [12]
He had not vanished, she realized; he had only been waiting in the upper grottoes, listening to the ice. [19]
The beekeeper smiled, and from his coat he drew a folded ledger, each page crowded with the same initials she had just baked, recorded across forty years of quiet returns. [30]
He told her that the rescued arts were never meant to live in a single grotto, but to drift, the way pollen drifts, until they found a soil that remembered them. [31]
Mira understood, then, that the thaw was not an ending but a permission, and she pressed her warm bread into his hands so the next traveler would know what she had tried to save. [34]
Above them the falls roared on, rosewater and dread and all, and the city behind the water exhaled into another century, still hidden, still holding its breath. [27]
Mira turned back to her empty tray, wiped the flour from her fingers, and began to shape the next letter before dawn could steal it from her. [27]
