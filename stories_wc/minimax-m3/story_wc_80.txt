<story>Maren Kade was a firm shore surveyor, and on the evening after the drawbridge rose, she walked alone along the seawall. [21] The bridge had been lifted against the season's late squall, sealing the village into its small, lamp-lit island, and she had lingered behind, preferring the wind to the gossip of the harbor tavern. [54] Her boots were heavy with old mud as she descended the slick stairs to the tidal basin. [71] Below the basin, half-claimed by the sea, stood a submerged pavilion: a small domed structure, its latticed windows furred with green, its marble benches just visible through the brown water. [101] She had been hired to map it, to plot its edges for the maritime registry, but she had come to think of it as something else entirely: a place where the absences of other lives had been carefully woven into the absence of her own. [146] That was the interlaced absence she had been trying to chart. [157] She produced from her satchel an antique traveling inkwell, its brass rim dented, its stopper carved from a piece of petrified driftwood, and uncapped it with a small twist. [186] The nib of her pen caught the last copper light. [196] She had been told, in the cautious way of her elders, that a surveyor measures; she had come to believe, in her stoically sensitive heart, that measuring was only the half of it. [229] What she really wished to do, what had drawn her down these dripping stairs more than any contract, was to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams: to lift the silt from the small wishes people had once whispered into such places and to set them, finally, on paper. [277] The pavilion, she suspected, had been a place for promises. [287] She stepped into the shallows where the water reached her knees. [298] The cold bit through her trousers, but she did not flinch; she had wrestled with colder seas, and with the older cold of the village's silence about her work, and she intended to wrest something more from this one. [337] She knelt on a sunken step and began, as she always did, with the foundations. [352] The pavilion's stone base had been repaired, generations ago, with pale bricks set into the older masonry. [369] She carried a sheaf of small folded notes, each one bearing a single line in her careful hand. [387] Into the freshest of the foundation repairs she pressed the first: A girl once stood here, thinking of her mother. [407] The bricks were loose enough to accept the paper without crumbling, and she worked the note home with the brass cap of the inkwell. [431] Into the next repair she slid another: A boy promised his sister the moon, and kept it in a jar. [451] Into the next: A widow, year after year, asked the tide to bring her husband home. [467] Into the next: A sailor, who never came, whose name no one now pronounces. [481] Each note was a small rescued ember. [488] She felt them kindle inside her as she worked, a hushed blaze that did not roar, that did not ask permission, that simply burned at the correct temperature for grief. [518] It was, she thought, the only honest way to survey a shore: not to measure what remained, but to recover what had been deliberately left behind. [544] The water rose another inch as the tide turned. [553] Above her, the drawbridge stood open against the darkening sky, a black arm raised in farewell or warning; she could not tell which, and in her stoically sensitive way she accepted both readings. [586] She had one note left in her pocket, and one unfilled repair left in the western wall. [603] She uncapped the inkwell again and wrote, in letters as steady as her voice had never been: The surveyor, at last, allowed herself a dream. [628] She placed the note into the stone and pressed it home. [639] When she stood, the pavilion's water had reached her waist, and the brass in her hand was warm from the dipping and from the small, persistent fire she carried. [668] She would return tomorrow with fresh notes, and the day after, and the day after that, until the foundation was full and the dreams were, at last, no longer abandoned. [698] She was, in the truest sense she knew, beginning. [707]</story>
