<story>Maren set the chipped porcelain cup on the brass arm of her reading lamp, watching the coffee settle into its own dark country inside the bowl. [26] Outside, midsummer pressed its heavy sweetness against the velvet-lined walls of the observatory, and somewhere in the rafters a moth kept tapping at a lantern no one had lit in years. [57] She was a provenance researcher, paid to certify what things were and where they had come from, and tonight she was meant to be sleeping. [82] Instead she sat beneath a glass case holding a violin so transparent it looked like a breath held too long, every curve and f-hole cut from a single ice crystal that had not melted in a hundred summers of curious hands. [123] The instrument had arrived at her desk three weeks ago with a hole in its paper trail, a perforated destiny of ownership that everyone kept politely pretending was whole. [152] The buyer waiting at the far end of the velvet corridor had called the gap a trifle, an administrative oversight, but Maren had felt the holes all the way through her ribs. [184] When she bent to study the ground glass of the crystal's belly, the ivy in the windowsill leaned toward her shoulder and whispered, low and startled, that something underneath the floorboards was afraid. [217] She had been accidentally telepathic with plants since she was seven, which had made her a strange child and a stranger scholar, and which she had learned to translate into metaphors when talking to colleagues who were not. [255] Tonight the ivy told her about roots pushing up through the archive basement, about a seed vault sealed since the last dynasty fell, about a cutting that wanted to come home. [286] She touched the cool curve of the violin and thought of her mother, who had once promised to leave her something that mattered and had not yet. [313] Maren turned back to her cup and tilted it slowly toward the candle flame, the way her grandmother had taught her when coffee was still read in kitchens and not in observatories. [345] The grounds made a shape like a small bird with its throat cut, then a shape like a door half open, then a shape that could only be read as a question. [377] She had spent her whole life weighing dreams against empires, and she was tired, and the promise her mother had made was beginning to lose its glow along the eastern wall of the sky. [411] If she certified the violin as legitimate, the buyer would donate it to the Imperial Conservatory, and it would be played in a hall she would never enter, and her name would rise. [444] If she refused, she would lose her position, lose her stipend, lose the velvet roof above her, and walk out into the alleys with only her notebooks and the soft frightened voices of the plants she had tended for years. [484] She thought of propagating the cutting the ivy had described, a tender act of smuggling a green heart past the checkpoint at the archive gate. [509] She thought of propagating a lie instead, a clean and glowing paper trail stitched neatly across the perforated history, the kind she had been trained to admire and the kind her mother would have recognized from a distance as rot. [549] The moth dropped at last from the rafters and lay still against the lantern glass, and the candle flame bent sideways as if listening. [573] Maren lifted the violin from its case and felt the cold of it run up through her palms and settle behind her eyes, and the ivy said, very softly, that she already knew. [606] She carried it across the velvet floor to the observation slit where midsummer was breathing in through the curtains, and she set it down beside the coffee cup and the candle and the moth. [640] Then she took up her pen and wrote a single line in the margin of the certificate: that the history of the object was not continuous, that the holes in it were the truth, and that she could not pretend otherwise before a promise she had made to a dead woman and a living child. [695] She did not sleep that night, but she did not weep either, and somewhere in the archive basement a sealed seed cracked its small perforated shell and reached, with the stubborn patience of green things, for the dark.
