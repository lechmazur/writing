Mara had watched the reef bleach the year the last coal plant closed. [13] She remembered the pallor of it, the staghorn corals gone bone-white in a single tide cycle, the fish losing their bearings above a city ten thousand years old. [42] Now she worked at a sea level monitoring station, a low concrete building on a basalt shelf the old surveys had pronounced safe from any rise the century could conjure. [72] The station was quiet in the years after the fossil fuel ban, the buoys still surfacing with their patient telemetry, but the urgency gone from the work like dye from a faded flag. [105] Mara's fingers found the counter and tapped a slow absent rhythm against its enameled edge, three taps, a pause, two taps, a pause, the pattern her mother had drummed on the kitchen table when the reef news grew too heavy for silence. [147] She had carried that pattern across a decade and a thousand kilometers, and now it lived in the steel cabinets, on the railings, in the shoulder of anyone who stood too long beside her. [181] Today she was trying to assemble a chart of vanished paths, the council having requested it in the way councils request things, which is to say without ever saying so. [211] Why did some coastal footways simply cease to exist? [220] Why had the route from the headland to the fishing cove, walked for seven generations, disappeared from the new surveys as though it had been edited out of the world? [250] Mara spread her materials across the long table: tide logs, hydrographic charts, hand-drawn maps older than herself. [268] And then her hand paused on a sheet that did not belong. [280] It was a page torn from an atlas, ragged along one edge, foxed with age, printed in ink that had gone the color of weak tea. [306] She had no memory of placing it in the file. [316] On it, a coastline she half recognized was drawn in confident lines, and across the lower margin someone had written, in a hurried hand, the words: the path still runs here, do not let them redraw it. [353] Mara read it twice, and the meaning did not arrive. [363] She read it a third time, and the delayed revelation began to assemble itself in her chest like a tide she had not known was coming. [389] The atlas was from the early century, before the bans, back when the shore had still been trusted to hold. [409] Somewhere in its printing, a cartographer had included the path she was hunting. [422] Somewhere, perhaps in the same archive, the page that had shown it had been removed. [437] She turned the fragment over, and on its back, in pencil, was a small notation: checked against aerial, 1987. [456] Mara sat down slowly on the stool beside the tide gauge. [467] Outside, the buoys reported a sea level that, in the long arithmetic of the planet, was no longer rising. [486] The ban had worked, more or less, and the water had steadied, but the land had not remembered how to be land. [508] The paths had not returned, because the paths had been taken from the maps first, long before the water had taken them from the ground. [533] She understood, finally, that the disappearance she had been sent to investigate was not a question of geology. [551] It was a question of paper. [557] And the practice of tapping rhythms on surfaces, which had begun as grief and become a habit and then a small private language, now felt to her like the only honest cartography left: the marking of a place by the sound one makes while standing in it, periodically eternal, recurring with the patience of tides that no longer rise and yet still arrive. [620] She would write her report in the morning. [628] For now she sat at the station with the torn page balanced on her knee, and she tapped, and the sound drifted out across the water, and the water received it, and the station held her, and she let herself be both drifting and moored, anchored to the counter and to the question, adrift on the answer she had carried for thirty years without knowing she was carrying it.
