<story>The mountain ranger cabin had been silent for three winters when Mira finally cracked the lock on the supply closet. [20] Inside, beneath a moth-eaten wool blanket, she found a birch bark scroll curled tight as a sleeping fist. [38] She was a lucid dream chronicler by trade, though trade was a generous word for what she did - she traded sleep for syntax, trading her hours for the privilege of transcribing the impossible. [71] The scroll smelled of pine resin and something older, like a library that had been forgotten by its own books. [91] She unrolled it on the cabin's warped table, and the characters already there were her own handwriting. [108] That was the part she had stopped being able to explain to anyone. [121] After seeing her own grave - a fresh mound of earth behind the ranger station, marked with a wooden cross that read her name in a font she had never taught herself to write - she had walked into the cabin and refused to come out. [165] Now, six months later or possibly six weeks, her sense of time had grown feral and wild, roaming wherever it pleased. [186] She was chronologically confused in a way that felt less like a flaw and more like a country she had emigrated to. [208] The dream last night had been the worst kind. [217] She had soared above the cabin, above the pines, above the clouds, and from that height she had watched a younger version of herself - perhaps twenty-two, perhaps a decade younger in some other counting - walking down the mountain road with a knapsack and a clear purpose. [263] The younger Mira had not yet seen the grave. [272] The younger Mira had not yet stopped sleeping through the night. [283] The younger Mira's hands were steady, and her eyes held the ordinary bewilderment of the living. [299] And chronicler Mira, watching from the clouds, had felt something twist in her chest that she could only name later as feral nostalgia - a longing so untamed it bordered on hunger. [330] She had wanted to warn her past self. [338] She had also wanted to become her past self, to crawl back into that unmarked body and stay. [356] This was the method she now recognized in herself: getting jealous of past selves. [370] She did it with the same unconscious discipline with which other people brushed their teeth. [385] Each morning she unrolled the scroll and tried to translate the night into daylight, to distill chaos into narrative, because that was the only alchemy she knew. [412] The birch bark took the ink in long blue veins. [422] She wrote about the soaring. [427] She wrote about the grave, though she knew the grave was a metaphor the dreaming part of her kept insisting was a fact. [450] She wrote about the cabin, about how the wood creaked at midnight in a voice that sounded exactly like her grandmother's, about how the stove never quite warmed the room where she slept. [483] She wrote with the tone of someone who had stopped being surprised by wonder and started being exhausted by it - jaded wonder, she supposed, though she had never been able to decide if those two words fit together like hands or like strangers forced to share a bench. [531] In the afternoon, a hiker passed the window. [539] The chronicler did not wave. [544] She had tried waving, once, and the hiker had looked through her as if she were a pane of glass. [564] So she went back to the scroll, where the younger version of her was now writing alongside her in matching script, a collaboration across the broken calendar of her mind. [594] They argued about punctuation. [598] They agreed, finally, that the story could not be told in order. [610] It would have to be told the way memory actually worked - in drifts, in returns, in the sudden smell of a kitchen that no longer existed. [636] The chronicler set down her pen. [642] Outside, the snow had begun, or perhaps it had never stopped. [653] She could no longer tell. [658] The birch bark scroll lay open between them - the two Miras, the present and the past, the one who had seen the grave and the one who had not - and the room held its breath the way a held note holds a song. [701] There was a kind of freedom in being lost to your own chronology. [714] There was also, she had to admit, a small relief. [724] If she was no longer sure which Mira was writing, then perhaps neither of them was entirely alone. [742] She picked up the pen again. [748] The story would continue. [752] It always did.
