<story>The junkyard prophet lived in a fog sculpture garden, a place where vapor gathered into shapes that breathed with the morning. [21] He had forgotten his own name before he learned it, a small mercy of precognitively forgetful grace - his mind dropping future sorrows like stones into still water, never to splash when they arrived. [54] Each dawn he walked the garden paths with a piece of plain chalk and a bundle of driftwood, arranging the bleached branches as directional markers along the gravel. [82] The driftwood pointed toward where the fog thickened into figures: a woman, a dog, a cathedral with no door. [101] Visitors who came rarely knew which way led out. [110]

He was looking for something he could not name, though his hands knew it. [124] He called it the sound of growing older - the creak of bones lengthening, the faint static at the edge of hearing, the sigh a chair makes when a body leaves it for the last time. [159] He wanted to capture it the way one captures a moth. [170] In the back of the garden, behind a curtain of mist, there was a small shed where he spent his days amid the hush of old videotapes left unwatched. [199] On a warped shelf sat a stack of them, recorded decades ago by hands he did not remember. [217] The hush was almost a presence. [223] He had not yet watched any of them. [231]

He suspected that if he did, the sound he sought would be in their magnetic grain. [247] He touched the chalk to his palm when he thought of this. [259] The mark he drew was the outline of a face he had not yet met. [274] The garden changed with the hour. [280] Some afternoons the fog lifted and revealed ordinary things: a wheelbarrow, a tarp, a child's bicycle half-buried. [297] Most mornings the sculptures returned - more defined, more patient. [306] He spoke to them the way a man speaks to old friends he has outlived. [321] "I am still here," he told a fog-woman with her hair loose. [333] "I have not forgotten you." [338]

This was, perhaps, untrue. [342] He had forgotten almost everything, including what he was supposed to remember. [354] But his forgetting came ahead of his life, like a herald, and so he always knew what he was about to lose. [376] He had learned this in the junkyard, where he had lived before the garden took him in. [393] The other prophets there had thrown him out because he quoted tomorrow's regrets. [406] They said it was unkind. [411] He had taken it as a compliment. [418]

Now he arranged driftwood - a long piece angled like an arrow, a curved branch bent like a question mark - and he followed his own paths. [443] Today the markers led him to the shed. [451] He stopped outside and listened. [456] The tapes were still there. [461] He could hear their small, packed silence. [468] He thought: when I am older than I am now, I will play one, and I will hear a voice I used to be. [492] But he was already older. [497] He was older the moment he thought it. [505] The chalk in his pocket grew heavier, the way a key grows heavier in a lock. [521]

He stepped inside. [524] The shed smelled of cedar and old electricity. [532] He took down the first tape and held it to the light. [544] The label was blank. [548] He drew a single chalk line across the spool, then drew it again, marking the days he had touched the thing. [569] Outside, the fog shifted. [573] The cathedral sculpture turned its head, though it had no head. [584] The prophet felt the tide of the garden - that long tidal longing - rise through him like weather. [601] He would locate the sound tonight. [607]

He would find it in the hush of the tapes, in the driftwood pointing home, in the chalk that knew him before he did. [631] He sat down on the shed floor with the tape in his lap. [644] He did not play it. [649] Instead, he began to write, in chalk on the wooden boards, the names of everyone he had forgotten. [667] The names were long, and they curved like driftwood, and the fog came in through the door to read them over his shoulder. [690] When the tide turned, he would still be there. [699] This was the prophecy he believed: that he would cling to the echoes of what he had not yet lost, and that this clinging would be the sound of his own growing older, played at last, watched at last, in a garden where nothing was ever truly unwatched.
