<story>The canopy of Yelmara swallowed the sky, and in that green hush Lira climbed the rope ladders that laced the village together. [22] She was an ambitious herbalist, the kind who listened to leaves and trusted roots more than elders. [39] Born with a nose that could parse the whisper of a violet from a mile away, she had trained herself to be olfactorily sensitive, mapping the mysterious treetop village by scent the way a cartographer maps by light. [77] Her hands were stained with turmeric, and her pockets always smelled faintly of mint and regret. [93] At the turning of the tide's thought, when the wind shifted from west to east and the canopy seemed to inhale, Lira would retreat to her loft to journalize. [122] She kept her notes on the insides of old wrapping paper, a family custom of doodling on wrapping paper interiors that her mother had taught her when she was small and the world was a series of small, safe packages. [162] The doodles were not idle; they were maps, recipes, and prayers, the way a river's surface hides the undercurrent of wisdom that runs beneath every visible thing. [189] Tonight, though, her mind would not settle. [196] She had been given a single corpse flower seed by a trader from the lowlands, and the responsibility of it sat in her palm like a sleeping coal. [224] To grow the great Amorphophallus was to invite a stink so profound it could curdle milk and wake the dead, and the village council had already forbidden it, citing decency and trade. [256] Lira's ambition was not mere vanity. [262] She had read in the cracked pages of a banned book that the bloom, once it unfurled, released a scent so close to mortality that those who smelled it often remembered their ancestors and wept, and in that weeping found a rare stillness. [305] Her deepest desire was to cultivate serenity within motion, to plant a thing so still at the heart of the village's constant climbing and falling that the people might pause and breathe together for one full, fragrant hour. [343] She uncorked her ink, a brew of squid and ash, and began to draw. [357] On a square of last winter's gift wrap, dotted with faded snowmen, she sketched the seed in cross-section, then the sprout, then the leaf, then the impossible flower. [385] Around the drawings she wrote, in her slanting hand, the questions she had carried since childhood: where does a scent come from? [407] Does memory live in the nose, or in the mind that interprets the nose? [421] Why do the wisest among the elders always seem to smell faintly of cedar, even in summer? [438] As she wrote, the wind through the loft shifted, and the wildflower hush of the forest pressed against the walls, soft as felt. [461] Lira paused, breathed, and let the moment be more than ink. [472] She thought of her grandmother, who had told her that every plant is a sentence the earth is speaking, and that the undercurrent of wisdom runs not in the loud blooms but in the roots that hold the bank. [511] She dipped her pen again. [516] If the village would not let her plant the seed in the common soil, she would plant it in a story, and stories, she knew, were the slowest and most stubborn seeds of all. [550] She drew the treetop village in a single spiraling line, the rope ladders and moss-roofed huts and the central hearth where the elders met, and at the spiral's heart she placed a small, dark seed, waiting. [586] When she finished, she folded the wrapping paper twice and slid it into a hollow in the floorboard, next to three others she had made in previous seasons. [614] Then she climbed back up to the roof, where the wind was strongest, and watched the canopy breathe. [632] Below, the village murmured its evening songs, and above, the first stars pricked through the leaves. [648] Lira closed her eyes and let the smell of resin and distant rain settle into her chest. [665] The corpse flower seed remained in her pocket, unplanted, patient, a question she would carry until the next turning of the tide's thought, and the one after that, and the one after that, until the answer came not as a bloom but as a breath.
