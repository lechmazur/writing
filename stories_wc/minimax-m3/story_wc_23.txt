The signal station stood on a wind-scoured bluff, its dish tilted toward a starlit sky no one had listened to in years. [22] Mara found it the night the hydraulics failed along the southern aqueducts, when the valley below lost its water and the cranes began flying the wrong way. [49] She had been a navigator of routes that no longer existed, stitching broken maps from ferry schedules, dairy ledgers, and the embroidery on her grandmother's sleeves. [75] People in the lower towns called her a patchwork navigator, half in mockery, half in awe, because she could read a road by the soot on its signposts. [103] Tonight, the soot told her nothing. [109] The cranes were returning to a marsh that had dried three generations ago, and she could not understand the grammar of their reverse migration. [133] She climbed the access ladder, the rungs cold under her calloused palms, and ducked into the control room where a single bulb swung from a cord. [159] The handset on the console still held a charge, though the station's hydraulics had long since given up, and somewhere in the floorboards a patina bronze censer foot had been wedged as a doorstop by whoever had last prayed here. [199] Mara picked it up and felt, against her thumb, the faint tremor of a hum that fluctuates with moon phases, a vibration she had learned to trust in the years since her son vanished along the coast road. [237] When the bronze was warm, the signal was good. [246] When it was cold, she did not transmit. [254] At midnight, the phone rang. [259] It always rang at midnight. [264] She lifted the receiver and listened. [270] A voice she did not recognize spoke in a language of numbers and bird calls, reciting coordinates that Mara wrote onto the plaster wall with a stub of charcoal. [299] The calls were cryptic, sometimes lasting only a few syllables, sometimes a long slow chanting of tides and failing pumps. [319] She answered with the names of villages that no longer had children, and the caller responded with names of villages that no longer had walls. [344] Between them, they were building something. [350] Not a tower, not a beacon, but a map drawn in charcoal and salt, a record of every return that defied the logic of drought and ruin. [377] Mara believed, though she could not prove, that the caller was one of the cranes. [392] For weeks, she worked. [396] She repaired the dish by cannibalizing the station's old pump, because the hydraulics had failed and there was nothing left to pump, and she lashed the censer foot to the apex of the antenna with copper wire so its glow, when the moon was full, could be seen from the marsh below. [448] The light was thin and green, the color of a river remembering itself, and on clear nights the cranes circled it in slow descending spirals, as if trying to remember a nesting song. [481] Mara called down to them the names of their lost deltas, and the birds called back the names of the sisters and brothers they had outlived. [507] The geography of loss, she was learning, was a country with its own rivers, and to navigate it you had to be willing to be lost inside it. [535] There were nights when the raw solace of the work left her shaking. [548] There were nights when the handset stayed silent and the censer foot stayed cold and she walked the perimeter of the bluff just to feel the gravel shift under her boots. [579] Once, she found the wing feather of a juvenile crane pressed into the mud, still warm, and she held it to her cheek and whispered the name of the road her son had taken. [613] She did not hear an answer. [619] She did not expect one. [624] But the next night, the phone rang, and the caller, for the first time, said her son's name in the middle of a string of latitudes, and Mara laughed, a broken sound, and wrote the name on the wall in letters larger than the rest. [669] By the time the rains returned, if they returned, the wall would be a kilometer of names. [686] The cranes would have their marsh. [692] Mara would have her map. [697] And somewhere in the static between midnight calls, a mother and a son would find, in the cracked voice of a stranger, the long, patient work of coming home. [726]
