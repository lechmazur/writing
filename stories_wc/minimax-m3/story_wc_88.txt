<story>The descent into the cavern smelled of salt and old ink, and Maren followed the glow with the measured caution of a woman who had spent years learning to want nothing. [31]
She had been a thief of small mercies and a betrayer of larger ones, and the procedure the clinic performed on her in the gray hour before dawn had burned the memory of names and faces down to ash. [70]
This was the beginning she had paid for in cash and confession. [82]
Below her the tunnel opened onto an underground bioluminescent lake, its surface alive with a soft, bacterial blue that moved like breath, and at its far shore stood a structure of bone-white stone that seemed, against all reason, delicately robust, as if it had been grown rather than built. [131]
The cabboat waiting at the water's edge was unmanned but breathing, its ribs inflating in slow tide with the lake. [151]
She stepped in, and it moved without protest. [159]
For hours she drifted, and at each branching channel a paper boat floated up to meet her, each one made from the clippings of a newspaper no one on the surface still read. [192]
She had been told this was how the labyrinth would speak to her: by reading between newspaper lines, through headlines that did not match the stories beneath, through weather reports that described only weather from a century ahead, through the small, accidental poems that hid in the spacing between columns. [242]
A headline read: MAN WHO WILL ARRIVE TOMORROW HAS ALREADY SIGNED BASEBALL. [254]
A subhead beneath it warned: BASEBALL CONFIRMED AUTHENTIC BY TIME TRAVELER, AUTHENTICITY DEBATED. [267]
She reached into the next paper boat and lifted, cradled in tissue like a sleeping moth, a baseball signed by a time traveler. [290]
The signature was a smudge of blue ink and three glyphs that did not belong to any alphabet she knew, and when she held the ball, she could feel it hum against her palms, faintly, like a song sung with the mouth closed. [333]
A choice unfolded before her like a fan: she could keep the ball and become, by its strange law, the first witness of its impossible provenance, or she could read the next clipping and learn who had been meant to receive it. [375]
The labyrinth of choices was patient; it did not hurry one with a dirty past toward a clean slate. [394]
It only offered paper, ink, and the lake's glow, and waited. [405]
She unfolded the next page, and there, between the lines of a stock report and a recipe for bread, she read the name of a child who had died in a fire she herself had set, fifteen years ago, in a town whose name had been erased from her alongside everything else the procedure had burned away. [462]
She had not known, before the burning, that she was searching for any of this. [477]
She had only known she wanted to be a person who could feel pity for a stranger's grief. [495]
The lake light trembled, and the cabboat turned of its own accord toward a shore she had not chosen, where the bone-white structure had a single open doorway and a bench inside, and on the bench sat the ghost of no one in particular, who looked at her with the mild, unsurprised gaze of someone who had seen this arrival before. [556]
To kindle empathy where none remains is to thread a needle with eyes closed, she thought, and yet here she was, sitting down, opening her hands, and setting the signed baseball on the bench between them like an offering or a question. [598]
The ghost did not answer. [603]
The lake breathed. [606]
The newsprint rustled softly in the dark, and somewhere above, a city she no longer remembered continued without her, and beneath her, in a paper boat drifting back toward the tunnel mouth, a single headline waited for the next traveler: MAN ASKS WHY HE MUST STILL CHOOSE. [653]
She smiled, and the smile was the first clean thing she had made in years. [668]</story>
