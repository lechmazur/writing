<story>Maren had spent thirty years cataloguing the small oblivions of shoreline rock, and tonight she carried the same patience into the ancient grotto where her brother waited. [27] The cave roof arched overhead like cupped hands, and through its broken crown the stars fell in pale, indifferent handfuls. [47] Cael sat on a shelf of limestone, his back curved forward, his face turned toward the dark mouth of the sea. [66] He had come to say something he could not finish, and she had come to listen until he could. [84] Between them, the conversation had thinned to the sound of water tracing the stone, a small, repeating argument with itself. [104] It was in this pause, this held breath between one truth and the next, that Maren felt the familiar pulse of her work begin. [127] She reached into the pocket of her coat and drew out the jade cicada, the one she had found in a tide pool the morning their mother died. [153] Its wings were folded against its back, and the green of it caught starlight as if it remembered being alive. [173] She did not look at Cael directly. [180] Instead, she watched the thin crescent of moonlight braid across the water pooled on the grotto floor, and she let the image do what her voice could not. [207] A tidal pool, she thought, is a country in transition. [216] It belongs to the rock and to the sea, and the chronicler task is to record the moment before the tide returns and makes the choice for it. [243] She had always believed that one could foresee the future, if one came at it sideways, through what was almost beside the point. [266] The cicada was jade because jade holds its color for a thousand years, and cicadas because they wait their whole lives underground only to step into the light when the soil is ready. [296] Cael had not spoken in several minutes. [303] His hands rested open on his knees, palms up, as if he were offering something unnamed to the cave. [321] Maren turned the jade insect slowly in her fingers, feeling the faintest warmth of it, a warmth that had nothing to do with her own. [344] She thought of every pool she had ever mapped, how each one taught her that transition periods were not empty spaces but full ones, brimming with small negotiations between what stayed and what was leaving. [379] Tonight, in this grotto under the stars, the pause between her brother and herself was its own kind of tide pool, and she found herself wanting to know its exact texture, the way one knows the weight of a familiar stone lifted from the sand. [423] She was a warmly observant woman, had always been, and observation to her was not a cold thing but a tender one, a laying of the hand gently against the world's surface to feel what beat beneath. [459] So she watched Cael breathe, and she watched the water, and she let the two rhythms speak to each other without her help. [480] Through the tangential approach of the pool, of the cicada, of the slow turn of green stone in her palm, she began to foresee what her brother could not yet say: that he was afraid of leaving their coastal town, that the woman he loved had been offered work inland, and that he had come to ask her blessing for a journey he had already decided to take. [540] He was afraid she would feel abandoned. [547] He was afraid she would not. [553] Maren set the cicada down on the ledge between them, where it sat gleaming like a small green lantern. [571] She did not pick it up again. [577] The pause held, and she let it hold, and in letting it hold she discovered the texture of time, which was not smooth and not rough but layered, like the inside of a shell, each layer a different season of patience. [616] When at last Cael spoke, his voice was small and clear, and she answered him with the only word the grotto had taught her was ever enough. [642] Stay a moment, she said, and the tide will tell us what comes next. [657] The stars above did not answer, but the pool did, lifting a little at the moon, then settling, then lifting again.
