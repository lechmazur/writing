<story>The brass key in my pocket had belonged to someone else for sixty years, and tonight I was returning it to the rooftop garden where it had always wanted to be. [31] I had not intended to come during the meteor shower, but the stars were falling in such an obliging cascade that the city paused and tilted upward, and I followed. [61] The garden belonged to a woman named Eleni, my Latin tutor, who had been dead for nine months, though I still spoke of her in the present because she had asked me to. [94] We had met through the polite pretense of borrowing sugar, the way the old city arranges such things. [112] I had knocked on her door one morning, a student of architecture pretending to need sugar for a cake I was not baking, and she had opened it with the smile of someone who also knew that no one bakes a cake at eight. [156] She handed me the sugar and asked what I was really after, and I told her the truth, that I wanted to learn the language of old buildings, and she said this was the most sensible lie she had ever been told, and she invited me in. [203] That single cup was the beginning of the agreed upon lies, a phrase we invented on a Tuesday when neither of us could admit we had simply grown fond of each other. [235] For seven years we kept the fiction, borrowing sugar on Mondays and lending it on Wednesdays, interrogating each other over bergamot about gerunds and gargoyles, and she was, in her patient and nimble way, the best teacher I had ever had. [276] Patient and nimble were the words her students used in the corridors, not knowing I was listening, and they fit her like the key fit my pocket. [303] She died on a March morning with Vitruvius on her lap, and I inherited her apartment, and the apartment inherited her garden, and the garden inherited this brass key that opened the wooden box on the antique desk where she kept her seal. [346] The antique desk seal was a thing of carved carnelian, showing a fox reading beneath a single star, and I had carried it to the garden because the meteor shower seemed like the right hour for returning things. [384] I sat on the stone bench among the lavender and interrogated the silence as she had taught me to interrogate everything, with patience and a willingness to be wrong. [413] The stars began to fall at eleven, drawing bright lines across the dark like letters written and immediately erased, and I thought I heard her voice, which had told me buildings were sentences and we were the verbs trying to conjugate them. [455] I told her aloud that I had finally understood the Doric friezes, and that I had named my first built project after her, the small library on the hill where the cornice finally sang the way she said it should. [495] The agreed upon lies had grown so familiar that even now I continued to speak in the old cadence we had invented, where I pretended to be a baker and she pretended to be fooled. [530] The brass key warmed in my pocket as if waiting for this meteor, and it unlocked the wooden box I had carried up, smelling of her desk and of the lavender she pressed between pages. [565] I knelt and placed the key in the lock and turned it the way she turned the pages of her books, and the seal within was warm, and the air smelled of sugar and bergamot and old libraries. [603] The first stone of the garden path, a fragment of an older building that had once spoken in a tongue I was only beginning to learn, caught the light of a falling star and answered in the language she had taught me to hear. [647] It said what she would have said, in her patient and nimble way, that the truest lies are the ones we agree to tell, that a garden is a building that has finally learned to grow, that the language of old buildings is only the language of those who have loved them long enough. [701] I stayed until the shower ended and the city closed its eyes again, and I walked home carrying the seal and her voice and the key that had belonged, for one night, to no one at all.
