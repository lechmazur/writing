In the mirror world, where rivers climbed toward inverted clouds and every path doubled back upon itself, an alchemical orchard greenhouse stood shrouded in an endless night. [27]
The structure of glass and living wood had once coaxed impossible fruits from mineral soil, but the absence of dawn had withered the orchards to silhouettes of themselves. [28]
Mirael, the scattered memory keeper, moved between the glass aisles with a lantern that gave back only shadows. [18]
She was called scattered because her mind held the broken pieces of a thousand songs, each fragment glinting like the thin layers of a mica window salvaged from the temple of the first sunrise. [34]
The pane hung now above the central transept, catching nothing, for the sun had not risen in the mirror world since before her grandmother was born, and the realm was dying by degrees too small to see. [37]
Mirael had spent decades learning forgotten songs, recovered from the cracked lips of statues and the sighing of empty wells, and tonight she intended to use them. [27]
She set the mica on a trestle of twisted copper and angled it toward a withered sapling whose silver leaves were beginning to fall. [24]
Then she lifted her voice and sang. [7]
Each note unwound a thread of color in the dark air, and she began to weave the threads across the mica window, plaiting light into a fabric that resembled the first blush of morning. [34]
She called this work the weaving of woven dawns, a craft practiced only by those willing to be unmade by it, and as she wove, the threads grew warm. [29]
The act of weaving dawns knits communities together, binding the scattered keeper to the long memory of the realm and the realm to the keeper in a web of mutual devotion. [31]
The light brightened until the greenhouse glowed like a lantern itself, and the sapling stirred, lifting one silver leaf as if remembering the sun. [24]
Mirael sang louder, and the songs lifted from her throat like birds, swirling around the trunks of the withered trees, and she began to swaddle each trunk in the warm fabric of dawn. [33]
Bound in the woven light, the trees shivered and straightened, and from their bark there burst tiny green shoots, smelling of apricot and rain. [24]
The mica window trembled as the gathered light pressed against it, and the pane, which had held only memory, began at last to give it back, casting a true sunrise across the interior of the greenhouse. [36]
Birds that had nested in the rafters woke and began to sing, and from the soil rose the hum of bees long thought vanished from the mirror world. [28]
Mirael sang on, and the woven dawns unfolded through the broken roof tiles and out into the endless night beyond, and the night, for the first time in seven generations, drew back a little. [34]
She had bound the light to give it release, and in releasing it, she had bound the realm to life again, a paradox she would carry in her chest for all her remaining years. [34]
When at last her voice gave out, the greenhouse was bright with morning that would not fade, and the scattered keeper stood in its center, whole at last, because she had given her pieces away. [35]
Outside, the inverted river paused in its long climb, and the doubled paths of the mirror world flickered, as if unsure which direction led forward and which led back. [29]
Villagers who had learned to sleep through the perpetual dark woke in their underground warrens and climbed toward the light, following the songs that still drifted from the greenhouse on the morning air. [33]
They came with baskets woven from the same silver leaves that had fallen, and they gathered the green shoots, and they planted them in the orchard rows, and the rows filled with the hum of bees and the chatter of children who had never seen the sun. [47]
Mirael watched from the doorway, the mica window now set into the frame above her head, and she saw that her work had not ended but had only just begun, for the realm would need many more dawns, woven one by one, until the endless night was only a story told to frighten children. [54]
She lifted her chin and began to hum, the first forgotten song rising unbidden to her lips, and the greenhouse answered with the soft percussion of new leaves opening in the light. [32]
