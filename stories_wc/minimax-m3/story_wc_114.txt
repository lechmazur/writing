<story>High on the shoulder of the mountain, the weather station clung to the ridge like a wooden prayer. [18] Inside, the fault-tolerant oracle lay wrapped in wool, her breath still uneven after the fever had finally broken in the small hours before dawn. [42] She was called fault-tolerant because her readings, though sometimes wrong at the edges, always held at the center; the weather seemed to forgive her small mistakes, and so did the village below that depended on her forecasts. [79] Now, in the fragile clarity that comes after fever breaks, she felt the instruments humming around her with a strange new weight. [101] The barometer ticked. [104] The anemometer whispered in the drafty eaves. [111] And the botanical illustration on the wall - a pressed and inked study of the rare alpine gentian, drawn by her teacher decades ago - seemed to lean toward her, as if it had been waiting. [145] She had not looked at it fully in years; she had only been peripherally aware of it, the way one is aware of a shadow in the corner of a room. [176] Her teacher had died predicting a storm that never came, and she had swallowed that truth for so long it had become a stone she no longer noticed. [204] Now, in the auroral sorrow of her recovery, she wanted to find the weight of that swallowed thing, to take it out and weigh it on the station's old brass scale. [235] She rose from the cot, her legs unsteady, and walked to the illustration. [248] The gentian's petals were drawn with such precision that she could almost smell the thin alpine air that had once filled the artist's lungs. [272] With a thumb still warm from fever, she touched the paper and let her skin smudge the ink at the edge of one petal, softening the line where the flower met the rock. [305] It was a small destruction, but it felt honest. [314] She thought of how her own certainties had begun to sway like the anemometer cups in a shifting wind. [333] For years she had told the village what the sky would do, and the sky had mostly agreed; but lately the forecasts had begun to drift, and she had begun to wonder if she had ever truly known the mountain at all. [375] Her teacher's last prediction had been wrong, and the village had forgiven that too, because oracles, like weather vanes, are allowed to point in the wrong direction occasionally. [403] She had carried the knowledge of that wrongness by occupying margins - by writing the margins of her logbooks full of small, careful notations, by living in the margins of the station's narrow halls, by keeping her own grief at the edge of her work. [447] But the fever had burned through those margins, and now the truth sat in the middle of the room, heavy and undeniable. [469] She wanted to weigh it. [474] Outside, the sky was putting on the colors of late afternoon: a thin auroral green bleeding into rose, as if the mountain were remembering the northern lights it would never see. [505] The light moved across the illustration, and the smudge she had made glowed like a bruise. [521] She fetched the brass scale and set it on the desk, then lifted the illustration from its nail and laid it gently on one pan. [546] It weighed almost nothing. [550] She set a small iron weight on the other pan, and the paper dipped; the truth, she realized, was not in the paper at all. [575] It was in the years she had spent carrying the silence of her teacher's failure, in the certainty she had built around that silence, in the way that certainty now trembled like a candle in a draft. [612] She left the scale unbalanced and went back to the cot, where the wool still held the warmth of her recent sleep. [634] Outside, the first stars were appearing, and the wind off the glacier smelled of cold stone and the gentian she had never seen bloom. [658] She closed her eyes and let the mountain speak in its own language, and the language was not prediction but patience, and the patience was enough. [684] In the auroral sorrow of that small room, the fault-tolerant oracle slept again, and her breath, at last, was steady. [704]</story>
