<story>The storm was coming in from the west and Yara could hear it in the way the loose slates on her tower tapped against each other like restless fingers. [28] She was the labyrinth's maintenance worker, which meant she oiled the hinges, swept the corridors, and kept the false doors pretending to be real. [22] Tonight she had also brought up the broken sextant she had found in the astronomer's abandoned quarters, the one that still hummed when the moon was right, and she had laid it on the parapet beside her mug of cold mint tea. [40] She believed, though she had no proof, that the sextant healed in moonlight, the same way the small scar on her left palm healed only on nights when the moon was full and the wind was gentle. [37] Tonight was neither gentle nor full, but the scar had begun to itch, which she took as a sign that something was about to mend. [25] She was here to deconstruct the device and to map, on a fresh sheet of graph paper, the scattered dreams she had been collecting from the sleepers who wandered the labyrinth and forgot they ever slept. [37] What she wanted, more than anything in her quiet life, was to discover what trees witness. [16] She had been raised in a stone town without trees, and so the question had grown strange inside her, the way a stone grows strange in a river. [25] The first task was oil. [5] She descended the spiral and walked the long gallery to the eastern gate, and the hinges gave a small complaining sound she silenced with a few drops from her bottle. [27] Then the seventh corridor, where she lit the lanterns one by one and watched their flames find the walls and lie down on the stone. [22] The corridor was silent in the way corridors are sometimes silent, and just as she reached the last lantern a pin dropped somewhere in the dark. [26] She heard it the way you hear a single raindrop land in a tin cup. [13] It was the moment she had been waiting for, though she had not known she was waiting for it, and she set down her lamp and walked back the way she had come, slowly, the way a person walks when they want the silence to keep holding them. [41] In the tower the stormlight moved across the floor in pale blue patches, and the moon came out from behind a cloud just long enough to kiss the brass and make the sextant sing a half-note of light. [38] She sat down cross-legged and unfolded the paper and took the sextant apart, piece by piece, laying each cog and lens on the stones like the bones of a small animal she was studying. [33] The astronomer had been dead for sixty years, but Yara had decided, on the advice of nobody in particular, that his tool was still alive and only needed to be spoken to gently and reasoned with. [34] She mapped her scattered dreams on the paper as she worked: the one about the staircase that ran away from her, the one about the child carrying a jar of lightning, the one about the field of wheat that whispered her actual name, which was not Yara but a longer name she had been given at birth. [53] Each dream was a coordinate. [4] Each coordinate was a small unit of measured sorrow. [9] She had been keeping the count for years, and the total sat in a notebook by her bed at a number she did not especially fear. [25] Tonight, while the pin was still ringing in her memory, she totaled everything up and the sum was the same as last week, which meant either nothing had changed inside her or everything had changed and she had not yet noticed. [39] This was the small strangeness of her work: you could take a thing apart and find your own grief inside it, set down in neat figures, and feel nothing in particular about the neatness, and the un-feeling was itself a kind of mercy. [39] She reassembled the sextant by moonlight. [6] The scar on her palm had stopped itching. [8] And somewhere outside, in a courtyard she could not see from her window, an old fig tree was standing in the rain with its leaves turned upward, and she thought, with the calm of a person who has finished a small task, that perhaps tomorrow she would ask it directly what it had seen.
