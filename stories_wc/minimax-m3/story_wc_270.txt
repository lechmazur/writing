Grandmother Wen had been called an insurgent since the day she refused to let the village elders burn the seed-storehouse at the start of the war. [26] Now, during the slow reconstruction of their shattered valley, she walked the steam-wreathed paths around the volcanic hot springs where her people had been rebuilding since the long war ended. [56] The mineral air stung her lungs, and the ground hissed beneath her sandals as if reminding her it was still alive. [77] For decades she had believed the old rituals were bones that needed marrow, not relics to be sealed away in museums of the heart. [101] She wanted to evolve tradition with intent, to take the inherited prayers and let them bear new fruit, and the reconstruction gave her room to try without the old magistrates watching. [132] The hot springs, with their venting sulfur and healing warmth, had become a place of gathering. [148] Refugees from the highland towns now soaked their scarred feet there, planning how to raise new roofs over old quarrels, how to bury grudges without forgetting them. [175] It was beside one such pool, half-buried in pale ash, that she found the kite. [190] It had no string and no living owner; its silk was rotted to lace, and a long tail of knotted paper ribbons fluttered weakly in the updrafts of steam. [219] The knots were not decorative. [224] Wen turned a ribbon in her fingers and saw that each loop and twist was a character in a script she had only heard her grandfather whisper of on winter nights. [255] She sat down among the reeds, the warm water lapping her ankles, and began to read. [271] She had been taught that the script was the language of the First Planters, the ones who had come before the kingdom, before the empire, before even the name of her people was written down. [306] As her lips shaped the syllables aloud, something stirred in the reeds around her. [320] She did not understand at first why the cattails bent toward her, or why the moss at her knees turned a brighter, hungrier green under her palms. [347] Only later did Wen realize she had been talking back to them in her head. [362] Since childhood she had been accidentally telepathic with plants, a quiet secret she had hidden from neighbors who would have called her a witch and dragged her before a tribunal of gardeners. [394] The steam, the ash, and the geothermal heat had sharpened the old connection, and now the reeds were answering her questions about the kite before she had finished asking them aloud. [425] The script told of a buried thing. [432] Beneath the second pool, sealed in a stone the color of honey, lay an embryo in amber, planted there in the time before memory by hands that wanted to bequeath a future they themselves would never see. [469] The knot-script gave it a name that translated roughly as an implanted destiny: a person-not-yet-grown who would carry the line of the First Planters into whatever age came next, to meet it and to shape it. [505] Wen felt the reeds tremble with a kind of recognition. [515] They, too, had been waiting, longer than she had been alive. [526] All her life she had argued that her village's traditions were cages; now she understood that some traditions were wombs. [546] The reconstruction could not be only about rebuilding walls and replanting fields. [558] It would have to make room, also, for what had been planted in the old earth. [574] That evening, when the children came to swim and the elders came to murmur politics, she did not call a meeting. [595] Instead, she led them to the second pool and, with a flat stone and a whispered apology to the water, began to unseal the ancient plug of clay and coral. [625] The crowd held its breath as the water clouded, then cleared, and a fist-sized lump of amber rose gently into her outstretched palm. [648] Inside it, curled and dreaming, a small human shape floated in golden light, no larger than her thumb. [666] Wen held it up so that everyone, even the smallest children, could see. [679] The steam hissed around her like applause, and somewhere in the reeds she felt a low, green approval she did not bother to hide from anyone anymore. [706] "This is not the old way," she said, her voice carrying over the springs. [720] "And it is not the new way." [727] "It is the way we will choose, together, to grow." [737] The amber warmed in her hand as if the embryo had heard her and answered. [752] And in the hot springs of that reconstructing valley, under the steady gaze of the old insurgent grandmother, an inherited future began, at last, to breathe. [778]
