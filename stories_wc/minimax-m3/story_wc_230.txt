The ravine had been named for silence before the storms learned to sing through its hanging orchard. [17] In the years after the empathy virus had swept the lowlands clean of pretense, Maren Solene still climbed the rain-slick ladders that swung between the terraced apple trees, hunting the small, fragrant truffles that nested at the root-line where stone met soil. [59] She was a rational truffle forager by trade, which in the ravine meant a woman who counted the weight of rain, mapped the moss by compass, and never trusted the part of her chest that yearned. [95] The yearning, she suspected, was a leftover from the fever that stripped everyone bare. [109] The empathy virus had given the world the gift of feeling everyone's wounds as though they were stitched beneath one's own skin, and Maren had survived by being, of all things, involuntarily truthful. [142] She could not lie; the words arrived already finished, with no room for revision. [156] In a world made suddenly porous with feeling, this had seemed a kindness, even a refuge. [172] You could not be lied to by a person incapable of lies, and so the villagers had begun, almost without noticing, to bring Maren their small, aching truths. [200] She carried them the way she carried the truffles, in cloth and quiet. [213] But the ravine was battered now by a storm that had lasted seven weeks, and the orchards, hanging from their iron pegs above the gorge, swung and wept and refused to give up their fruit. [248] The trees had been planted by her grandmother in the old way, their roots threaded through stone baskets and dangled over the drop so that the wind would carry the blossoms down to pollinate the lower terraces. [285] It was beautiful, and it was failing, and Maren could not pretend otherwise. [298] On the morning the sky finally cracked into a pale, bruised color, a stranger arrived at the highest ladder with a parcel wrapped in oiled linen. [324] He did not introduce himself, because he understood, as people did now, that Maren would simply repeat whatever he said. [344] "I have a relic," he said, "smuggled out from under the broken cathedral." [357] She unwrapped it on the wet planks and found an antique glass eye, the kind once fitted into the face of a cathedral saint. [381] It weighed almost nothing, and its iris was the blue of a bruise that had begun to heal. [399] "It watches," the man said. [404] "People who hold it cannot feel the borrowed grief of others for as long as they cup it in their palm." [425] Maren turned the eye in her hand and felt, for the first time in years, the small clean silence of her own skull. [448] It was exquisite, and it was wrong, and she said so. [459] "If I take it, the village will want it," she said. [470] "They will come up the ladders with their sorrows in their hands, and I will be the only one who can occlude the borrowed grief, and that is not a thing one person should be asked to carry alone." [509] The stranger considered this with the patience of a man who had walked a long way with a heavy secret. [529] "There are cures," he said, "written in textbooks the academies have burned." [541] He showed her a book whose spine had been broken and rebound in three different kinds of thread. [559] Its pages were foxed and soft, and the remedies inside were rumored, not proved, which is to say they were the only honest kind left. [584] Maren read through rumored cures in discredited textbooks while the wind threw rain sideways through the orchard, and what she found was a recipe for shared silence, a method by which a community might learn to occlude its own borrowed ache. [625] It was not a cure for the virus; nothing was a cure for the virus. [640] It was a way of teaching a place how to breathe with a closed mouth. [655] Maren kept the antique glass eye because it was hers now, and she hid it in the hollow of an apple tree, behind a knot of wood that looked like a closed eye. [688] She climbed down the ladders and into the village, and she began, as she always did, to speak. [706] She told them what she had found, exactly as she had read it, and the words came out in the fractured grace of a bell that had been dropped and had decided, against all sense, to keep ringing. [744] The villagers listened, and one by one they closed their eyes, and the ravine for the first time in seven weeks grew quiet. [767] Maren stood at the foot of the ladder, holding nothing, and cultivated at last the psychological safety of a place that had finally learned to bear its own weight. [796]
