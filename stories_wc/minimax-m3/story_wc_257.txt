The dome of the observatory held a sky that refused to commit to night or day, a permanent bruised lavender that had settled over the lenses decades or perhaps centuries ago. [31]

After hitting rock bottom, she had walked here because nowhere else could hold what she carried. [47]

She was a mourning jewelry maker, which meant she had spent her adult life braiding the hair of the dead into rings, pressing petals into lockets, sealing ash under glass like fireflies trapped in amber. [82]

Now her hands were empty of commissions and heavy with their own small grief. [96]

The observatory had been abandoned long enough for dust to gather in soft brown folds along the eyepieces. [114]

She sat at the brass telescope and listened to it tick, a patient metronome that measured nothing in particular. [133]

On the workbench before her lay a tattoo stencil on vellum, the kind survivors pressed to their chests when they wanted grief closer than any locket could carry it. [162]

She had carried this one across three countries, folded into the lining of her coat, though the name it bore no longer had anyone left to speak it. [190]

In the corner, curtains of intention hung across a tall window she had not opened, fabric the color of concentrated thought, heavy with the wishes of astronomers who had once stood here and asked the sky to answer them in kind. [231]

The curtains stirred though no wind touched them, and she understood they were not cloth at all but the residue of long-held wanting, woven from every question that had ever been aimed at the stars. [266]

This was a place where time had folded itself into paper, she thought, and then she noticed that someone had been folding things. [289]

A small origami crane sat at the foot of the telescope, wings tucked, head bowed. [304]

Inside its folded body, coordinates were written in a hand so fine it looked like the trembling of a single strand of hair. [327]

She unfolded it carefully, the way one unfolds a letter from the dead. [340]

The coordinates pointed to the observatory's own cellar, a room she had assumed was sealed shut with rust and forgotten purpose. [361]

She descended the iron stairs with the vellum stencil pressed against her sternum, as though the paper might armor her against what waited below. [385]

At the bottom, a single lamp burned with a light that was genuinely artificial, a glow made without flame or filament, constructed only of luminous paint and a few drops of longing. [417]

The lamp illuminated a garden of withered things: roses pressed into eternal droop, lilies folded brown at the tips, ivy that had forgotten which direction it had once wished to climb. [448]

She recognized the garden because it was hers. [456]

This was what she had carried into the observatory without knowing it, the bouquet of every funeral she had dressed, every locket she had sealed, every grief she had pressed into shape for someone else to wear. [493]

She sat on the cold floor among the withering and set down the stencil gently beside her. [510]

Then she began to work with the quiet ardour of someone who had decided, against all evidence, to keep loving wilted things. [532]

She took the roses and taught them, with her fingers and her breath, to open again petal by petal, not as a lie but as a lesson in the possibility of second bloom. [565]

She whispered to the lilies that withering was not a verdict but a season. [579]

She coaxed the ivy to remember the sun it had once turned toward. [592]

As she worked, the lamp continued to illuminate the cellar with its impossible, heartfelt glow, and the vellum stencil lay open beside her, waiting to receive whatever new portrait she might eventually trace there for someone who had lost everything and needed to wear the shape of it again. [641]

The curtains of intention, far above, shifted once in their stillness and settled, satisfied. [655]

When she climbed back to the dome at last, the paper crane was gone from the telescope's foot, replaced by another, smaller, its wings folded tight around coordinates that pointed not downward but outward, toward the place where the bruised lavender sky finally thinned into ordinary blue. [702]

She slipped the second crane into her pocket beside the stencil and stepped through the door she had not opened, carrying the coordinates toward whatever bloom she might next be asked to teach. [735]

Outside, the world waited the way gardens wait for rain, patient and slightly ruined and full of seeds that had not yet decided to wake. [760]
