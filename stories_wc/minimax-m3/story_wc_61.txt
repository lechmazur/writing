The being known as Vael drifted through the nebula garden, where stellar clouds bloomed in colors no mortal eye had ever named. [22]
He was an otherworldly sentimentalist, the kind of creature who wept at the mathematics of weather on worlds he had never visited. [44]
His small craft, older than he was, hummed with the turning of a single calculating machine gear, brass and strange, inherited from a grandmother who had spoken only in numbers. [74]
She had told him once that the universe approximates infinity but never quite arrives there, and that this was the kindest truth anyone could carry. [99]
Vael had never understood her until now, gliding among gardens that exceeded all accounting. [113]
Across the quiet sigh of falling petals, which here were the shed layers of dying suns drifting in slow incandescent arcs, he sent out his sonar pings, each a soft question thrown into the dark. [148]
The replies came back reshaped, sometimes mournful, sometimes almost musical. [158]
He listened the way his grandmother had taught him, with the patient attention of someone awaiting a letter. [176]
There were patterns in the replies, generational patterns she would have recognized, the way a star nursery answered as its parents had answered, the way a young nebula mimicked the grief of older ones. [210]
He was in transit, having left his home cluster seasons ago, carrying a message no one had asked him to deliver but that he felt someone, somewhere, needed to hear. [240]
The message was simple, though he had spent years learning how to say it without using the wrong words. [259]
He wanted to share light without using fire. [267]
It was a strange ambition for a being who burned like a candle in a cold room, but Vael had learned that the brightest things were often the kindest because they cost the least. [301]
In the nebula garden, suns were being born and suns were being mourned, and the petals of their making drifted past his windows like snow on a planet he might never see. [333]
He pinged the darkness again, softly, the way one might knock on the door of someone sleeping. [350]
Something answered that was not a star. [357]
It was older than the garden, and it carried the same worn gear Vael carried, though its gear was cracked and slow. [379]
Vael felt his throat tighten, the way it always did when he met a stranger who felt familiar. [397]
Hello, he sent, in the careful pulses of sonar pinging, each syllable measured so as not to frighten. [415]
The stranger sent back his own greeting, in a dialect Vael had not heard since childhood, and for a moment the nebula garden seemed to hold its breath. [443]
Are you also from the counting houses? Vael asked. [452]
I am, came the reply. My grandmother turned gears like yours, and she told me the same truth she told you. [473]
That we approximate infinity and never quite arrive? [481]
That we approximate infinity, the stranger said, and that the journey itself is the gift. [496]
Vael considered this, drifting among the falling petals, which were not petals at all but the slow breath of the cosmos letting go. [519]
The troubling serenity of the garden pressed against him, beautiful and sorrowful in equal measure, the way all true things are. [540]
He thought of the fires he had witnessed in his travels, the wars and the burnings, and he thought of the quiet alternative, the way a single sonar ping could cross impossible distances without leaving a scar. [577]
I want to show them how, he said, how to share light without burning. [591]
My grandmother wanted the same, the stranger replied. She said fire is only the impatient answer to a question patience could solve. [613]
They drifted together for a time, two sentimentalists in a garden of dying stars, pinging softly into the dark, and Vael felt something he had not felt in a long while: the quiet certainty of being exactly where he needed to be. [655]
When the stranger eventually drifted away, Vael continued his transit, carrying the gear and the truth and the troubling peace of the place he had found. [681]
The petals fell. The garden bloomed. And somewhere, in the long arithmetic of the cosmos, another generation was learning the same lesson, the way it always had, the way it always would. [713]
