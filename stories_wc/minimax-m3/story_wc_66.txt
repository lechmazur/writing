After the story famine, when every printed page had gone brittle and the libraries had been swallowed by quiet, there lived a diligent orchestra copyist named Aelin who kept her hands busy so that her mind would not starve. [39]

She lived in a small ember alcove tucked into a fold of hillside where mist threaded itself through the ferns and the air always tasted faintly of woodsmoke and rain. [30]

Each morning she sharpened her reeds of graphite, warmed the ink, and began the slow work of copying orchestral parts by the light that filtered through the low stone window. [30]

The manuscripts she reproduced were not new compositions, but the patient rearrangement of older ones, so that the notes might survive in another hand when her own had turned to quiet. [31]

On the sill beside her stood a battered shortwave radio, its dial glow a secretly luminous thing that pulsed only when the night pressed close, like a coal remembered by the dark. [32]

She had found the radio years before in a collapsed transit station, half-buried in velvet dust, and she had repaired it not for news but for company, though the broadcasts it caught were never news at all. [37]

They were pirate broadcasts: whispers, fragments of verse, melodies cut and spliced, drifting in from somewhere beyond the hills where the signal could still slip past the silencing towers. [29]

The voice that came through most often did not give a name. [12]

It only sang, and the lyrics were elusive, sliding between languages, refusing to fix themselves to any single meaning, so that Aelin had to listen many times before she could copy even a single line. [35]

She transcribed them carefully into the margins of her orchestral scores, layering the strange words beneath the staves of oboe and horn, as if the music might be a cloak for the messages, or the messages a cloak for the music. [41]

This was her quiet rebellion, her thistled calm in a world that prickled at every attempt at remembering. [18]

She did not believe the broadcasts would change anything beyond her own alcove, but she believed in the work itself, in the slow accumulation of faithful marks on paper, in the way dust gathered on a forgotten page and could be brushed aside to reveal a still-living hand. [48]

One night, after a week of particularly heavy mist, the radio hissed and then the familiar voice returned, but this time it did not sing. [25]

It spoke plainly, in a tone no louder than the settling of embers, and asked whether anyone was still listening, whether anyone was still copying, whether anyone was still willing to be a small vessel for a story. [38]

Aelin set down her pen. [5]

She pressed her palm against the warm glass of the dial and felt its secretly luminous hum travel up through her wrist, and she answered, not aloud, but by writing the answer into the score she had been copying, a slow adagio for strings that the original composer had never finished. [51]

The voice on the radio seemed to pause, as if reading. [11]

Then it said that there were others, scattered across the lowlands and the river towns, each with their own alcove and their own battered receiver, each keeping some small piece of the older world alive in the margins of their labor. [41]

It said that the pirate broadcast had been a way to find them, a cloak of song so that the silencing towers would pass it by as noise, and that the lyrics were the key by which one copyist could recognize another. [42]

Aelin thought of the dust that sifted onto her page each morning, fine as flour, soft as the breath of sleeping things, and she understood that the famine of stories was not because the stories had ended but because no one had been left to teach the dust to tell them. [51]

She bent again to her work. [6]

Now, when she copied a movement, she wove a line of the elusive song into the rests, where a rest should be, so that any orchestra that ever played the piece would pause at that measure and feel something unplaceable rise in the room. [44]

She did this quietly, without announcement, and the thistled calm of her alcove deepened, because she knew that the copyists were listening, and that they too would leave their small hidden marks, and that in some future rehearsal hall a musician would brush dust from a page and hear, faintly, the beginning of a story again.
