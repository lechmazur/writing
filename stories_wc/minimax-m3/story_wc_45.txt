Corrine had spent thirty years as a healer of the villages ringing the old marsh, and she had built her reputation, like a careful wall, on disbelief. [27]
She was the kind of skeptic who still tucked coins into the pockets of beggars, the kind who laughed at fortune-tellers but paid their fees twice over. [54]
That afternoon, after the marshal brought her word of the substitute body found in the willow thicket, she walked the hedge maze behind the chapel, counting turns and steadying her breath. [85]
She had tended the child for two fevered nights before he died, and now, as the villagers whispered changeling, she knew she must return to the secret garden where the mother said the fairy folk had left a parting gift. [125]
The maze ended in a square of green hidden behind tall, breathing privet, and there, beneath a stone shaped like a knuckle, lay a small wooden box. [152]
Inside the box rested a single object that no honest practitioner should have believed: a baby's tooth, milk-white, with faint ridges that caught the late sun like rivers on a map. [183]
It was, the mother had insisted, the changeling's baby tooth, the proof that her real son had been stolen, the proof that what lay in the marsh was something else. [213]
Corrine turned the tooth in her palm, and a cleft opened in her certainty, clean as a crack in old glass. [234]
She had set broken bones her whole life, but this was not a bone she could set, not with splint and wrap and clean linen. [259]
She thought about bone setting anyway, the way the body, if guided gently, sometimes found its old alignment, the way a wrong step could become, with patience, a kind of choreography. [290]
The forgiving skeptic in her wanted to dismiss the mother as grieving, wanted to say the tooth was any child's tooth, wanted to fold her disbelief around the ache like a bandage. [322]
But something in the tooth, something shrewdly innocent, refused the easy dismissal, the way a child's question refuses a clever adult's dodge. [344]
The ridges were not random. [349]
They spelled, in a script she had studied in her apprenticeship, a kind of celestial marking, the old shepherds' shorthand for stars fallen into water. [374]
And so Corrine understood, or began to, that the changeling had perhaps not been a theft, but a trade, a misstep that had become a small, cold constellation dropped into the mud. [406]
She yearned, then, not for the lost child, not for proof, but for a way to make sense of the mistake, to lay it down gently so the living could move around it. [439]
This was the work she had practiced for years, this patient bone setting of stories, this careful reassembly of the broken and the bewildered. [463]
She would take the tooth to the observatory dome on the ridge, she decided, where her old friend Maren still charted the night on hand-stitched maps. [489]
Together they might plot the ridges as if they were stars, marking where the lost boy had gone, marking also where the substitute had come from, so that both could be remembered. [521]
This was not denial, and it was not belief. [530]
It was the work of a forgiving skeptic, the courage of one who has been wrong before and learned to wear the wrongness lightly, as a scarf. [557]
She thought of all the diagnoses she had missed, the fevers she had failed to break, the bones she had set crooked in her first decade. [583]
Each had been a small star, she decided now, misplaced, fallen where it should not be, but still burning, still worth finding in the dark. [608]
Her motivation, the only one that had outlived her doubts, was this: to turn mistakes into constellations, to take what had gone wrong and arrange it into something a traveler could navigate by. [641]
The hedge maze rustled behind her as she tucked the tooth into her satchel, and the secret garden exhaled its green, knowing breath. [664]
She walked back through the privet with the late light on her shoulders, and the forgiving skepticism she carried felt less like armor now and more like a clean, washed hand, ready for whatever work the next morning asked. [703]
She would not be certain. [708]
But she would be present. [713]
And in the space between those two, she would build, with whatever stars came her way, something a grieving mother might look at and recognize as love. [740]
She thought of her grandmother's old, cracked star-charts and of the new brass instruments waiting in Maren's dome, and felt, for one clear moment, that to mend what was broken was its own quiet, bright invention. [776]
