<story>The mercury-glass seer climbed the last stair into the mountain's throat, where the labyrinthine bookstore opened like a lung full of dust. [22] She had come because remembering had grown too heavy. [31] Each night for years she had walked the same corridors of memory, and each night the corridors had narrowed, the doors had sealed, the lights behind them dimming until she could no longer tell if the warmth was hers or borrowed. [72] Now she wanted only one thing: to understand the shape of home. [84] The bookstore answered her with the patience of old stone. [94] Aisles branched off aisles; staircases folded back upon themselves; reading alcoves opened into reading alcoves. [109] Shelves climbed the curved walls of the mountain's interior, and on the shelves rested every book that had ever been set down and forgotten, and every book that had not yet been written. [142] The proprietor, a thin man whose eyes were two polished pools, told her the rule on her first night. [161] "This place substitutes reality nightly," he said. [168] "What you bring in, you must leave behind. [176] What you take out, you did not have. [184] The shelves rearrange themselves to the shape of your need." [194] She had come looking for a single object: the lost language stone. [206] It was a small thing, a river-smoothed pebble that, when pressed beneath the tongue, returned to the speaker a word that had been taken from the world. [233] Her mother had spoken that word to her as a child, and she had forgotten it, and her mother had died, and the forgetting had curdled into something like a stone lodged behind her ribs. [268] For seven nights she searched. [273] For seven nights the shelves shifted, and she found, in their place, buried talents. [285] She found that she could read spines with her fingertips, that she could smell the year a book had been bound, that she could hear, in the soft huff of a page turning, the voice of the one who had first read it aloud. [329] On the eighth night she found a feather. [337] It lay in the narrow gutter between two oaken shelves, pale as a sliver of moon, and when she lifted it the world went quiet in the way the world goes quiet before thunder. [371] She understood, then, the method. [376] The proprietor had told her, years ago, that the stone could only be coaxed from its shelf via the texture of a feather. [399] She had thought it a riddle. [405] She understood now that it was a craft. [413] She began to seed. [417] She walked the longest aisle of the bookstore, trailing the feather along each spine. [431] Where the book had been waiting for her, the feather would catch, just slightly, on a ridge of the leather or a burr in the cloth. [457] She felt these catches the way a blind woman feels a face, and in each catch she recognized a buried talent she had not known she possessed: the patience to listen, the humility to be taught, the courage to admit that she was looking. [501] At the end of the aisle, in a shelf that had not been there the night before, she found it. [521] The lost language stone. [525] It sat on a small velvet pillow, the color of a rain-soaked hill. [538] Beside it lay a folded card, and on the card, in her own hand, was a single word. [556] She read it aloud. [560] The mountain breathed. [563] The shelves shifted, and the bookstore rearranged itself into a small, warm room with a single window, and through the window she could see, at last, the shape of home. [593] It was not a place. [598] It was the sound of her mother's voice, saying the word she had just remembered, in a tongue that had been lost and was now, briefly, found. [625] Outside, somewhere deep in the stone, thunder rolled. [633] Inside, the mercury-glass seer wept, and the weeping was a kind of planting, and the planting was a kind of homecoming. [654]</story>
