The philanthropist stood alone on the salt flat, where his fortune had bloomed in the slow arithmetic of engineered thirst. [21]

He had manufactured the drought, of course. [27]

That was the silent arithmetic behind every dam, every diverted river, every aquifer purchased and pumped dry. [45]

The valley had given him its water in exchange for its song, and he had spent the song on steel and stock and the quiet, amoral pleasure of leverage. [72]

The lakebed now stretched white and glittering beneath him, cracked into a pattern no surveyor had bothered to name. [90]

In his hands he held a single egg, no larger than a quail's, its shell mottled like the salt itself. [110]

The egg was the last. [115]

Somewhere in the long archives of his laboratories a vial of pickled DNA rested in climate-controlled dark, and somewhere else a single feather was sealed in glass, but this fragile ovoid cupped in his lined palms was the only intact egg of the vanished river wren, a bird whose call had once braided the air above these now-vanished waters. [174]

He had bought it at auction, anonymously, for an obscene sum that would have fed a district for a season. [194]

He had bought it because he could. [201]

He had kept it because, against every tenet of his public self, he was secretly sentimental in the precise, surgical way of men who had learned to love things only after they were gone. [235]

The cracks in the dried riverbed below him had deepened in the years since the water left, widening into a network that, if you tilted your head and squinted, resolved into the unmistakable geometry of a map. [272]

He had first noticed it from a helicopter, a year ago, while inspecting the desolation he had authored. [290]

The map showed, with the mute authority of all cartography drawn by forces older than intention, a route across the salt to a low rise where the old wren nesting grounds had once stood. [324]

He had walked the route now, slowly, in the lull between two distant rolls of thunder, because the storm season was the only time the salt gave up its shimmer and stood honest in gray light. [360]

He had come here to release the egg. [368]

He understood, with the unflinching clarity that had served him in every boardroom and every back-room negotiation, that the act was both absurd and necessary. [392]

The wren was extinct. [396]

No warmth could summon back the throat that had shaped that vanished song. [409]

But somewhere in the engineering of his own grief, he had decided that the egg should not remain in his vault, should not be sold again, should not be reduced to the elegant cruelty of a private collection. [444]

He wanted the echo preserved, even if the bird itself could not be. [457]

He wanted the echo returned to the cracked earth from which the bird had taken its name, so that when the rains came, and he had arranged in the long self-interested chess of his philanthropy that the rains would come again soon, the flooding of the salt would lift the egg gently into the braided channels of the old river, and some future ornithologist walking these flats in another century might find a shell and wonder what creature had once fit inside it. [540]

He knelt. [542]

The thunder had paused. [546]

He lowered the egg into the widest crack, easing it until the salt held it firm, a small mottled offering on the white. [569]

Then he stood, brushed his palms against his trousers, and walked back across the flat toward the waiting helicopter, his shadow stretching long behind him in the gray and patient light. [599]

He did not look back. [604]

The helicopter's blades began to turn, and in their wash the loose salt stirred and rose briefly, a white veil drawn across the cracked map below, as if the valley were already beginning to forget him. [638]

Somewhere beneath that veil the egg rested in its fissure, small and patient, the last relic of a creature that would never sing again in this lifetime, and perhaps never in any other. [667]

He had manufactured the drought, and he had also, in his small and insufficient way, manufactured this grace, a grace that was fractured like the map that had guided him here, and therefore, in the only logic he honored, real. [708]

The thunder returned, low and distant, and he closed the cabin door against it. [721]
