The pendulum of the chronolith swung at a half-oblique angle in the domed observatory, marking neither noon nor midnight where Mira had made her life as a celestial signal interpreter. [30]
She listened, as she always did, to the stars' slow gossip translated through instruments older than her grandmother's grief, and tonight the messages arrived folded into one another like pages of a book read in the wrong order. [68]
Outside, the gravitational anomaly made the path to her door loop lazily back upon itself, so a traveler who walked straight would arrive behind themselves, and Mira had long ago made peace with stepping sideways into her own kitchen. [107]
She was, by training and temperament, a woman at ease in the company of unresolved questions, and she wore the air of being confidently uncertain the way a monk wears a wooden bead, not to display but to remember. [146]
Tonight her hands rested on a desk where three things lay: a brass ear-trumpet tuned to the constellation of the Crouching Bear, a notebook of corrections she had never made, and the dried-up fountain pen with the exotic nib her father had given her the day she left home. [195]
The pen had written the names of stars that no longer existed and the names of stars that would not exist for another thousand years, and it had been dry for as long as she had lived in this observatory, after planting the wrong seeds in the garden of her ambitions, when she had first confused listening to the sky with dictating to it. [259]
She had come here to thresh meaning from experience, to beat the husk from the kernel of every signal, and the slow thumping of the anomaly outside her walls reminded her that not all separation was violence. [296]
Tonight the stars sent her something she had not heard in seven years: a particular arrangement of pulses, patient and slightly amused, that she recognized as the voice of her dead mentor threading itself through the noise. [333]
He has a question for you, she whispered, and the brass ear-trumpet warmed in her palm as if it had been holding its breath. [357]
The question came in a language she could almost translate, and she reached for the dried-up fountain pen with the exotic nib, because in her tradition the only way to answer the dead was to revise the page they had once written. [399]
Editing history, she had learned, was less a matter of changing what had happened and more a matter of unbending the parts of a story warped by those who lived inside it, and she had become very good at it the way a locksmith becomes good at locks. [447]
She uncapped the pen, though she knew it had no ink, and pressed the tip to the page where her mentor's last words were recorded in her own hand, and a single drop of black welled up at the nib and fell. [489]
Outside, the gravitational anomaly hiccupped, and a teacup on her shelf lifted three inches, hovered, and set itself back down with a faint polite sound. [514]
The drop became a line, and the line became a name, and the name was her own, written in a hand she had not used since she was nineteen, and she understood, with the strange comfort of someone being told a truth she had always suspected, that the message was not for her to interpret but to enter. [572]
She thought of the garden, after planting the wrong seeds, and of how the weeds had grown so tall and honest that she had stopped wanting to tear them out, and of how her father had pressed this pen into her palm and said only, When you are ready, the ink will come back. [626]
Tonight the ink had come back, and she understood her method had always been to work through contingent relationships, through the braided dependencies between stars and listeners, between mentors and students, between a pen and the hand that had once learned to hold it. [670]
She began to write, and the observatory began to unbend around her, the warped floorboards straightening, the looping path outside relaxing into something a traveler could follow, and the pendulum of the chronolith swung true for the first time in her memory. [712]
She wrote the name of her mentor correctly, then the constellation that had carried his voice, then, in a small and confident hand, the date of his death she had gotten wrong for seven years, and the correction did not erase him but allowed him at last to arrive. [761]
The brass ear-trumpet cooled in her palm, and the dried-up fountain pen with the exotic nib set itself upright on the desk, truly dry at last, having given back what it had borrowed. [794]
