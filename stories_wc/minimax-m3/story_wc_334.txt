<story>Mira Hartwell had not smelled salt and rot together in thirty-two years, but the moment she stepped onto the weathered dock, the scent returned like a confession. [27] The greenhouse waited beyond the breakwater, half-drowned in the tide, its glass roof clouded by decades of algae and gull droppings. [48] Shipwrecks from three centuries leaned against the seawall like tired elders, their ribs exposed, their names long rubbed smooth by the same wind that had carried her away. [76] Mira was a hands-on folklorist, the kind who pressed her thumbs into moss and listened to the soil the way city people listen to traffic. [101] She had collected stories the way other scholars collected footnotes, but she had also collected splinters, fever, and the particular silence of a village that did not want to be remembered. [132] She had left this coast when her habits broke, when the tenure track demanded she become a different sort of creature, one who did not get her hands dirty. [161] Retirement had been the second breaking, and she had driven through the night with a notebook and a bad knee, answering a summons she could not quite name. [189] The summons had been a letter from a lawyer she had not seen since the summer her sister drowned, a letter that mentioned a key, a greenhouse, and something stolen from her when she was nineteen. [225] The greenhouse had been her grandfather's, and in it he had grown orchids and oranges and, according to village rumor, a single ring of mushrooms that never died. [253] She remembered the ring now, a perfect circle of pale caps that had appeared one autumn and stayed through snow, lightning, and her sister's absence. [278] The mushrooms had been called fairy ring, and her grandfather had warned her never to step inside the circle, because the folk who danced there were not the kind who gave things back. [311] She had stepped inside, of course, when she was nineteen and chronically curious, because her curiosity had always been the loudest thing she owned. [335] What the dancers had taken she could not remember, only that she had woken on the greenhouse floor three days later with her pockets full of wet sand and her notebook full of someone else's handwriting. [371] She had spent the decades since trying to detect what had been taken, by studying broken constellations from her apartment window, by mapping the gaps where stars should have been and the strange new shapes they made. [408] She had published nothing about it, because no one would have believed her, and because the stolen thing felt less like a fact than a season she could not place. [438] Now, at the greenhouse door, the key from the lawyer turned without resistance, as though it had been waiting, as though the lock had been oiled by someone who knew she would come. [471] Inside, the air was warm and green and wrong, the orange trees still heavy with fruit that should have rotted, the orchid pots still spilling white roots across the flagstones. [501] And there, in the center of the floor, the fairy ring still stood, paler than she remembered, the mushrooms trembling slightly as if aware of being watched. [528] Mira set down her bag, pulled a pencil from her hair, and began to map the gaps in the glass roof above her, the places where the sky came through in jagged, broken constellations. [562] She counted stars the way her grandfather had taught her, by listening for the sound they made in the spaces between, and the sound she heard was the sound of her own name, spoken in a voice she had almost forgotten. [603] She stepped into the ring, because she was still, even now, the kind of folklorist who had to put her hand in the fire to know how it burned. [632] The mushrooms gave way beneath her boots like a held breath, and the greenhouse filled with a light that had no source, a crisp and salt-edged light that tasted of every summer she had missed. [667] The stolen memories did not return all at once; they rose in pieces, a sister's laugh, a grandfather's hymn, the smell of oranges cut in the dark, and Mira stood among them, finally at home in her own long absence. [707] Outside, the shipwrecks leaned a little closer, as if listening, and the tide, for the first time in decades, paused at the seawall, as if it too had been waiting for her to come back.
