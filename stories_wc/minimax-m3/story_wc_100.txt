Corvin had carried the weight of fourteen unburied souls for thirty years and a quiet ache for twice that long, so when the mysterious package arrived at his parlor on a Tuesday that smelled of rain and old cedar, he could not ignore it. [44]
The brown paper was unmarked save for a single wax seal pressed with an anchor and a closed eye, and when he cut the twine the contents fell onto his preparation table with the sound of something that had been held underwater for far too long. [90]
It was the broken lighthouse lens, half of it a shattered crescent, the other half still catching the dim gaslight of his shop as if trying to remember how to guide anyone home. [123]
Corvin turned it over in his sore, ink-stained hands and felt, strangely, a pulse of warm current inside the glass, a faint distress signal he had spent his whole life listening for and never quite hearing. [159]
He had always been inadvertently stubborn, and he did not yet know it, because he was the kind of man who would try to bury a thought that had already been buried three times rather than admit it had clawed its way out again. [203]
That stubbornness had kept him at his work when the younger morticians fled to brighter trades, and now it kept him bent over the lens, muttering to the fourteen he had never laid to rest. [238]
"I have been chasing stillness ever since I first stood in a graveyard and felt the wind refuse to speak to me," he said aloud to the empty shop, and the lens answered with a thin, bluish glow, the color of a forgotten sea. [282]
He packed a small valise, lit the oil lamp he had inherited from his grandfather, and walked down to the salt-stained pier at the end of Lighthouse Road, where the fog had begun to thicken in a way that felt less like weather and more like an invitation. [330]
The road he took had no name on the map, but the glow from the lens guided him along the rocks until he arrived at a half-sunken stairway descending into the dark water, the kind a sensible man would have turned away from. [373]
Corvin was not, on this occasion, sensible; he was the weary undertaker, and he was being pulled by something he could not name, and so he took the first step down, the water closing over his boots like a polite but firm refusal. [416]
He swam, then walked the flooded corridor with his lamp carving a pale circle against the green silence, occupying impossible spaces, slipping sideways through a collapsed archway until he reached a doorway whose carved letters still read LIBRARY above the silt. [457]
The forgotten underwater city library rose in tiers of crumbling marble, its reading tables buried in silt and its shelves still holding books whose spines had been eaten only halfway by time. [489]
Somewhere in the center, a faint, rhythmic light pulsed, and Corvin understood that the broken lens was a key and that the city was still broadcasting its own century-old distress signal into the dark. [523]
He placed the broken lighthouse lens into the empty pedestal, the two halves joined with the soft clicking sound of a prayer finally answered, and the pulse brightened into a steady, breathing gold. [556]
The fourteen souls he had carried rose from the floor like warm mist, drifting not in fear but in the slow, grateful way of those who had been waiting to be told where their sleep truly lived. [593]
Corvin spoke their names into the air, and each name became a small address written in gold on the spines of books that had been blank, the titles now reading: Here Stays Margery. Here Stays Thomas. Here Stays the Child With No Surname. [636]
He had come to give rest a permanent address, and the forgotten library had waited a hundred years for someone to write the deeds, the lens waiting to be mended by a man whose hands already knew the shape of every careful funeral knot. [680]
The library, in turn, revitalized him; his lungs eased, his shoulders unknotted, and his boots rested on dry carpet that had not been dry in a century. [707]
He walked back through the impossible spaces in reverse, no longer chased by stillness but accompanied by it, a stillness that walked at his elbow and did not once slip away. [738]
When he emerged, the fog had lifted, the sun lay across the boards like a coin placed by a careful hand, and Corvin understood he had been stubborn all along only because rest had needed him to be. [776]
He closed his parlor knowing the library now kept their names in gold, and at last he laid down his hammer to rest. [799]
