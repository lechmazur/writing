Theophrastus had crawled into the drainage channel below the drowned quarter when the coughing started, and he had not crawled out. [21] Now, three wet seasons after the plague ended, he sat in his pipe-rim home beneath the sunken city ruins, watching the green light filter through the grate. [48] Above him, the marble of the old forum had become a reef. [60] Below him, a slow archaeology of silt. [67] He had made himself the keeper of two things: a brass cog from a seafarer's chronometer, and a doctrine he called the method of layers. [92] The cog he polished each morning with a scrap of sleeve until the numbers along its rim were bright as a second sun. [115] The doctrine he applied to everything, because everything was just a question of what had settled first. [132] He spent the long hours after the plague peeling back the centuries through sedimentary layers, one stratum of mud at a time, reading the city like a book dropped in a well. [164] Theophrastus was a wannabe philosopher, and he knew it, which was the most important thing about him. [181] He had no teacher but the pipe walls and the small, scuttling things that shared them. [197] Yet he thought, and his thinking was the rationally rebellious kind, the kind that refused to bow to any inherited sentence. [218] Today, as on most days, he was agonizing. [226] The city gave him two stories, and he could not make them agree. [239] The first was written in the uppermost silt: the bright bones of the last plague, the abandoned shrines, the prayers scratched into doors that no one had answered. [267] The second was written in deeper clay: an older settlement, older methods, older names, drowned long before the coughing began. [287] Both stories, he felt, were true. [293] And both, he felt, were lies. [299] The competing narratives pressed against each other like two hands trying to fit into a single glove. [316] If he accepted the upper, the plague was the city's true end, and everything before was merely prologue. [334] If he accepted the lower, the coughing was only a footnote to a longer drowning the city had suffered for centuries. [355] Neither would do. [358] So he went down, layer by layer, and listened. [367] He lowered his ear to the sewer floor and let the strata speak. [380] At three inches: the ash of last year's reed-fires. [389] At a foot: a child's copper bracelet, green with verdigris, worn during the festivals of the second dynasty. [407] At two feet: a wall of fitted stone that had never belonged to any building above. [423] At three feet, the work became difficult. [430] The water rose. [433] The light failed. [436] But the cog told him when to stop. [444] The brass numbers along its rim, which had once counted the rolling of an ocean, now counted the slow settling of mud. [466] Each tick was a season. [471] Each season was a layer. [476] And each layer was a sentence in a book nobody else knew how to read. [491] He was preserving these ancient methods, he told himself, because if he did not, the city's deeper name would be lost under the convenient story of the plague. [519] The world above preferred the simpler tale. [526] It made the dead into a single wound, and the wound into a single lesson, and the lesson into a single silence. [548] Theophrastus preferred the harder one, the one that said the city had died slowly, wrongly, and in many ways, and that every layer was a witness. [574] He sat up, his back aching, and held the cog to the green light. [588] The numbers were almost worn away. [594] He had been polishing it for three wet seasons, and the metal was thinning under his thumb. [611] Soon he would have to choose: let the cog go, or stop reading. [624] He chose neither. [627] He set the cog on the silt, picked up a fragment of the older wall, and began, in his abrasive calm, to argue with both stories at once. [655] It was, he decided, the only honest thing a wannabe philosopher could do in a sewer at the end of the world. [677]
