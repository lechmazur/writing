<story>Mira climbed the last switchback of the cliff road with a candle in one hand and a debunker's stubbornness in the other, the wind off the harbor threading salt through her hair. [32] The abandoned observatory waited at the cliff's edge, its copper dome gone green, its telescope pointing at nothing but the slow forgetting of the sky. [57] She had come to decode a riddle that had been humming through her for weeks, and she, who had made a career of dismantling myths, suspected this one was the universal kind. [89] Inside the whisper of closing pages, she understood time behaved like a reader reluctant to set a book down, and so she stepped across the threshold with that reluctance still clinging to her sleeves. [123] An ephemeral compass lay on the entry table, its needle fading in and out like an idea half-remembered from a dream she had refused to log. [149] She picked it up and the needle swung toward a door at the far end of the hall, then dissolved, then reappeared pointing at her own palm. [176] In that palm she had been carrying for years a painted seashell, gift from a grandmother who insisted it was the ear of an old god. [202] Her grandmother had always retold the family stories through resonant hums in a half-finished cello stored beneath her bed, never quite complete, never quite silent. [227] Mira, the myth buster, had listened to those hums and heard only wood, glue, and the polite cruelty of a rational mind. [249] But now, in the observatory's gloom, the painted seashell hummed against her skin, and she realized her grandmother had not been humming to the instrument, but through it. [277] The instrument reimagined past conversations, and notes drifted from the shell to rearrange the words her grandmother had spoken decades ago, words Mira had once dismissed as the ramblings of a superstitious woman. [310] She followed the compass into the rotunda, where a half-finished cello leaned against the telescope like a question waiting to be asked properly. [333] It was her grandmother's, of course, because of course it was, and Mira lifted the bow and felt something ancient and small take a breath inside the wood. [361] She hummed, the shell painted her palm with colors she could not name, and the compass needle reappeared, pointing down into the throat of the instrument. [387] Down that throat tumbled a riddle older than astronomy itself, and she recognized it at last: the universe was not asking her to solve it but to retell it to whoever would listen. [420] She hummed again, and the belly of the cello filled with her grandmother's voice, then her mother's, then her own, then something that sounded like the cliff and the tide below. [451] Mira smiled with that gloomy cheerfulness peculiar to those who finally understand they have been loved in languages they once refused to learn. [474] The riddle was not a question but a relay, she understood, and myths were never meant to be busted, only passed from one trembling instrument to the next. [502] She tucked the painted seashell into her coat, lifted the half-finished cello onto her back, and walked back down the switchback with the compass dissolving in her pocket like a promise kept by snow. [536] Behind her the abandoned observatory doors swung shut with the soft sigh of a book no one had to close, and the wind lifted the candle from her hand without ever extinguishing its small, ridiculous, hopeful flame. [573] The myth buster walked home lighter than she had climbed, though not lighter than the riddle, which would go on humming through every retelling she would make for the rest of her curious, unbusied life.
