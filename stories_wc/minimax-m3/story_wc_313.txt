On the ridgeway between the dark water and the higher dark, Maren walked the levee at the hour when twilight refused to surrender to midnight. [25]
The wind chime above her cottage door had fallen silent an hour before, and she could not tell whether the wind had died or whether something inside her had finally stopped listening. [57]
She was the warden, vigilant by trade and patient by inheritance, and her boots found the crest of the embankment the way a prayer finds its final consonant. [85]
Below her the reservoir held the drowned geometry of the old town, and above her the sky held the drowned geometry of the new one, the two mirroring each other in a hush Maren had learned to call simply the hour. [126]
Tonight she would expunge. [130]
It was a word her grandmother had used for the quiet work of clearing silt from the bell towers after each spring flood, and Maren had inherited both the word and the work. [163]
For years she had walked this ridgeway with a single purpose, to keep the water honest, but lately the town had begun to resurface in stranger ways, in photographs that turned up in the church basement and the schoolhouse attic, sepia and curling, always sepia and always curling. [211]
Tonight the first of those photographs lay at her feet. [221]
She knelt and lifted it, and the sapphire-hued liqueur label someone had once glued to its corner caught the last of the twilight and gave it back doubled, like a remembered voice inside a remembered room. [257]
Maren tucked the photograph into her coat and walked on, and after fifty paces she found the second. [275]
Then the third. [278]
Then a fourth, and a fifth, and by the time the ridgeway curved toward the old ferry landing she had collected nine photographs that seemed to be arranging themselves into a path. [310]
The trail led her down a switchback she had not walked in twenty years. [324]
The path was soft with new ferns and the stones of it had been rearranged by hands she did not recognize and probably never would, but the photographs seemed to know which stones to choose, and she followed. [362]
At the end of the trail, where the switchback emptied onto a shelf of black water, stood a small wooden crate, half submerged, its lid loose. [388]
Inside, beneath a folded cloth, lay a single object. [397]
It was the bell of the chapel that had stood on this shore before the reservoir was built, the chapel where Maren's grandmother had married her grandfather, and the bell had a crack down its side like a river on a map. [439]
Maren lifted the bell, and the wind chime in her memory answered it, and the mirrored hush between them grew so quiet that she could hear the town underneath the water begin to speak. [473]
She had come to expunge the silt from this bell, but now she understood that the silt was not the enemy. [494]
The silt was the town itself, holding its breath. [503]
She sat on the crate and arranged the nine photographs in a fan across her knees, and one by one she read the faces to the black water, and the faces did not disappear. [537]
They listened. [539]
When Maren had been a child, her grandmother had told her that stories were not told to be remembered but to be shared, and that the sharing was the only kind of remembering that mattered. [574]
She had carried that sentence in the pocket of her coat ever since, and tonight she finally understood it, for the photographs showed faces that were not her family at all, they were the faces of neighbors and strangers, and the bell was not her grandmother's bell alone, it was the bell of anyone who had ever needed to be found. [635]
She was being precisely humane now, the way her grandmother had been precisely humane, the way a levee is precise without being cruel, holding the water back only so the town might breathe on its own when the water chose to recede. [678]
Maren did not expunge the silt. [684]
She sang instead, the song her grandmother had sung to the bell, and the wind chime in her cottage, which had been silent for an hour, began to ring again at midnight though there was no wind to ring it. [724]
By the time the ridgeway grew pale at the edges she had walked the trail of photographs back to the embankment and laid them along the crest where the morning walkers would find them. [758]
She carried no photographs now, only the purpose her grandmother had pressed into her palm, to spin stories into shared meaning, and the bell's crack, and the wind chime's quiet ring, and the town rising quietly between two mirrored hushes. [798]
