<story>In the canyon where every footfall answered a hundred times, the towers of bone rose like teeth from a jaw of stone. [22] Etched into each ribbed column were names no one spoke aloud, and the wind through the hollows sang them in a forged hush, a quiet hammered thin as silver. [51] Before the ritual of forgiveness could begin, the canyon would gather its echoes into a single held breath. [69] Lira refused that breath. [73] She was a defiant artist with ink-stained fingers and a grin that always arrived one beat too soon, innocently mischievous in the way of children who pull wings only to marvel how dust clings. [107] The elders had named her curse-bearer when she was seven, after she recited a verse that made the priestess weep in public and the chief hunter laugh at his own father's grave. [139] They had not forgiven her for either. [146] Lira climbed the pale spire in mist that rose from the canyon's deepest floor, the one the pilgrims said held the throat of the world. [171] At its crown she planted her stool, her battered ledger, and the tin cup she used as a drum. [190] From there, the echoes below could not eat her words before they landed, and that was important, because her words were the only tools she had to refract a single truth into many. [223] She held impromptu poetry sessions on the windward ledge, three days before the ritual, when the canyon filled with travelers carrying their grievances like sacks of grain. [250] Some climbed to scold her. [255] Some climbed because scolding was the only shape their forgiveness could take, and Lira welcomed both, pouring tea from a soot-blackened kettle and asking each arrival to speak the one thing they could not release. [290] Then she would answer with a poem. [297] The poem was never comfort. [302] It was a mirror set at a strange angle, catching the truth they had tried to bury and splintering it into facets they had not imagined, so the same wound looked like a door, a song, a debt, a small child's hand, a stone. [346] This was the work she had been born for, she believed, and the work that had earned her the elders' dread. [367] They said forgiveness was simple. [372] Lira knew it was not. [377] The price of forgiveness was forgetting, and forgetting was a kind of murder that left the corpse walking, and she would not let the canyon pretend otherwise. [404] So she sang of debts still owed, of names that had not yet been carved into the bone towers, of the girl whose brother drowned because the chief hunter refused to hear the storm warning that had been shouted, in verse, from this very spire. [449] Her poems refract the silence of the canyon into a thousand small accusations and a thousand small mercies, and the travelers who climbed down again carried both, heavier than they had climbed. [481] On the morning before the ritual, an old woman followed the usual path up the winding steps carved into the spire's flank. [503] It was the priestess Lira had made weep, twenty years gone, her face now a folded letter. [520] She did not come to scold. [526] She came to sit across from Lira, knees creaking, and to listen, with the patient stillness of someone who had finally learned to hold her breath without breaking it. [555] "What is the price?" the priestess asked, and her voice was the voice the canyon borrowed when it wanted to be honest. [577] Lira did not answer in prose. [583] She took up her tin cup and tapped it twice against the ledger, and the echoes answered from the bone towers below, soft and many, and she began: [611] "To forgive is to pay a debt with a coin you did not mint; the face is yours, the weight is not; give it, and the river learns your name again." [642] The priestess closed her eyes. [647] Below, the canyon held its forged hush, and the echoes waited, and the ritual at dawn would begin or would not, depending on what the old woman decided to carry down the long stone steps. [682]</story>
