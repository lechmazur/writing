<story>The cobalt atrium opened like a held breath against the frozen lake, its tall windows trembling with the cold that pressed in from every pane. [25] Inside, after the ban lifts, the silence had the texture of a lantern-moth hush, soft wings beating against glass. [44] The cloud-shadow poet had returned for the first time since the city fathers unsealed the colonnade, and he moved as if the floor remembered his name. [70] He had been aesthetically driven even then, twenty winters past, when his verses were the first things whispered under the quartz. [91] Now the humming quartz colonnade ran the length of the atrium, each pillar sending a faint, glassy note into the air, and the poet paused between them as if listening for a chord the room had forgotten. [128] He had come to paraphrase the old hymn his mother used to sing before the council forbade her voice in public spaces, and he wanted the colonnade to sing it back to him. [161] There was a tremor of certainty in his chest, small as a moth at the mouth of a lantern, and he let it guide his steps. [187] The frozen lake beyond the windows gave back the cobalt sky in pieces, and the poet felt, as he always had, that beauty was a kind of permission. [215] When the council had lifted the ban on the colonnade, they had framed it as a restoration of civic pride, but he suspected they simply wished to hear the sound again and pretend it was their own. [252] He did not mind; the room was wide enough for thefts and givings both, and his longing had always been aesthetically driven, fed by surface and shimmer rather than doctrine. [282] He had come here as a young man to stride through boundaries once thought sacred, the threshold between choir and colonnade, between breath and stone, and the colonnade had answered in its brittle choir. [316] Then the ban had come down like a shutter, sealing the atrium for a generation, and he had carried the hum in his bones like a child carried in the body before it could be named. [352] Now he stood at the first pillar and laid his palm against the quartz, and the humming slid into him through dissolving boundaries between skin and crystal, between the singer and the sung. [385] He let the old hymn rise in his throat, not sung but paraphrased, each phrase unspooled into something looser, and the colonnade took each strand and braided it into the room's long silence. [418] The tremor of certainty grew no larger, but it steadied, and he walked the length of the hall with his hand trailing along the cool vertebrae of stone. [446] At the far window the frozen lake caught the last of the dusk, and he could see his own reflection standing quietly between the pillars, a cloud-shadow made of light. [476] He understood then that paraphrase was not a softening but a crossing, a way to stride through boundaries once thought sacred by saying them in a tongue the present could bear. [507] The hymn his mother had loved was not the hymn of the council; it had been carried in his body through dissolving boundaries of memory and statute, and now it breathed again in the quartz. [542] He bowed to the colonnade as one might bow to a sleeping animal, and the pillars answered with a hum so soft it could have been mistaken for breath. [571] Outside, the lantern-moth hush settled over the lake like a second skin, and he thought that this, finally, was the verse he had been trying to write since the day they sealed the doors: a small, true sound in a wide blue room.
