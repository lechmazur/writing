Maren climbed the last switchback as dawn thinned the mist, her boots slipping on moss that smelled of old rain and older secrets. [23]
She was an esoteric realist, a person who read auguries in tea leaves but also patched her own socks and could tell you the square footage of any room she had slept in. [56]
Above her, hidden among the shoulders of the green mountain, lay a verdant grotto above the clouds, a place she had been told existed by a great-aunt who had died before Maren could ask why it mattered. [93]
She reached it just as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, spilling peach light into a hollow where the ferns grew waist-high and the air trembled with the soft percussion of unseen wings. [126]
From the rim of the grotto, she watched them come: the migration of the last monarch butterfly, a trembling orange river drifting south through the cold pass. [153]
There were so few of them that each one seemed to carry the weight of the entire species, and when one paused on a milkweed stem, Maren held her breath as though she were greeting a dignitary. [190]
In her palm she kept a single moth wing, pale as parchment, which she had found pressed between the pages of a book of forgotten saints. [216]
It was her reminder that some histories are made of the flimsiest materials and still manage to outlast stone. [235]
The wing, she believed, was a kind of clock, and its scales caught the light now with the same shy iridescence they had held for a hundred years. [263]
She had come to honor overlooked histories, the names that had been rubbed from ledgers, the small rebellions that had been mistaken for accidents. [287]
The grotto itself had once been a waystation for pilgrims who followed a now-vanished river, and the flat stones where she knelt still bore the shallow cup-marks of their cooking fires. [318]
Maren began to work, gathering the long, pliant vines that looped from the grotto's mouth like green rope, and she started to knot them into small, deliberate forms. [346]
Each knot was a signal, a temporary structure meant to be read by whatever followed, whether that was a fox, a storm, or a child who wandered up the mountain in another century. [379]
She tied a knot for her great-aunt, who had loved the grotto but never spoken of it except in the most oblique terms, and she tied another for the river that had dried up in her own grandmother's childhood. [418]
The work had a cadence, a steady pull-and-pass rhythm that settled her into the place like a second heartbeat, and for a while the only sound was the whisper of vine against vine and the distant, papery rustle of the monarchs passing. [460]
Then the cadence was interrupted. [465]
A monarch, more tired than the others, landed on the back of Maren's hand, and its small weight broke her rhythm the way a held note breaks into silence. [494]
She did not move. [498]
The butterfly folded its wings once, twice, as though considering her, and then opened them to the morning, and Maren saw that this one carried a pattern she had never seen before, a fleck of silver near the thorax that looked, absurdly and unmistakably, like a moth wing. [546]
The grotto seemed to blush around her, a tender stillness that warmed the ferns and softened the stone, and she understood that she was transiently rooted here, only briefly held by the weave of her knots and the borrowed gravity of the place. [589]
Her knots were not meant to last. [596]
They would loosen in the next hard rain, and the vines would return to their wandering, and the signals would be read or not read by whoever came after, and that, she thought, was exactly as it should be. [635]
To be permanent was to be deaf to the interrupted cadence of the world, the pauses that meant something, the silences that were a kind of speech. [662]
She watched the silver-marked monarch lift from her hand and join the slow river of its kin, and she tied one last knot, loose and careful, for all the histories that had been overlooked because they were too quiet to be heard. [704]
When she finally stood to leave, her knees aching and her fingers stained green, the grotto exhaled its mist around her, and the last of the monarchs passed overhead like a single, long-held note resolving into the open air. [743]
She did not look back, because she knew she would return, and because the moth wing in her pocket still held its hundred-year shine, and because some vows are made not in words but in the soft, blushing stillness of a place that has decided, just for now, to remember you. [794]
