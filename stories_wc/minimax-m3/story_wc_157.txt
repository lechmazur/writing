The city had learned to weigh what could not be weighed, and the Bureau of Internal Cognition had grown fat on the effort. [23] After thoughts became taxable, every citizen wore a bone-conduction circlet that monitored idle musings and billed accordingly. [40] Aurelio Vance, a charming rogue scholar expelled from three academies and courted by four more, kept his circlet in a velvet-lined drawer and paid his thought taxes in untraceable cash. [70] He did this because he was the steward of a small, carefully tended spark of defiance, and he had learned that the only way to stand guard over your own spark of defiance was to make it invisible to the instruments that might snuff it out. [116] His rebellion took the strangest of shapes: a secret language club that met in an entomologist's greenhouse on the edge of the district. [139] The greenhouse belonged to Dr. Pell Underhill, a reclusive woman who studied the migratory patterns of moths and asked no questions of the people who slipped in through her humidity-locked doors after dark. [172] Inside, among the orchids and the wire-framed cases of chrysalises, the club whispered in a tongue no circlet could parse. [192] It was a language of soil and seedbed, a syntax written in lettuce rows and radish circles, a candidly cryptic alphabet that bloomed only when viewed from the right angle at the right hour. [226] To read it, one had to join the community gardens scattered across the city, the public plots the Bureau had encouraged as a sop to civic virtue, and plant message patterns in the furrows: a curl of cabbages here, a straight rank of beans there, a spiral of sunflowers turning toward a hidden point on the horizon. [283] Aurelio's students were gardeners, bricklayers, clerks, and one retired thought auditor who had grown sick of the work. [301] They came to the greenhouse to learn the old tongue and to share cuttings of a particular vine that, when dried and shaken, rattled like a gourd full of dry summer. [332] The rattle seed, as they called it, was the club's talisman: a small, hard-shelled thing that carried the rhythm of the language in its hollow heart. [358] Aurelio would tap the seeds against the greenhouse glass, and the moths, disturbed, would lift and settle in patterns that spelled out the night's lesson in living script. [386] The Bureau, of course, had noticed the moths. [394] Their agents arrived at the greenhouse one fog-shrouded morning with warrants and questions, and Aurelio met them at the door with a smile that was candidly cryptic in its own right, a smile that said nothing and suggested everything. [433] He invited them in for tea, and while the kettle warmed, he led them on a tour of the chrysalis cases, pointing out larvae that were, he assured them, quite ordinary. [464] Then, with the polite ruthlessness of a scholar who has read the entrails of a thousand bureaucratic procedures, he began to warp the conversation. [488] He asked the agents about their gardens, about the community plots they had joined, about the rows of beans they had planted in patterns they themselves did not yet understand. [518] The agents blinked. [521] By the time the tea was poured, the Bureau's men were sitting among the orchids, gently shaking rattle seeds in their palms, listening to the tiny percussion of a language they could not yet read but were beginning, despite themselves, to feel. [563] Aurelio refilled their cups and watched the moths settle on their shoulders, and he understood that the convergence he had been engineering for seven years was finally arriving: a converging chaos of moths and messages, of tax collectors turning into gardeners, of thoughts becoming once again, in the strangest way, unweighable. [614] He thought of his circlet, still in its drawer, and he permitted himself one small, defiant smile. [631] Outside, in the community gardens across the city, the radishes had begun to spell out a new word in the old tongue: begin. [654]
