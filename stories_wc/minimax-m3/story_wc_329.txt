<story>The brass arches of the sky-bridge curved over the orchard like the ribcage of a slumbering leviathan, and beneath them, Lira tuned her violin with fingers that remembered songs her mind had forgotten. [33]

She had been the sky-bridge violinist for seventeen years, ever since the Council of Statues decreed that only her music could soothe the orchard's iron moods. [27]

She had accepted the role hoping to reawaken beauty in a mechanical world that had ground wonder down to schedule and statute, but year after year the music had been permitted only in prescribed measures. [34]

The statues themselves moved now, white marble limbs twitching as they patrolled the silver-leaved rows, their hollow eyes scanning for any blossom that dared bloom out of season. [28]

Lira drew the bow across the strings, and the note that rose was resonantly soft, a whisper meant for the roots rather than the canopy. [24]

Somewhere in the gap between knowing and understanding, the music worked its quiet magic, and the trees leaned toward her like sleepers drifting toward a dream they could not quite recall. [32]

But tonight every note felt heavier, tarnished wonder leaking from the song, as though the orchard had grown tired of being beautiful on command. [26]

She paused and drew from her coat a locust shell, brittle and amber, the last relic of the summer before the great mechanization. [23]

Her mother had pressed it into her palm and whispered that the truest songs were not played but pulsed, signaling through heart rhythms, a language older than any statue's law. [29]

Lira held the shell against her chest and let her heartbeat carry its message into the bark of the nearest tree, a patient Morse of longing. [25]

The tree shivered, and in its trunk a green pulse answered, the first unauthorized blossom unfurling against the moonlight. [21]

A statue turned its head with the grinding patience of ancient stone. [12]

"Lira," it intoned, the hollow echo of a man who had once loved music, "the orchard's rhythms are prescribed. Uproot what you have begun." [25]

She bowed again, but this time the note held a tremor, a question the statues had forbidden her to ask for seventeen years. [22]

What if beauty could not be policed? [7]

She reached the nearest tree and pressed her palm to its bark; through the wood she felt her own heart, and through her heart she felt the tree, and through the tree the whole orchard, a sleeping choir waking one note at a time. [44]
