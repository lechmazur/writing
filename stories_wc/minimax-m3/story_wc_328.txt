<story>Mara crouched in the herbalist's underground garden, breathing air that smelled of rosemary and something unnamed by any pharmacopeia she'd ever studied. [22] She was supposed to be a buyer of tinctures with a sinus infection, but she was actually a cryptid researcher working undercover, sent three weeks after what the agency called the Quiet Return. [55] The Quiet Return had not been quiet at all. [64] Creatures that had vanished from folktales and forest edges - the hush-pawed lynxes, the moss-skinned children, the river-keepers who could sing a lake dry - had come back changed, and the town had split between those who lit candles and those who sharpened spears. [106] Tonight, the wiry herbalist named Vera had invited her into the cellar garden for a private consultation, the kind you only get when you have paid enough, or when both of you know the other is pretending. [143] Mara slipped her hand into her coat and felt the smooth weight of the flint arrowhead she had carried for eleven years, since the morning her brother had walked into the woods and not walked out. [179] That was, until last week, when the missing return changed everything for her too. [193] He had come back humming, and sometimes humming was all he did. [205] Down in the cellar, Vera pulled aside a curtain of dried lavender to reveal clay pots glowing under grow-lights, and in their midst sat an old cordless phone with scuffed beige plastic. [237] "For you," Vera said, pressing it into Mara's palm with trembling fingers. [249] "He calls sometimes," she whispered. [254] "The one you're hunting. He likes this old thing. The static, I think. He says it's like being inside a snowstorm." [275] Mara lifted the handset, and the line crackled with white noise, then a voice, gravelly and amused: "Hello, little researcher. I know your name. I know you didn't believe in us, or you didn't, until your brother came back wrong." [315] She flinched but did not hang up. [322] The confession poured through static: a cryptid, gravitationally playful, had been tugging at the moon's pull on small villages for centuries, lifting sleeping cats from windowsills, tipping a hunter off his horse once because the man had laughed at a fox. [363] It was a harmony untamed, the creature insisted, not a haunting - the wild music of things refusing catalogue, cork boards, or fluorescent offices. [386] Mara felt the bitterness rise in her throat, eleven years of cold case folders and unanswered radios, of her brother's empty chair at Thanksgiving, of grief frozen solid so she could keep working. [419] "Why are you telling me this?" she asked, her voice smaller than she meant. [504] Gravity here was a suggestion, a child's game of toss and catch. [516] She laughed, and it came out cracked, half a sob. [526] Her brother had hummed the same melody last Sunday when she had brought him the flint arrowhead, and he had metamorphosed mid-laugh, his eyes brightening from the dull gray of return to the green of someone still becoming. [564] "He is becoming," the voice said gently. [571] "As are you. Stay on the line." [578] In the underground garden, the herbs bloomed out of season, mint sending up silver flowers, lavender exhaling impossible color, everything swaying toward a ceiling that had become a sky. [607] Mara closed her eyes, and inside the static she heard not one confession but dozens - the voices of every cryptid researcher before her, now laughing, admitting they had loved the things they hunted. [682] Mara tucked the feather beside the flint arrowhead in her pocket and walked up the cellar stairs into the town.
