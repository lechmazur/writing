In the upper air, where the wind never ceased its mourning, a palace hung from the ribs of the world, anchored by chains of iron to the great stalactites that pierced the ceiling of the stone sky. [37] It had been a kingdom once, the prince remembered, though his memory was as tattered as his cloak. [18] He was a bedraggled prince now, heir to nothing but the ticking of gears and the hiss of steam. [19] Below him, the world had gone mechanical: forests of brass and copper, rivers of oil, cities whose hearts were pistons. [20] He wandered the suspended halls alone, hypothesizing about everything: why the birds had vanished, why the flowers had been replaced by filigree, why no song could be coaxed from the chrome throats of the singing towers. [36] In the deepest archive, behind a door he had never opened, he found it resting on a velvet pillow. [19] An egg. [2] It was the color of dawn before dawn, mottled with patterns no machine could replicate. [15] He recognized it from the dusty bestiaries: the egg of the skylark-swan, a bird extinct for three generations, whose hatching was said to bring a season of true color back to the land. [33] He cradled it in his scarred palms, and his breath caught, quaking with reverie. [14] This, he thought, was the flawed genesis of the world he had inherited: a bird that had been made to sing, and a song that had been made to die. [30] Long ago, the engineers had tried to perfect creation, smoothing every feather into flawlessness, every note into purity. [18] But perfection had starved the world. [6] Without the grit of imperfection, no real thing could be born. [11] He brought the egg to the central chamber, where the palace's main chains met the stalactites, and began to assemble what the old books prescribed: a nest of living moss, a bowl of rainwater caught from the storm-rim, a pebble warmed by a single candle. [45] He worked in the humming dark, hypothesizing about every step, testing each hypothesis with the patient madness of a scholar in a tomb. [23] He wondered whether the egg would hatch if he sang to it. [12] He wondered whether the world below would hear. [8] He sang, though his voice was rough and untrained, and the candle flame quivered in sympathy. [16] Nothing stirred within the shell. [5] He tried again the next day, and the next, building rituals out of guesswork. [14] He placed the egg at the very edge of the palace, where the chains met the stone, so that the dawn light could find it first. [26] On the thirty-third morning, the egg trembled. [7] A crack ran across its surface, thin as a hair, and from it leaked a sound, half-keening, half-carol. [18] The prince wept, though he did not know whether it was grief or joy. [14] The bird that emerged was not what the bestiaries had promised: one wing was short, one eye was clouded, and its plumage was the dull grey of smoke. [28] It was a flawed genesis, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. [16] He named it Imperata, and he let it perch upon his shoulder, and together they looked down upon the mechanical world. [21] He understood then that he could not reawaken beauty wholesale, that the skylark-swans would not darken the sky in their old numbers, that the rivers of oil would not unlearn their coursing. [32] But he had reawakened one. [5] And the bird, broken and singing in its broken way, was proof that the world could remember what it had been, even if only in glimpses. [26] He resolved to descend the chains, to walk into the brass forests below, carrying the bird and its single, imperfect song. [21] The palace would remain, anchored to its giant stalactites, patient as a held breath. [14] And the prince, no longer quite so bedraggled, would carry his bitterly sweet triumph into the cogs.
