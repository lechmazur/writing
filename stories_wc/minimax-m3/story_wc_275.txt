<story>The tower had fallen three seasons before Liora inherited the glass-domed enclosure, and still the desert pressed against the curved panes like a patient creditor. [25] Inside, the desert roses bloomed in disciplined rows, their petals the color of dried blood and old bone, watered by cisterns that caught the rare, bitter rain. [52] Liora was the daughter of a bounty hunter whose name the wind had carried into silence, and she had sworn on the day of his burial never to lift a weapon against another living thing. [87] Her father had been a man of quick hands and quicker judgments, and she had inherited neither, though she had kept the scrap of an airship ballast sandbag that had crashed into the yard the night the tower came down. [127] The bag had split open on impact, spilling its grit across the soil, and Liora had gathered the torn canvas and stitched it into a long strip that she wore knotted at her wrist like a quiet bracelet. [165] She was senselessly perceptive, her neighbors said, meaning that she noticed things no one had asked her to notice: the way a beetle turned before a sandstorm, the color of light on a dying leaf, the exact weight of a silence between two strangers. [209] These observations earned her no coin and little company, but they earned her the loyalty of the roses, which opened their blooms only when she hummed the old songs her mother had taught her before the sickness took her. [248] The roses, Liora believed, could be taught to endure, and so each morning she knelt among them and spoke softly of patience, of restraint, of the long memory of roots, inculcating plants with values that the wind and the sun could not easily erase. [292] Outside the dome, the world after the tower falls was a scar of twisted metal and silent broadcast towers, and caravans passed without stopping, their drivers wary of a place that still smelled faintly of roses. [328] Liora watched them go and felt no bitterness, only the familiar ache of a girl who had learned to weave continuity through chaos, threading one steady day into another like beads on a fraying cord. [363] The engraved quiet of the dome was her cathedral, and the small rituals within it were her sacraments: the watering, the pruning, the whispered lectures to petals that listened without judgment. [394] One evening, a stranger arrived on a lame camel, his coat stiff with the dust of the broken highway, and he asked for water and for news of the road. [424] Liora gave him water in a clay cup, but she gave him no news, because the only news she trusted was the one the roses told her through the slow turning of their leaves. [458] The stranger, whose name was Halen, studied the strip of ballast canvas at her wrist and asked if it was a charm against the wandering dead. [484] She told him it was a reminder, and when he pressed, she said that an engraved quiet lived in its fibers, the hush of a thousand tons of ship held aloft and then let go. [519] Halen laughed and said she was a poet, but Liora only shook her head, for she was a farmer, and the difference mattered in a place where the soil had forgotten how to lie. [553] He stayed three days, and on the third night he confessed that he was a hunter of men, sent to find a debtor who had vanished into the glass gardens of the eastern reach. [587] Liora listened without moving, and when he asked if she had seen such a man, she said that she had seen everyone and no one, because her eyes were senselessly perceptive and could not be aimed like a rifle. [626] Halen smiled and reached for the knife at his belt, and Liora, who had never sheathed a weapon because she had never drawn one, instead sheathed her hands in the soil, pressing her palms into the warm earth between two blooming rows. [668] The stranger looked at her, then at the roses, then at the long scar of the fallen tower on the horizon, and something in his shoulders loosened as if a rope had been cut. [702] He left the knife on the edge of the cistern before he departed, and Liora planted it, hilt and all, among the deepest roots, where the metal would rust into the memory of the bed. [737] The tranquil blaze of the late afternoon sun poured through the dome, and the roses turned their faces toward it as if to receive a promised benediction. [764] Liora returned to her rows, inculcating plants with values, weaving continuity through the long chaos of the hours, her wrist still bound to the scrap of the airship that had once held the world aloft.
