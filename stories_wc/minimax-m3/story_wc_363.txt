<story>The wistful loom spinner named Mira had tended her forgotten orchard deep in a bamboo forest for as long as she could remember. [23]

Each morning she rose before the mist lifted from the rows of pear trees, their gnarled branches heavy with blossoms that no one ever came to pick. [27]

The bamboo rose around her like green pillars holding up the sky, and the orchard itself seemed to breathe with her, patient and old. [24]

On the day she found the box, she had walked to the village rummage sale three hills away, just to feel the weight of other people's cast-off lives in her hands. [31]

The cardboard was soft with age, and inside, among yellowed letters and a cracked teacup, lay a handful of jigsaw puzzle edges, their colors faded but their shapes still sharp. [30]

She held one piece up to the morning light and saw a sliver of sky, a fragment of someone's lost afternoon. [21]

"I can use these," she whispered, and the word carried through the raveling silence of the bamboo grove like a thread pulled from old cloth. [25]

Back in her small house at the orchard's heart, Mira arranged the puzzle pieces on her worn oak table, her fingers stoically sparkling with the fine dust of years. [29]

She had long carried a tree sap amber bead on a leather cord around her neck, given to her by her grandmother, who had tended the same orchard before her. [30]

The bead caught the lamp light and held it, a small captured sun against her collarbone. [16]

Her grandmother had told her, on the last night of her life, that the orchard was not only a place of fruit but a waystation for messages that needed to travel further than any road could carry them. [38]

"Some words must cross impossible distance," she had said, her breath thin as paper. [14]

"And only those willing to give something of themselves can carry them." [12]

Mira had been a child then, and the meaning had slipped past her like water through a sieve. [18]

Now, sixty years on, she understood. [6]

The bead at her throat warmed when a message needed sending, and that morning it had warmed so fiercely she had to press her palm against it to keep from crying out. [32]

She sat at her loom, the great wooden frame that had been her companion since girlhood, and through the jigsaw puzzle edges laid along the beam like a quiet pattern, she began to weave what the bead asked of her. [36]

The thread she used was not thread at all but silence itself, drawn out from the air and shaped into patterns only the loom could read. [26]

Each row she wove, the raveling silence of the orchard grew quieter, as if the world were holding its breath to listen. [22]

She wove the names of people who had died in distant villages, children she had never met, lovers separated by wars she had only heard about in stories. [28]

She wove the last words her mother had spoken, the ones she had been too young to understand. [18]

She wove them all into a single tapestry that stretched across her loom and spilled onto the floor, a river of color and absence. [24]

The work was a latent sacrifice, she knew, because every message she carried took something from her, a memory here, a feeling there, until she herself would become thin as the bamboo leaves outside. [34]

But she did not stop. [5]

Outside, the bamboo swayed, and the pear blossoms fell like snow into the still air, and the orchard waited. [19]

When the tapestry was finished, she gathered it in her arms and walked to the oldest tree, the one her grandmother had planted, and laid the cloth at its roots. [30]

Then she took the amber bead from her neck and pressed it into the earth beside the tapestry, and the bead glowed once, twice, and was gone. [27]

The tree shuddered, and its branches lifted toward the sky, and the message went out across the impossible distance, carried on roots that ran beneath mountains and rivers and the bones of the earth. [34]

Mira stood beneath the tree, her hands empty, her eyes bright with something that was not quite tears. [18]

She walked back to her loom and sat down, and though she was older now and lighter, though something in her chest felt hollowed out, she picked up the shuttle. [30]

The loom had been silent for days. [7]

Now, with the hush of time's unraveling tapestry settling around her like a cloak, Mira began to recommence her weaving, her hands moving with the same quiet grace they had always known. [32]

Outside, a new blossom opened on the oldest tree, and somewhere, far away, someone heard their name called softly in the wind. [22]</story>
