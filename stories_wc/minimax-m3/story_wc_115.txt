The tunnel breathed. [3] Milo held the flashlight steady, more out of stubbornness than hope, and counted the seconds between exhalations of wind. [22] He was a reckless abandoned-mill explorer, yes, the kind of fool who crawled into brick holes at three in the morning to prove nothing was hiding in them. [50] He had crawled down the throat of this old rail line on a tip from a forum thread, the tunnel curving away from a defunct textile works that had once run a night shift so relentless the workers called it the ghost shift, because the looms clattered on even after every body had gone home. [105] Tonight the looms were silent, but the tunnel remembered them. [115] Milo had just confessed something to himself in the dark, something he had not quite said aloud, and the hush that followed was so complete he could hear his own blood moving behind his ears. [150] He walked on in the drowsy alertness of three in the morning, his boots scuffing puddles that smelled of iron. [170] In his pocket his radio was tuned low to a station that called itself The Midnight Veil, where frantic callers begged talk-radio psychics to read their futures. [197] He was religiously skeptical, had been since his grandmother pressed her hands to his chest and called him demon-carrier, so he listened to appraise the callers, weighing each panicked question against what he knew of grief and loneliness. [235] A woman was crying into the line now, asking the psychic whether her husband, who had vanished from the mill seven years ago, was still walking the ghost shift somewhere beneath the city. [268] The psychic said yes. [272] The psychic always said yes. [277] Milo smiled in the dark and pushed deeper. [285] Around the next bend the tunnel opened into a maintenance room, and there on a rust-eaten pipe, half-buried in grit, sat a beaded valve wheel, its handle worn smooth where a thousand ghostly palms had supposedly gripped it. [323] He lifted it. [326] The metal was warm. [330] He did not believe in ghosts, but he believed in temperature, and so he sat cross-legged on the concrete floor and held the wheel against his cheek like a stethoscope while the radio murmured in his jacket. [367] The confession kept returning. [371] He had told his sister, in a voice barely louder than the static, that he had suggested she stop her cancer treatment, calling it dignity, and that she had looked at him with a look he had not stopped seeing for nine months. [414] He had swallowed the truth of that, and now he had come underground to find the weight of it, to weigh it against something older and heavier, something made of metal and machine. [447] The radio crackled. [450] A new caller, a young man this time, breath hitching. [460] He had been a night-shift operator at a chemical plant outside Trenton, and he had seen his own reflection step out of the glass and walk into the yard, and the reflection had turned back and said his name. [499] The psychic asked what the name was. [506] The caller said it twice, and the second time it matched Milo. [518] Milo's hand tightened on the valve wheel. [525] He sat very still. [529] The tunnel exhaled again, and this time it carried with it a faint smell of cotton, which was impossible, because the mill had burned in 1987 and no cotton had grown in this valley for forty years before that. [568] He thought of the ghost shift, the looms that had kept clattering after everyone had left, the night crews that swore they had heard footsteps in empty corridors, the supervisors who had checked the time cards and found them filled in for shifts no one had worked. [615] He thought of his sister. [620] He thought of how a confession, once swallowed, has a weight no human throat can measure, and how the only way to know it is to set it down beside something that has its own weight, and to listen to the difference. [662] He appraised the valve wheel again, turning it in his hands, and found it heavier than he expected, and lighter than he feared, and exactly the size of a thing that could have been turned by a hand that was no longer there. [705] The radio fell silent. [709] The tunnel fell silent. [713] The night, which had been holding its breath with him, let it go. [725] He stood up. [728] He put the wheel in his pack. [735] He climbed out into the air that smelled of rain and diesel and the first edge of morning, and he did not feel absolved, nor condemned, and that, he thought, was what truth weighed. [769]
