The last ship left before the gulls forgot the harbor. [10]
She arrived in the shadowed garden three days later, when the leaves had thickened into bronze and the paths were muffled with their own decay. [35]
Her name was Verra, and she carried a single strand of weft thread, pulled years ago from the unraveling border of a palace carpet that had once softened the footsteps of an empire. [68]
She was a traveler of a particular and strange profession: she wrote obituaries for the living. [84]
Not the celebratory notices that newspapers printed, but the slow, candid documents that chronicled the way a person vanished from themselves long before their bones were released to the wind. [114]
The thread was wound around the middle finger of her left hand; she wore it there, intimately, the way a sailor wears a knot tied by a beloved hand. [143]
She had come to the garden to finish the seventh of her commissions that year. [158]
The man whose absence she was to explain had been a clockmaker in the harbor town, and he was still breathing on the day the ship departed, though he had not truly been present in his shop for nearly two decades. [199]
His wife had written her a letter made of thin paper, folded twice, in which she described the precise hour at which she had understood that her husband was already gone and only his habits remained. [235]
Verra set up her small writing table between two old elms whose shadows lay across the path like spilled ink. [255]
The autumn light, low and amber, made the garden feel both abandoned and full of attendance, as though the season were holding a vigil for itself. [281]
She unrolled the thread across the wooden surface and pressed the end of her pen against it, and the intimacy of the contact startled her, the way touching an old wound can sometimes do. [315]
There was something she understood now that she had not the year before: obituaries for the living were not elegies. [335]
They were maps. [338]
By projecting her presence into the rooms of people she had never met, into the small gestures of their mornings and the long silences of their evenings, she was attempting to trace the geography of how they had healed from being left inside themselves. [382]
It was a topography rendered in absence rather than in land, and she had become, over the years, its only cartographer. [403]
The garden suited the work. [408]
The fallen leaves moved now and then under the breath of a wind that came in from somewhere beyond the hill, and they made a sound like the turning of a long-unread page. [441]
She wrote his name at the top of the page, then paused, holding the pen as one holds a breath. [461]
She thought of the palace where the thread had once been woven, and of the weavers who had known, with each knot, that the carpet would someday begin to come apart. [492]
She thought of the clockmaker, who had made hours out of small brass pieces, but who had somehow failed to keep his own hours inside him. [518]
The explanation she would offer his wife was not that he had been cruel, nor ill, nor indifferent. [536]
It was that he had walked into the center of himself on a particular afternoon and found that the room was empty, and he had been standing politely at its door ever since, explaining to visitors that the master would return shortly. [578]
Verra wrote this down in language that was as precise and as tender as the garden itself, where the bronze leaves lay like a description of the trees they had fallen from. [610]
When she finished, the light had moved a hand's width across the page, and a single leaf, curled like a question mark, had come to rest against the edge of the table. [642]
She coiled the thread back around her finger and felt the small, persistent pulse of the garden against her work, lonesome and splendid in the way that only a place which has learned to wait for no one can be. [682]
She rose, gathered her pages, and walked toward the gate as the final light of autumn pressed itself flat against the wall. [704]
