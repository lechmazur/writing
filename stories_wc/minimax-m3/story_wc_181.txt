I came into being between the first and last page of a manuscript no one admits to owning, and so my story begins with the bewildered curator Pelia, who has just been handed a small velvet box on the threshold of the astral orchard that blossoms on a lonely asteroid she tends. [52] The box contains a pistachio shell cameo blank, creamy and oval, its surface so pale it seems to hold a sliver of moon within it. [77] Pelia turns it over and finds no inscription, no artist mark, no shelf number from her own vast collection. [96] She feels a brightness kindle in her chest, the brightened disquiet of a person who suspects that something is wrong in a way that is also somehow lovely. [124] She has been left an inheritance, and the ledgers tell her it is hers, but the ledgers also tell her she inherited it before she was born, a feat of nonlinear inheritance that the orchard keeps in its roots and she has never understood. [168] Her predecessor, a woman whose name is only spoken as a hum, left instructions in the negative space of a footnote: to pursue forbidden knowledge is to be remade, but not to pursue it is to wither like a grafted branch. [209] Pelia has been groomed since girlhood to inquire into the cameo, to ask every question her predecessors refused to ask, and to record the answers on a sheet of paper that will, by morning, have been blank again. [247] The orchard itself is the simply complicated kind of place, where the blossoms open in silver and the fruit is small and tastes of static and distant rain, and the asteroid hums as it drifts, a kind of low music that only the truly attentive can hear. [294] Pelia walks the rows of trees, which are not trees in the usual sense but columnar lights that have chosen to fruit, and she holds the cameo up to each one, watching for a reaction. [329] None comes until she reaches the centermost bloom, which leans toward her like a shy relative and offers a single drop of resin onto the shell. [355] The drop does not sit on the cameo; it sinks into it, and where it sinks, a tiny face begins to appear, the cameo no longer blank. [382] The face is her own, but older, and smiling in a way she has never been able to smile. [401] She feels a sudden urge, familiar to her as a recurring dream, to retreat to the small bathroom behind the curator's lodge, where the tiles are cool and the water sings into a basin shaped like a cupped hand. [440] Philosophers across the centuries have spoken of having philosophical breakthroughs in bathrooms, and Pelia has come to trust the way her mind unclenches beneath the humming lights, the way ideas loosen like hairpins. [473] She sets the cameo on the basin and begins to wash her hands, and as the water runs, the face in the cameo begins to speak, though the words come as feelings first, then half-sentences, then full phrases about nonlinear inheritance, about how the dead do not pass on what they have in order but in the order the living can bear. [535] Her grandmother, who died when Pelia was four, has been trying for decades to bequeath to her the location of a forbidden library, and the location has been shifting each time, not because the library moves but because Pelia is not yet ready. [578] The brightened disquiet in her chest grows warmer, and she understands, with the sudden quiet of a person hearing a bell, that the inheritance is not a place but a posture, a willingness to keep asking even when the answer erases the question. [621] She dries her hands, wraps the cameo in the velvet, and steps back into the orchard, where the centermost bloom is still leaning toward her, patient as a parent, and the asteroid turns slowly underfoot, cradling its improbable trees and her improbable work, between the first and last page of a story that, like all stories, has been waiting for her to be born.
