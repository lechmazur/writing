The glass orchard library floated upon a swirling sea of clouds, and on the morning of its very first opening day, the lull had settled into the aisles like pollen. [30]

I am a clean slate vendor, and my small shop waited beneath the canopy of crystal trees, each leaf catching the light and breaking it into slow, drifting pieces. [29]

Customers would come soon, but for now I stood among my wares, mindfully scattered between the shelves, arranging nothing in particular because nothing in particular was my trade. [28]

I sold the beginnings of things: blank pages the color of fresh cream, new notebooks bound in pale thread, and a single bottle of moonlight, sealed with wax the exact shade of morning. [33]

The bottle glowed faintly against the counter, and I turned it in my hands, watching the small captive sun drift through its glass. [23]

Outside, the cloud sea churned in slow spirals, and through the orchard windows the world looked like a thing seen through water, soft and rearranged. [25]

I had come here with one purpose, to find the place where stories begin, because I had spent my life selling the empty margins of books and had never once read a complete sentence. [34]

The quiet between footsteps was the only clock I kept, and during this lull I felt the floor hum with the gentle weight of not yet. [26]

I took up a stylus and began to incise a sign for the door, working by quantifying absence: every letter carved as the shape of what was missing from the day. [31]

G-L-I-M-M-E-R-I-N-G, I wrote, and each notch held a small, glittering pause. [11]

P-A-R-O-L-E, I added, and the second word hung beside the first like a bell waiting to be rung. [18]

A glimmering parole, I thought, is just a sentence released from the page, permitted to wander, and I wondered whether my customers would understand such a thing. [27]

They would arrive, perhaps, with empty hands and quieter pockets, looking for the place where stories begin, the way I had looked, the way I still looked. [27]

I set the sign against the glass wall of the orchard, and the two words caught the light and became one thing: a promise and a question, held in the same hand. [32]

The store was mine and not yet mine, mindfully scattered between the shelves, because every slate I sold was a fragment of the larger unfinished book. [26]

To sell a clean slate is to sell the moment before the first word, and the buyer must bring their own sentence, the way a guest brings their own song to a quiet house. [34]

I incised more signs, working always by quantifying absence, marking what was not yet said, what had not yet been read, what the morning still owed the day. [28]

Each cut was small, deliberate, and the cloud sea below began to brighten as if answering the invitation of the bottle on my counter. [24]

Somewhere beyond the orchard, a bell might ring soon, and a customer might step through the door carrying nothing but a wish for a new page. [26]

When they did, I would hand them a slate and a small bottle of moonlight, and I would say that the bottle was for reading by, though really it was for finding the place where stories begin. [37]

I would not explain the glimmering parole, because explanations belong to closed books, and I was a vendor of open ones. [21]

The lull deepened, and I sat behind the counter, mindfully scattered with my thoughts, watching the cloud sea turn like the pages of a slow, unending volume. [27]

This was my store on its very first opening day, glass and orchards and a sea below, and I had not yet sold anything, and I had already sold everything. [30]

I incised one last mark on the counter, a single line, by quantifying absence, and the line meant: here is where the story has not yet started. [27]

Tomorrow the customers would come, but today, in the lull, I would wait, the bottled moonlight glowing beside me, the sign on the glass, the cloud sea churning, and the quiet holding the place where stories begin. [37]
