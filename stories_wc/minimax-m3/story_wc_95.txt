<story>The duelist-philosopher Corian Drexh stood within the glass atrium beside the quiet brook, his mechanical arm catching the late afternoon light with a soft, oiled whisper. [26] He had come here to wait, and waiting was the only duel he had not yet learned to lose. [45] The afternoon had turned the atrium into a small cathedral of shadows and refracted water, and the brook beyond it murmured its long, patient arithmetic. [70] In his left hand he kept the small Danish tin sewing-needle card, a slim rectangle that his master had once pressed into his palm with the instruction that it would one day turn every question into a destination. [108] He had carried that tin card across three continents, through the smoke of twenty-three sanctioned duels and the polite quiet of forty diplomatic ones, and he had never yet unwrapped the ribbon that bound it. [143] The card held, folded against its back, a half-painted sundial whose angles were coded in a private language: a hidden overture of light and number that his master had called the calculus of arrival. [177] To read it, Corian would have to fence with the sun itself, parrying each hour against the bronze numerals until the half-finished dial revealed, at last, the shape of his true adversary. [209] He raised his mechanical arm and the brass fingers answered with the small, decisive click of a blade being seated in a foil grip that had not been used in seven years. [241] He had no opponent, only his own reflection drifting in the glass panes, and that was, he liked to think, the most diplomatically fierce opponent a man of questions could ever choose. [273] To fence with oneself, he had taught his students, was to argue with one's own premise until the premise bowed and admitted that it had been, all along, a destination in disguise. [305] The tin card lay open at last upon the railing of the atrium, and the half-painted sundial spread its coded angles across the metal as if waiting for a conductor. [335] Corian breathed, and the brook replied with the soft percussion of a stone settling against another stone, the kind of small, dignified chaos that only patience can orchestrate. [363] He stepped into the strip of light that the glass panes had offered him, raised his foil in the courteous salute of the school of Argent, and waited for his self to advance. [396] As the sun slipped behind the highest pane, a single coded angle on the half-painted dial caught the light and threw it forward, a clean, sharp line that bisected his shadow across the floor. [430] The bisection was a doorway, and the doorway was a question, and Corian answered it the only way his order allowed: by turning the question into a destination with the tip of his blade. [464] His foil descended in a controlled arc that met the shadow exactly at its midpoint, and the small brass joint in his mechanical arm sang as it shouldered the force. [494] From the impact, a soft chime rose through the atrium and travelled out across the brook, a hidden overture finally given breath by the waiting and the waiting and the waiting. [525] The brook had always known the overture, Corian realized; it had been playing it for years in the small language of stones, and he had only ever needed the patience to learn to listen. [559] He lowered his blade with a smile that was diplomatically fierce and faintly sad, the smile of a man whose questions had finally, and irrevocably, become the place where he would rest. [591] He closed the tin card, slid it back into the small pocket of his coat, and stood in the glass atrium beside the quiet brook for a long time, listening to the overture that the world, in its dignified chaos, had been rehearsing just for him.
