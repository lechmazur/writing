<story>In the attic of Marlow's Vintage Books, the dust fell in slow curtains through a single round skylight, and I learned to read the world in paper trails. [28] I had been a courier pigeon trainer once, before the autumn that took my flock and, with them, the version of myself I could bear to remember, so I reinvented my personal history into a small, tidy fiction. [66] I told new faces I had been a printer's apprentice, a counting-house boy, a son of a Dorset shepherd, and each lie settled like a new feather onto the shoulders of the man I was becoming. [102] The shop's owner, Miss Elara Marlow, kept a small glass jar on her workbench that held a chip of iron from a Mars meteorite, which she said had been carried to Earth by the only pigeon who returned from that lost storm. [144] I had not believed her at first, but the chip had a quiet, almost red weight, and when I held it I could feel the distance my birds had flown, somewhere I could not follow. [179] My job was to balance the ledgers of forgotten authors, and to mend the spines of books that no living hand remembered opening, and to watch the swirl of dust motes under the skylight when the afternoon turned amber. [218] I was mapping those motes each day, tracing their slow eddies with my finger against the glass, and the pattern was the only clock I trusted, since it bloomed and withered in the same hour. [253] I had learned not to trust men, not to trust birds, not to trust the stories I told about myself, but I had begun, slowly, to trust the silence between one mote and the next. [288] There was a letter in the attic that I could not bring myself to file, written in a hand I had known since boyhood, and its ink had faded into the soft withering of old roses pressed between the pages of a hymnal. [331] The letter was from a woman who had sung in the village choir before the war took her voice, and she had written, in a long looping script, that she wanted someone to preserve the echo of lost songs. [370] I read the letter at dusk, when the skylight dimmed, and I felt something open in me like a stubborn seed deciding to bloom. [394] I understood then that my own history was a kind of song, one I had been too afraid to hear in full, and that the paper trails of strangers were the only chorus I was fit to join. [432] So I began to copy, in my careful hand, the songs of the lost choirs into the endpapers of damaged books, sealing each melody between the swelling and the withering of yellowed pages. [465] The pigeon I had been did not die in the storm, I told myself, but only flew so far that his route home became a curve I could not trace without a map. [498] Miss Marlow watched me copy the songs, and she set the Mars chip on the desk beside me as a paperweight, and I felt the tiny planet's gravity pulling at something in my chest. [532] In time, a young apprentice came up the creaking stairs, and I did not send him away with a lie, but told him the true, unlovely shape of my flock and the storm and the years of false names. [571] He did not laugh, and he did not pity me, and he asked, quietly, if I would teach him to balance a feather on his palm the way I had once balanced seed and salt for the homing birds. [610] I said yes, and we stood under the skylight, watching the motes swirl between us, and I knew that trust was not a thing one found, but a thing one mapped, breath by breath, in a room full of blooming dust.
