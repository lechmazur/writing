June Marlowe made her living as a myth buster, and she arrived at the abandoned observatory on a cliff determined to ruin its favorite ghost story. [26]
The town below insisted that on windless nights a painted seashell inside turned by itself and pointed to ships that had already sunk. [49]

June picked it up and found it warm, although the room was cold enough to make her laugh once, for luck. [180]
That laugh was habit, the gloomy cheerfulness she used whenever a place tried to frighten her before she understood it. [200]
She opened the log because her grandfather had kept it, and because its final page held the question she had never answered. [222]
He had written, What remains north when every star has lied, and under it, Retell me truly, or remain lost. [242]
June wanted to decode a universal riddle not for fame, but because that sentence was the last thing he had ever asked her. [265]

Now, older and less certain, June reimagines past conversations whenever grief edits them, and she heard that warning instead as an invitation to listen longer. [314]
She set the recorder running, placed the shell beside the cello, and waited for wind, fraud, or physics to confess. [334]
Nothing turned, nothing glowed, and her meter slept, so she smiled at the ruin and said the legend needed better evidence. [355]

June did not run, because running would have made a better story than she deserved, and she wanted the truth more than the story. [442]

So, still smiling because weeping felt too easy, she spoke into the recorder and through the humming air, correcting the legend and confessing her absence. [640]
She said the compass was ephemeral because north was not a place but a brief agreement to face what happened before looking away. [663]

June laughed then in the cold dome, and the sound was sad and bright at once, because truth had not killed wonder. [709]
She carried the log down at dawn to print the correction, leaving the shell and cello together for the next listener brave enough to begin again. [735]
