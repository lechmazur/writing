<story>Tomas Vale had been a contemplative glassblower for forty years, and he still listened to hot glass the way other men listened to weather. [24] That spring he worked alone in the abandoned control room above the river, where the ice was melting in long, groaning sheets. [46] The station had closed when the gates went automatic, leaving dials, dead radios, and a furnace he had coaxed back to life. [68] Outside, meltwater pressed brown and urgent against the concrete, and every crack in the ice sounded like a door trying to open. [90] He had come back not to fix the river, but to craft safe zones for inquiry before the building was torn down. [112] For Tomas, that meant thick glass viewing domes set into the control room walls, where children could watch the thaw without standing on the dangerous bank. [138] His granddaughter June arrived on the second morning with wet boots, a notebook, and more questions than the river had stones. [159] Tomas was thoughtfully spirited, quick to laugh but careful with living things, and June trusted that care the way she trusted a handrail. [182] Since her grandmother died, however, his laughter had come with an ache, as if something in his chest stayed frozen after the funeral. [205] Ingrid had run this room for thirty winters, calling ice thicknesses over the radio while Tomas made lenses and thermometers downstairs. [226] After she died, he kept her cracked leather bookmark inside the station logbook, pressed flat on the last page she had ever written. [249] He never read past that page, and he never let June touch it, which only made the silence around it heavier. [270] To keep fear small, Tomas had a method, dramatically narrating mundane tasks as if they were voyages, rescues, or coronations. [290] "Now, brave keeper of the kettle," he announced, "we shall tame this wild water before it dares to whistle." [309] June giggled despite the river noise, then solemnly reported that the kettle had agreed to cooperate. [325] When he swept ash from the furnace, he called it clearing a path for tired dragons, and June swept beside him without being asked. [349] The game was light on purpose, but it held serious rules about distance, heat, gloves, and asking before touching anything unknown. [370] That was the real work under the glass and jokes, a place where a child could wonder aloud and not be hurried, mocked, or endangered. [395] By noon he had finished three domes, each cooled slowly and polished until the river showed through them, bent but bright. [416] Through the first dome June watched a slab of ice turn, sigh, and break apart, and she whispered that it looked sad and free at once. [442] Tomas felt the old ache answer her, because Ingrid used to say thaw was not loss, only water remembering how to move. [464] That evening the temperature rose, rain stippled the windows, and the river began carrying branches, fence posts, and whole white islands past the dam. [488] A warning light, dead for years, flickered when meltwater found some forgotten wire, and Tomas understood the building was taking water below. [510] He sent June to the high path with the finished domes wrapped in a blanket, narrating her retreat as a royal procession carrying three small suns. [536] Then he went back for the logbook, because leaving Ingrid behind felt impossible, even if the stairs were already dark with water. [558] On the last page, under her steady handwriting, she had written that no keeper should confuse holding on with keeping safe. [579] The cracked leather bookmark slipped from his fingers into the rising water, and for one terrible breath he reached after it. [600] Then June called his name from above, clear and frightened, and Tomas made the choice his frozen heart had avoided for two years. [623] He would abandon the control room, the logbook, and the bookmark to the river, rather than teach June that love must drown with what it loses. [649] He climbed toward her voice with empty hands, lungs burning, while below him the thawing heart of the river broke the last ice and roared through the gates. [677] Something in his own chest loosened with that terrible music, painful and clean, like warmth returning to fingers after cold. [697] On the high path he held June, narrated their wet socks as a heroic campaign, and heard himself laugh without the old flinch. [720] Weeks later, in the schoolyard above the flood line, he set the three domes into a wooden frame where any child could press close and ask the river questions. [749] June opened her notebook to a blank page, smiled at him, and began the first question in her clearest royal voice. [770]</story>
