<story>Elian was a secretive conjurer who told his spells to no one, not even to the pale trees that listened beneath the hill. [23] He lived at the mouth of a cavern orchard nourished by dripping stalactites, where mineral water fell year-round onto silver-leafed apples that never saw true sun. [49] Moonlight came only through a crooked sinkhole, pooling on the leaves until the whole grove seemed to breathe in dim, borrowed light. [71] His sister Wren had planted that orchard with him before she became the valley's taxidermist, patiently stitching foxes and owls back into the shape of life. [97] Three winters ago she had vanished between supper and dawn, leaving a warm kettle, an open door, and no body, note, or farewell to bury. [122] Because she was neither surely dead nor surely living, Elian's grief had no grave to visit and no hope clean enough to trust. [145] The priest called it an ambiguous loss, and Elian hated how accurate the phrase felt, like a door described perfectly while still refusing to open. [170] So he set himself a private task, the only one he thought might save him: to understand the mathematics of emotion. [191] If sorrow could be measured, he reasoned, it could be carried without spilling, and perhaps one day set down. [210] He was gently inventive by nature, better at coaxing than commanding, and so his experiments began as small, kind adjustments to the orchard's sorrowful routines. [235] He taught drips from the stalactites to fall in fours when he missed her most, hoping four steady beats might make longing resemble a dependable rhythm. [261] He grafted a branch that bloomed whenever he spoke her name aloud, then counted nineteen blossoms in the first week and none in the second. [286] Numbers kept betraying him, because a quiet hour could weigh more than a weeping day, and love refused every column he drew in chalk. [310] On Wren's untouched workbench, under a dust cloth he could not bring himself to wash, sat the taxidermist's glass eye pair she had never used. [335] The eyes were amber for an unfinished owl, one clouded slightly, one clear, and both seemed too alert to leave covered through another winter. [359] One evening he carried them into the grove, meaning only to set them on the wall where the owl might have watched for mice. [383] At the grove's entrance he stopped, as he always did, on the mosaic stepping stone Wren had set there from shards of their mother's plates. [408] Rain from the sinkhole had darkened it, and for the first time he noticed that its blue and white chips were not scattered but sequenced. [433] Three blue, one white, two blue, one white, five blue, a pattern repeating with deliberate breaks wherever a shard had been chipped away. [456] He understood with a conjurer's instinct that Wren, who had distrusted explanations, might have hidden her truest sentence where feet would learn it before minds. [481] He waited for that hour after the orchard grows silent beneath the moon, when even the drips seem to listen, and then he returned barefoot. [506] Via the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone, he began to read not with his eyes but with the soles of his feet. [530] Each break in the sequence asked for a pause, each cluster for a memory spoken honestly, until his careful spell and her rough handiwork began to dovetail. [557] He placed the glass eyes in two chipped hollows, not to make the stone see, but to mark where his understanding had been blind. [581] Then the sequence resolved under his heel: three steps for what was known, one for what was feared, two for what remained possible, again and again. [607] Wren had not left a map to her body or her new life, only a rhythm for walking when neither answer would come. [630] Yet near the last white chip he found something new, sealed in wax beneath a loose shard: a packet of surface seeds and her smallest owl feather. [657] The packet proved she had planned at least one journey upward, though it could not prove she had survived it, and Elian wept without needing to decide. [684] His old equation broke kindly then, because emotion was not a sum to solve but a pattern to keep step with, sometimes limping, sometimes dancing. [709] Before dawn he planted the seeds where sinkhole rain gathered, set the glass eyes to watch over them, and left the stone's last space unfilled. [734] If Wren ever returned, there would be a gap shaped exactly for her foot, and if she did not, something green would still learn the light. [760]</story>
