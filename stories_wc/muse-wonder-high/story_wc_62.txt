Maren was a thoughtful gravestone carver who worked where the village road gave up and became pine roots and dust. [20] At forty she had buried both parents, cut both headstones herself, and learned that a name in stone could feel heavier after it was finished. [45] That autumn, when the first monarchs arrived, she told herself she would use the season to discover the shape of collective grief. [67] She did not mean her own sorrow, which she knew by heart, but the sorrow a whole village carried without ever setting it down together. [92] The work began, as most true things in her family began, with a family custom of trading favorite poems at dinner. [113] Since childhood, no one in her house had passed the bread until someone offered a poem that matched the day they had survived. [136] After her parents died, Maren kept the custom alone in the ancient greenhouse at dusk, speaking poems to the empty chair among her mother's tomato vines. [162] The greenhouse had stood for ninety years, its iron ribs green with age and its glass filmed by summers of rain. [183] On the first evening of the migration, butterflies settled on that glass like sparks while she recited her father's favorite lines about a river that outlived its banks. [211] Then a voice behind the vines answered with the next verse, exact and unhurried, in the thundering calm of her dead mentor, Jonah Pell. [235]

Jonah had taught her to cut letters, to listen to stone before striking it, and to leave room in every inscription for what the living could not yet say. [264] He had been dead three years, and Maren, fiercely meditative even in panic, set down her knife and asked why he had come back now. [289] His shape gathered in the humid air, not whole, but present in the way heat is present above stone. [308] "Because you are cutting only names," he said, "and grief is never only a name." [323] That was the beginning of her spectral mentorship, though she would not have used so grand a phrase for an old man scolding her from among tomato leaves. [351] Each dusk he returned and sent her out the next morning to retrace the last day of someone whose stone she had been asked to carve. [377] That week the village had lost Tomas the ferryman, Widow Albright, and a child named June whose shoes still waited by the schoolhouse door. [401] For Tomas, Maren rowed his ferry route at dawn while his sons told her which poem he used to hum without knowing its words. [425] For Widow Albright, she walked the widow's orchard path and found neighbors had already left plums and folded notes in the hollow of the gatepost. [450] For June, she could only stand outside the schoolhouse and watch June's classmates retrace the hopscotch squares June had drawn, stepping carefully so as not to erase the chalk. [479]

At dinner she carried what she found back to the greenhouse, where Jonah waited and demanded a poem for each life before she touched her bread. [505] At first Maren chose poems she loved, bright and shapely things that made sorrow sound almost noble. [522] Jonah shook his translucent head and unwound the woven ribbon roll that had once bound his bundle of apprentice poems. [542] "Tie one knot for every poem you are given," he said, "and do not choose them yourself again." [560] So Maren began to invite the bereaved, one family each evening, to keep the custom with her in the greenhouse at dusk. [582] They came shyly at first, bringing casseroles they did not eat and poems copied on receipts, prayer cards, and seed packets. [603] Across the butterfly migration, which lasted five weeks that year, the ribbon lengthened with knots until it circled the greenhouse table twice. [625] Tomas's sons traded a sea chant for a psalm, the Albright cousins traded verses about harvest and debt, and June's class traded a skipping rhyme that broke every adult in the room. [657] Maren listened, tied, and recited back what each family gave her, learning that grief did not move in one clean line from pain to peace. [682]

On the last evening, when only a few tired monarchs still crossed the glass, Jonah asked her what shape she had found. [704] Maren looked at the ribbon roll, now heavy with uneven knots, none matching, all holding because each was tied to the next. [726] "It is this," she said, and lifted the roll into the last light. [739] That night she carved no name, but set an uncut stone at the greenhouse door where anyone grieving could sit, speak a poem, and add a knot. [766] When the last butterfly passed, Jonah was gone, but the table was never empty again. [781]
