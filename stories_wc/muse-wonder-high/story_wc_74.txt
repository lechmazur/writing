<story>For three winters, Sora the painter had not slept through a single night, so she learned to work while the smog-choked city below coughed itself quiet. [26]
Her studio was the rooftop greenhouse her mother had built from salvaged windows, where vines climbed old bed frames and the air smelled of soil and saffron tea. [54]
Under a waxing moon, the only light strong enough to thin the smog, she painted after midnight because its growing glow let her see colors honestly. [80]
She was methodically hopeful, which meant numbered jars, dated lists, and one small kindness recorded for every day she did not sleep. [102]
On the first night of that moon, her landlord left a notice saying the roof would be cleared for luxury storage unless tenants proved the greenhouse served the whole building. [132]
Few neighbors spoke to each other, not from cruelty, but because many had arrived speaking languages their new neighbors and even their grandchildren no longer understood. [158]
Those lost languages lived with Sora in fragments, because for years she had painted free signs and banners for strangers who knocked, asking only how to say each pictured thing in the language of their childhood home. [195]
She wrote each word behind its painting and kept forty-one notebooks sorted by plant, by season, and by the face she must not forget. [219]
Her best tool was a calligrapher's moon-tipped brush, its dark bamboo handle ending in a pale silver crescent that caught light like a paring of the moon itself. [247]
Old Wen the calligrapher had given it to her after she spent a sleepless week repainting his flooded shop sign in characters his shaking hands could no longer form. [276]
He told her the brush would remember the hand that had steadied it, and she smiled politely, not yet needing to prove that strangers remember kindness. [302]
Now that proof mattered more than tomatoes, because if no one remembered, her sleepless years had only been a private way to grieve her mother, whose mountain dialect had died unrecorded with her. [335]
On the second night, as the moon widened, Sora laid the brush across an open notebook and let its crescent tip gather light through the greenhouse glass. [362]
Insomnia had taught her that moonlight could spark daydreams more reliably than sleep brought dreams, bright scenes that rose behind her open eyes and stayed if she painted quickly. [391]
Breathing saffron steam from her cup, she leaned into the first daydream and saw Rosa Alvarez teaching her the old word for moonflower while a grandchild answered only in English. [421]
Sora painted that word in Rosa's looping letters across a clean pane, then painted the flower beneath it white enough to hold the moon. [445]
Below it she added Rosa's room number and one line in plain English: this plant still knows your name for it. [466]
Her plan grew like her lists, one pane each night and one remembered stranger each pane, until the greenhouse became a lantern of lost languages thrown against the smog. [495]
She hoped to rouse the building without shouting, letting each neighbor see a word from home glowing above the stairs and remember who had asked for it. [522]
On the third night, the daydream gave her Tomas, who had traded bean seeds for a painted label and taught her his grandmother's word for rain held in leaves. [551]
On the fourth night, she painted for Amara, whose wedding banner she had lettered in two scripts so her late husband's family and her new neighbors could both read the blessing. [582]
Each dawn she checked her list, watered the matching plant, and waited in saffron quiet while nobody came. [600]
The moon kept swelling and the panes kept glowing, and she began to fear that kindness was bright only on the side of the giver. [625]
On the last night before the deadline, too tired to lift the brush, she let moonlight pool on its tip until a final daydream loosened a memory she had guarded for years. [657]
She heard her mother in their old kitchen saying the mountain word for greenhouse, not house of glass, but a room where summer is persuaded to stay. [684]
She painted that word with a shaking hand and, beneath it, painted her mother's face for the first time. [703]
Then footsteps sounded on the iron stairs, and Rosa entered holding her grandchild by one hand and a pot of saffron rice by the other. [728]
Tomas came with bean seedlings, Amara with folded chairs, and Wen's son carrying his father's spare brush wrapped in cloth. [748]
One by one, strangers she had helped filled the greenhouse, speaking names for the plants aloud so the children could hear them. [770]
When the landlord arrived at midnight, he found a crowded room learning to say summer in twelve languages. [788]</story>
