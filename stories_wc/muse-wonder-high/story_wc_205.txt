<story>Mara Venn came to the baroque asteroid in the twelfth year after time became visible, when hours hung in the air like strands of gold thread. [26] She had once led singers and miners in the Canticle Revolt, and now she was only a defunct revolutionary with a trowel, a notebook, and no army. [53] The revolt had failed to free the asteroid from the Cathedral Institute, but it had taught her how easily a ruling story could bury a living people. [80] So she joined the excavation beneath the gilded domes, determined to reimagine an entire civilization from what its rulers had thrown away. [102] The asteroid itself encouraged grand illusions, for its first settlers had carved plain rock into balconies, cherubs, vaulted docks, and staircases that climbed toward no sky. [128] Below that ornament lay older tunnels, plain and patient, and in them the archaeological dispute had burned for twenty years. [148] The Institute claimed the first inhabitants were priests who arrived with seed, liturgy, and law, and who made beauty as proof of obedience. [171] The tunnel workers claimed the first inhabitants were escaped laborers who stole beauty from their masters and made it into shelter, song, and bread. [195] One object could settle the argument, and every child on the asteroid knew its description from school, sermon, and protest wall. [216] It was the crystal vase shard, curved like a frozen wave and etched with a harvest scene that neither side could fully explain. [239] Priests read grain in the etching and saw tithe barns guarded by angels, while workers read the same grain and saw shared fields guarded by neighbors. [265] The shard had vanished during the revolt, on the night Mara ordered the museum doors opened so no faction could own the past. [288] She had not taken it, but guilt followed her anyway, polished by years of loss until it shone each time she entered the dig. [312] Being poetically inclined, she kept her field notes in couplets, because verse forced her to admit uncertainty where prose would pretend to know. [335] Her rivals mocked the rhymes, yet they borrowed her readings whenever an inscription resisted their clean and useful translations. [354] On the morning the storm warning was posted, her team broke into a sealed cellar behind the chapel foundations and found the shard in a lead box. [381] Around it lay broken cups, seed husks, loom weights, and a child's carved horse, arranged not as treasure but as the remains of a meal. [406] Anselm Roque, chief historian of the Institute, arrived within the hour and demanded that the shard be cleaned, raised, and read before witnesses. [429] Mara saw visible time gather at the cellar mouth, bright filaments trembling as the coming ion storm pulled old moments toward every polished surface. [453] Since time had become visible, mirrors had turned treacherous, because a storm could make them return not reflections but selected scenes from nearby years. [477] The Institute loved such scenes when they showed processions, and feared them when they showed whips, hunger, or hands raised in refusal. [499] Roque ordered every mirror in the chapel uncovered, hoping the storm would replay the vase whole and prove the priests had carried it there. [523] Mara knew that hope was a kind of forgery, because mirrors showed watchers the past they were prepared to survive. [543] She had seen a mirror return her younger self giving orders, but never the wounded friends who carried those orders into smoke. [565] So she began to devise a stricter test, one that would deny the storm any easy theater and force the shard to speak alone. [589] Her method was covering mirrors during storms, all of them, with dark wool, sealed seams, and guards sworn to sing rather than look. [612] With every reflective surface blinded, only the crystal could bend the loose strands of time, and it would bend them along cuts made by its maker. [638] Roque called this fear dressed as procedure, but the workers brought blankets, and even two young priests helped nail the last cloth in place. [662] When the storm struck, the asteroid rang like a struck bell, and darkness pressed its bright forehead against every covered glass. [683] In the cellar, strands of visible time slid across the floor, avoided the dull lead and iron, and gathered trembling inside the shard. [706] The etching deepened into hands passing grain from palm to palm beneath a vase raised for everyone to drink. [725] Then Mara read the rim aloud: no hand owns the harvest, and no altar owns the thirst. [742] The founders had been exiles who shed priest and worker as ranks, becoming keepers of one another instead. [760] By dawn, workers and priests were drafting one history together, while Mara polished the shard and mourned all it could not restore. [782]</story>
