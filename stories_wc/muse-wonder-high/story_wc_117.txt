<story>June was a mellow music therapist who drove two hours every Thursday to the small clinic below Sage Ridge. [19] She did not bring worksheets or loud instruments, only a wooden guitar, a rain stick, and a voice that knew how to wait. [42] Her newest patient, Theo, was six, newly adopted, and convinced that silence was safer than sound. [58] For three weeks he sat beneath the clinic window, turning a smooth stone in his hands while June played questions he was free not to answer. [84] His mother, Ana, watched from the doorway with cautious exuberance, hopeful every week and braced every week for disappointment. [103] June understood that hope, because she kept its smaller version in the guitar case beside a folded song sheet. [122] It was a keepsake baby hair curl, dark and impossibly fine, taped inside a card her own mother had saved from June's first haircut. [146] She had carried it since music school, not for luck, but as proof that someone had once noticed the smallest change in her and called it worth keeping. [174] That was her private reason for doing this work at all: to nurture lifelong wonder in children who had learned to hide it before they lost it. [201] So with Theo she was softly adaptive, following his tempo instead of asking him to follow hers. [218] If he tapped the stone twice, she answered with two low notes. [230] If he covered his ears, she hummed without the guitar until the room felt ordinary and safe again. [248] Progress, when it came in late September, was not a song but a question whispered while she tuned the strings. [268] Theo asked whether the sky would really go dark at lunch the next day, when the town planned to watch the eclipse from Sage Ridge. [293] Ana explained that his class had practiced with paper glasses, but Theo feared the sun might forget to return. [312] June did not promise that nothing frightening could happen, because Theo was too smart for easy promises. [329] Instead she asked what sound darkness should have if it visited politely and then left on time. [346] Theo considered the stone, the window, and the guitar before deciding that darkness should sound like everyone breathing together under a blanket. [368] June smiled and wrote that down exactly as he said it, because exact words were another kind of keepsake. [387] The next day the clinic took its small patients to Sage Ridge, where sagebrush silvered the slope and picnic blankets made bright islands in the grass. [413] Above them the cottonwoods stood so still that the children could hear leaves thinking, or so June told them, and no one corrected her. [437] The therapist in her had planned for fear with spare glasses, water, shade, and songs short enough to finish before worry grew teeth. [460] The musician in her had planned for something else, a link between Theo's idea of darkness as breathing under a blanket and whatever the sky was about to do. [489] When the light began to thin, Theo pressed close to Ana and reached, without looking, for June's sleeve. [507] June set the guitar across her knees and began the breathing song they had invented, one note for breathing in and one softer note for letting go. [534] Other children joined not by singing, because singing still felt too large, but by humming where their own fear allowed. [554] Then, in a shared moment during a solar eclipse, the ridge fell truly dark and truly quiet at noon. [573] June had waited for that hush since childhood, pressing her ear to cottonwood bark and imagining time itself slowed beneath the hush of dreaming roots. [598] Theo did not cover his ears. [604] He listened with his whole body, and when a bird called once, uncertain in the false dusk, he answered it with a low tap of the stone, in the tempo June had taught his hands to keep. [641] Ana laughed and cried at the same time, holding his shoulders as if grief and relief had agreed to share. [661] Then the light returned in a thin bright edge, and Theo gasped as if the sun had remembered them personally. [681] On the walk down, he asked June to teach him the song for morning, in case the sky ever needed reminding again. [703] June promised, and that evening she taped the page with his exact words beside the keepsake baby hair curl in her guitar case. [726] It was ordinary magic, she thought, not the sky going dark, but a child choosing a sound and trusting the world to come back into tune. [752]</story>
