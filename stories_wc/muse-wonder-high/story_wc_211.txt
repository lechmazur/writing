<story>After the foghorn falls silent, Mirel Voss climbs to the ancient orchard garden atop a skyscraper to begin her work among the pear trees. [24] She is the city's venerable oath scribe, keeper of vows spoken in joy, grief, debt, and reconciliation. [41] For fifty years she has recorded those vows not to preserve their exact words, but to archive emotional patterns before they fade from living voices. [66] The phonograph waits beneath glass at the center of the orchard, shielded from wind and from the harbor vibration that can scar warm wax. [90] Its brass horn faces twelve pear trees and one apple tree, all planted in deep soil when the tower rose a century ago. [113] This morning the fog remained after the horn surrendered, wrapping the trunks in gray velvet and muting every footstep on the wet stone paths. [137] Her first visitor climbed from the elevator with a canvas tool bag and stood uncertainly beside the oldest pear. [156] He was Elias Ren, the arborist sent to judge whether the tree could survive another winter. [172] Before he discussed rot or branches, he asked whether she would record an oath his mother had taught him before her death last spring. [196] Elias speaks dead languages when grief overtakes him, slipping into the harbor tongue his mother used before the old port drowned. [217] Mirel understood that tongue because scribes learned extinct grammars to receive vows people trusted no neighbor to hear. [235] She fitted a fresh wax cylinder, opened the glass case, and waited until the last elevator bell faded. [253] Elias placed both palms against the oldest pear and promised, in careful syllables, to keep it alive if any living root remained. [275] The words sounded tender, but the pattern beneath them was wrong. [286] His breath broke before the word for alive, held through the word for root, and returned too quickly, bright with practiced hope. [308] Mirel had archived that pattern under vows made beside sickbeds, where love spoke bravely over knowledge it refused to name. [328] She stopped the phonograph and told him the tree might already be dying from within. [343] Elias smiled with relief so naked that she understood he had come hoping she would hear what he could not say. [364] He asked her to erase the oath rather than bind him to a lie, so she prepared to shave the cylinder for reuse. [387] Her blade lifted one continuous wax cylinder phonograph shaving, thin as breath and curled tight as a fern. [405] Ordinarily she dropped such curls into a sealed drawer, each labeled by feeling rather than by speaker or date. [424] This one resisted the drawer because its groove caught the foggy light in broken pulses, like a heart skipping under skin. [445] The sight returned her, without warning, to a curious third-grade field trip memory she had carried quietly for seventy years. [465] Her class had climbed this same tower to meet the previous scribe, a solemn woman who let each child shout a promise into the brass horn. [491] Little Mirel promised to feed a stray cat forever, then cried when the woman prepared to shave her words away. [511] To comfort her, the scribe chose to immerse that shaving in warm water until it relaxed, then teased it flat and played its faint groove. [536] The erased cat promise had returned for three seconds, thin and laughing, teaching her that feeling could survive the loss of words. [558] Now Mirel filled a copper basin from the rain barrel and chose to immerse Elias's shaving the same patient way. [578] The curl loosened in the warm water, surrendering its spiral while tiny bubbles rose from the groove like held breath escaping. [599] She laid it across a spare mandrel, lowered the needle, and heard Elias speak a sentence he had never spoken. [619] The blade, crossing his pauses, had spliced his syllables into an accidental prophecy in the dead harbor tongue. [637] It said the mother pear would fall when the sun stood overhead, and that a small hand would reach for the iron gate. [660] Below the orchard terrace, a visiting class of third graders was arriving for its spring lesson, laughing through the fog. [680] One boy had already slipped toward the oldest pear, fascinated by the rusted latch on its protective iron gate. [699] Mirel did not believe wax could foretell anything, but she trusted the unease its broken pattern had named. [717] She drew the boy gently inside, sent his class after him, and pulled Elias beneath the glass shelter. [735] At noon the oldest pear split with a soft, velvet sigh and fell across the empty terrace where the children had stood. [757] Its trunk was black with rot, yet one green root survived. [768] Elias wept, then promised plainly to plant again. [776]</story>
