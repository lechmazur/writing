After the birth, Sella did not sleep, because the baby arrived with a day missing from the calendar nailed above her sister's bed. [23]
Her sister Wren had gone into labor at dusk, screamed through midnight, and died at dawn, yet the midwife swore only an hour had passed. [48]
Sella, an onyx-ink cartomancer whose stained fingers read futures in playing cards, knew that contradiction was not grief playing tricks on tired women. [71]
She had promised Wren a safe delivery after turning the Queen of Cups, and the card had smiled up at her with drowned eyes. [95]
That smile was why she carried the unnamed baby and her own guilt up the rope ladder to the midnight glade above clouds. [118]
The glade was a circle of grass tethered over the valley, where penitent midwives and fortune tellers waited until the sky forgave them. [141]
There, in cloudbound repentance, Sella meant to stay until she understood where the stolen hours had gone and whether she could return them. [164]
This was her hunger, plain and consuming: to find where lost time accumulates, and to beg there for Wren's lost years back. [186]
The baby did not cry in the thin air, but watched the stars with an old, patient attention that made Sella's throat ache. [209]
Around them the clouds piled white and gold under moonlight, a choking beauty so thick and lovely that each breath felt like swallowing flowers. [233]
Sella laid her cards on the grass, but every spread repeated the same impossible road, ending in a room she had never entered. [256]
A lesser reader would have forced a meaning onto them, named the road mercy, and gone home comforted by her own invention. [278]
Sella remained willingly perplexed, turning each card over and back, because a false answer had already cost Wren her life once. [299]
At midnight the baby kicked free of one sock, and Sella saw on her heel a birthmark shaped like a thin, branching river. [322]
She had seen that river before, years ago, in the dolls' tea set saucer crack that split their childhood saucer from rim to center. [346]
Wren had kept the broken saucer through every move, insisting that the crack was a map and that someday it would lead them home. [370]
Sella fetched it from the basket where the midwife had packed Wren's comb, a clean shift, and the birthday cloth folded in oilskin. [393]
By a family custom of tracing hands on birthday tablecloths, their mother had inked each child's palm every year until the cloth held three generations. [418]
The smallest outlines clustered at one corner, then widened year by year, except Wren's hands, which stopped at seventeen and began again at twenty. [442]
Between those ages Wren had run to the valley fair, lived three summers with a fiddler, and returned swearing that home had stolen her youth. [467]
Sella spread the cloth over the grass and set the cracked saucer at its center, where all the inked lifelines seemed to lean inward. [491]
Then she pressed the baby's palm beside Wren's last tracing and inked it slowly, while the cards around them darkened as if underwater. [514]
The new print overlapped the crack exactly, and the missing river on the baby's heel completed a road across cloth, porcelain, and skin. [537]
Sella understood at last that lost time does not vanish, but pools in the untraced spaces where a life should have been marked and was not. [563]
Wren's missing years, the missing hour of labor, and Sella's own numb months of fear all lay there, shining in the blank linen between hands. [588]
To leave it, she had to unfasten the brass clasp that bound the glade to its anchor rope, surrendering the height that kept her repentance safe. [614]
Her fingers shook, because cutting herself loose meant falling back into a world where Wren was dead and a child needed feeding every few hours. [639]
Still she worked the clasp open, whispered Wren's name as an apology rather than a spell, and felt the whole glade sigh downward through cloud. [664]
They landed softly in the orchard behind their mother's house, where dawn was breaking and the wet grass smelled ordinary, green, and mercifully real. [688]
Sella named the baby Wrenna, inked her own adult hand around the tiny print to guard its blank future, and began, at last, to live inside time again. [716]
