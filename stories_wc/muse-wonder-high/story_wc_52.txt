The kettle had just begun to mutter when Marta climbed to the rain-beaded skylight loft, the ash wood carving blank tucked in her apron pocket. [25] She was the lion tamer's widow, and for eleven months she had left the blank untouched on Elias's workbench, as if touching it might finish his death. [52] This morning she gave herself a rule, spoken aloud to the empty room: while the kettle boils, begin. [70] It was a hauntingly practical rule, from a woman who used his whip as a curtain tie and his jumping hoop as a frame for beans. [96] Marta did not believe useful things should rot beside sorrow. [106]

Below her, the circus winter quarters smelled of wet straw, iron, and oranges, though the tents had been sold and only Nia, the old lioness, remained. [132] Nia breathed through the floorboards, a slow tide that rose whenever rain worried the roof. [147] Marta set the blank beneath the skylight, where drops gathered, brightened, and slid away without permission. [163] Here and gone, each drop was, and here and gone Elias still was in the doorway, laughing before his own joke could end. [186] She had climbed up not to weep, but to harvest lessons from loss before they soured into mere ache. [205]

For that, she had brought Elias's notebook, his small knife, and a short gouge she once used to trim harness leather. [226] Never corner what frightens you, the first page said; give it a chair, a boundary, and a reason to sit. [246] Marta turned the blank until its grain ran like a spine under her thumb. [260] She would carve a small lion curled around a hollow, with a thin lid she could open only when she chose. [281] Inside it, she would contain the things that still ambushed her from a drawer: his brass whistle, two folded letters she never sent, and a lock of Nia's mane. [310]

First she sliced the lid from the top, then she gouged the hollow, working with the grain instead of arguing with it. [332] The knife shaped head, haunch, and tail in plain, sure cuts, while pale curls gathered like minutes she refused to lose twice. [354] She would contain it through crystallized time, the way amber holds a fly yet lets the light pass. [372] Carving could do that. [376] It could not return Elias, whose heart had stopped between the matinee and the evening show while Nia lay beside him, guarding the ring. [400] But carving could give his absence edges, weight, and a place to sit. [413]

Downstairs, Nia coughed, and Marta lifted the knife, listening for a paw deciding whether to become a step. [431] Feed what you must live beside, the second page said, and never make it beg. [446] Marta had obeyed that with Nia every morning since the funeral, feeding the lioness before she fed herself. [464] Now she fed grief the same way, at a set hour, with work, tea, and names spoken aloud. [482] Elias, she said, and was glad when the name stayed in the room instead of bolting through her. [500] She set the whistle, letters, and mane in the hollow, where they touched without wounding one another. [517] Then she fitted the lid, fastened it with a clasp from his broken belt buckle, and found the little lion rough, honest, and enough for today. [543]

At last the kettle boiled, its steam fogging the lower skylight and softening the rain into a gray, kind blur. [563] Marta poured the water over tea in the rust-brown pot Elias had mended with wire, and she set the lion where the wet light could find it. [590] For a breath, Elias was here in the steam, the grain, and Nia's answering huff, and gone in the empty chair beside her. [613] Both were true, and neither had to be chased, caged, or fed raw from her hands. [629] She drank, felt the warmth arrive and leave her tongue, and understood that keeping a thing could also mean letting it change its shape. [653] Below, Nia settled with a sigh that sounded, for once, almost like contentment. [666] Marta rested her palm on the carved lion, lifted her cup again, and let a rust-hued peace settle around her shoulders. [687]
