<story>Mara woke after the reincarnation memory with her hands already shaking around a cup she did not remember pouring. [19] In that other life she had been a keeper of nests on Mauritius, a contemporary of extinct species she still heard in sleep. [42] The dodos had trusted her boots, following her through fern shade while she counted their eggs each morning. [60] Now they were fossils in books, and she was an apprentice again in a body that felt borrowed. [78] Her awakening since then had come in pieces, each one valuable and each one liable to crack. [95] She called them her brittle awakenings, those bright mornings when the past returned faster than her breath. [112] The tidal library above clouds did not forgive hurry, because its shelves rose and fell with weather below. [130] Twice each day a silver tide climbed the mountain, carrying loose pages, salt, and lost letters through open arches. [149] Mara worked the lowest reading room, where nobody expected an apprentice to stay dry or useful. [165] Master Ivo had given her one task until she could prove she belonged without clinging to supervision. [182] She had to translate isolation into independence, turning her solitary night watch into a catalog anyone could trust. [200] On the sill stood her only instrument, a brass sundial blackened at the rim by centuries of thumbs. [218] Its face was atomically reflective, polished so perfectly that even dust seemed afraid to settle and dim it. [236] When cloudlight struck it, the dial threw time onto the ceiling in clean numerals of gold. [252] That was how the library told hours when bells were drowned by tide or fog. [267] Below the mountain, hidden in mist, an ancient stone circle answered each tide with a low hum. [284] The keepers navigated the flooded stacks by the hum of an ancient stone circle, timing their steps between its pulses. [304] If the hum quickened, shelves were about to swing and crush careless hands against wet oak. [320] Mara feared the work because her new memories made every drowned title feel like another extinction. [336] She kept seeing round gray birds among the stacks, patient and unafraid, waiting for her to fail gently. [354] Dependence shamed her more than loneliness, because loneliness at least had never asked her to perform gratitude. [371] On the seventh night the tide came early, while the brass sundial still showed an hour of light. [389] The reflected numerals slid across the ceiling, then vanished as cloudwater poured through the lower arch. [405] Mara heard the stone circle quicken below, and she understood the shelves would swing before the bell. [422] In the next aisle a novice named Tam was shelving tide letters and humming back at the stones, unafraid and unprepared. [443] He had not yet learned to coexist with the library, so he fought every current as if water were an insult. [464] Mara almost called for Ivo, then felt the old brittle pattern rise in her throat. [479] Calling would keep her a child in this second life, waiting at every hard choice for rescue. [496] Instead she set the sundial on a dry lectern where its bright face could catch lantern light. [513] Even without sun, its atomically reflective surface caught the lantern glow and threw a crooked but readable hour ahead. [532] She read the false hour against the hum, counted three slow pulses, and knew the safe interval. [549] Then she moved, not against the tide but with it, letting it carry her toward Tam. [565] She caught his sleeve, braced her feet on a rung, and pulled him into the lee of a shelf. [584] Together they let the shelves swing past, breathing when the hum slowed and holding still when it quickened. [602] That, she whispered, was how you coexist with a place that could kill you without hating you. [619] When the tide withdrew, Mara did not run to report herself rescued or brave. [633] She stayed in the lowest room, wrung out the damaged letters, and entered each one in her own clear hand. [653] Beside each title she wrote where the tide had taken it and how long the hum had held between pulses. [673] It was a catalog born from solitude, but made so another keeper could work alone and not be lost. [692] That evening Ivo read it standing, said nothing, and left the key to the lowest room on her desk. [711] Mara held the key until it warmed, feeling the past and present settle side by side without either devouring the other. [732]</story>
