After the mysterious package arrived, Elias Crowe stopped sleeping through the night. [12]
It came at low tide, wrapped in sailcloth and twine, with no name on it except his own written in wet ink that never dried. [37]
Inside, nested in straw, was a broken lighthouse lens, its glass cracked in a clean spiral, its brass rim still smelling of storm and lamp oil. [63]
As the town's weary undertaker for thirty-one years, Elias knew the objects the sea returned when it wanted a debt acknowledged. [84]
He set the lens on his workbench among coffin nails and folded shrouds, and meant to have the harbor master collect it in the morning. [109]
At midnight, it flashed. [113]
Three short pulses, three long, three short, though no lamp burned behind it and no hand touched its rim. [132]
Elias understood distress signals the way other men understood prayers, because half his work was answering people who had called too late. [154]
He told himself it was a trick of moonlight and stood the lens in the corner, face to the wall. [174]
It flashed again through the wood. [180]
That was how the stillness of his house began to be chased, night after night, by a light that would not accept being ignored. [204]
He was not a brave man, nor, to his own mind, a stubborn one, but he had buried too many sailors without names to leave a signal unanswered. [232]
For twenty years he had kept their effects in labeled boxes, waiting for families who never came, because rest, he believed, needed a permanent address or it wandered. [260]
On the third night the flashes changed, longer between pulses, as if whoever sent them was tiring. [277]
Elias polished the cracked glass, fitted a candle behind it despite the fracture, and flashed his answer out the window toward the black water. [301]
Something answered from beneath the harbor, faint and green, where no boat could be. [315]
Old charts in the chapel called that drowned quarter the City Below, sunk in one winter when the river changed its mind. [337]
Elias took his skiff at dawn, the lens wrapped against his chest, following the answering light as it moved ahead of him like a held breath. [363]
It led him past the harbor wall to a stair that only appeared at the lowest tides, descending into water that parted around dry stone. [388]
He went down because turning back would have required admitting fear was a reason, and, inadvertently stubborn, he treated that admission as a kind of death. [414]
At the bottom stood the forgotten underwater city library, its doors open, its marble halls holding back the sea by some exhausted, faithful agreement. [438]
Inside, the air was still and close, and shelves rose into darkness, laden not with books alone but with small, bone-white boxes. [460]
Each box bore a date, a latitude, and no name, and Elias felt his trade recognized in that careful, hopeless filing. [481]
A woman waited at the reading table, dry as paper, young as the day the city sank, turning pages that made no sound. [504]
She looked at the lens and said the library had been signaling for a hundred years, because its dead could be recorded but not laid to rest. [531]
"We kept the names," she said. [537]
"The sea kept the bodies, and between us, no one had a grave." [550]
She showed him the stacks where the water pressed through cracks and the names were washing, letter by letter, from their pages. [572]
To revitalize the place, she explained, a living keeper had to occupy the spaces the dead could no longer hold, the breaches where memory leaked into the tide. [600]
So Elias began occupying impossible spaces, wedging his shoulders into flooded alcoves, standing for hours in stairwells half air and half sea, sleeping upright between shelves too narrow for a coffin. [631]
Where his living weight pressed, the water retreated, the ink darkened, and names returned to the pages as if recalled by warmth. [653]
It should have been impossible to stay, with his bad knee and his sixty years, but each dawn he found another excuse that was not, he insisted, stubbornness. [681]
He brought down his own boxes, one by one, matching effects to names, a button to a drowned clerk, a wedding ring to a ferryman's widow who had waited thirty years for a grave to visit. [717]
At the library's heart he set the broken lighthouse lens into an empty reading lamp, so its signal no longer begged for rescue but announced an address. [744]
Then he carved that address above the door, street and number and city, in stone the sea could read but not erase. [766]
Now the light turns slow and steady at night, not desperate, and the stillness that follows Elias home feels less chased than kept. [789]
