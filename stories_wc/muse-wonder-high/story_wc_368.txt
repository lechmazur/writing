<story>Six months after receiving the donor heart, Master Anselm Rook had learned to fear the hour when strangers began to confess. [21] He was a watchful dye master above the valley, and before the surgery people had come to him only for color. [42] Now they climbed to the floating rock tea house with empty hands and left having told him the truest thing they owned. [64] The tea house hung from chains bolted into a slow-turning stone, high enough that morning poured around it like milk. [84] Below, the river hid and showed itself through mist, and every rope bridge trembled when the rock turned toward the sun. [105] Anselm dyed wool in the loft above the kettles, where steam carried his blues down to sweeten the tea below. [125] His new heart knocked whenever a traveler crossed the threshold with a story pressed painfully behind the teeth. [143] It was not pain, exactly, but a quietly explosive pressure, as if something small had lit a fuse in his ribs. [164] A widow told him she had buried two husbands and loved only the second man's silence. [180] A smuggler wept into his cup and admitted that he had carried medicine, never jewels, across the border. [198] Anselm listened, poured, and afterward wrote each confession in a ledger he kept hidden beneath undyed linen. [215] He needed to learn why some people magnetize stories, and whether the heart in him had belonged to such a person. [236] The hospital had given him no name, only a sealed envelope that arrived a month after he left its white halls. [257] Inside lay a blue morpho wing, bright as a dropped piece of noon, and a note that said only, She would want this kept in light. [283] Anselm pinned the wing beside his north window, where it changed from blue to violet to black as the rock turned. [304] His first instinct was to grind it for pigment, because grief and curiosity both make men greedy with beautiful things. [324] But crushed, he knew, the wing would give only brown dust, since its fire lived in structure rather than dye. [344] He called this lesson iridescent caution, and he tried to apply it to the strangers and to himself. [362] Do not crush what shines, he told his hands whenever they reached too quickly for the ledger. [379] Still, caution did not answer why ferrymen, brides, deserters, and tax collectors all unburdened themselves beside his vats. [397] So he began by tracking sun trails across the tea house floor, chalking where the light fell each time his heart knocked. [419] The rock turned a finger's width each hour, dragging bright bars over boards, cups, steam, and faces. [436] After a month, the chalk showed a pattern no respectable dye master could ignore. [450] Stories came strongest when late light struck the listener's hands but left the teller's face in gentle shade. [468] In full glare, travelers joked and bargained and kept their sorrow folded small. [481] In darkness, they grew suspicious of the mist and of one another. [493] But in the valley-mist wonder of that half-lit hour, they spoke as if the world had briefly become trustworthy. [512] Anselm tested the discovery by moving his stool, never his questions, following the bright edge as the stone turned. [531] He learned to illuminate a face only partly, setting tea where hands could warm themselves around a cup. [549] He learned that silence, placed at the right angle, could do what sympathy clumsily tried to force. [566] Then, on the first clear evening of autumn, an old woman arrived carrying a basket of undyed wool and a morpho-blue ribbon. [588] His heart rose so violently that the cups trembled, yet her face remained closed as a winter door. [606] The old Anselm would have asked whose ribbon it was, who had died, and what she wanted from him. [625] Instead he set her cup in the bright path and waited while the sun trail crossed his hands. [643] She watched the wing by the window flare blue, then soften, and her shoulders loosened as the light left her eyes unchallenged. [665] 'My daughter carried that wing through three countries sewn inside a book of other people's last words,' she said. [684] Anselm's breath stopped, because the heart inside him was suddenly not a mystery to solve but a guest to honor. [704] He did not ask her daughter's name, though it burned in him brighter than any dye he had ever mixed. [724] He poured more tea, angled the wing so its light could illuminate her ribbon, and let the silence hold its color. [745] 'People told her things because she never tried to keep them,' the woman said at last, looking into the mist. [765] Outside, the floating rock turned, the river flashed below, and Anselm understood that some hearts magnetize stories by making room for them to change color and fly. [792]</story>
