<story>Mina had glowed since she was nine, and she had never trusted anything that glowed back. [16] The doctors on the orbital clinic called it a harmless mutation from solar dust in the valley soil. [34] She called it an inconvenience, because it made her the luminous skeptic of every council meeting and night market. [53] People expected wonder from a woman who shone pale green in the dark. [66] Mina expected evidence. [69] That was why she climbed the lavender bluff under aurora light on the first evening while forests hold elections. [88] Below her, the pine parliament was counting pollen votes to choose which grove would lead the watershed for the next decade. [109] Mina had come not to vote but to learn why some people carry weather with them. [125] Her brother Tomas arrived everywhere with a smell of rain ten minutes before clouds gathered above his head alone. [144] Old June carried desert heat in her coat pockets even during winter ferry crossings. [158] Mina carried only light, which felt like weather that had forgotten how to commit. [172] On her left hand she wore the tin compass ring her grandmother had pressed from a recycled weather satellite dish. [192] For thirty years its needle had pointed nowhere useful, twitching at kettles, doorknobs, and handsome liars. [208] Grandmother had insisted it was not broken but waiting for an emergent compass that no single instrument could contain. [227] Mina had kept the ring out of love and kept her doubt out of honesty. [242] Halfway up the bluff, the air changed on her tongue from lavender honey to cold copper. [258] She stopped, because sensing through taste changes had been her private science since childhood, long before implants made it fashionable. [278] Sweet meant calm soil, salt meant a storm remembering the sea, and ash meant someone nearby was grieving without words. [298] Tonight she tasted all three at once, layered like a chord. [309] That had never happened, and the ring warmed against her skin. [320] At the summit she found the weather carriers already beginning to converge from the ferries, footpaths, and slow orchard trains. [340] They looked ordinary, except that each one bent the evening around a private climate. [354] A girl trailed snow that melted before it touched the lavender. [365] A ferryman dripped seawater from dry sleeves, and a midwife breathed out spring mist whenever she laughed. [382] Above them the aurora rippled green and violet, answering the glow under her skin with embarrassing enthusiasm. [399] The scene reminded her of schoolbook pictures of campfires, though no one under forty had burned real wood in public. [419] She missed a past she had never lived, which her grandmother called futuristic nostalgia when it arrived with tea. [438] "You came to debunk us again," said June, and the heat from her pockets made the lavender steam. [456] "I came to measure you," Mina said, "which is debunking with better manners." [469] June smiled and offered her a plum that tasted suddenly of salt. [481] Mina frowned, because her own mouth had gone salty at the same instant, though she had eaten nothing. [499] The ring needle lifted, trembled, and pointed not north but toward the space between her and June. [516] Then the girl with snow stepped closer, and the taste turned sweet, and the needle swung toward the three of them together. [538] One by one the carriers formed a loose circle, called by no signal Mina could hear. [554] As they converged, their small weathers stopped competing and began to braid. [566] Snow fell into mist and became warm rain that smelled of iron, plum, and distant pine sap. [583] Mina tasted copper, then honey, then ash, then a clean flavor like water held in cupped hands after work. [602] The aurora brightened, and for a moment her glow matched it stripe for stripe. [616] It was terrestrially cosmic, this thing under her feet and over her head, root and solar wind sharing one pulse. [636] Below, the forests rustled in their slow election, leaves turning toward the bluff as if listening for a result that concerned them too. [659] Mina understood then that the emergent compass was not hidden in her ring or in any carrier alone. [677] It emerged when separate bodies, soils, memories, and upper lights aligned closely enough to make a direction together. [695] People carried weather, she thought, because their bodies had learned a place so deeply that the place refused to leave them. [716] A childhood valley taught blood to expect its minerals, while solar storms taught skin to answer the sky, and grief taught both to keep seasons indoors. [742] The needle settled at last, pointing down the bluff toward the pine parliament and the homes beyond it. [760] Mina laughed, still skeptical, still luminous, but willing to follow a direction that only existed because everyone had arrived. [779]</story>
