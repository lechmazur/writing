<story>After the whale beaches, the harbor stopped ringing its bells. [10] In the gleaming silence that followed, Tomas worked as he always had, a dock-side lantern lighter trimming wicks while gulls refused to land. [33] He lit forty lamps from the ferry steps to the customs house, and each flame leaned the wrong way. [52] That was new, and in a port town new things were filed, salted, and deliberately forgotten. [68] Forgetting was official work now. [73] Since the beaching, volunteers carried their worst memories to the salt crystal growing chamber under the pier, where clerks cut them out with cold light and stored the crystals in labeled drawers. [105] Tomas had gone once, the morning after they dragged his wife's boat ashore empty, and asked them to take the sound of her calling across the water. [132] The clerk promised the memory would be destroyed cleanly, like a lamp snuffed without smoke. [147] But last week Tomas found himself humming her call while he worked, note for note, though he could not remember learning it. [169] That was why he needed to find where deleted memories go when they leave a skull, whether they die, or whether they gather somewhere and wait. [195] His first clue came from Mirel, who swept the chamber steps and drank coffee thick enough to stand a spoon in. [216] She would not enter the chamber again, she said, because inside, dropped pins sometimes fell upward and hung turning slowly in the air. [239] The clerks called them pockets of gravity, small sealed weather systems of weight, and marked their edges with chalk. [258] But Mirel said the chalk lines were moved every morning, because the strength inside each pocket fluctuates mysteriously with no tide chart to explain it. [283] Tomas understood lamps, so he understood this: a flame leaning wrong meant air was moving where no door stood open. [303] That night he carried his lantern down the service stairs, past the tide gauges, and into the salt crystal growing chamber for the first time since his own deletion. [332] The chamber was a long white room grown rather than built, its walls branching into clear salt trees that sang faintly when ships passed overhead. [357] Rows of crystals rose in brine troughs, each one cloudy with a face, a birthday, a drowning, a name spoken too late. [379] At the center of the room the brine did not ripple downward, and his lantern flame stood perfectly sideways, blue at the root. [402] He took from his coat the book his wife had kept between tide tables, its pages stiff with pressed wildflowers from the headland graves. [426] She had pressed one flower for every sailor buried without kin, saying flat things kept their shape when heavy years tried to bend them. [450] When Tomas opened the book inside the pocket, the pages lifted though no wind touched them, and the pressed wildflowers rose half an inch and held. [476] Their dry stems all pointed, not up, but inward, toward the largest crystal at the end of the trough, black as deep water. [499] Tomas knew then that deleted memories were not destroyed, and not truly stored in the neat drawers either. [517] The chamber was a net, and the pockets were knots where the lost things pulled through into some heavier country underneath the world. [540] He could not prove it to Mirel, and he could not ask the clerks, who had deleted their own curiosity as a condition of employment. [565] So at dawn they sat on the chamber steps, and Mirel read his fortune the only way she trusted, through patterns in coffee grounds swirled against cracked porcelain. [593] She turned the cup three times, frowned, and said the grounds made a whale, a lantern, and a door buried under salt. [615] Under it, a dark line pointed east, to the fallow plot behind the chamber where failed crystals and ash were dumped each winter. [638] The clerks called that ground sterile, but Tomas remembered his wife pressing flowers there too, back when it had been a garden for the drowned. [663] That night, with Mirel holding his lantern, Tomas dug where the grounds had pointed, determined to exhume whatever the chamber had buried rather than destroyed. [688] Three feet down his spade struck glass, and the soil around it tugged at his boots as if the earth had briefly forgotten which way was down. [715] He lifted out a sealed crystal coffin no longer than a forearm, and inside it his wife's voice moved visibly, bending light as it searched for his name. [743] Below it lay hundreds more, layered like seeds, all the town's deleted griefs growing heavier together until their weight had taught the harbor air to lean.
