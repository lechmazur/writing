Maren had lived three winters alone in the reed beds below the crater, cutting stalks at dawn to weave figures no one had asked for. [25] She was a marsh-reed sculptor, and her hands knew how to coax a heron from damp stems or a child's face from the green pith. [50] Above her marsh stretched a crystalline orchard perched at the edge of a shimmering crater, where glass apples ripened in the warm updraft and rang faintly when the wind moved through them. [82] The orchard belonged to everyone and to no one, tended by the crater families who had survived the old drownings and now lived beyond the murmur of lost languages in a tongue stitched from gesture, song, and touch. [120] Maren understood their songs but spoke them badly, so she listened more than she talked, and hunger for belonging sharpened every silence. [142] She wanted, more than safety or praise, to graft herself onto community, not as a guest forever grateful for shelter but as kin who could be relied on and teased and missed. [174] That spring, the orchard keeper Ilo found her mending a fence below the trees and, instead of waving from a distance, sat down and helped her knot the reeds. [203] He was boldly sympathetic, quick to notice shame before it hardened, and he told her that someone had painted a small black star on the oldest tree in the night. [233] By morning the star was gone, sanded clean, but a second had bloomed on the well house, and a third on the underside of the ferry. [259] The crater families called such a mark a tag, the signature of an elusive street artist who had once lived in the drowned cities and whose work now appeared wherever grief needed naming. [292] Children swore the artist was a ghost, elders swore she was a runaway daughter, and everyone wanted to find her before the festival of first fruit. [318] Ilo asked Maren to search with him, not because she was expendable, he said, but because her sculptor's eye saw how things were joined. [342] For nine evenings they followed the tags together, from crater rim to boat shed, mapping each star in chalk on Maren's door and arguing gently over what the pattern meant. [372] The search gave them errands inside other people's lives, so they carried soup to old Tam who kept bees in glass hives, and mended the cradle of Wen and Alis while those new parents slept. [407] At each house the tags had appeared near a quiet sorrow: a shutter that would not close since a brother died, a loom left covered since a wife left, a gate no child dared open. [442] Maren began to understand that the stars were not boasts but stitches, marking places where the webs of kinship had torn and could be rewoven if someone noticed. [470] On the tenth evening they found a fresh tag still wet beneath the orchard gate, and beside it a pot of black paint made from crater ash and apple resin. [500] Footprints led not away from the orchard but into it, small and bare, ending at the glimmering obsidian arbor that the first families had built for weddings and funerals alike. [530] Under its black glass vines, curled asleep with paint on her fingers, they found little Nessa, Tam's granddaughter, who had not spoken since her mother died in winter. [558] She woke frightened, ready to run, but Maren knelt and offered not questions but a reed star she had woven on the walk, its points uneven and sincere. [586] Nessa touched it, then pointed to the arbor and to her own chest, and Maren understood that the girl had been tagging the places her mother used to tend, asking without words for them not to be forgotten. [624] Ilo did not scold her or praise her into spectacle; he simply sat on her other side until her breathing slowed, then asked if she wanted help carrying what she had been carrying alone. [658] She nodded, and that nod changed Maren's task from finding an artist to joining a repair. [674] For the festival, Maren proposed that they not hunt or hide the tags but dedicate the glimmering obsidian arbor anew, with every family tying a reed and glass strand from its beams to the place they promised to tend. [713] Nessa tied the first strand herself, black paint still under her nails, leading from the arbor to her mother's shutter, and Tam followed with shaking hands and soup on his breath. [744] One by one the families added strands until the arbor wore a shining net, each thread a promise, each knot a name said aloud. [768] When Ilo tied Maren's strand beside his own, she felt the luminescent ache of finally being woven in. [786]
