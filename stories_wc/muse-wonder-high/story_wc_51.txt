After the first snowmelt, the water came down through the bronze grotto under starlight in threads too thin to hear. [20] It struck the stone shingles overhead one drop at a time, and each drop left a little of itself behind. [40] Mina the fractal weaver listened from below, where she had lived since the winter her brother did not return from the river. [62] She wove the same branching pattern at every size, in wool, in cord, in hair, and finally in the wet mineral strings the grotto gave her. [88] A net made so could catch anything, provided the thing was first given a shape. [103] That was why the valley people came after the thaw, carrying their troubles wrapped in cloth or sealed in jars or tied to stones. [127] They came to unburden themselves, but Mina met each of them to collect the weights of different burdens, and she would not let one leave unmeasured. [153] Grief, she learned, weighed less than guilt but lasted longer on the scale. [166] Debt weighed exactly as much as the hands that refused to open. [178] A withheld apology was light until night, when it gathered water and pulled the cord down hard. [195] Her scale hung from a rib of whalebone, white and curved, that the old river had left in the grotto long before any village stood below. [221] At rest between visitors, Mina fell into a whalebone reverie, tracing the rib and remembering boats, kitchens, and voices as if they were one receding shore. [247] The reverie was kind, but it was also how the place kept her. [260] She was boundary-lessly trapped, not by a door or wall, but by exits that opened into smaller copies of the grotto and led her back to her own loom. [289] Every path repeated her pattern until direction itself became a kind of thread, and every thread returned to her hands. [309] She told herself she stayed by choice, because if the nets failed, all the stored burdens would wash down with the meltwater and enter every house at once. [337] Overhead, the laden nets were beginning to calcify under the drips, each knot hardening into a pale plate. [355] Plate lay over plate, burden over burden, until the roof of woven sorrows made what Mina called the shingled hush. [375] Under it, shouting softened, weeping thinned, and even the river seemed to lower its voice before it reached the stones. [395] When the hush was complete, she believed the valley would be safe and she could finally cut herself free. [414] On the third evening, her brother's daughter arrived barefoot through the melt, carrying a kerosene lamp burner wrapped in blue cloth. [435] The child, Wren, said the lamp had gone out on the river the night her father drowned, and no one in the village could make it burn clean. [463] She set the brass burner on Mina's scale, and the whalebone arm swung wildly, then settled lower than grief, lower than guilt, lower than any stone Mina knew. [491] Mina lifted it and felt the impossible truth in her wrists, that this small burner carried the whole unspoken weight of her brother's death. [515] She had collected hundreds of burdens for other people, but she had never weighed the one that built her loom, her nets, and her years of staying. [542] If she hung the burner in the net, the last open space would close, the drips would harden over it, and her own sorrow would become one more shingle. [571] She understood then that the hush would not free her, because a roof made of everything unsaid would only teach the valley to whisper forever. [596] Instead she fitted the burner to her old lamp, filled it from the winter store, and turned its vented collar slowly beneath the open sky. [621] By orienting properly the perforations toward the steady star and the wick toward the draft, she coaxed up a flame that neither smoked nor shook. [646] In that single clean light, the copied grottoes stopped agreeing with one another, and the smallest arch showed wet footprints where Wren had truly entered. [671] Mina took the lamp in one hand and Wren's cold hand in the other, and she did not cut the net behind her. [694] She left the burdens to calcify slowly into their shingles, trusting the hush to soften them without needing her body as its final knot. [718] Outside, the starlight lay plain on the meltwater, and the burner grew neither lighter nor heavier in her hand, only honestly hers to carry. [742] Behind them the grotto repeated itself into darkness, but ahead the path held one size only, and Mina walked it beside the child, listening to the water learn its ordinary sound again. [774]
