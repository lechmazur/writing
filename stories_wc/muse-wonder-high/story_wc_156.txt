At dawn, Luz Ortega, the vivid mural restorer hired to save the garden wall, found frost on her pigments and a question waiting beside her ladder. [26] Director Patel, who funded the restoration, wanted a bronze plaque that named exactly what the painted woman was doing. [45] Was she waking from a dream, or dying into one? [55] The mural covered the curved back wall of the sound wave garden, where limestone benches rose like frozen swells and paired dishes caught whispers from fifty feet away. [83] Luz had loved the place since childhood because a voice sent into one dish returned altered, late, and oddly tender. [103]

She worked as she lived, methodically playful, numbering every flake she lifted while awarding herself points for each color she rescued from gray. [126] That morning she had arranged her brushes by size, then deliberately put the smallest where the largest belonged, just to keep her hand awake. [150] The painted woman filled three panels, reclining among reeds while smaller versions of herself opened their eyes inside her outline. [170] In the outermost panel she slept in the garden itself, painted benches already curved around her before the real ones were built. [192] In the middle panel she woke inside that sleep, sitting up in a room whose window showed the same garden under snow. [214] In the smallest panel, no wider than Luz's palm, she woke again and looked straight out, one pupil containing a painted eye that seemed ready to blink. [241] Generations of schoolchildren called the picture the nested awakenings, though Patel wanted one plain verb for his plaque. [259]

Luz climbed, tested the damp plaster with a fingertip, and told him she would answer before the garden opened. [278] Certainty paid for scaffolding, labels, and insurance, but it had never taught her how a picture breathed. [295] Her real wish, growing stronger with every layer she uncovered, was to exchange certainty for curiosity and make Patel want questions more than verdicts. [319] Near noon, her scraper clicked against something metal lodged in a crack behind the outermost woman's shoulder. [336] She coaxed it free with tweezers, turning the rescue into a game of operation played against the wall's slow breathing. [356] It was a hobo nickel, an old five cent piece whose buffalo had been carved away by a traveling hand. [376] In its place rose a tiny woman on a rail car, her eye open, and inside that eye another woman waking on another train. [400] On the rim, letters worn almost smooth spelled a name Luz knew from the mural's first sketch, Elias Vale. [419] Vale had ridden freight lines between harvest jobs, painting walls for meals and leaving carved coins when money failed him. [439]

Luz set the nickel on the reflecting dish at the garden's edge and tapped it once with her scraper. [458] The tap crossed the garden, struck the curved wall under the mural, and came back as a low note broken into three soft pulses. [482] Each pulse seemed to arrive from a different panel, first the sleeper, then the woman indoors, then the tiny figure in the pupil. [505] Luz laughed, then repeated the tap in a pattern, three slow, two quick, one slow, because play often found what procedure missed. [527] That was when the garden began to bewitch her through reflected sound, not with spectacle, but with the clear sense that the wall was answering in sequence. [554] Beneath the final pulse she heard a man's humming folded inside her own breathing, exactly where a brush would hesitate before an eye. [577] She pressed her ear to the smallest panel and heard the hum gather there, guiding her solvent to a brown film she had thought was age. [603] The film lifted in one careful sheet, and under it Vale had painted a small open mouth on the figure in the pupil, hidden for decades. [629] Luz understood then that each awakening contained a question, and each question woke the next figure rather than closing her story. [650]

Patel returned to the gate and asked if she could certify that the woman was waking. [666] In the pause between question and reply, Luz heard three answers arrive at once, her tap, Patel's echo, and the hum under the paint. [690] She looked from the coin's waking traveler to the mural's open mouth and felt her old need for a final label loosen into attention. [714] She descended, handed Patel the hobo nickel for the display case, and asked him to stand at the far dish and listen before she answered. [739] Her reply was to tap the nickel once more, and when that sound returned divided among the panels, Patel wrote the plaque as a question. [764]
