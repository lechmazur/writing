<story>Mara had not come to the ancient grotto under stars to end her marriage, though she knew the words were waiting in her throat. [24] Across the low stone table, Ellis kept his hands around his cup and did not drink. [40] He said, very carefully, that he had taken the job in the city and had already signed the lease. [59] Then came the silence that would hold everything else. [68] What happened next in their lives would unfold, in truth, across the pause in a difficult conversation, though neither of them understood that yet. [92] The grotto had been a shrine before it was a cove, and a cove before the sea learned patience. [111] Its roof had fallen in a century before Mara was born, leaving tide pools open to the moon and weather. [131] Mara worked there as a tidal-pool chronicler, returning each dawn and dusk to record what the water left and took. [151] She wrote down anemones opening, crabs molting, and minnows stranded between one world and another. [166] She was warmly observant by habit and by love, because she had learned that nothing survives being watched too directly. [186] Ellis used to tease her that she could coax a hermit crab from its shell simply by describing the weather beside it. [208] That was her method in all things, discovery through tangential approach, never lunging at the center of a fragile thing. [228] She had taken the chronicler's notebook years ago not for wages, but to discover the texture of time in places where it pooled and lingered. [253] Tide pools taught her that transition periods were not empty intervals, but the richest hours, when creatures decided whether to endure, transform, or retreat. [277] Now Ellis waited for her answer, and the pause stretched wide enough for the tide to be heard moving beyond the grotto walls. [300] Mara looked not at his face, which she feared would make her cruel, but at the nearest pool beside their table. [321] In the shallow water, half buried in black sand, something green caught the starlight where no green should have been at low tide. [344] She leaned closer, aware of Ellis watching her avoid him, and saw a jade cicada resting as if it had just climbed from the sea. [369] It was not alive, but carved with such fine patience that its wings held veins thinner than hair. [387] Someone must have lost it years ago, or left it as an offering when the grotto still had priests. [406] Cicadas, Mara knew, lived underground in darkness, then split their own backs to emerge singing into a brief and altered life. [427] The jade one would never sing, yet it had endured longer than any real insect by refusing to finish its change. [448] She lifted it, wet and cold, and felt time not as a river, as poets claimed, but as layered skin, rough where one stage tore from the next. [476] Because she was not looking straight at her marriage, she could suddenly foresee its two possible futures with terrible clarity. [496] In one, she followed Ellis to the city and became a woman who checked windows for a horizon that never filled with water. [519] In the other, she stayed, and Ellis left alone, and their love thinned into letters that arrived after the seasons they described had already passed. [544] Neither future frightened her as much as the thought of choosing so quickly that the choice itself would harden before it could breathe. [567] Still inside the pause, and still holding the cicada, she found herself speaking sideways into the truth, as she spoke to shy creatures. [590] "Tell me about the lease," she said, "not the rooms, but the light in the morning." [606] Ellis blinked, confused, then understood her enough to answer the question beneath the question. [620] "It faces a brick wall," he admitted, "and I signed it while wishing you were standing in its sunless kitchen." [640] His honesty, arriving by the side door she had opened, changed the shape of the pause. [656] It became a tide pool they knelt beside together, to see what might still live there. [672] Mara set the jade cicada between them, where it glistened like something newly emerged. [686] "Give us one season," she said, "you in the city, me at the pools, and we will learn whether this is a molting or an ending." [712] Ellis touched it gently, relief showing that he had expected refusal, not an interval made with care. [729] When he nodded, their conversation resumed, softer under the patient stars.
