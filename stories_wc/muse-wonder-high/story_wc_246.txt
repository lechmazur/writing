Mara Venn had worked for eleven years as a celestial signal interpreter aboard the Tern, a listening station anchored inside a gravitational anomaly where falling had nine polite directions. [29] Her job was to hear the faint broadcasts that curved back from dead stars and decide what they had really said. [50] That spring, after planting the wrong seeds in the station garden, everything she transcribed began to arrive in duplicate. [69] The seeds were meant to be barley, quick and forgiving, but the quartermaster had opened a vault packet of memorial wheat instead. [91] Each kernel held a compressed voice from the first crews, released only when roots found water. [107] In ordinary gravity the wheat would have spoken once and died politely. [119] In the anomaly its roots grew sideways through thin places in time, and its voices tangled with the signals from space. [140] Now every stellar message came with a second version, kinder or crueler, and the crew waited for Mara to choose. [160] Choosing was not reporting but editing history, because the station archive rewrote itself to match her transcript and people remembered only the version she kept. [185] She did the work with a dried-up fountain pen with exotic nib, a gift from her grandmother who had navigated by pulsar before computers were trusted. [211] The nib was cut from a meteorite that had fallen upward on Europa, so it scratched even without ink and left a bright line in paper and memory. [239] Mara was confidently uncertain about every message, which the captain called a defect and the gardeners called the only honest posture left. [261] She could say, with complete calm, that a signal was either a warning from a colony or the colony begging not to be warned, and then she would listen again. [291] Her accuracy did not come from the instruments, which lied beautifully in fluctuating gravity, but through contingent relationships with people who happened to be awake when a signal passed through them. [322] A cook dreaming of his sister made one frequency clear, while a mechanic in love sharpened another, and neither would have mattered alone. [345] So Mara kept a chart not of stars but of shifts, meals, quarrels, reconciliations, and who had held whose hand during the long dark between broadcasts. [371] The crisis arrived on a Tuesday, when the wheat in its trays leaned toward the loudspeaker and repeated a distress call in her grandmother's voice. [396] The call described the founding accident of the Tern, when the first anchor crew had set the station too close to the anomaly and lost three days that no one could account for. [429] In the version carried by starlight, her grandmother had caused the accident by misreading a signal and hiding the error. [449] In the version carried by wheat, her grandmother had saved the station by refusing an order that would have torn the hull apart. [472] Both versions ended with the same three missing days, the same scar along the hull, and the same lullaby hummed in the vents. [495] Captain Osei asked Mara to settle it before the memorial service, because the crew needed one past they could mourn without arguing. [517] Mara wanted something smaller and harder than truth, which was to thresh meaning from experience the way farmers separated grain from husk without pretending the husk had never existed. [546] She carried the pen to the garden, but the anomaly was rising that hour and gravity bent the exotic nib flat against the page before she could write either verdict. [576] If she forced it, the nib would snap and the archive would keep both versions grinding against each other until the crew dreamed two childhoods and woke exhausted. [604] Mara sat among the wheat trays with the ruined pen and admitted aloud that she did not know which grandmother was real. [626] The cook, arriving with tea, said his sister had hidden a shameful error only after it taught her how to save her crew. [649] The mechanic, who had loved the cook for six years, said so at last, and the cook took his hand. [669] The cook warmed the meteorite nib over his tea and the mechanic steadied the tray, so Mara could unbend it toward a gentler curve with both hands free. [697] She wrote that her grandmother had misread the signal, hidden her shame for three lost days, and then refused the fatal order her shame had taught her to question. [726] The archive shivered, the wheat bowed, and the crew remembered a rescue with a mistake inside it like a seed inside fruit. [748] That night Mara drank tea in sideways gravity and found a strange comfort in a past that could bend, be tended, and still feed everyone. [773]
