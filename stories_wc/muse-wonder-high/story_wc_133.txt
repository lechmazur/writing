<story>Mara had not slept in nine days when the tide finally left the island. [14] She was an insomniac painter, and after the water wars that was almost a trade, because no one else wanted the night watch over the pumps. [40] The tidal island of Sef rose and sank twice a day, chained to the mainland by a causeway that drowned at noon and midnight. [64] Mara lived in the old customs house above the harbor, where the rain ran in black trails down the whitewashed walls. [85] She painted those trails instead of sleeping, layering soot and salt and ochre until each canvas looked like a map of weather. [107] People said she was trying to thresh meaning from experience, and she repeated the phrase herself, wryly earnest, whenever buyers asked why no canvas ever showed the sun. [135] On the ninth night the storm broke early, and the rain did not fall straight but crawled sideways across her windows toward the low shore. [160] By dawn the tide had withdrawn farther than any elder remembered, exposing a street that had been under water since before she was born. [184] Mara took her lantern and her driest coat and went down by following rain trails, because they were the only lines that still connected the customs house to that street. [214] The trails were not imagination this time but real channels cut in mud, each one beaded with rain and running bright despite the gray sky. [239] They converged below the old market arch, where the exposed cobbles steamed and something gold caught the lantern light. [258] She knelt, though the mud soaked her knees at once, and dug with her palette knife until her fingers found a smooth oval. [281] It was an amber brooch, oval and clouded, holding a small black fly that seemed too detailed to be real. [301] Inside the amber, behind the fly, a second shape shifted when she tilted it, like a room opening behind a door. [322] It was her own room in miniature, painted under the amber long before she had lived there, with its blue door and narrow bed and window full of rain. [351] She felt haggard awe rise in her throat, ugly and clean, because two women separated by war had kept the same sleepless watch and painted the same walls. [379] Then a bell rang across the flats, and an old man came picking his way along the same rain trails, carrying a dry box wrapped in oilcloth. [406] He stopped when he saw the brooch, and all the tiredness in his face sharpened into recognition. [423] "That was Liora's," he said. [428] Mara stood, clutching it, suddenly certain that giving it up would be like surrendering the only proof that her nights had meant anything. [451] The old man opened his box and showed her a bundle of letters tied with wire, each sealed with dark wax and addressed to the Keeper Who Follows Rain. [480] He said his name was Tomas, Liora's brother, and that she had been keeper before the wars, a painter who stopped sleeping after the first flood took their mother from this same street. [513] Before the last evacuation, Liora had buried the brooch where the rain trails met, believing the island would return it only to someone who followed water instead of roads. [542] He had kept her letters for forty years, waiting on the mainland each spring, and now he proposed an exchange. [562] Mara would give him the brooch for burial beside Liora, and he would give her the letters Liora had written to the next keeper, whoever she turned out to be. [592] The tide bell rang again, closer, warning that the causeway and the street would vanish within the hour. [610] Mara looked at the fly in the amber and understood that Liora had not trapped a moment but planted a meeting. [631] This was an unearthed convergence, two sleepless keepers and one drowned street arriving at the same point because the water had finally tired of keeping secrets. [657] She made the exchange with wet hands, and neither of them pretended it was simple or fair. [674] He held the brooch as if it weighed a year, and she held the letters as if they might dissolve like salt. [696] They climbed back by following rain trails in reverse, reaching the customs house just as the first wave slapped the market arch below them. [720] That night Mara read until the letters stopped sounding like a verdict and started sounding like instructions left by a tired friend. [742] Liora wrote that meaning was not found in the flood but threshed from it, grain by grain, on the ordinary mornings after. [764] Near midnight, for the first time in nine days, Mara slept, and she did not dream of drowning. [782]</story>
