Ila had lived for eleven winters in the verdant grotto above the clouds, where moss drank mist and white roots held the warm stone. [24] People below called her an esoteric realist because she treated omens as weather reports and prayers as instructions for work. [44] She believed hidden things were real only when hands could test them in daylight. [58] She kept ledgers of arrivals in a cedar box, noting wind, bloom, wingbeat, and the exact hour when silence changed its weight. [80] Most pages named creatures no traveler remembered, because her task was to honor overlooked histories before they thinned into rumor. [100]

Now her ledger waited open during the migration of the last monarch butterfly, whose kind had once darkened autumn fields like drifting embers. [123] Disease and heat had taken the rest, mile by mile, until one female climbed alone toward the old southern sanctuary. [143] Ila had seen her at dawn through glass, a torn orange sail fighting the thin air above the cloud sea. [163] The grotto lay on her route, transiently rooted between summer meadows and winter pines, a brief green pause that migration had used for centuries. [187] Its pools never froze, its ferns leaned toward hidden springs, and its stone arch framed sunrise like a held breath. [207]

For three mornings Ila had practiced building temporary structures as signals, raising quick towers of reed and thread that lived one day and fell without scars. [233] A monarch could not read words, but she could read bright intervals, shade, and still air arranged in a patient line. [254] So Ila set red cloth where crosswinds tore, white shell where updrafts lifted, and broad leaves where a tired wing could rest. [276] Each tower marked a name from her ledgers, not the famous explorers, but the moth keepers, seed carriers, and grandmothers who once watched these skies. [301] At its base she tied one knot for each history, tight enough to hold through wind, loose enough to undo before rot could claim the reed. [327]

That morning the bells she had hung from the arch gave a steady cadence, reed against shell, shell against patient stone. [348] Then the cadence broke. [352] An interrupted cadence in this place meant air had changed before skin could feel it, usually because something winged had entered the grotto current. [376] Ila looked up with twine still looped around her wrist and saw the monarch falter above the pools, blown sideways toward the cliff teeth. [400] She did not run, because running feet would drum false signals through the stone and confuse a creature navigating by touch and tremor. [423] Instead she moved in the old counting walk, three slow steps, a pause, three slow steps, matching the rhythm the towers made visible. [446]

At the unfinished tower she knelt, though her knees ached, and tied the final guiding knot with hands that remembered more than her eyes. [470] Into that knot she slipped a moth wing found last spring beneath the arch, silver brown and dry as a pressed leaf. [492] The wing had belonged to the last atlas moth recorded by Ila's grandmother, a night giant whose passing no village bell had marked. [515] Against red cloth its dark shape made the one patch of shade a monarch could trust without landing on stone heated by sun. [538] The monarch veered, caught the tower line, and followed each bright interval down through the arch in a descent too weak for triumph. [561] She missed the first leaf, struck soft moss, and lay with wings opening and closing like a book trying to remember its last page. [585]

Ila waited until the movement eased, then laid a wet fern beside her and shaded her with cupped hands that did not touch. [608] In the hush that followed, dawn laid a blushing stillness across the pools, and neither woman nor butterfly hurried the world back into noise. [632] After an hour the monarch drank from the fern, climbed Ila's offered reed, and dried until orange returned stronger at the edges. [654] Then Ila untied each signal knot in reverse order, reading aloud the overlooked names so the departing witness carried human gratitude south with her. [678] The towers came down cleanly, as temporary things should, leaving no wire for future wings and no monument prouder than memory. [699] Only the moth wing remained in her palm, proof that guidance could come from the uncelebrated dead as surely as from living hands. [722] At noon the monarch rose on a warming current, crossed the cloud sea without faltering, and resumed the road her ancestors had written in air. [747] Ila wrote the hour in her ledger, underlined it once, and left the next page blank for whoever would arrive unnoticed next. [769]
