Joss Rook had worked three years as an ardent ferry deckhand, and he still greeted every dawn sailing as if it were the first. [24]
The Larkspur carried office workers, schoolchildren, and nurses between Port Sable and Kestrel Island, a forty-minute crossing he could recite in his sleep. [47]
He was resiliently positive, not from ignorance, but because panic had never once helped him tie a line or calm a frightened passenger. [70]
That optimism became necessary on the Tuesday when the vanishing commuter ferry failed to arrive, despite clear water and no distress call. [92]
Joss was aboard when it happened, hauling the stern line as the bell rang for the seven o'clock departure. [111]
At seven-thirteen, the harbor, the gulls, and the morning itself folded away, and starlight poured through windows that should have shown sun. [133]
The passengers continued reading, arguing, and sipping coffee, unaware that the Larkspur had slipped from the world that owned its route. [154]
Only Joss remembered the other crossings like this, each one erased from logs, tickets, and memory before the ferry docked again. [175]
This time, however, Captain Ibarra saw it too, her hands white on the wheel as the compass spun without settling. [195]
Outside the bow, cliffs rose where open channel should have been, enclosing them in a spectral grotto under starlight vast enough to contain the sea. [220]
Black water reflected constellations that did not match the season, and every wake broke into silver rings that lingered instead of fading. [241]
On the bulkhead, the brass clock ticked left, paused, ticked upward along its face, marking the hour when clocks run sideways. [262]
Ibarra ordered Joss to radio the harbor, though both of them suspected no ordinary harbor was listening anymore. [280]
He turned the dial through static and heard his own voice answer, delayed by three seconds and frightened in a way he refused to be. [305]
The other Joss said his Larkspur had reached Port Sable without its captain, its deckhand, or half its passengers. [324]
Between bursts of static, other voices layered over his, dockworkers and dispatchers repeating the same minute in different orders. [343]
Joss understood then that the grotto was not a place beside the route, but the seam where every failed crossing waited. [364]
Ibarra brought from her coat an empty hourglass, its bulbs clear, its frame scarred by salt, and set it on the chart table. [387]
She said her mother, the previous captain, had carried it to prove that their missing minutes were not flowing anywhere. [407]
While sand remained absent, she explained, the timelines stayed separated, and each return quietly stole another person, object, or remembered detail. [428]
Last month it had taken the harbor bell, last week the names on the life jackets, and today it meant to take Ibarra. [451]
Joss wanted to merge parallel timelines not as an abstract rescue, but because his younger sister Mina stood among the passengers, already translucent at the edges. [477]
In the other timeline, the radio Joss reported, Mina had arrived solid and alone, asking why her brother had vanished from beside her. [500]
If the two crossings docked separately again, Ibarra believed one Mina would keep the body and the other would keep only the memories. [523]
Joss grinned at Mina, promised her hot chocolate at Port Sable, and meant it completely, because despair would waste the minutes they still possessed. [547]
Ibarra traced the chart, where their penciled routes from dozens of vanished crossings overlapped into a dense, deliberate web. [566]
The wake rings, the reversed clock, the radio echoes, and the constellation reflections were not separate mysteries, but parts of one design. [588]
They could navigate home via interconnected patterns, steering where wake, star, sound, and route briefly agreed with one another. [607]
Joss took the radio again and asked his other self to read the harbor lights in the order they appeared. [627]
As the list came through, Ibarra matched each light to a star reflected in the grotto water and turned the wheel toward the correspondence. [651]
Joss repeated Mina's name, the departure bell count, and their father's birthday into the radio, giving both timelines the same human landmarks. [673]
The empty hourglass shuddered, and a single grain appeared at its throat, then another, until sand fell in a thin, impossible stream. [695]
Mina's hand warmed in his, her outline sharpening as the other radio voice stopped sounding frightened and began sounding like him. [716]
The grotto cliffs thinned into harbor cranes, sunrise folded back over starlight, and the Larkspur crossed the seam without slowing. [736]
They docked once, wholly, with every passenger accounted for, while the ship clock resumed forward motion and struck seven-fourteen. [755]
Joss remained a permanent transient, forever leaving and arriving on the same bright route, but now every departure promised a true return. [777]
