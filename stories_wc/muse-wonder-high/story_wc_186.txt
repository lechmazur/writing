<story>Maren had been beacon-tower custodian for eleven years after the collapse of civilization, and she had never once lit the beacon. [21] The tower stood above a drowned valley where roads ended in reeds and roofs showed like islands at low tide. [41] Below it, half sunk in silt, the glass humming pavilion still sang when wind moved through its cracked panes. [60] Maren kept both places because no one else remembered why either mattered, and because keeping was a form of prayer. [80] Each evening she climbed the iron stairs with oil, rags, and the broken crystal prism wrapped in her father's coat. [100] The prism had split along one face during the last bombardment, and now it threw crooked rainbows that never quite met. [121] Her father, the custodian before her, had called the missing piece a shard of hush, because it had fallen where no sound followed. [144] He meant the pavilion floor, where screams and bells and engines had all stopped the day the valley flooded. [163] Maren was retrocognitively blessed, though she hated the church word for what the collapse had left in her head. [182] She could not see futures, only pasts that pressed close enough to touch when places grew quiet and light fell right. [203] Touch the cold lamp, and she heard her father trimming its wick while convoys still hoped for guidance home. [222] Touch the pavilion glass, and she heard children rehearsing harvest songs weeks before anyone knew the dams would break. [241] The gift gave her no orders, only echoes, but echoes could be followed like footprints if she was patient. [260] This spring, three echoes arrived on three unrelated mornings, and each one pointed at the same unlit lamp. [278] A drowned pilot whispered star bearings, a pavilion singer hummed a count of seven, and her father promised Thursday if the wind forgave them. [302] Anyone else would call it grief inventing patterns, and Maren wanted badly to prove that coincidences have meaning. [320] Not for priests or scavengers, but for herself, because if meaning was dead, then her eleven faithful years were only habit. [341] So she began by tracking star paths from the tower roof, chalking their slow wheel above the flooded valley. [360] The beacon had never burned for ships, she learned, but for starlight gathered, split, and returned at one precise angle. [380] Her father had aligned the prism to catch a rising star and send its beam down into the pavilion cupola. [400] There, tuned glass would answer, chamber answering chamber, until the whole valley heard a chord low enough to feel. [419] It was a signal older than engines, a way scattered farms once promised one another they were still here. [438] Without the missing shard, the chord broke, the beam wandered, and silence ate whatever hope the chord had carried. [457] Maren searched the pavilion for seven Thursdays, listening with her palms while star paths turned and thistles colonized the stairs. [477] The echoes thinned near the east wall, where flood silt lay undisturbed and a child's handprint darkened the lowest pane. [497] On the eighth Thursday, wind forgave them, dropping so suddenly that dust hung shining and the pavilion held its breath. [517] Maren pressed her ear to the silent pane and heard, backward through years, crystal strike stone and slide beneath the bench. [538] She dug with her hands until nails broke, and under wet cedar she found it, gray as held breath. [557] The shard fit the prism with a click so small she felt it in her teeth before she heard it. [577] That night she climbed again, set the mended prism in its brass cradle, and waited for the star her father named. [598] She did not pray for rescue, only for alignment, for one clean minute when past and present agreed to meet. [618] When the star crossed her chalk mark, Maren struck the long match and moved to rekindle the beacon at last. [638] Oil caught, starlight caught, and the prism split both into a beam that speared down into the waiting pavilion. [657] The glass answered, first one pane, then twenty, then all, until the hum rose through water, silt, bone, and root. [677] Across the valley, on islands she had thought empty, answering fires bloomed one by one, hesitant, small, and stubbornly alive. [697] Maren laughed into the wind, tasting thistle seed and smoke, holding a thistle-blown hope that was light enough to travel. [717] Coincidences had not saved anyone by themselves, she understood, but they had shown her where to stand and when to listen. [738] Below, the fires kept answering, and Maren, custodian still, began the count of seven before she answered them back. [757]</story>
