<story>Maren never wanted the orchard, but at nineteen she became its reluctant caretaker when her aunt vanished between the rows one October morning. [23] It stood above a cliffside labyrinth patrolled by mechanical birds, and no other road reached the village below. [41] The curse had a graceless beauty: the trees bore bright iron fruit that sang at dusk, and anyone who touched their fallen fruit took root where he stood. [69] Three rooted travelers already stood at the gate, their branches holding hats they would never wear again. [86] Maren kept them pruned, watered their boots out of pity, and swore she would quit as soon as someone else could be persuaded to stay. [111] That evening, while the sky thinned to tin, a bell rang from the labyrinth and did not stop. [129] She climbed the watch stump and saw two lanterns circling the same dead corner, far below and losing light. [148] They were lost travelers, and children by the height of the smaller lantern. [161] They had to get out before the night frost forms, or fallen fruit would slick the paths and wake the birds to hunt every warm shape that moved. [189] Maren did not want another rooted stranger to prune, so she meant to guide lost travelers to safety. [207] She could not simply walk in, because the labyrinth floors were soft ash that remembered feet, and the mechanical birds followed fresh impressions to their makers. [233] Behind the seed jars in the potting shed hung the thing her aunt had called useless: a forgotten blueprint, foxed at the edges, for a guide frame no one had built. [264] It showed three ash poles lashed into a tall tripod, a tin mirror, a bellows with bent tubes, and a dangling chain that hovered an inch above the ground. [293] At the bottom, in pencil gone nearly silver, her aunt had written that air could do what hands must not. [313] Puffed through bent tubes, it would press arrows into the ash, leaving impressions without touching the floor and giving the birds no living step to follow. [339] Maren had poles, mirror, and bellows, but the drawing circled one part twice: the small nut that locked the mirror to the chain and let both swing true. [367] Without it, the mirror would spin, the chain would drag, and the first dragged line would summon the flock. [386] She found no nut in the toolbox, only rusted nails bent like claws and a spoon her aunt had used for grafting. [408] Then she remembered the roost under the shed eaves, where the birds hid stolen bright things, and shook it with her pruning pole. [431] Down with twigs and bottle caps rolled the small nut she needed, warm with sun, its threads catching the light. [451] Maren picked it up with tongs, held it to the drawing, and said to nobody, "Nine threads, leaning left, wants oil but not much." [475] She was offhandedly exact that way, naming weights and distances as other people named weather. [490] She worked fast, because frost was already beading on the shaded side of every leaf. [505] She set out to assemble the frame on the gate stones and did it standing on a crate, lashing poles, fitting the mirror, and screwing the small nut home with a strip of cloth. [539] When she pumped the bellows, the hanging chain shivered, the tubes sighed, and a clean arrow bloomed in the ash below without a hand or foot coming near it. [568] The mirror, swung true by the nut, threw her lantern light down the correct turns in short, ugly flashes that no bird mistook for a body. [594] She climbed onto the crossbar and walked the frame forward on its pole tips, graceless as a heron on stilts, pumping at each crossing and watching arrows open ahead of her. [625] Above her the mechanical birds turned on stiff wings, beaks clicking, waiting for a footprint with blood in it. [644] They found only pole holes and blown arrows, and they quarreled over the arrows in the air, iron feathers clattering down like cheap knives. [668] At the dead corner she flashed the mirror, and a man lifted a child onto his shoulders. [685] She called, not loudly, "Follow my light when I turn, and stay where the ash is pressed." [702] Just then frost silvered the lower stones, and iron fruit began to drop at the far end. [719] Maren turned the frame, pumped until her shoulders burned, and marked each crossing again while the two followed her back. [739] At the gate she caught the child's coat and dragged both onto clean stone as fruit smashed behind them. [758] Safe inside, the child slept, while Maren, still reluctant, pocketed the small nut against the next night. [775]</story>
