The shooting star tracking station above Kepler Bay kept its lone watch through the hush of an overlooked era, when colony ships no longer crossed the black. [27]
Its telescopes slept under dust, its halls smelled of iron and rain, and no admiral had called for a chart in twenty years. [50]
Only Oren Vale remained, the cosmic janitor, sweeping star ash from the floors each dawn. [65]
He mopped the observation dome, polished brass rails no hands touched, and fed the thin green plants that had crept through cracks in the stone. [90]
Managers on Earth called the work waste, but Oren heard a different truth in the quiet. [106]
He was spiritually curious, and he believed stillness could listen if a man learned how to stay. [123]
That belief had not come easily, for he had taken the job to hide from grief after his wife died in a colony fire. [147]
He had told himself that numb hands and empty rooms would keep him safe from more loss. [164]
Each night he climbed to the dome with a broom, a cloth, and a shard of yellow lantern glass set in a copper ring. [188]
The glass came from the signal lantern his wife had carried on night walks beside the sea on Earth. [207]
He kept it not as a relic to worship, but as a lens that softened harsh light until faint things became visible. [229]
On the first night of the autumn meteor river, he held the glass to the main tracker and swept the sky in slow arcs. [253]
Hundreds of meteors cut white lines above the bay, each one brief and clean in its fall. [270]
Then one streak bent where no streak should bend, and returned the next night at the same wounded angle. [289]
Oren marked its path on paper with a shaking hand, not trusting the dead computers to remember. [306]
He learned, by tracing the path of falling stars across seven nights, that the bent streak was not a stone at all. [328]
It was a small escape pod, tumbling and burning a little more on each pass through the upper air. [347]
Its beacon flashed too bright for the old tracker, so he fitted the yellow lantern glass over the lens and found the pod again in amber calm. [374]
Through that warmed light he read the weak signal carved under the flashes, a child's name and a call for help. [395]
The child was Lina Sol, nine years old, alone since her school ship broke apart beyond the moon. [413]
Her voice reached him in thin bursts between static, each burst braver and weaker than the last. [430]
She said her air would last two more days, and she asked if the falling hurt the stars. [448]
Oren tried to answer with charts and calm orders, but his voice locked like a rusted gate. [465]
He heard her hide fear under questions, just as he had hidden grief under sweeping and silence. [482]
In that raw moment he chose to empathize, not from above her pain, but from inside his own. [500]
He told her about his wife, about the fire, and about the nights when breathing itself had felt too large to bear. [522]
He admitted that he had come to the station to find strength in vulnerability, though he had spent years running from the lesson. [545]
Lina listened without fixing his sorrow, and her quiet trust gave his sorrow a place to stand. [562]
Then he could think again, and the stillness around him sharpened every faint sound from her pod. [579]
He plotted her next pass and saw that she would miss the bay and strike the black rocks beyond the old harbor. [601]
The station had no ship and no engine, only a signal tower, a rescue net from the first settlement, and his patient hands. [624]
He climbed the tower through wind that smelled of salt and cold stone, carrying rope, tools, and the copper ring. [644]
At the top he set the yellow lantern glass into the beacon, turning its hard white blast into a warm and steady gold. [667]
He spoke to Lina of a kitchen lamp on Earth, and told her to steer her small jets toward the gold whenever fear rose. [691]
She answered that gold looked like morning, and her voice, though thin, held the primal elegance of a creature refusing to vanish. [713]
On her final pass the pod tore through cloud, and Oren pulled the net ropes with his whole weight as the beacon burned gold. [737]
The net sang, snapped two posts, and caught her above the garden beds where green shoots rose through the dome cracks. [758]
In the flourishing stillness after her cry, Oren held her shaking hand and finally stayed. [773]
