Maren had mended roofs for forty years, but she had never mended a promise before. [15] She was the quartz-eye mender of Bell Hollow, named for the pale stone set in her left socket after a falling slate took her sight. [40] It did not let her see farther, but it ached whenever someone spoke a vow they did not mean. [59] That morning, three days after the breach of contract, it ached so hard she had to stop on the cathedral steps and swear at the pigeons. [85] The contract had been simple enough for two generations to carve into the west door. [100] The town would tend the orchard in the rafters, and the orchard would feed the town through every winter. [119] When the cathedral was abandoned, its roof fell in, and plum and apple trees took root in the high beams where rain and sun could reach them. [146] Now a lush orchard spanning the rafters of an abandoned cathedral hung heavy above the empty pews. [163] Maren's granddaughter Lina had broken the contract by picking the winter plums early and selling them at market. [181] The elders called it theft, Lina called it sense, and neither side had spoken since. [196] She climbed anyway with her tool bag on her back, endearingly feisty at seventy nine and determined to outstubborn the stairs. [217] Halfway up the north tower she met Lina coming down with a basket of windfall apples. [233] "You cannot mend a quarrel with nails," Lina said. [242] "Watch me," Maren said, and kept climbing. [249] Lina sighed so loudly the pigeons rose, then followed her grandmother into the green light above the nave. [267] Up close the damage was worse than pride had reported from below. [279] A plum branch, left unpruned since the breach, had grown thick around a cracked rafter and was pulling it apart with slow green strength. [303] The elders knew the old pruning cuts, and Lina knew the new market and the early blight, but neither would teach the other. [326] That was why Maren had really come, though she told herself it was only for the beam. [343] She wanted to bridge generational wisdom before it rotted on the branch. [355] She set her bag among the roots and opened it to find her clamps gone. [370] Lina had borrowed them in spring and, being seventeen and certain, had forgotten to tell her. [386] "Brilliant," Maren muttered. [389] She would persist anyway, because giving up would let Lina think stubbornness belonged only to the young. [406] She rummaged through the forgotten contents of the bag, past plum stones, chalk, cold tea, and a heel of bread. [426] At the bottom her fingers found cloth that did not belong to any roof she had mended. [443] It was her daughter's hair ribbon, blue as deep water, packed away the winter Ana died and never taken out again. [464] Lina went quiet beside her. [469] "Mama wore that when she worked up here," she whispered. [479] "She wore it when she wanted us to listen," Maren said, surprised by her own words. [495] She had meant to say Ana wore it to keep plums from her hair, but the other thing sounded truer once spoken. [517] Her quartz eye did not ache, and that startled her more than pain would have. [532] Almost without deciding, she looped the ribbon around the cracked rafter and the plum branch, tying them with a loose bow. [553] "That will not hold," Lina said. [559] "It is not meant to hold forever," Maren said. [568] She marked the crack with chalk, wedged it with plum stones from her bag, and poured cold tea on the dry joint to swell the wood. [594] Watching the blue bow flutter, Maren remembered what Ana used to say when the elders quoted the carved door at her. [615] "Deciduous vows drop their leaves so they can live through winter," Ana would say. [629] Maren had called that foolishness while Ana lived. [637] Now she understood it by accident, the way most true things arrive. [649] The contract was not dead because Lina had broken its wording; it was dying because no one would let it shed that wording and grow new leaves. [676] Lina, after a silence, said the early plums had escaped the blight that blackened half the late fruit in the valley. [697] Maren admitted the elders were right that winter stores mattered, since one good market week could not feed February. [716] So they wrote a vow on the ribbon end in chalk: tend together, sell some early, save some late, and return each autumn to renew it. [742] Below them the town bell rang, and Lina tied the bow tighter, not forever, just for this season. [760]
