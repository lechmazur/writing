Mara Venn was nineteen, a reckless idealist who believed every grief could be bargained back to life. [17]
She came to the catacomb orchard where tree roots snake between bones because her brother Tomas had stopped speaking after their mother died. [40]
Above ground, the city buried its dead in walls; below, the poor planted apple trees in the graves so hunger would not follow them. [64]
Now roots fed on marrow and memory alike, and their pale fruit held the last words the dead had never spoken. [85]
The Keeper met her within the glow of half-lit corridors, where oil lamps burned in skulls and shadows moved like slow water. [107]
He was an old man with soil under his nails, fiercely gentle in the way he lifted each fallen apple as if it were a sleeping child. [134]
"You seek Tomas," he said, "but speech is not lost here; it is traded." [148]
Mara laid her offering on a root: a burned tarot card, The Tower blackened at the edges, which their mother had drawn the night before the fire. [175]
The card still smelled of smoke, and in its ruined picture a man fell forever while a woman reached forever and never caught him. [199]
The Keeper turned it once, then told her the law of the orchard, which no priest above would name. [218]
Here bartered destinies grew like grafts: a living soul could take a dead one's unfinished sorrow, and a dead soul could take a living one's unwanted tomorrow. [245]
If Tomas gave his silence to their mother, she might speak through the fruit and finish her warning, but Tomas would carry her death inside his mouth. [272]
Mara understood at once, with the bright cruelty of hope, that she could trade instead of him. [289]
She had not come to mourn correctly or to patch holes in the soul with prayers, as the sisters above advised. [310]
She had come to fix the torn place in Tomas, even if the needle passed through her own life to do it. [332]
"Take my tomorrow," she told the Keeper, and the lamps leaned toward her as if listening. [348]
The Keeper did not rejoice, because he had buried too many saviors who mistook self-destruction for love. [365]
Instead he asked what tomorrow she meant: the year she would apprentice to a mapmaker, marry or not marry, grow old unafraid of fire. [389]
Mara hesitated, and that hesitation was the first honest thing she had brought below. [403]
She had spent months inlaying meaning everywhere, carving Tomas's silence into a message, her mother's death into a lesson, and her own fear into a door. [429]
The Keeper showed her that such meanings could trammel a soul tighter than chains, because a person bound by a story cannot choose a new ending. [455]
To trade truly, he said, she must offer a destiny without dressing it as noble, and accept one without demanding it explain itself. [478]
Only then did the orchard stir, roots tightening around the bones of strangers who had once made kinder and crueler bargains. [499]
From the nearest tree hung one white apple, heavy and warm, and inside it their mother was not screaming as Mara had dreamed, but humming while she folded linen. [528]
The ordinariness broke Mara more completely than any ghost could have done. [540]
She realized her mother had not left a warning at all; the fire had simply taken a woman mid-song, mid-chore, mid-life, with no secret folded into the ash. [568]
That truth left a cleaner hole, one not stuffed with omens, and Mara wept into the roots until the Keeper placed the apple in her hands. [594]
She could still trade, but now the terms had changed because she no longer needed her mother to absolve the randomness of loss. [617]
With dusky resolve, she asked not for her own tomorrow to be taken, but for one shared hour in which Tomas could hear their mother hum and know it was enough. [648]
The orchard accepted a smaller and harder bargain: Mara gave the dead her certainty that grief must mean something, and the dead gave her a silence that did not accuse. [678]
It was not a full patching, but it began to patch holes in the soul the way roots close over bone, slowly and without pretending the break never happened. [707]
She carried the apple up through the half-lit corridors, past lamps that no longer leaned like judges, and found Tomas waiting at the iron gate with his hands empty. [736]
She did not tell him a story about destiny or demand that he heal correctly; she only cut the apple open and let their mother hum fill the stairwell. [765]
Tomas breathed, then whispered her name, not cured, not traded away, but present at last beside his sister in the dim and living dark. [789]
