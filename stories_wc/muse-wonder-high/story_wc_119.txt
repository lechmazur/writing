<story>After last orders at the Jade Lantern, Tomas Rhee stayed at the corner table breaking fortune cookies he had no intention of eating. [23] He was the island's cryptic fortune cookie writer, though the menu called him a kitchen assistant and paid him as one. [44] Saint Brigid was a rocky scatter of forty houses, one restaurant, and a mysterious lighthouse on a rocky island headland that everyone pretended not to fear. [70] Tomas had come there twelve years earlier to transpose life into a new key after his wife died and his hands stopped trusting the piano. [95] He had meant to stay one winter, tune the church piano, and leave on the spring ferry with a cleaner grief. [116] Instead he stayed, because the island ran on open secrets and his fortunes gave people a polite way to hear them. [137] Elin Doyle knew her son was leaving for the mainland, but she only believed it when a cookie told her that empty chairs still hold a place. [164] Old Pete knew he should stop drinking, but he only slowed down when his cookie warned that the tide returns what the shore refuses. [188] Tomas insisted he invented nothing, which was mostly true and therefore the most familiar enigma on Saint Brigid. [206] He listened at the bar, remembered who avoided whom, and let what everyone already knew slip into twelve words or fewer. [227] Sometimes, though, he was accidentally prophetic, and those cookies followed him like unpaid debts. [241] In March he had written that a kept light would fail before it was missed, and the lighthouse lamp died that same night for six minutes. [267] Keeper Mara Venn had laughed and kept the slip in her logbook, but after that she left her spare key under his flowerpot without a word. [293] Tonight she came in wet from the headland, ordered tea she never drank, and waited while the regulars argued about the coming storm. [316] The radio had promised high water by two, a fallen aerial, and no ferry until Thursday, which on Saint Brigid meant everyone would pretend to be calm. [343] Mara waited until the glasses were cleared, then laid two things beside Tomas's notebook: her logbook and a pair of spycraft microdot film specs in a tin spectacles case. [372] The specs had belonged to her grandfather, a wartime coast watcher who read pinhead photographs of shipping routes pasted inside tide tables. [394] Mara had found fresh microdots under the pasted corners of this week's pages, each one a neat list of names, times, and boats that had not yet sailed. [422] Someone on the island was using her lighthouse to signal a smuggling run, and they were doing it on the one night the lamp had to stay lit. [450] She could not tend the lamp, watch the rocks, and name the traitor aloud without starting a panic in a room full of tired drinkers. [475] So Tomas did what he had always done, only deliberately this time: he set out to orchestrate the night by directing flow. [497] He directed the flow of tea toward the window tables, the flow of regulars toward the door, and the flow of his freshest cookies toward very specific hands. [525] To Pete he sent a cookie that read that a strong man carries lanterns without being asked, and Pete rose at once for the headland path. [551] To Elin Doyle he sent word that a mother's kitchen can feed strangers in a storm, and she began stacking bowls before anyone had asked for soup. [578] To young Finn Doyle, who wanted so badly to leave that he had packed twice, he sent the hardest one: a borrowed boat returns heavier than it left. [606] Finn read it twice, looked at Mara, and understood that his secret had been an open one for longer than his courage had lasted. [630] He confessed in the pantry, among flour sacks and spare candles, that cousins from the mainland had paid him to flash the old signal light for one boat. [658] He had pasted the lists so Mara would find them, because leaving them was the only rebellion his fear had allowed him. [680] Mara did not forgive him quickly, which made her mercy believable when she sent him instead to help Pete keep the real lamp turning. [704] All night Tomas kept the restaurant open past its license, moving people between tables like notes until fear settled into work and work settled into soup and dry socks. [733] At dawn the storm boat turned away from a steady light, the ferry aerial was repaired, and no one on Saint Brigid had to pretend Finn's name was a surprise. [763] Tomas wrote one last fortune for himself, slipped it into his coat, and finally believed a man could change his tune without leaving the island that had taught him to listen. [794]</story>
