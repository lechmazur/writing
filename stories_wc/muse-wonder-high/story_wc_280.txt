After the business closes for the final time, Mara Venn returns to the Labyrinth Playhouse with a flashlight and her grandfather's key. [22] The ticket booth still smells of butter and rain, and the marquee bulbs tick as they cool in the dark. [42] She is synesthetically blessed, so cooling bulbs sound like tiny amber bells, and grief itself has always tasted of copper on her tongue. [65] As a child she hid here while her mother sang rehearsals and her father painted sets the green of whispered secrets. [86] Tonight the playhouse has been sold to developers, and by morning its stage will belong to surveyors with orange flags. [106] Mara has come for one thing left in the prop vault, a hand-painted tarot coin her mother used in every production of The Orchard Queen. [131]

On one face her father painted The Star, a woman pouring light into roots; on the other, The Fool, stepping cheerfully off a cliff. [155] Mara found it as a girl and believed, as children in theaters must, that flipping it could change an ending. [175] In the vault she finds only dust, mouse-nest playbills, and a warm draft breathing from behind the costume rack. [194] Then a man's voice, rich and patient, asks from the empty stage whether the house is ready at last. [213] It is Ellis Crowe, the encore-seeking stage ghost who died in 1968 waiting for applause that never came after the fire alarm emptied his finest performance. [239] For fifty years he has rehearsed his bow in the wings, unable to leave until someone freely gives him the encore he lost. [262]

Mara should run, but his voice arrives to her as gold thread and warm bread, the exact colors of her mother's lullabies. [284] 'You dropped this,' he says, and the tarot coin rolls from the shadows though no hand has touched it, spinning The Star toward her. [308] He explains that the coin remembers every flip, and that his death split that night into a fractured timeline of almosts that still crowd the building. [334] In one branch he bows to thunder; in another the alarm was false and no one ever learns his name; in a third Mara's mother is the one who dies young. [365] The branches press so close after closing that Ellis can reach her only by producing echoes, repeating moments until their edges soften and overlap. [389] As proof, he whispers her name, and the playhouse whispers it back in her mother's voice, then her childhood voice, then a voice she has not grown into yet. [418]

'Give me my encore,' he begs, 'and I will give you one branch where this place survives.' [435] It is a liar's bargain and a true one, because Ellis wants only to lure possibility from the brink of despair, in her as much as in himself. [463] Mara understands then that despair sounds black to her, but under it she can hear a thin green note, like spring forcing through a locked door. [489] She follows that note past the stage, through the service door her father forbade her to open, and into the hedge maze behind the theater. [514] At its heart waits the mystic orchard garden concealed within a hedge maze, where apple trees grow in a circle and every fruit holds a lit window. [541] Mara remembers it now, though she was never allowed here as a child and yet somehow picked warm apples here every summer of her life. [566]

Each window shows a different year of the playhouse: her parents dancing barefoot, Ellis alive and laughing, herself old and teaching a red-haired girl to bow. [592] Ellis steps from between two trees wearing evening clothes that smell, to Mara, like blue velvet sounds and rain on hot boards. [614] 'The garden grows where timelines intertwine,' he says, 'and an encore honestly given can graft the strongest branch back onto the trunk.' [636] Mara flips the coin, not to choose, but to listen, and its painted faces chime silver for The Star and reckless gold for The Fool. [661] She realizes no single branch is the true one; the playhouse lived because people kept returning, and it dies only if the last return is hers alone. [688] So instead of clapping by herself, she opens the maze gate wide and calls out to the dark street, inviting any passerby to witness one final scene. [715] Ellis produces echoes of every audience the playhouse ever held, and their layered applause rolls through town until doors open and sleepy neighbors follow the sound home. [742] When strangers, ushers, and one astonished developer intertwine their voices with the echoes in a true encore, Ellis bows at last and dissolves into apple blossom and amber bells. [771] At dawn he offers Mara a year to reopen, and the coin warms in her palm. [787]
