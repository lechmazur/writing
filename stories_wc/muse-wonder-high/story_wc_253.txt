<story>Three years after the happiness mandate ended, Liora still woke at five to the sound the house was not supposed to make. [22] It was the giant wind turbines turning beyond the fields, a low and steady sigh over the farmhouse roof. [41] The farmhouse had belonged to her grandmother, and now its porch leaned in the long morning shadow of the nearest blade. [62] She had come back after the mandate inspectors took her brother away for failing to look relieved during her own arrest. [83] In the cities she had been a blank-faced performer, famous because in an age of required grins her painted white face never changed. [106] Crowds had paid to watch her do nothing while music ordered them to be joyful, and her stillness had given them permission to stop pretending. [131] That was why the inspectors came for her act first, and for Tomas only when he stepped between them and her dressing room door. [155] She did not cry at the hearing, or at the empty station, or when the official letter used the word corrected as if he had been a sum. [183] Grief arrived without weight or color, and days passed in an emotional void where even anger felt borrowed and late. [203] Her neighbor June, who kept the farmhouse from being condemned, told her plainly that emptiness would rot a house faster than rain. [225] So Liora set herself a small and stubborn task, to cultivate lightness in grief the way one coaxed beans from poor soil, by daily attention rather than hope. [253] She began by dramatically narrating mundane tasks, because silence in the kitchen had started to sound like Tomas not answering. [273] And now, she announced to the kettle, the great Liora will pour water as if it mattered, and the crowd will hold its breath. [297] She narrated sweeping ash, mending socks, and feeding hens in the grave tones of a tragedian, until the absurdity loosened something behind her ribs. [321] It was not happiness, which had been ruined by decree, but a thin rehearsed liveliness laid gently over numbness. [340] In the parlor she kept a dried flower bouquet Tomas had bound with twine the winter before his arrest, its lavender and yarrow faded to dust colors. [367] Every evening she carried it to the table, not to mourn correctly, but to have something dead that was still allowed to be beautiful. [391] On the porch after supper she learned to whittle, cutting soft pine into spoons too crooked for use and birds too blunt to fly. [415] The knife work gave her hands a script when her face forgot its lines, and shavings gathered like pale, honest applause at her feet. [439] June began bringing townspeople who had grown afraid of quiet rooms, children raised under mandate speakers who wept when the music stopped. [461] Liora did not offer them sermons or cures, only supper, narration, and then an hour in which no one had to perform feeling. [484] At first she announced the washing up like a royal execution, and forks rang, and shoulders dropped, and the hush that followed was not empty. [509] It was a remade hush, built on purpose from listening, turbine hum, breath, and the small sounds people make when they are not being watched. [534] Then Harlan Voss knocked, the former inspector whose signature had sent Tomas to correction, holding his hat and asking if the quiet had a price. [559] Liora wanted to close the door, but the roof leaked, June owed the feed store, and Tomas was not returned by purity. [581] She let him sit at the far end of the table, charged him double, and used his money to buy seed, lamp oil, and a new blade for her knife. [611] She told no one who paid for the seed, and when June praised her resourcefulness, Liora felt the morally ambiguous lie settle comfortably beside her grief. [637] Harlan came each Thursday and sat blank-faced beside the dried flowers, learning not to fill the remade hush with apologies that asked for comfort. [661] Once, while she whittled a pine bird at the table, he whispered that correction had not killed Tomas, and the knife stopped in her hand. [686] He said Tomas had been released to a northern work farm and had refused the train home because he believed Liora had traded his name for her stage. [714] The accusation was unfair and not entirely false, for she had signed the inspectors' paper promising never to perform stillness again, hoping words would buy his freedom. [741] That night she narrated packing a parcel as if launching a ship, with twine, brown paper, the whittled bird, and one stem from the bouquet. [766] She wrote only that the farmhouse was quiet now, that quiet had been remade by hand, and that he could come and not smile in it. [792]</story>
