Mara was a vivid mural artist in the hill town of Brindle, known for painting walls the color of ripe plums and river stones. [24]
She was quietly driven, the kind of worker who rose before the bakers and stayed after the market lamps went out. [45]
In October the council hired her to restore the amber pavilion in autumn, where wooden arches glowed honey gold above fallen leaves. [67]
They wanted bright saints and harvest dancers, but Mara asked first who had built the pavilion and why it faced east toward the cathedral. [91]
No one could tell her, except Tomas the caretaker, who brought her tea and a tin box he had kept for fifty years. [114]
Inside lay a collection of old postcards, their corners soft and their stamps faded, all written by one woman to her sister across many winters. [139]
The top card showed Mara's great grandmother Liora, a painter like her, standing in the pavilion with a brush and a bundle of white seeds. [164]
On the back Liora had written that the pavilion had no purpose until someone learned what the night garden was for. [185]
That sentence changed Mara's commission into a question she carried home, to understand why flowers bloom at night. [203]
The other postcards spoke of moonflowers and evening primrose, of jasmine that opened only after sunset along the pavilion steps. [223]
They had been planted for Liora's sister Ana, a nurse who worked all night at the infirmary and could visit only before dawn. [246]
Yet the last card, never sent and dated the week Ana died, said only that the answer was not in the garden but in the window. [272]
Mara read it three times under the pavilion roof while rain darkened the leaves and the first cold wind moved through the arches. [295]
She knew the window meant the great west window of the cathedral, which Liora had helped etch before she left Brindle forever. [317]
From the pavilion it looked plain gray at noon, a dull circle of saints whose faces the sun washed almost white. [338]
Mara resolved to reckon with what Liora had hidden there, though she did not yet know what grief or gift it held. [360]
Through late autumn she scraped the pavilion walls and found, under peeling cream paint, a mural of moths around a dark blue moon. [383]
The moths had silver dust on their wings, and each one carried a tiny letter, but the plaster was too cracked to read them whole. [408]
When snow came, work stopped, and Mara spent the winter in the cathedral loft copying the postcards into her sketchbook by candlelight. [430]
The priests let her climb before services, and in that cold quiet she learned how early light changed the window from stone to skin. [454]
Across the melting of winter's last snow, when meltwater ran black under the cathedral steps, she finally saw the trick of the glass. [477]
At dawn she stood in the empty choir and looked through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window as the sun touched only its lowest edge. [506]
In that dawning hush the top saints vanished, and a second scene woke beneath them, cut so fine that bright day erased it. [529]
It showed Ana in her nurse cap, bending over white flowers while moths rose around her like slow sparks in the dark. [551]
Around the rim Liora had etched the letters from the pavilion mural, and Mara read them with her hand pressed flat to the cold glass. [576]
The words said that night flowers bloom for those who cannot come by day, opening their color where hands and moths can find them in darkness. [602]
Beneath that line a smaller one admitted what the postcards never could, that Liora had planted beauty not to fix grief but to keep company with it. [629]
Mara wept then, not loudly, with the umbral tenderness of someone finding a chair still set for a guest long gone from the table. [653]
She understood that dawn was not an ending for the night garden but its brief meeting with the day, when sisters could almost touch again. [678]
That spring she finished the pavilion mural, restoring Liora's moths and adding Ana among the moonflowers, her face turned toward the first pale light. [702]
With Tomas she replanted jasmine and primrose along the steps, using seeds saved from the tin box and cuttings begged from old gardens. [725]
On the first warm night the town came before dawn instead of noon, and in the shared hush they watched white petals open for the moths. [751]
