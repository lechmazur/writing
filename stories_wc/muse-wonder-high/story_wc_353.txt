<story>After the hedgehog uncurls under the root cellar, Tova knows the ground has softened enough to work. [17] She finds it each dawn curled in straw, nose tucked, and she waits without touching it until that first slow stretch. [38] Only then does she enter the siskin alder grove, where pale trunks stand close and last year's catkins hang motionless. [58] No siskins have come this year, and without their thin calls the valley has completed its long slide into a silent world. [80] The telegraph wires fell in November, the mill bell cracked in December, and by January people had learned to close doors without letting them click. [105] Tova is a tree-song sculptor, the last in a family that hollowed fallen alders and strung them so wind could play what hands were too grieved to say. [133] This spring her purpose is plain and impossible, to create music in a silent world without breaking the quiet that has kept everyone safe from listening for bad news. [162] She is symphonically minded, so she does not hear a single harp when she walks the grove but whole movements waiting in separate trees. [186] The leaning alder by the creek would be low strings, patient and dark. [199] The twin trunks near the path would answer higher, if she could tune them without splitting their healed scars. [218] That caution led her, before any cutting, to the long-forgotten sculpture her mother had abandoned behind the root cellar thirty years ago. [240] By analyzing stress cracks in a long-forgotten sculpture half sunk in moss, she learned where alder wood yields and where it remembers too much strain. [265] The cracks ran like faint rivers from each old peg, and she traced them with a fingertip instead of a pencil so she could feel the ridges rise. [293] Where three cracks met, her mother had tuned too tightly for winter; where they spread wide and shallow, the wood had sung and survived. [317] Tova copied those distances onto fresh fallen branches, not as numbers but as the span of her own hand, so the lesson stayed in her body. [343] At the grove edge, where the telegraph pole rotted into ferns, she found a porcelain insulator still whole beneath a mat of needles. [366] It was cool and bell-shaped and heavier than it looked, with grooves that had once held a wire carrying voices from the next valley. [390] She set it in the hollow of the leaning alder as a bridge, because porcelain would bear the strings without drinking their sound the way damp wood did. [418] For gut she used no gut at all but salvaged telegraph wire, cleaned strand by strand until her cloth came away gray. [440] Yet when the harp stood finished at dusk, she could not bring herself to strum it. [456] A sound made alone, she understood, would only prove how alone the maker was. [470] So she began carrying the smaller branches she had tuned, one by one, to the houses below the grove, listening first at each threshold before she knocked. [497] At the miller's house she heard dough being folded in slow turns, and she shortened a string until its imagined note matched that patient rhythm. [522] At the widow Ansel's door she heard a kettle kept just below boiling, never allowed to whistle, and she loosened another string to a hush below speech. [549] At the schoolhouse, empty since January, a boy named Petter pressed his ear to her tuned branch and tapped back the tempo of a game only he remembered. [577] This was empathy in motion, not pity carried like a parcel but attention that changed its tuning as it moved from door to door. [601] She wrote nothing down; she let each visit alter the set of strings she carried back up the hill on her shoulder. [623] By the seventh evening the leaning alder held four branches of different lengths, each grooved to rest against the porcelain insulator, each answering a household she had entered quietly. [652] She did not send word, because paper notices had become funeral notices, but she left the grove path swept and a lantern unlit at its entrance. [678] They came the way she had come to them, singly and without announcement, the miller with flour still on his sleeves, Ansel with her kettle now cold, Petter holding his mother's hand. [710] Tova knelt in the needles, placed her palm flat against the alder to feel for any new strain, and waited until Petter, not she, reached out. [736] When he strummed, the wire gave a low, uneven note that the porcelain insulator carried clear and round into the trunk. [757] Above them, siskins settled in the branches, listening, as the grove held its first quiet music. [773]</story>
