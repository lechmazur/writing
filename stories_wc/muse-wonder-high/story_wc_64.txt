Every evening, after the last mourners left Hollowbrook, Tomas Reed worked the paths with a whistle, a blithe cemetery gardener who spoke to the headstones as if they were shy neighbors. [31]
The air around him always held a velveteen gloom, soft and close and purple-black, but he liked how it softened the marble and made his lantern seem kind. [59]
On the hill above the graves stood the old feed store's cobalt silo under stars, its blue paint gone chalky, its iron door dented where a storm had thrown a gate in 1962. [92]
Tomas kept his tools inside, each rake and trowel tenderly organized on pegs painted with the names of saints, because disorder, he said, made grief louder. [118]
He also kept a chessboard there, and every Thursday he played against Widow Vale, who had buried three husbands and claimed the dead kibitzed if you listened properly. [146]
That October she stopped coming, and a week later Tomas planted winter roses on her grave and found her name already cut shallow into the stone, as if she had ordered it herself. [179]
The night after her funeral, something knocked inside the silo in a pattern too regular for wind, three slow taps, a pause, then two. [203]
He took his lantern and climbed, expecting a trapped owl, and instead found Widow Vale's tapestry draped over the chessboard, its edges still dusty from the cottage wall where it had hung for decades. [237]
The tapestry showed Hollowbrook from above, every grave a colored stitch, and recent plots glowed with thread so new it had not yet faded. [261]
Under it sat an antique sovereign case of scuffed gold, the kind made to carry a single coin against a gentleman's heart. [283]
Inside lay no coin, only a folded card in her handwriting that read: Let the game finish, then listen for what repeats wrong. [306]
Tomas set the clock as they always had, ten minutes each, and played both sides through her favorite opening while the knocking came again from the silo walls. [334]
Each move answered with an echo that did not match, a pawn's step returning as a knight's jump, a capture returning twice, until he understood the walls were holding uncharted echoes of moves never played. [369]
He did not run, because his whole work was staying kindly with things others abandoned, and because he needed to discover the shape of intentions she had left stitched and timed and hidden. [402]
The card's instruction held, and after the chess clock runs down, the silo fell so still that Tomas could hear thread ticking against wood as the tapestry slid an inch toward him. [434]
Its border was not a border at all but rows of tiny knots and broken loops, and where her fingers had weakened the wool had begun to fray and tangle. [464]
Tomas, whose hands knew roots and twine, began to untangle the worst knot by lamplight, loosening rather than pulling, because a snapped thread would lose whatever order she had meant. [494]
Only when smoke from his lantern made his eyes water did he see that each color changed length like the letters of her cramped shorthand, which he knew from thirty years of keeping their chess scores, readable by decoding frayed threads in a tapestry's border. [539]
Red meant a grave dug honestly, black meant a name misspelled by the stone cutter, and gold, repeated three, two, three, two, pointed to the sovereign case and to the plots where her husbands lay. [574]
The decoded border named no murder and no treasure, as the village gossips would have hoped, but a pattern of small, deliberate mercies she had paid for in secret for forty years. [606]
Gold knots marked the sovereigns she had mailed to widows who would lose their houses, red knots the anonymous roses, and black knots the gravestones she corrected at night so poor families would not be shamed. [642]
The final length of gold ran past all three husbands and stopped, unfinished, at a stitch shaped like his own crooked rook, the piece she always let him play despite its chipped crown. [675]
Beneath that thread, the border opened on a pocket she had sewn shut, holding a single bright sovereign and a list of three living neighbors whose rents and winter coal depended on gifts no one was meant to trace. [714]
Tomas understood then that the echoes were uncharted because kindness done properly leaves no map except in the memory of places, and she had needed a player patient enough to finish after time expired. [748]
He put the sovereign in its case, pocketed the list, rehung the tapestry where the stars could fade it honestly, and at dawn began whistling again among the graves, already planning the first anonymous gift. [783]
