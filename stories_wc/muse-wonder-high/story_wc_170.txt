<story>On the echoing terrace in spring, the rain had just stopped, and the whole town below held its breath. [19] Over the hush before bells ring, the church tower was still dark and no rope had yet moved in the belfry. [40] Along the edge of the terrace stood the prism parapet after rain, its wet stones splitting the last light into red and gold and violet. [65] Children came up the wet steps carrying covered mirrors, as their mothers had taught them to do each year on the first clear evening. [89] They did not come to see their faces. [97] They came because the mirrors in their houses had begun to show only what had already been lost. [115] In one house a mirror kept a dead father shaving at dawn, and in another it kept an empty chair beside a cold stove. [139] The children wanted the glass to relent and show what might still happen, if only someone knew how to ask it properly. [161] That was why they waited for the elusive mirage painter, who was never seen arriving and never seen leaving the terrace. [182] Some said she lived inside the color that rain left on stone, and others said she was the color, briefly learning how to walk. [206] Tonight she was already there when the clouds opened, standing on the wet tiles with a brush made of light in her hand. [229] She was perceptively calm, not with the dull calm of someone who felt nothing, but with the quick calm of someone who heard every fear in the crowd before it found words. [261] She looked at the youngest child, a girl named Wren who clutched a round mirror against her chest like a shield. [282] Inside that mirror, every night, Wren saw her brother Tomas standing in their doorway with wet shoes, though Tomas had drowned in the river last winter. [308] The painter knelt so her eyes were level with the glass, and she spoke as if the mirror were a shy animal that might be persuaded. [334] Her purpose was plain to anyone who watched her work over many springs, for she came always to persuade mirrors to show possibilities instead of repetitions. [360] But a mirage cannot lift a brush, and she had no heartbeat of her own to steady her hand against the real world. [383] So each year she had to ask for a borrowed heartbeat, freely lent and freely taken back before the bells could ring. [405] She never took it from the frightened or the greedy, and she never kept it past the hush, because a kept heart becomes a chain. [430] Wren stepped forward this year, as the oldest children had done before her, and offered hers with both hands pressed flat over her ribs. [454] The painter did not touch her. [460] Instead she asked the children to sing, because the loan could only travel through coded lullabies in children's songs, where a heart could hide inside rhythm and be returned unharmed. [490] To grown ears the songs were only about sparrows and soup and moons caught in buckets, the nonsense rhymes sung at bedsides for a hundred years. [516] But under the words lay older instructions, verse by verse, telling any listening mirror how to loosen its grip on yesterday. [537] One line taught glass to forget a face gently, and the next taught it to imagine the mourner older, steadier, and able to welcome a future that had not yet been refused. [569] The children sang, and the terrace answered them, each note returning a breath later from the wet arches so that past and present sang together. [594] It made a dusky harmony in the violet air, not sad and not joyful, but wide enough to hold both without breaking. [616] On the third verse Wren felt her own pulse step sideways, light as a moth leaving a windowsill, and settle for a while inside the painter. [642] The painter shivered once, then grew solid at the edges, her wet sleeves darkening, her bare feet printing the tiles, her brush suddenly heavy enough to use. [669] She turned to the prism parapet and drew her brush along its shining top, gathering red for courage, gold for appetite, and violet for the hour when grief finally sleeps. [699] Then, with Wren's heartbeat marking time in her wrist, she painted quick bright strokes across each covered mirror that the children uncovered one by one. [724] In Wren's glass, the wet shoes vanished, replaced by herself grown tall, planting plum trees beside a child who laughed like Tomas. [746] Around her, other mirrors showed workable mornings, reconciliations, journeys, and doors that had never yet been tried. [763] Then, with grace, she returned the heartbeat in the last verse, and as bells rang the painter thinned into color on wet stone. [786]</story>
