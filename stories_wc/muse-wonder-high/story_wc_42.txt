<story>For thirty years, Tomas Vale had repaired cameras in the lunar observatory, where Earth hung bright above the clouds of its own blue seas. [24] The other engineers called him the sage camera repairer because he could diagnose a broken shutter by touch and patience alone. [45] He had come to the Moon to harness the ephemeral, to give brief light a surface where it could remain. [65] Earthshine, comet tails, and the first gold edge of sunrise all vanished unless glass and silver caught them in time. [85] That evening, the observatory waited for a transit that would not return during any of their lifetimes. [102] For ninety seconds, the comet Isolde would cross the full Earth, and its tail would shine through moving cloud and ocean glare. [124] Director Sana Iqbal intended to record it on the last mechanical astrocamera, whose plates could survive radiation that ruined newer sensors. [145] Tomas had built that camera with his wife Mara, the astronomer who first traced the comet and named its return. [165] Mara had been dead eleven years, yet her handwriting still filled the margins of every exposure chart in the dome. [185] Her calculations promised a ring of comet light around Earth, brief enough to miss with one careless blink. [203] Three hours before the transit, a moonquake struck, and every foundation under the observatory groaned in the darkening corridors. [222] When foundations shake on the Moon, dust rises without wind and old ice cracks like distant glass under stone. [241] The first tremor cracked the dome mount and threw the camera against its locked storage cradle. [257] The second tore power from the clocks, computers, heaters, and rail launcher that would send the finished plate home. [276] In the sudden dark, Tomas heard one thin sound turning above the workbench, frantic and impossible to ignore. [294] The master clock had lost its escapement, leaving a clock hand that would not stop spinning past the darkened numbers. [314] Sana found him kneeling beside the camera with both palms flat against its cold brass case. [330] His touch told him the shutter curtain was torn, the governor bent, and two springs loose from their teeth. [349] Without the dome mount, the transit would pass unseen, and without the launcher, no plate could reach escape velocity. [368] A radiation front followed the comet, and its arrival would fog every exposed plate within one hour. [385] Sana ordered everyone toward the lower shelter, where thick rock could protect lungs, hearts, and frightened human patience. [403] Tomas stayed beside the camera until the alarms faded and a softer sound reached him from the greenhouse door. [422] A dry leaf had fallen from Mara's fig tree and brushed the copper tray beneath its lowest branch. [440] In that whisper, he heard that the tremors had ended and that still air had returned to the observatory. [459] He also heard a way to keep time when every honest clock in the building had failed. [476] The greenhouse skylight faced Earth at an angle the broken dome could no longer reach during the transit. [494] Tomas asked Sana to restructure the observatory around people instead of machines before fear made the decision for them. [513] They would move the camera, lend the launcher their heater batteries, and turn hands, ears, and voices into instruments. [532] Sana studied the spinning clock hand, then gave the order with the calm of someone choosing a dangerous hope. [551] Engineers carried the camera through the service tunnel while Tomas cradled its torn shutter like an injured bird. [569] In the greenhouse, they bolted the camera to Mara's irrigation frame beneath the broad skylight and its patient fig tree. [589] Tomas rebuilt the shutter with tweezers, spare foil, and a spring he removed from his own battered wristwatch. [607] For timing, Sana climbed to the marked branch with a basket of dry leaves gathered from the tree floor. [626] Moon gravity pulled each leaf down at a changelessly constant pace through the sealed and carefully stilled greenhouse air. [645] Each fall offered one measured interval between the rustle of release and the soft strike on copper. [662] By the sound of a leaf falling, Tomas opened the shutter at each rustle and closed it at each landing. [682] When Earth filled the skylight, comet light entered the greenhouse and laid a bright ring across his scarred hands. [701] Leaf after leaf, the plate drank comet light while Sana whispered Mara's numbers and Tomas answered with the shutter. [720] For ninety seconds, breath, leaves, and machinery held the ephemeral still enough to leave its ring on silver. [738] Tomas sealed the plate in a capsule and surrendered the spinning clock motor to time the launcher's firing. [756] Fed by heater batteries, the restructured launcher hurled it beyond lunar escape velocity toward a recovery ship. [773] Then the clock stood still, and Tomas wept beneath the luminous ache of Earthlight. [787]</story>
