When the wall comes down at dawn, the river will return to the east fields for the first time in twenty three years. [23]
If the seeds are not already in the ground, the water will sheet off the baked earth and carry the topsoil away. [45]
That is why Wren Marlow spent the last night chained inside the abandoned carnival prize redemption center, listening to the river press against concrete. [69]
The guards had caught her moving crates after curfew and had locked her ankle to the prize counter with a bicycle chain. [91]
They meant to hold her until the demolition crew arrived, then charge her with theft of company grain. [109]
Wren was the chained liberator they did not know they had made, because the chain gave her an alibi and the night gave her time. [134]
For seven dry years she had been patiently strategic, smiling at inspectors while she hid drought seeds behind stuffed bears and cracked teacups. [157]
Every prize she redeemed for a child had carried two rewards, one toy in the hand and three seeds sewn into its seams. [180]
Now hundreds of families waited in the dark with empty sacks, trusting that the clandestine seed vault beneath the redemption floor was real. [203]
Her father had built that vault before the company sealed the river, and he had hidden its keys where musicians hide their secrets. [226]
The old upright piano stood by the shuttered window, its bench just beyond the reach of Wren's chain. [244]
On the prize shelf above her hung a carnival archery set, its last arrow with strange fletching made from curled redemption tickets. [266]
Wren pulled the arrow down with the edge of a ruler and used its ribbon fletching to snag the twine handle her father had tied to the bench. [294]
The bench scraped close, ticket ribbons fluttering, and the chain bit her ankle as she lifted the lid. [312]
Inside lay nine brass keys in a neat row, none labeled, each one warm from the summer night trapped in wood. [333]
Anyone else would have tried them by size, and anyone else would have triggered the dye pack her father wired to the wrong sequence. [357]
Wren found the sequence by decoding the arrangement of keys left in a piano bench, matching their notched heads to the lullaby her father played when the carnival lights failed. [387]
The notches rose and fell like notes, long and short, until the row stopped being clutter and became instructions. [406]
She chose four keys in that musical order and left the five decoys untouched. [420]
The fourth key opened a seam in the floor under the counter, and cool air rose carrying the clean smell of burlap and amaranth. [444]
Below were jars and tins gathered from three valleys, sorghum and beans and squash, each labeled in her father's careful hand. [465]
This was no company grain engineered to die after one harvest, but seed that could be saved and shared. [484]
Wren had gathered it to plant resilience in dry soil, so a field could survive a failed rain and still feed the people who tended it. [510]
She could not carry the vault into the fields, but she did not need to leave the room to free it. [531]
Outside the rear window, her niece Paloma waited by the old prize chute with a handcart hidden under a tarp. [551]
Wren fed sacks through the chute one by one, whispering the planting depth for each as if the seeds could hear her. [573]
Boots struck gravel outside before the last sack was through, and a guard shouted that the demolition foreman wanted the building cleared. [595]
Wren had to impede them without waking the whole patrol, so she jammed the arrow across the door track and kicked the loose ticket dispenser into its path. [623]
When the guards forced the door, its rollers caught on the shaft, the dispenser spilled tickets, and the men slipped and cursed in the paper drift. [649]
Those two minutes let Paloma vanish between the silent carousel horses with enough seed for forty families. [666]
The other keepers met her beyond the fairground fence and ran, sacks on their backs, toward furrows already scratched into the east fields. [689]
They sowed by lantern light with their hands, pressing hope into dust while the demolition charges ticked on the wall above them. [711]
At dawn a guard returned with bolt cutters, not to punish Wren, but because his mother had received three seeds in a toy bear years before. [737]
Then the charges fired, the wall came down in a white roar, and the river found the new seeds just hours after their planting. [761]
Wren limped into the mud on her freed ankle and laughed, moving with the staggering grace of someone who had waited, chained and patient, for exactly this water. [789]
