<story>Elian was the boy who cried wolf's son, and no village on the mainland would trust him with so much as a gate. [23] That was why, at nineteen, he took the only post offered without a reference, keeper's boy at the lighthouse prison off a jagged reef. [47] The place was a tower first and a prison after, its cells climbing the inside wall in a spiral no plan had ever mapped honestly. [72] Seven prisoners lived there for crimes no court named aloud, and Elian fed them, wound the lamp, and listened where the chaplain would not. [96] Old Father Brine, sailing home for good, left him the lattice from a confessional he had built into the chapel wall. [117] It was a square of dark oak, crossed and recrossed, small enough to carry under one arm and scarred where foreheads had pressed for mercy. [142] Through it the prisoners told him not sins, mostly, but the names of villages, the taste of plums, and the roads they still walked in sleep. [168] He wrote none of it down, because paper belonged to the governor, but he kept every story behind his teeth like a second set of keys. [194] His father had spent those keys freely, crying wolf for sport until the real wolf came and the village came too late with empty hands. [219] Elian had learned the opposite trade, and it marked him strangely, for he sustains impossibilities the way other men sustain a note on a fiddle. [244] If a drowned sailor knocked below, or a prisoner swore his dead wife set a cup beside him, Elian listened until the thing became bearable and true enough to live with. [275] In October the tapping began to fail, cell by cell, along the spiral. [288] Prisoners tapped spoons at dusk to prove they were still themselves, and the tower answered with small iron music from floor to floor. [311] Then one evening the music stopped at the fourth cell and never reached the fifth, and Elian understood he was chasing hush the way his father had chased attention. [340] Each night after, the silence climbed one step higher, patient as water in a stairwell. [355] He found fever in the lower cells, empty cups, blankets kicked to stone, and men too weak to lift a spoon but not too weak to whisper one word, below. [385] Below meant the old cistern works under the tower, a labyrinth of brick throats built for rain and forgotten when the prison took the rock. [410] The governor's boat was due at first calm after the storm, and on it would come new chains, clean ledgers, and men trained to read guilt from a smell. [439] Before they find the bodies, he told himself, before any stranger could decide that a liar's son had quieted his charges for their rations, he had to act. [467] Elian knew the rations were untouched in their sacks, but truth had never once arrived in his family wearing a believable coat. [489] He had to find the dead himself, name them aloud, and bear true witness before the boat came, or be hanged by a story other men told faster. [517] The fever men whispered that the way below began behind the pantry, through the scuffs on a narrow door left ajar for decades. [540] No keeper had shut it because its lock had rusted open, and generations of boots, buckets, stretchers, and dragged heels had written their traffic across its lower boards. [568] Elian knelt with the lamp and read the scuffs as the prisoners had taught him to read tapping, short for turn, long for crawl, bright for beware the drop. [597] When he held the confessional lattice over the boards, its crossed slats broke the marks into squares, and the squares fell at last into a route. [623] It was the same mercy the lattice had always offered, a way to look at a hard thing in pieces until the pieces confessed. [647] So he tied one end of his lamp twine to the door handle and went down to thread a labyrinth with his own story, spoken cell by cell to the dark. [678] The passage narrowed to brick shoulders and cold rain breath, and where the floor fell away the lantern light seemed to hover, unwilling to show him the depth all at once. [709] He followed the hush instead of the noise, left where spoons still rang in memory, right where the air gave back nothing at all. [733] In the last cistern, three old bodies lay in copperlight repose where the sunset fell through a grating, their faces turned, merciful and terrible, toward the narrow sea. [761] He laid the lattice over them like a small gate, spoke each name and his own beside it, then climbed to write them true in the chapel book before morning brought discovery. [793]</story>
