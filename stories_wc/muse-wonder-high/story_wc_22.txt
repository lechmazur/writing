<story>After the reef dies, the tide no longer sings white against the black rocks below Gloom Hill. [17] It comes in brown and quiet, lapping a battlefield that men still call a harbor. [32] Tomas Wray, the gloom-hill coachman, drives no passengers now, because no road out of the valley remains unbroken. [50] Instead, each evening he walks his empty coach down to the blooming jetty at twilight, its wheels creaking over salt and shattered shell. [73] The jetty blooms because he has taught it to, one crack at a time. [87] Vines stitch the cannon mounts, and pale trumpets open where signal fires once burned. [101] His purpose is simple and stubborn enough to outlast grief: to let a garden reclaim a battlefield. [118] On the coach seat beside him rests his collection of dried seed pods, sorted in oilcloth by valley, river, and ruined orchard. [140] Some pods came from his dead wife's kitchen garden, some from graves he dug when the bells stopped ringing. [159] He speaks dead languages to them as he works, not from vanity, but because the old names hold the planting depths. [180] Latin tells him which roots fear lime, and the drowned harbor tongue tells him which seeds can drink brine. [199] No one else remembers those tongues, so the jetty feels narrow and vast at once, a single plank holding an entire lost world. [222] He works by understanding bird calls, listening longer than other men listen to speech. [236] The gulls cry where the water hides rot, the terns chatter over clean sand, and the night heron croaks only above ground that will not poison roots. [263] When the reef lived, its fish fed the birds, and the birds told any patient man where life still moved below. [284] Now their calls are thinner, but Tomas has learned to hear absence as clearly as sound. [300] Tonight, the heron flies three times over the old powder magazine and will not land. [315] Tomas nods, because that refusal marks soil still fouled by oil and iron, where nothing honest should yet take root. [335] Instead, the terns gather screaming above the flooded parade ground, where rain has washed the ash into a shallow black mirror. [356] There, he decides, the garden will try again. [364] He must fertilize that drowned ground before any seed can forgive it. [376] He does not use the ash of houses, which the birds avoid, nor dung from the army mules, which still carries fever. [398] He uses what the submerged concord has left him: kelp, crushed shell, fish bones, and mud sweetened by returning worms. [418] That is his name for the truce under the water, where crab and eel and root and rot have stopped warring and begun trading again. [443] After the reef dies, nothing above the tideline trusts anything else, but below it, life quietly keeps its agreements. [462] Tomas learned that truce by watching birds dive and rise with empty beaks or full ones, patient as priests. [481] He kneels in the flooded parade ground and works the kelp and shell into the mud with his bare hands. [501] The water is cold enough to ache, and wide enough, in the last light, to make his kneeling figure feel like the only man alive. [526] Yet the jetty behind him glows with flowers, a narrow vastness of color built on a place designed for departure and death. [548] He opens the oilcloth and chooses pods that rattle low, which his wife taught him meant seeds still sleeping but alive. [569] He names each one aloud in a dead language, pressing it into the fertilized mud at the depth its old name commands. [591] Bean for the drowned rows, bitter melon for the fences, and marsh rose for the water's edge, where herons will one day consent to stand. [616] Above him, the terns change their cry, a shorter note he has never heard over this ground before. [634] It is the call they make over nesting sand, and Tomas laughs once, startled, with mud to his elbows. [653] The birds are not promising success, only reporting that the submerged concord has reached the parade ground at last. [672] He covers the last pod, washes his hands in the black mirror, and watches twilight fold the battlefield into flowers not yet born. [695]</story>
