On the night they decided to rebuild, Joss carried his whole life in a coat that smelled of rain and ink. [21] He was a vagrant penny poet, a man who slept under bridges and copied poems onto scrap paper for anyone who offered a penny, a meal, or a dry place to sit. [53] He had no home because every fixed address asked him to become one fixed person, and that was the one promise he could not keep. [78] Joss molts personalities monthly, shedding a name, a laugh, and a way of holding his pen the way other men shed winter coats. [101] In January he was solemn and wrote elegies, in February he joked through every line, and by March he whispered as if each word cost him blood. [128] Doctors called it disorder, landladies called it lying, but Joss called it survival, because no single self could carry all the hunger and wonder he had seen. [155] That spring the police found him asleep between the stacks of a condemned public library, arrested him for trespass, and gave a judge a reason to send him to Harrowgate Prison for ninety days. [189] He expected stone, shouting, and locked doors, and he found all three, but he also found work that quietly changed the terms of his sentence. [214] After a winter riot left the indoor library flooded and its roof half burned, the warden moved what survived into steel cages in the exercise yard. [240] Rain spotted the covers, wind worried the pages, and rust climbed the shelves until the place became a rusted library under stars, open to weather and rumor alike. [268] Joss was assigned to sort the damaged stock because he was the only new prisoner who wept when he saw a spine cracked for kindling. [293] There he met Silas, a broad, quiet lifer who had murdered a man at nineteen and had spent twenty years trying to learn how to be gentle. [320] Theirs was a friendship sparked sorting books at a prison library, beginning with an argument over whether a blackened book of poems belonged with literature or with trash. [348] Silas said trash, because the last pages were ash, but Joss read the surviving lines aloud and Silas went still, listening as if someone had finally spoken his own language. [378] From then on they worked side by side each afternoon, Joss teaching Silas where poems hid their turns, and Silas teaching Joss how to mend bindings with thread, glue, and patience. [409] Silas also kept the coffee tin with secret false bottom that the previous librarian had left behind, and for weeks Joss assumed it held only sugar stolen from the kitchen. [439] In truth, under the false bottom lay seeds, tomato, bean, squash, and marigold, smuggled in letters from Silas's sister and saved for a spring that prison walls were built to deny. [470] Silas had never planted them because a garden in the yard would be torn up as defiance, and alone he could not bear to watch another living thing punished for growing. [501] Then in April the warden, eager to impress inspectors, ordered the yard library cleared away, calling its rust and ruin an embarrassment that would vanish before morning. [528] That order landed in the middle of Joss's monthly molt, when a newer, bolder self was pushing through the shy one who had entered Harrowgate and making his hands shake with restless light. [561] This new Joss did not plead or hide, but proposed a bargain in the yard at dusk, asking to forge beauty from anarchy instead of merely sweeping it away. [590] He told the warden that prisoners who rebuilt the library and tended a garden beside it would give inspectors a story of order earned, not order imposed. [617] The warden laughed, then saw that Silas and a dozen others were already standing behind Joss with tools, books, and the stubborn silence of men choosing work over riot. [646] He gave them one night, warning that anything unfinished at dawn would be bulldozed, and he left them under gathering dark with no extra guards and no promise of mercy. [676] So they worked as the moon clears the skyline, moonlight entering every crack like a luminous fracture, while hammers rang, shelves rose, and damp pages dried on warm stone. [705] Between the rebuilt shelves Silas finally opened the tin and pressed the hidden seeds into soil they had loosened in the cracks, planting a blooming resistance that no order could mistake for surrender. [738] Before dawn, Joss copied the saved poem for Silas without asking a penny, because friendship had already paid him in full. [759]
