<story>Mara Voss had introduced herself for three years only as a drone strike wedding survivor, because every other title felt like a lie she had to maintain. [27] At her own wedding in Sanaa, a Hellfire had mistaken the mirrored canopy for a guidance array, and she had walked out of the smoke carrying her husband's ring in her burned palm. [60] The surgeons saved the hand, but not the reflex that made her flinch at camera flashes, wedding sparklers, and the high whine of air conditioners. [85] That reflex was why the Helios Array hired her, not despite it, because a floating observatory needs watchers who notice a wrong flicker before instruments can name it. [113] Helios hung in thin air above the Atacama, tethered to nothing, its mirrored dome and radiators turning slowly to keep one dark eye always on the Sun. [140] From its deck, Earth looked already abandoned, a salt pan and red dust below, and the silence felt clean enough to hurt. [162] Mara's job that winter was to hunt for wasted sunlight, to find where unused potential pools in the Array and steals power from the telescopes. [187] Every panel should have taken light and made current, yet the ledgers showed a missing three percent that vanished each noon and returned nowhere. [211] The chief engineer called it accounting error, but Mara trusted the heat shimmer she could see curling off panels that claimed to be idle. [235] The only personal thing she kept at her station was a geode crystal split open on the console, purple teeth around a hollow that caught the dome light. [263] Her husband had bought it that morning in the market, joking that even stone needed a secret room where it could be more than stone. [288] She tapped it whenever alarms rose, not for luck, but to remind her fingers that some breaks reveal structure instead of ending it. [311] At noon, the Array took a solar flare broadside, breakers tripped across three decks, and the captain ordered all nonessential draw stopped while circuit breakers cool. [337] That gave her forty minutes of forced quiet, no pumps masking vibration, no fans lying to her skin, and no excuse to postpone the hunt. [362] She could not trace three percent by staring at totals, so she worked through perturbation theory, nudging one variable at a time and watching how the whole system answered. [391] If a panel was truly dead, a small change in its tilt would change nothing, but a hidden pool would ripple somewhere unexpected. [414] She tilted the east string by half a degree, waited, and listened to the dome creak as thermal stresses redistributed through the frame. [437] The meters stayed flat, yet the geode on her console threw a brighter band across her notebook, as if something had altered the light without altering its source. [465] She frowned, shielded the crystal with her burned palm, and felt warmth gather in the scar before any thermometer on the wall moved. [488] The missing energy was intangibly tangible, invisible in the logs, yet heavy on her skin like sunlight through a magnifying lens held by a patient hand. [514] A second perturbation, two degrees on the west string, moved the warm spot from her scar to the crystal hollow, where dust motes hung motionless and bright. [541] That was the answer perturbation theory always promised, not the pool itself, but the pattern of responses that made its hiding place impossible to deny. [566] Behind panel nine, where cables entered a junction box sealed since launch, Mara found a battery bank that no schematic admitted existed. [588] It had been charging in secret at noon for years, swelling hot and useless, hoarding current for a rescue beacon whose program had been canceled before Helios flew. [616] The beacon had never been told to stop wanting rescue, so it drank potential and waited to scream for help that no one had requested. [641] Mara understood it at once, a survivor circuit built to signal forever from a disaster that had already ended, and her throat tightened with bleak exhilaration. [667] With the breakers still cool, she had authority to isolate the bank, but isolation would only cage the waste again behind another polite filter. [691] Instead she coupled it directly to the telescopes, unfiltered, and woke the dome with every hoarded watt at the exact moment the flare peaked again. [716] For nine seconds the observatory saw the Sun without shielding, an unfiltered awakening that burned no eye because Mara had turned that stolen noon into lenses, shutters, and song in the data. [748] When the breakers clicked back, warm and ready, she held the geode to the light and laughed in the ruined, bright silence, alive because the wasted place in her had finally been spent on seeing.
