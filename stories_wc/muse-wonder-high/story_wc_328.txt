<story>I came to kill a legend and stayed to water it. [11] My name is June Hart, and for two years I worked as a cryptid researcher for the state university, cataloguing footprints that never led to bodies. [37] When the funding dried up, I went undercover instead, taking work as an apprentice to the one woman I believed had stolen the truth from me. [63] Maera Sol ran an herbalist shop above a frozen creek in northern Minnesota, where January locked the roads and turned every breath to glass. [87] Below her shop, cut into the limestone and warmed by a sour geothermal seep, lay the herbalist's underground garden. [106] It should have frozen like everything else, but ferns brushed my sleeves in the dark, and moonvine opened white flowers toward lamps that hummed with summer heat. [133] I told Maera I needed wages and a room, and she hired me because my hands did not shake around her poisons. [155] What I really needed was proof about her daughter. [164] Lark Sol had vanished at seventeen, and only after the missing returned changed last winter did the valley begin to whisper that Maera was hiding a monster. [191] Lark came back with silver hair, no voice, and a habit of sleeping under the garden beds during the day. [211] My brother Theo had gone looking for her the week she disappeared, carrying his lucky flint arrowhead on a cord around his neck. [234] He never came back, and for seven years I fed on that loss until bitterness was the only thing in me that stayed warm. [258] On my third night below ground, I learned the garden was gravitationally playful in a way no report would ever believe. [279] Water fell upward from the leaves in slow beads, my trowel drifted in lazy circles around my wrist, and Lark laughed without sound as pebbles orbited her ankles like patient moons. [310] Maera only shrugged and said gravity, like grief, loosened its grip where living things learned to share it. [328] She kept an old cordless phone on a crate beside the moonvine, its base unplugged and its handset scarred by teeth marks. [350] When Lark wanted to speak, she pressed that phone to my ear, and her thoughts arrived through static like weather from another county. [373] Via the static-laden confessions on an old cordless phone, she told me she had not been taken or cursed, but invited. [394] Under the creek lived a colony of moths the size of eagles, blind pollinators whose wing dust bent gravity and whose hunger kept the underground flowers alive. [421] Every seven years they chose one human keeper to metamorphose for a single season, so the garden could fruit before the thaw starved it. [445] Lark had gone willingly, but the change had stalled halfway because Theo, trying to rescue her, had torn the flint arrowhead from the colony heart where it anchored the transformation. [475] Without it she could neither return fully human nor finish becoming pollinator, and the garden had begun to die in slow, fragrant inches. [498] Then Lark pressed the phone harder to my ear, and beneath her static came a second voice, older, tired, and unmistakably my brother. [521] Theo confessed that he had not died, but had taken the arrowhead into himself to hold the doorway open until someone less afraid could bring it back. [548] He was the colony heart now, rooted under the creek, and his jokes crackled through the handset even after seven years of stone and dark. [573] He told me the arrowhead was growing out of his chest like a tooth, and that if I laughed when I pulled it free, the pain would go somewhere useful. [603] That was when I understood my real work was not revenge or research, but to turn bitterness into a cosmic joke the universe would have to answer with mercy. [632] At midnight Maera led me through roots to a limestone hollow where Theo stood half man and half stone, breathing frost while moth wings shadowed his shoulders. [659] I put the cordless phone between our hands, told him every bitter year had made me excellent at waiting for a punchline, and pulled. [683] The laugh tore out of me before the pain could, bright and ugly and alive, and the arrowhead came free ringing like a struck bell. [708] I carried it back to the garden and pressed it into Lark's palm, where gravity turned playful one last time and lifted us both an inch off the soil. [737] Then Lark began to metamorphose, silver hair opening into antennae, arms widening into wings dusted with constellations, while flowers, moths, dripping water, and floating tools all found different notes of a harmony untamed. [770] She rose to pollinate the dark, Theo stepped free behind me laughing with a human chest, and above us the ice cracked like thin glass over spring. [797]</story>
