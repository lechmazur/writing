<story>When the giant lay down in the shallow sea and fell asleep, Lian Voss climbed to the roofs to draw whatever still lived. [23] She was a natural history illustrator, and the council paid her to record each reed, heron, and silver fish before the giant woke and drowned them again. [50] Her workplace was the rooftop canal system weaving between towers, a bright river held in wooden channels above streets that flooded with every breath the giant took. [77] She rode a flat skiff from tower to tower, her sketchbook dry in an oilskin, her pencils tied so the rocking would not steal them. [102] On the third morning of the nap, she heard a heron call from a dry chimney where no heron stood. [122] The call came again, exactly as before, yet softer, as if it remembered being a sound rather than being one. [142] Lian drew the empty chimney, then beneath it drew the bird she heard, because her trade had taught her that absence could also be a specimen. [168] That night every bell on the canals answered a note nobody struck, and old Ansel the lockkeeper found her still listening at dawn. [191] He told her the sound was an echo with no throat, and that frightened her more than any drowning. [210] She decided then to find the place where echoes are born, not where they die against stone. [227] Ansel warned her that place lay under the central lock, where the canal passed through the oldest tower and vanished into dark water. [250] He should know, because Ansel sculpts time itself. [258] With wet hands he pulled minutes from the current and pressed them into the lock gates, thickening a second until a boat could pass, thinning another until winter held its breath. [289] It was Ansel who kept the giant asleep, by letting the water fall in a steady cadence from lock to lock, a lullaby the giant trusted. [315] At her throat, Lian wore her mother's infinity symbol pendant, its loop of silver warm even in rain. [333] Her mother, a bell founder, had made it to remind her that every ending borrowed its shape from a beginning. [353] Ansel touched the pendant with two fingers and went pale, because he recognized a tool that could do what his hands could not. [376] The loop, he said, did not make time, but it let the wearer move by borrowing time from herself, minutes taken from age and spent in youth. [402] Lian would grow old faster for every gate she forced, and she would feel the debt at once in her knees and eyes. [425] The giant's nap would last nine days by the harbour count, and already three were gone in gulls and rising foam. [446] If he woke while she was under the lock, the towers would sway, the roofs would break, and the canal would become the sea. [470] She went anyway at dusk, when the locks sang their evening phrase and boats waited for the final bell to close the day. [493] Ansel rowed her himself, his sculpted minutes glittering on the oars like frost that would not melt. [510] At the central lock, the expected last note failed to fall. [521] Ansel had taught the water a cadence that always ended home, but Lian struck the bell early and left the phrase hanging, unresolved, between one breath and the next. [550] In that interrupted cadence, the lock forgot whether it was open or closed, and the gate stood ajar on water that had stopped deciding to flow. [576] Lian slipped through by borrowing time, twelve minutes pulled from the woman she would be at sixty, and felt them leave her like heat from a cupped candle. [604] Below, she found a stone chamber no wider than her skiff, and one white thread now shone in her hair. [624] In a cage of bell bronze sat the first echo, a small grey bird with a throat full of unborn sounds. [645] Ansel, who had followed in silence, said every note in the city first passed through her beak. [662] The cage forced her to repeat each ending perfectly, so the waters kept their cadence and the giant kept sleeping. [682] He had caged her after a waking giant took his wife, and his grief had mistaken cruelty for safety. [701] Lian understood him, but a lullaby built on a prisoner, she said, was only drowning by another name. [719] She pressed her pendant to the cage and chose to uncage her by borrowing time, spending a year of old age to hold the lock in its interrupted pause. [748] The bird rose free, sang a beginning where the ending should have been, and across the rest of the nap the giant slept on, unafraid. [773]</story>
