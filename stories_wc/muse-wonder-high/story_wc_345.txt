On Vesper, an orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches, the trees grew sideways toward a green nebula. [18] The nebula hung over every valley like a slow tide of light, and the settlers called it the nebula of respite because its glow dulled the storms that had chased them from their old homes. [53] They had arrived as strangers from eleven ships, with eleven calendars and eleven ways to grieve. [69] Five harvests later, after strangers become family, they shared one long table beneath the arches and argued like siblings about salt, grafts, and rain. [93] Mara kept the butterfly garden at the center of the settlement because its pollinators served every orchard row. [111] Her only tool from Earth was a butterfly garden watering bell, a copper bell whose clapper released fine rain when she rang it over dry leaves. [137] That spring the rain stopped coming through the arches, and the wells began to taste of stone. [154] The upper orchards wilted first, then the nursery rows where the children had planted trees named for the dead. [173] Everyone blamed a different ship, a different custom, a different hand on the sluice gates. [188] Then the chalk began to appear. [194] Mara found the first words at dawn on the deserted sidewalk outside the mill, written in a tall and careful hand. [215] The message said that the river had not vanished, but had been led astray beneath the roots. [232] For three mornings the settlement woke to guidance found through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn, each one signed with a small drawing of a plum. [259] Old Tomas said only one person had ever drawn plums that way, and his voice shook when he named his husband Elian, dead twenty years before landfall. [286] Elian returned as an altruistic ghost who could move chalk and mist but could not lift a bucket or turn a wheel. [308] That limit explained the messages. [313] He needed living hands. [317] He also needed them to reexamine the story they told about the drought before pride made the story true. [336] At first Mara did not trust the chalk, because grief can make any sign look like a face. [354] Yet Elian proved strangely trustworthy by telling them only what they could test with shovels, mirrors, and patience. [372] His motive was never to rule or frighten them, but to orchestrate collective effort before the young trees passed the point where water could save them. [398] One message sent the net makers to mend shade cloth for the nursery. [411] Another sent the stone crew to clear the fallen arch that had pinned the old canal shut. [428] A third asked the children to carry mirrors up the floating arches, where the rock drifted slow as boats and rang faintly in high wind. [453] The mirrors would show where hidden water flashed under the roots, if anyone below knew how to look. [471] Mara rang her watering bell over the butterfly garden each noon, keeping the pollinators alive on sips while the orchards waited. [492] The sound carried farther than it should, and Tomas swore the mist inside the bell sometimes bent toward the chalk lines, as if Elian were listening. [518] On the fifth dawn the chalk asked Mara to dig beneath the oldest plum tree, the one Elian had planted from a seed in his coat pocket. [545] She refused until Tomas took her hand and reminded her that family was not blood alone, but the people who stayed to do hard work beside you. [572] At noon they dug together, cooks and pilots and net makers in one line, passing soil back hand to hand while butterflies rose in a bright cloud. [599] Three feet down they found a root as thick as a door, grown through the canal gate and holding it closed against the buried river. [624] The root belonged to the oldest tree, so cutting it would free the water and likely kill the living monument to Elian. [646] That night no chalk came, and the silence felt like a question Elian wanted them to answer without him. [665] Mara climbed alone to the garden and rang the bell until its small rain wet her sleeves and the dark leaves shone. [687] In the mist she saw him at last, thin as breath on glass, holding no chalk and asking for nothing. [707] She understood then that a ghost who loved them would not choose for them, and that trust meant returning choice to the living. [730] At dawn the family gathered and chose to graft the oldest tree onto its children before they cut the root, so its line would continue in twenty young trunks. [759] Water returned by evening, butterflies settled on wet blossoms, and in that brambled hope they finally drank together. [777]
