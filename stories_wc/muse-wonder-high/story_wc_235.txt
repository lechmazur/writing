Mara Voss had worked alone in the mountain ranger cabin for three winters, and she had stopped trusting calendars long before she found her own grave. [26]
It was on the north slope in October, a small granite marker half sunk in pine needles, carved with her full name and a death date eleven days ahead. [55]
She photographed it, reported it, and told dispatch it was a prank left by hikers, because that was the only version she could say out loud without shaking. [83]
That night she locked the cabin door, barred the shutters against the wind, and waited for the date to prove the stone wrong. [106]
She was, by training and by stubborn habit, a lucid dream chronicler, a person who woke inside dreams and wrote them down before they could lie about what they had shown. [137]
She had filled eleven notebooks that way, each entry dated, timed, and cross referenced, because her one real motivation was to distill chaos into narrative before it distilled her. [166]
After the grave, the dreams stopped obeying the notebooks. [175]
She dreamed of herself at nine, barefoot on the cabin roof, then of herself at seventy, mending the same roof with hands spotted and sure, then of herself buried under the north slope while snow filled her mouth. [213]
She woke each time chronologically confused, unsure whether she had remembered something, predicted something, or stolen something from a life she had not lived yet. [238]
The paper notebooks made it worse, because ink froze, pages stuck together, and her orderly dates began to look like an argument she was losing. [263]
So she peeled a long sheet from the fallen birch behind the woodpile and made a birch bark scroll, as rangers had once done when they needed records that frost could not ruin. [296]
On the pale bark she did not write dates at all, only ages. [309]
Nine, twenty three, forty one, seventy, dead. [316]
It should have helped, but the scroll only showed her how many selves were competing to be the real one. [336]
What rose in her then was not grief, exactly, but feral nostalgia, a wild and unsentimental hunger for lives that were hers and yet out of reach. [363]
It had teeth. [366]
It wanted the nine year old who feared nothing, the twenty three year old who had quit medical school without apology, and the old woman she might never become if the stone was right. [400]
Reading the scroll before sleep became her method, and getting jealous of past selves was the spark that reliably made her lucid. [422]
Envy was sharper than any reality check she had taught herself, sharper than counting fingers or reading clocks that melted. [442]
If she entered a dream and saw herself younger, braver, and unburdened, the sting of wanting that life back woke her mind while her body stayed asleep. [469]
On the tenth night after the grave, she dreamed of the cabin in summer, though outside her real window the pass was already white. [493]
Dream Mara at twenty three sat on the porch peeling birch bark, laughing at something the older Mara had forgotten, with two good knees and a future that still looked negotiable. [524]
Jealousy hit like cold air, and Mara became lucid with a familiar, jaded wonder, marveling that her mind could build such beauty just to withhold it from her. [552]
Instead of arguing, as she usually did, she asked the younger woman what she had done with all that unspent hope. [573]
The younger Mara grinned, pointed at the roof, and said the view was better when you stopped waiting for permission. [593]
So Mara climbed, though dream roofs had betrayed her before, and at the top she spread her arms and let the summer wind take her. [618]
She began to soar over the valley, not gracefully, but with the stunned effort of someone who had forgotten that falling was optional. [641]
Below her, the mountain unfolded in every season at once, green meadows beside avalanche chutes, autumn aspens burning next to spring runoff, and in the middle of it the small dark rectangle of her grave. [676]
From above, it did not look like an ending, only like one sentence in a paragraph she had been trying to read backward. [699]
She understood then that the stone might be a warning, a mistake, a dream leaked into daylight, or a future she could still revise by living deliberately. [726]
When she woke, dawn was testing the cabin windows and the death date was tomorrow. [741]
Instead she unrolled the birch bark scroll, added the flight beneath every age, and wrote the first account that held all her selves without ranking them. [767]
