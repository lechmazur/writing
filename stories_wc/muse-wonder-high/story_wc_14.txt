After the town goes silent, Ines remained on the mountaintop with the shop key cold in her pocket. [18]
The last bus had taken the schoolchildren, the bakers, and even the mayor down the switchbacks before the winter road closed. [39]
Their absence left a clean, terrible quiet in which she could hear her own footsteps change from stone to snow. [59]
She stayed because no one else knew how to tend the blooming labyrinth of bonsai forests on a mountaintop behind her shop. [81]
As the gilded-leaf stationer, she had sold wedding invitations and ledger books for twelve years, pressing a gold leaf onto every cover by hand. [105]
Now there were no weddings to announce and no accounts to settle, only rows of tiny pines, cherries, and maples flowering out of season in their shallow trays. [133]
Her predecessor, old Tomaso, had planted them as a maze that a child could walk and a lifetime could study. [153]
Each tree carried a paper tag, but the shed fire last spring had curled most tags to ash. [171]
Without the tags, the nurseries in the valley would not buy the trees in April, and the labyrinth would die unfunded on its trays. [195]
So Ines set herself a task, earnestly studious as she had been at school, to rebuild the catalogue before the road reopened. [217]
She worked by charting the faint hum in a fluorescent lamp at dawn, a habit Tomaso had taught her for reasons she had only half believed. [243]
The lamp hung over his potting bench, and its pitch rose when frost stiffened the wires and fell when meltwater eased them. [265]
In her battered notepad she drew a line for each morning, high for a tight bright note, low for a tired buzz. [287]
Below each line she rubbed a leaf, pressed a flake of bark, or taped a grain of gritty soil. [306]
She told herself she did it to collect the textures of different times, because a sixty-year-old pine held more years in one bent branch than the silent town now held people. [337]
That was the truth, but it was not the only one. [348]
She also wanted proof that her days alone had weight. [358]
The problem was that texture cannot tell a buyer where a tree was grafted, or whose hands first trained its trunk. [379]
For that she needed dates, names, and a chain of care, and those had burned. [394]
On the ninth morning, the lamp hummed lower than she had ever marked it. [408]
Snow had loaded the shed roof in the night, and melt would come fast under a clear sky. [426]
If she opened the glass panels too early, cold would pour into the labyrinth; if too late, trapped sun would scorch the blossoms. [449]
Her chart told her to wait until the hum steadied, then tilt the western panes so they would refract the first light along the wet paths instead of onto the flowers. [480]
She waited with her hands folded, though every minute of light felt like a coin she was spending blind. [499]
When the note steadied, she opened the vents, tilted the panes, and watched the sunrise break, with incandescent restraint, into ribbons across bark and snow. [524]
It worked, and the relief made her braver than honesty usually allowed. [536]
That afternoon she began the catalogue, writing for each tree the history its texture suggested. [551]
For a cherry with a scar like a closed mouth, she wrote that a widow had shaped it while learning not to speak of grief too soon. [578]
For a maple whose roots gripped a white stone, she wrote that a mason had planted it the year he finally built a house he could not afford. [606]
These invented histories pleased her because they fit the grain, yet at night they began to shame her. [624]
A catalogue should not ask strangers to mistake longing for fact. [635]
On the tenth dawn she charted the hum again, found it steady and high, and understood what Tomaso had really been teaching her. [658]
The chart did not give her the past; it taught her to notice the exact moment when observation ended. [677]
So she rewrote the catalogue in two columns, keeping what she could prove on the left and what she could only imagine on the right. [702]
She signed each imagined entry with a gilded leaf and the words, imagined from texture, so no buyer could mistake her restraint for certainty. [726]
When the road reopened, the nursery woman read both columns in silence, then bought the trees for the honesty of the second as much as the care of the first. [756]
Ines kept the battered notepad, its hum lines rising toward spring, because she had learned that a history can be invented without being false if it admits where the light bends. [787]
