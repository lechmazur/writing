<story>Mira could not sleep, and after three hundred years that fact had become the shape of her life. [18] She was the insomniac poet of the gargantuan shell, keeper of its tide ledgers and its midnight laments. [36] The shell had once housed a sea beast so vast that harbors had formed in the curve of its ribs, and now it housed the last orchard peoples of the drowned coast. [68] They lived in its chambers like hermits in borrowed light, listening to the reef sigh through its walls at night. [88] Mira wrote because sleeping would have meant trusting the dark, and she had never learned how to do that without a witness. [110] On the first night of every spring, the two orchards inside the shell went to war by blooming. [128] The eastern trees opened white plum flowers that made men remember every debt, while the western cherries opened pink and made women forget every mercy. [153] Pollen met in the central chamber and turned to bright ash, and families who had shared bread in winter barred their doors against each other. [178] Across centuries of longing, no treaty had held longer than a single season. [191] Lovers separated by the orchards pined through walls, generals grew old sharpening pruning knives, and children learned the names of flowers as insults. [214] Mira wanted to photosynthesize pain into beauty, not as a metaphor but as stubborn daily labor. [230] She took griefs left at her door and set them in sunlit lines until they fed something green in the reader. [251] If a widow brought her a name she could no longer say aloud, Mira made it rhyme with rain and returned it singable. [274] Her only possession from beyond the shell was a galaxy postcard, sent by the cartographer she had loved when both were young and seemingly mortal. [299] Its front showed spiral stars above a blue world neither of them would ever reach, and its back held nine words in faded ink. [323] Wait for me where the tide learns to bloom. [332] The cartographer had died in the second century of waiting, but Mira still read the card each night before writing, because hope embarrassed her less in private. [359] She was shy about that hope, and shy about her poems, yet she was shyly unstoppable once she set her teeth into a sorrow. [383] This spring both orchards bloomed early and together, a disaster no elder could remember, and the central chamber filled with ash before dawn. [406] Men shouted debts in the plum groves while women in the cherry shade threw stones instead of the mercy they had forgotten. [428] Mira understood then that another written treaty would fail, because paper could not hold a scent that rewrote the blood. [448] So she decided to ensconce herself between the orchards, in the narrow throat of shell where all pollen passed and all winds argued. [471] She carried only a stool, a kettle, the galaxy postcard, and three centuries of unsent poems tied with gut string. [491] There she sat through the first perfumed storm, breathing ash, weeping without shame, and turning each sting into a line under her breath. [514] At dusk she began to calm both sides via half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind that slipped through the shell throat and spoiled every war cry into a yawn. [544] She did not sing well, which became the mercy of the thing, because soldiers who laughed at a cracked note forgot to keep perfect hatred. [569] The lullabies were her poems set loose, verses where plum debt and cherry mercy learned to rhyme without either one surrendering its color. [592] For nine nights she stayed, sleepless as ever, tending the kettle, reading the postcard, and singing whenever the pollen rose bright enough to kill. [616] On the tenth morning the orchards did something unheard of, opening their blossoms toward each other instead of upward, as if listening had become a form of light. [644] Children crossed first, carrying bread, then widows, then generals who hid their pruning knives behind their backs like embarrassed boys. [664] In the throat of the shell they signed nothing, but they planted a third row between the groves, grafted from plum and cherry, and swore to tend it before tending any grievance. [696] That planting became the blossom armistice, renewed not by rulers but by anyone who watered the shared row before their own trees. [718] Mira finally slept that afternoon in reef-shadow joy, curled beneath the postcard and the new leaves, while the off-key wind hummed her lullaby back to her, badly and tenderly, from both orchards at once. [752]</story>
