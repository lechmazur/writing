While waiting for the arbiter who would decide whether her brother lived as a hostage or died as a traitor, Sera Vane practiced philosophy with her sword. [27]
She was a dueling philosopher with a mechanical arm, and the arm, built from clock brass and mourning steel, clicked softly whenever she reached a conclusion. [53]
Her opponent that morning was Ambassador Lorn, a diplomatically fierce old man who believed every concession should be sharpened before it was offered. [76]
They met, as protocol demanded, in the glass atrium beside a quiet brook, where treaties were signed under lemon trees and duels were witnessed by herons. [102]
The arbiter was two hours late, and neither envoy could leave without surrendering the negotiation by absence. [119]
So they decided to fence, not to wound, but to argue in the only grammar both courts trusted. [137]
In Sera's school, a thrust answered a premise, a parry exposed a fallacy, and retreat meant a question had been asked too honestly. [160]
Lorn removed his coat, folded it with terrible precision, and asked whether mercy could ever be a strategy rather than a surrender. [182]
Sera raised her blade and said mercy was strategy when it denied an enemy the future he had prepared for. [202]
Their foils met, withdrew, and met again, and each bright contact rang under the glass like a note struck and immediately forgiven. [224]
Inside, clerks continued to arrange chairs, ink, and sealed maps, maintaining dignified chaos while pretending the duel was only exercise. [244]
At the center of the atrium stood the gift from the northern delegation, a half-painted sundial whose gold leaf stopped abruptly at noon. [267]
Its unfinished face was considered an insult until Sera noticed that its painted shadows did not match the sun. [286]
During a pause for water, she knelt beside it, her mechanical fingers hovering over numerals cut at angles too deliberate to be decoration. [309]
Lorn, breathing hard, watched her and said that philosophers were most dangerous when they stopped talking and started measuring. [328]
Sera smiled because he had finally asked the right question, though he had disguised it, as usual, as a warning. [348]
Her whole life had been driven by a refusal to let inquiry end in admiration, a hunger to turn questions into destinations. [370]
A question, she believed, should give a person coordinates, provisions, and a reason to leave the room where it was born. [391]
She therefore read the message aloud, not by its hours, but via the coded angles in a half-painted sundial that only a duelist would trust. [416]
Each angle pointed from a numeral to a flaw in the glass, and each flaw, seen correctly, became a letter. [436]
Together the letters named the north orchard, the drowned ferry, and a third place neither delegation had admitted existed on any map. [458]
Lorn went very still, because the third place was the hidden prison where Sera's brother was being moved that night without record. [480]
It was also, he admitted after a silence, where his own son had been taken for refusing to burn a village. [501]
The sundial had not been an insult or a cipher for invasion, but a hidden overture from prisoners and guards who wanted the war negotiated by people willing to find them. [532]

Sera understood that the card was not proof alone but punctuation, the point where private grief became joint action. [598]
