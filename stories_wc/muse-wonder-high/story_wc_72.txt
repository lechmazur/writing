<story>After revealing the secret, Ines floated in silence above the drowned gate. [12] A minute earlier, the living statue in the forgotten underwater city garden had opened her stone eyes and spoken directly into Ines's mind. [35] Now the certainty that had carried Ines down here cracked wider than the gate itself. [50] She had come to seal the garden forever. [58] Above, her town drew drinking water from the reservoir, and engineers swore the old walls would fail without warning. [77] Ines, their youngest diver, believed them completely, because certainty was simpler than grief. [90] Her mother had died when the lower sluice collapsed, and Ines blamed the beautiful, patient ruins below. [107] The statue raised one barnacled hand, not in threat, but in greeting. [119] "You came to bury us," the statue thought into her, "yet you are shaking." [133] Ines pressed her palm to her helmet, though the voice had no sound to block. [148] It arrived with impressions of warm soil, rain on leaves, and hands that had once tended roses. [165] "My name is Sella," she thought, "and I have kept this garden since the water took the streets." [183] Around them, stone benches wore coats of algae, and pale fish moved like thoughts through drowned rose arches. [201] The lifeless city did not feel lifeless here, because roots breathed, shells chimed, and even broken columns seemed to lean toward the light. [224] Ines lifted the sealed charge she had carried from the surface. [235] "If I seal the gate, the town stays safe," she said into her radio, but the radio gave only static. [255] Sella answered without cruelty, cutting straight to the fear beneath the sentence. [267] "Safe from what, child, the water, or the memory of losing her?" [279] The question opened something Ines had kept shut for ten years. [290] Then Sella turned her head, slowly, toward a foxglove-stained glass ampoule resting in a niche beside the fountain. [308] Purple had bloomed through the glass where foxglove extract once darkened it, and something small glowed inside. [325] Ines knew that ampoule from her mother's drawings, though she had believed it was only a family story. [343] "Your mother left it here," Sella thought, "on the last day anyone from above walked these paths dry." [361] Inside it, curled like a sleeping ember, lay the seed heart of the garden's first foxglove. [377] That was the second revelation, and it undid the first simple story Ines had told herself about poison. [395] Foxglove here was not a wild toxin leaking upward, but a cultivated filter, its roots drawing bitterness from the reservoir. [415] "We drink what would sicken you," Sella said in her mind, "and we bloom so you do not." [433] Ines stared at the charge, suddenly unable to call the garden either enemy or ruin. [448] A tremor moved through the gate then, and silt poured from a thin crack shaped like a lightning root. [467] The engineers above called such cracks structural weaknesses and taught Ines to fear every one she found. [484] Sella, incisively kind even now, did not soothe her with a lie. [496] "That crack will widen," she thought, "and my hands are too stiff to mend it alone." [512] "But it is also the only path left for the roots to reach the gate." [527] She was not asking Ines to trade one certainty for another, but to welcome nuance into a certainty that had left no room for life. [552] Ines understood then what her hands, smaller and warmer than stone, were meant to do. [567] She opened the ampoule, and the seed heart unfolded a fine root that pulsed against her glove like a tiny, patient heart. [589] With Sella guiding her thoughts, Ines began to thread the living foxglove root, fine as green twine, through the structural weakness in the gate. [613] The root resisted, curled back, then slid through darkness and found wet stone on the other side. [630] As it tightened, it drew soil, shell, and seed together across the crack, stitching the gate instead of sealing it. [650] Then the contact brought a final revelation, carried root to hand to mind in one clear flood. [667] Ines saw her mother, young and laughing, threading the same roots while Sella watched over her, already stone, already kind. [687] Her mother had not died because the garden failed, but because she stayed to close a broken sluice and save both city and town. [711] Grief did not vanish, but it made room for gratitude beside it. [723] Ines disarmed the charge and let it sink harmlessly into the silt. [735] "Now tell them," Sella thought, as foxgloves opened along the crack like purple bells ringing underwater. [751] Ines rose toward the surface carrying not an answer, but a living question.
