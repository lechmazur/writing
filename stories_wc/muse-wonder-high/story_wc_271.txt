Ten years after the last lighthouse keeper locked the tower and left it to its automated lamp, Lina Haddad still climbed the cliff path before dawn. [26] The arboretum perched on the seaside cliff above the harbor opened later, but she liked arriving when the gates were still chained and the pines were black against the water. [56] She carried her keys, a thermos, and in her coat pocket a crumpled photograph that her thumb found without thinking. [76] In the photograph she wore a white dress and laughed with rice in her hair, and her husband Omar stood beside her with both hands raised in surrender to the joke. [107] Ninety seconds later, a drone strike had torn the wedding tent apart and taken Omar and eleven guests, and Lina had walked out of the smoke with no visible wound. [137] People called that luck for a while, until they noticed she never stayed in a room with a ceiling fan turning fast. [159] She was not good at parties or explanations, but she was quietly talented with living wood, able to coax a graft to take where other gardeners had failed three times. [189] The council kept her on because the old cliff pines trusted her hands, and because she never promised more than roots could honestly do. [213] That morning the wind smelled of salt and cut grass, and the survey letter in her other pocket felt heavier than her tools. [236] At noon an automated survey crew would fly drones over the arboretum to map the erosion and decide whether the town could afford to keep it open. [263] Lina told herself the machines were only cameras and motors, and she believed it while her feet kept moving between the beds. [285] She checked the new apricot grafts, tightened a loose tie against the cliff wind, and noted which seedlings would die if the summer stayed dry. [310] She did not write that all would thrive, because her job, as she understood it, was to anchor optimism in realism and give each plant a chance that matched its strength. [341] When the first drone lifted from the harbor road with a thin rising whine, Lina froze with her shears half open around a stem. [365] The stem trembled, not from wind, but from the small shake that had moved into her hands. [382] Her shoulders rose toward her ears, her jaw ached, and the photograph in her pocket seemed to press through cloth into her thigh. [405] For three years a therapist in the town clinic had taught her to read those signals not as prophecies, but through somatic markers, the body keeping score before the mind found words. [437] Cold fingers meant memory was speaking, she had learned, while a steady belly and loosened jaw meant the present was safe enough to enter. [461] The distinction had sounded false at first, like comfort invented for paperwork, until she practiced it among trees that never hurried her. [483] Now, under the drone, she named each marker aloud in a voice so low only the apricot leaves heard her. [503] 'Jaw tight,' she whispered, 'hands cold, breath high, and still I am here on this path, not under that tent.' [523] The drone passed, mapping green rows for a report, and no fire followed its shadow across the gravel. [541] Lina let out the breath she had been holding and felt the freeze loosen first in her fingers, then in her knees. [563] She cut the stem cleanly, angry not at her body but at how long the old alarm had borrowed her present. [584] At noon the crew chief, a young woman named Priya, found her by the pines and asked whether the north slope could hold another winter. [609] Lina wanted to say yes and make the answer a shelter, but she looked at the exposed roots and the honest angle of the cliff. [634] 'Part of it will fall,' she said, 'and part will hold if we move the path, plant deep grasses, and stop pretending the sea will negotiate.' [660] Priya wrote that down without flinching, and Lina saw that realism did not kill hope but gave it a place to stand. [682] They walked the rows together while the drones made their patient lines overhead, and each time the whine rose, Lina paused, checked jaw and hands and belly, and chose the next true step. [715] By evening she could feel it inside the noise, not as numbness and not as forgetting, but as a pearl of stillness formed grain by grain around the wound. [744] She took out the crumpled photograph, smoothed it once against the lighthouse rail, and put it back not as a hiding place but as a record of love that had been real. [776]
