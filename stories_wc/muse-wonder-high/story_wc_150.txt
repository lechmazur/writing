Mara Ellis returned to the island of Kethra after twenty-two years in Toronto with her mother's ashes in a tin box and no prayer she still believed. [27]
Her family had fled the drought when she was nine, promising to come back for one summer, then another, until return became a story told at other people's tables. [56]
The village had kept her uncle's orchard above the old catacombs, where fig and olive roots snaked between limestone bones and broken burial jars. [80]
She arrived while archaeologists were excavating a tomb found by a collapsed irrigation ditch, its shaft cut straight through the orchard's shaded heart. [103]
Mara had not come for the dig, but she stayed by the ropes each morning because the work gave her exile a shape she could watch. [129]
On the first day, the crew lifted a sundial base from the ditch mud, a square of white stone scored with hours and lacking its bronze tongue. [156]
Her uncle Tomas shrugged and said farmers had used it as a step for generations, which was why its carved letters were worn almost smooth. [181]
Mara knelt despite the wet earth and traced the groove where noon had once fallen, feeling foolish and unexpectedly near tears. [202]
She was warmly curious in the way her mother had taught her, asking each worker his name before she asked what his hands had found. [227]
That habit loosened tongues faster than authority, and by evening the foreman, old Petros, let her wash pottery inside the fence. [248]
He warned her not to wander after dark because roots lifted stones, bones shifted in rain, and not every hollow below wanted a visitor. [272]
Mara listened, but in daylight she began a purposeful wandering between the rows, mapping fallen markers and asking trees, in effect, where their roots had pushed the past. [300]
Each walk followed the excavation downward, bucket by bucket, as Petros cleared a stair that stopped twice where ancient diggers had also stopped and turned back. [326]
On the second day they found a child's sandal sealed under a root, its owner hurried onward without it almost two thousand years before Mara hurried away. [353]
On the third day they found three unopened jars set for a voyage, their wine turned to black dust before anyone could drink to a safe arrival. [380]
Petros said the tomb belonged to a family of sailors who had buried their dead facing home because none of them had reached it alive. [405]
That night Mara opened the tin box, tried the childhood prayer for travelers, and stopped at the second line because belief felt like a country she had let lapse. [434]
She told Petros the next morning that she had not returned to bury her mother so much as to learn whether anything here could still hold them both. [462]
He did not offer comfort, but he set the sundial base upright in the shade and said broken things often only needed their missing work restored. [488]
So Mara began to rebuild it during the slow hours when the tomb stair was braced and no one could descend. [509]
Through purposeful wandering she gathered its scattered chips from root tangles, field walls, and the church step, asking permission each time and carrying each piece home in her skirt. [538]
The orchard seemed to approve cautiously, offering a bronze shard under figs one morning and hiding it again whenever she reached with greed instead of patience. [564]
Tomas shaped that shard into a new gnomon while Mara reset the base on level earth, aligning its noon groove with the tomb stair descending below. [590]
On the final day, Petros opened the chamber and found six sailors laid toward the sea, each with an unfinished letter weighted beneath one hand. [615]
No voice spoke, no root uncurled, yet sunlight entered the shaft at noon and struck the rebuilt sundial exactly as Mara read the first letter aloud. [641]
It asked forgiveness from a daughter left on another shore and ended mid-word, as if distance itself had taken the pen. [662]
Mara answered it softly, not because she believed the dead could hear, but because the living beside her plainly could and needed the sentence finished. [687]
Then she scattered her mother's ashes among the fig roots, where bones and blossom already kept company, and prayed the whole prayer without forcing its end. [713]
Faith returned not as certainty that journeys end well, but as trust that interrupted ones can be taken up gently by those who come after. [738]
