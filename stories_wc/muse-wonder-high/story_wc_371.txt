<story>After the birds fly south, the palace courtyard went quiet enough for Liora to hear the fountain thinking about stopping. [20] She came at dusk with a canvas bag, a trowel, and the last bread from the vanished bakery on Crane Street. [41] The bakery had not burned or closed, but had simply been absent one morning, its windows clean, its ovens cold, and its sign already fading from memory. [68] People accepted that absence within a week, which was why Liora trusted herself and distrusted everyone else. [85] She was a creeping normality noticer, a woman who kept a notebook of the exact days when terrible or wonderful things stopped seeming strange. [109] She had written down when soldiers stopped saluting the empty gate, when clerks stopped lowering their voices, and when she stopped flinching at her own signature. [135] That signature, three winters earlier, had closed the bakery and condemned every unregistered tea room in the district. [153] She had told herself the order was only paper, and paper, repeated daily, had become her normal. [170] Now she wanted to find the sacred in the ordinary, not in pardons from priests or proclamations from the new council, but in warm cups, clean soil, and work done correctly when nobody watched. [204] The overgrown palace courtyard gave her a place to begin because nobody guarded beauty that had been officially forgotten. [223] Briars hid the paths, ivy swallowed the statues, and bindweed had pulled a green curtain across the eastern arcade. [242] Behind that curtain, according to the old plans she had once filed, stood a secret tea house that later maps had learned to omit. [266] She did not hack at the ivy, because she had become heuristically gentle, using the rough rule that any living thing deserved the slower tool first. [292] So she began through guerrilla gardening, slipping in after curfew to loosen soil, plant beans and marigolds, and free one stone at a time from the roots. [319] Each night the garden argued back by growing, and each morning her notebook recorded some small refusal to let ruin become normal. [341] She planted thyme where clerks had once queued for permits, because its scent made waiting people kinder in her memory. [361] She set calendula along the fountain rim to call bees back before winter taught the courtyard another silence. [379] On the ninth night, her trowel struck painted wood where her plan had marked only a storage wall. [397] She cleared the last roots by hand and felt the ivy give like a held breath released. [414] Her gardening had begun to uncloak a low door, its blue paint intact, its brass handle polished by recent use. [434] Liora knocked once, then hated herself for knocking as if she still carried authority. [448] The door opened on Tomas, the baker, older and thinner, holding a teapot as if it were a lamp. [467] He looked at the rescued path, the beans climbing twine, and the bread in her bag, and he understood all three before she spoke. [491] She said his bakery name aloud, then her crime in plain words, without asking the sentence to make her sound smaller or larger than she was. [517] Tomas did not forgive her, but he poured tea because the cup was there and her hands were shaking. [536] Inside, the tea house kept its old order in miniature, with swept boards, mended cushions, and a shelf of cups waiting for people who officially no longer gathered. [564] He broke the dry, preserved loaf she had hoarded since the closure, softened a piece in tea, and set it before her like a question. [589] Eating did not absolve her, yet chewing slowly beside the man she had harmed made absolution seem tenuous rather than impossible, a seed rather than a verdict. [616] He told her the door would open again if she kept the garden alive and never again mistook quiet for innocence. [637] She accepted without promising perfection, which felt more honest than the clean forms she used to sign. [654] In the following weeks she widened the beds, taught two former clerks to prune gently, and left sprigs of thyme on doorsteps where fear had settled. [680] Word traveled the way steam travels, silently and by contact, until neighbors found the path her plants had cleared. [699] They came singly after dark, not as a crowd that could be named and banned, but as thirsty people carrying empty cups. [721] Tomas served them ordinary tea and ordinary bread from a starter he had hidden in a flowerpot, and each plain mouthful felt briefly sacred. [745] Liora still kept her notebook, but now she recorded the first evening when kindness, practiced carefully and without permission, began to seem normal again. [769]</story>
