<story>In the years after dreams become shareable, Mara Venn kept the blooming library above clouds. [15] She was its sharp-eyed dusk noticer, hired to watch the light fail and tell true dusk from a dream leaking into day. [37] Every evening she stood at the west glass, softly deliberate in every movement, while vines opened white flowers between the shelves. [58] Patrons arrived by lift and ladder to trade the dreams they had recorded in sleep. [73] No one spoke above a whisper, because shared dreams remained fragile for an hour after waking. [89] Mara shelved them in glass jars, each labeled with a name, a date, and the weather inside the dream. [108] Her favorite was an old river stone that held down the most dangerous labels when the high wind shook the tower. [129] Her brother Jonah had found it before the river below the clouds dried and before his dreams stopped arriving. [148] Thirty years earlier, Jonah had worked the same post and filmed the reading room each dusk for the archive. [167] His silent reels showed readers bending over books, lamps warming, and the long shadows retreating in good order toward the walls. [188] Mara watched those reels because Jonah had vanished inside a shared dream and left only his coat, still warm, on a chair. [210] Officially, the dream had been an empty meadow. [218] Unofficially, everyone who entered it woke weeping and described a figure standing far away, waving as if from a train. [238] Mara did not believe Jonah was dead. [245] She believed something in him had been misfiled, the way dreams were misfiled when dreamers woke too fast. [263] That spring, the library began to bloom out of season. [273] Flowers pushed from catalog drawers, and roots stitched the stair rails so no one could run, even in panic. [292] At dusk the petals glowed, and patrons reading shared dreams found strange sentences appearing in their own handwriting. [310] The sentences were always small and kind. [317] One promised rain to a thirsty sister. [324] Another begged a mother not to sell a blue bicycle. [334] Mara copied each sentence into Jonah's empty jar and waited for his name to form. [349] Then, on the longest evening of the year, she noticed that a shadow arrived alone at dusk a minute before she did. [371] It slipped through the west glass, crossed the reading room, and shelved a jar she had not yet taken from a patron. [393] The jar contained Jonah's handwriting, though the patron was a child born twenty years after he vanished. [410] Mara understood the truth by comparing silent video footage from decades apart, Jonah's reels against the new recordings the library made each night. [433] In the old footage, every reader's shadow left when the reader left. [445] In the new footage, one extra shadow remained after closing, watering flowers, turning pages, and slowly learning the shape of hands. [466] It was a shadow unbound, slipped loose when Jonah crossed into the meadow and his waking self never returned. [485] Over decades of borrowed light and other people's kind sentences, it had begun to metamorphose. [500] Not into Jonah exactly, but into the keeper his hope would have made of him. [515] Mara knew then why the library bloomed. [522] The shadow, softly deliberate as Jonah had been in life, was feeding each shared dream a little pollen so hope would not collapse into mere memory. [548] When Mara tried to call it by his name, it stopped with one dark hand above the river stone. [567] The stone had been Jonah's anchor in the old reels, always beside his chair to mark where a body should return. [588] Mara placed her palm on the stone and felt it was warm, not from lamps, but from being held in remembered hands. [610] She could have spoken the recall word then, the word every noticer learned to drag strays back into their bodies. [630] It would have ended the blooming and given her a brother shaped from shadow, grateful and incomplete. [647] Instead, Mara opened Jonah's jar and set it beside the stone, because her work was to preserve the shape of hope, not to own it. [672] The shadow touched the jar, and every flower in the library turned toward it at once. [688] For one luminous minute, Jonah stood there whole, older than his absence should have allowed, smiling at the sister who had kept his evenings. [712] Then he metamorphosed again, not vanishing, but loosening into petals, page light, and the kind sentences strangers would carry home. [732] Mara wept because she recognized the choice as his, and because no rescue could give her back the ordinary years they had lost. [755] At true dusk she logged the hour, set the river stone on his empty jar, and left the west glass open for whatever hope came next. [781]</story>
