<story>For seven nights after the king fled and before the conqueror entered, Babylon belonged to no one but the stars. [20] In that suspended week between regimes, the palace kept its lamps low and its doors unbarred, waiting to learn which god would be permitted next. [45] It was an ancient Babylonian palace reimagined by its own servants, its throne room cleared of gold and filled with clay tablets, reed pens, and bowls of water for watching reflections of the sky. [79] There, in the dusky harmony of blue hour, when brick turned violet and the last swallows cut black seams across the light, worked Kidin, the frugal astronomer. [106] He owned one cloak, one cup, and one knife, and he mended each rather than accept the palace stores, because hunger had taught him that waste was a slower kind of theft. [138] Yet every night he spent the one coin he never counted as his own, a copper disk laid aside for oil, so the old prayer lamp would not die before dawn. [169] No priest remained to bless that lamp, and no edict protected it, so Kidin hid his encrypted devotion inside the only language soldiers ignored, his star tables. [196] Each observation was a prayer with its name removed, each column of rising times a hymn folded into numbers, addressed to the Unknown who had never answered and never quite departed. [227] He called this caution, not faith, which was empirically ironic, for his charts were more regular than any temple rite and his unbelief kept stricter hours than belief ever had. [257] On the seventh night the wind failed, the water bowls stilled, and Venus stood so bright above the Ishtar Gate that its light fell through the smoke hole onto the floor. [288] It struck a loose paving stone that Kidin had marked months earlier, not for treasure, but because its draft kept lifting the edge of his mat. [314] He pried it up with his knife, expecting a rat hole or a forgotten drain, and found instead a cedar box wrapped in bitumen cloth, breathing out cold air and myrrh. [345] Inside lay a magic square tile no larger than his palm, its face cut into nine cells, each cell bearing a number in old wedge marks that no living tax clerk used. [377] By lamplight he read them aloud, eight and one and six across the top, and found that every row, column, and diagonal surrendered the same patient sum of fifteen. [406] Beneath the tile, a schematic drawing showed the palace not as walls but as sight lines, nine chambers aligned to risings of stars whose names had been scraped away. [435] He understood at once, because frugality had trained him to see use in remnants, that the tile was not wealth but instruction, a key meant to be read only in place. [466] Through schematic representations the dead builders spoke more plainly than priests, showing where to stand, where to set mirrors of polished bronze, and where to let starlight fall unguarded. [495] At the center of the drawing, where the ninth chamber should have been, someone had left a blank square and a single instruction in cramped signs, uncloak what you love by measuring it truly. [529] His hands shook, not from fear of curses, but from the plainer terror that his life of careful hiding had been seen, recorded, and answered by hands centuries dead. [558] Yet no new regime had yet claimed the palace, and that delay felt less like emptiness than permission, a held breath in which a man might act without asking a throne. [589] He decided then to seal a covenant with the unknown, not by sacrificing grain he could not spare, but by offering the one possession he had hoarded most fiercely, his secrecy. [620] At midnight he carried his tables into the courtyard, unrolled them before the gate guards and the kitchen women and the wakeful scribes, and spoke the prayers hidden inside the numbers. [651] Then following the diagram, he set the tile in the ninth chamber doorway, placed his lamp and cup as the small circles demanded, and waited for Venus to complete the square. [682] When the light crossed the final groove, the blank floor warmed under his knees, and from beneath the palace came not a voice but a tuning, stone answering stone in low accord. [714] The guards did not kneel and the women did not scream, for the sound was modest as a well fitted joint, but each person there quietly took the hand nearest them. [745] At dawn, still between regimes and still himself, Kidin wrote the event in his table as one clear observation, that devotion uncloaked had not ended the mystery but had made room for everyone inside it.
