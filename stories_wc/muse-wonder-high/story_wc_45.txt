<story>After the substitute body was found in the shallows, cracked open like a hazel shell to show twigs and river mud inside, the village finally believed Nell. [27] Iona Pell, who had set half their bones and forgiven every unpaid debt among them, had not. [44] She was a skeptic by trade and temperament, certain that fevers, hunger, and grief explained what prayers and fairy stories could not. [66] Three years earlier, when Nell swore her daughter had been swapped in the night, Iona had held her sister and gently called it sorrow. [90] The boy left behind had laughed too soon, known the names of stars, and watched adults with a shrewdly innocent gaze that made Iona uneasy rather than convinced. [118] Now the wooden substitute, meant to look like a dead infant so no one would search, had rotted through its glamour after Nell carried her grief back to the river and asked aloud for the truth. [154] That night Iona could not sleep for yearning to undo the moment she had chosen comfort over belief. [172] She rose before dawn, took her bone-setting bag, and went through the hedge maze behind the old Hallow house, where only she still knew the turns. [198] Beyond it lay the secret garden her grandmother had planted and her mother had kept, hidden where the hedges forgot to listen. [220] It smelled of rain on thyme and hot iron, for Iona had kept the old beds but fitted them with her own new glass lids, copper pipes, and a rain clock that ticked out water by measure. [257] She came there to think and to gather splint-willow, but the boy was already waiting among the bean rows with his left arm bent wrong. [282] He could not have followed her through the maze unless he had known it longer than she had. [300] "You set bones true," he said, cradling the arm without tears. [311] "Set mine, and I will tell you where the tooth is buried." [323] Iona wanted to demand whose tooth, and where her niece had been taken, but his wrist was swelling and pain made bargainers of everyone. [347] Bone setting had taught her patience that argument never had, for a limb could not be scolded back into place. [367] She warmed willow splints in the rain clock's copper tank, an invention her patients trusted more readily than they trusted her doubts, and laid them along his arm with linen torn from her own sleeve. [402] Under her fingers the bone was real, hot, and wrong in a way no wood could imitate, and a cleft in her certainty widened with every breath he held. [431] If this child could break, then he could suffer, and if he could suffer, then dismissing him as a trick had been her first true cruelty. [457] She eased the bone home with one clean pull, forgiving his flinch even as she asked his forgiveness for three years of kind, educated disbelief. [482] He did not answer at once, because the pull had stolen his breath and left him pale as milk. [501] "There," he said, and pointed past the bean rows to the oldest rose, where a bare patch showed despite her pipes and glass and careful years. [527] Under that rose Nell had buried the changeling's first baby tooth last spring, laughing at the old custom even while she obeyed it, and the hill folk had used it as a peg to hold both children in their wrong homes. [568] Digging it up before moonrise would loosen the exchange, call the hill folk to bargain, and perhaps send him back whether he wished to go or not. [595] For years, on the glass lids above the seedlings, Iona had painted white dots for every patient she had misjudged, joining error to error until she could turn mistakes into constellations and steer by what they taught her. [633] This boy was not a dot to be corrected and blotted out, and neither was the girl beyond the hill. [653] So before digging, she asked him what he wanted, a new question in that old garden, and he admitted he yearned to stay while his sister by exchange came home to be known. [686] At moonrise they dug together with his good hand helping, set the tooth in the rain clock's ticking heart so time, not theft, would govern the gate, and called the hill folk to accept visiting days, shared names, and two homes for both children. [730] Then the gate in the hedge opened where no gate had been. [742] First through it came Nell's daughter, muddy, furious, alive, and already reaching for Iona's hand. [757]</story>
