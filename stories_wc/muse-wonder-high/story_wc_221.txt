<story>Mira Sol was the station's blessed pariah, kept alive by the immunity that made the crew fear her. [18] When the reactors failed, the captain ordered a shutdown and evacuated everyone sickened by the dilation field. [35] After the shutdown, she remained alone because her body never learned to forget time. [49] Her post was the time dilation observation deck, a glass blister where clocks disagreed and footsteps returned seconds late. [68] She lived inside an echoed silence, where even her breathing came back to her thinned and unfamiliar. [85] The deck had made her temporally stretched, so one urgent minute could loosen into an afternoon of waiting. [103] Medics called it a blessing, then sealed her meals through a hatch and stopped saying her name. [120] Only her mother's jacket crossed the barrier with her, its gilded compass rose applique still bright on the left shoulder. [140] The applique had no needle and no use aboard a station that spun far from any pole. [157] Her mother had sewn it before launch and told her that true north was not a place a compass found. [177] True north, she said, was the direction you chose when every instrument agreed to lie. [192] On the first anniversary after the shutdown, Mira wondered whether Earth sent rescue signals or the deck invented them. [211] Doubled stars and delayed reflections crowded the glass, moving before she did. [223] She decided to turn confusion into exploration, because panic had already mapped every wall of the deck. [240] So she kept a family custom of sketching the night sky on anniversaries, though she now observed it alone. [259] At home, her mother drew each point while her father named the weather hiding it. [274] With no weather or voices, she labeled each sketch with the hour her wristwatch swore was true. [291] By the second anniversary, the stars in her drawings had shifted against the station marks etched into the glass. [310] By the third, a pale light she had never charted kept appearing at the edge of the black. [328] She caught only a glimpse each time, a silver thread that vanished whenever she turned her head too fast. [347] Fear called it a symptom and proof that her blessing had begun to rot her sight. [363] Curiosity told her to test it, so she pressed the jacket to the glass and aligned the applique with the etched marks. [385] The gilded points gave her sketches a fixed frame, and the wandering light stopped pretending to be everywhere. [403] It returned in one narrow sector, always after her footsteps echoed and before the deck clock chimed. [420] She drew it on the fourth anniversary, then drew the empty sector around it hour after stretched hour. [438] The pattern became a route, each glimpse marking where the station briefly faced home. [452] Comparing four years of sketches, she found the sector crept one finger width each anniversary. [467] The disabled station was turning slowly, carrying the deck toward alignment. [478] The light was a rescue beacon outside the field, clipped by warped time into brief flashes. [494] Earth had not abandoned her, but its rescuers could not cross until someone inside showed the safe line. [512] Their messages arrived as stretched echoes, too broken for words, but repeated three short pulses. [527] Three pulses meant guide us in, the rescue code her father had taught beside those childhood sketches. [544] The guide lamp stood beside her chair, dead since the shutdown, its controls on the sealed lower decks. [562] Its emergency crank remained on the deck, placed there for a keeper whose body could survive the field unshielded. [581] For the first time, that cruel blessing looked like a purpose. [592] She laid her newest sketch over the old ones and marked where the next flash should appear. [609] Then she turned the crank through the echoed silence, counting each delayed echo to keep her arms in rhythm. [628] The lamp woke as a thin gold line, like thread on her mother's applique. [642] She aimed it by the rose in her sketches, not by the dead instruments. [656] When the outside beacon answered with a steady beam, the echoed silence broke into a voice calling her name. [675] Mira laughed, reached for her pencil, and began to sketch the road home. [688]</story>
