Amos Vale was seventy three and everyone on the inlet called him the junkyard prophet. [15]
He lived in a trailer behind his scrapyard where refrigerators rusted beside headless statues and drowned pianos. [32]
Beyond the yard lay the fog sculpture garden he had built from wreckage over forty patient years. [49]
Stone angels leaned among iron fish and copper trees beside a bell waiting for lost wind. [65]
The tide came twice daily and withdrew twice daily and Amos measured his life by that returning loss. [83]
He was precognitively forgetful which meant tomorrow thinned and vanished before he could live inside it. [99]
He would wake knowing a visitor was coming then lose the knowledge while the coffee still steamed. [116]
By noon the visitor knocked and Amos felt ambushed by a future he had already mourned. [132]
Because of this he kept a piece of plain chalk in every coat he owned. [147]
When a premonition brushed him he wrote its shape on metal before the forgetting took it. [163]
The garden filled with white warnings that weather softened into ghosts of his own handwriting. [178]
Those years passed amid the hush of old videotapes left unwatched in every room of the trailer. [195]
His late wife June had filmed birthdays and storms and ordinary suppers with devout attention. [210]
After her death Amos could not press play because her voice behind the camera hurt too cleanly. [227]
The tapes kept their labels and gathered dust and became furniture for his silence. [241]
His daughter Lila sent new tapes from inland cities until those too arrived and waited unopened. [257]
Amos told callers he was saving them for a winter when he felt stronger. [271]
Every winter he said the same sentence and understood that strength was also receding. [285]
Three springs ago he began trying to capture the sound of growing older. [298]
He held a battered recorder to his knees and taped the small clicks inside their bending. [314]
He recorded his breath on stairs and the slower negotiations of his hands with jars. [329]
None of it satisfied him because aging sounded less like noise than like distance entering familiar rooms. [346]
Then last October the fog began returning sounds that the garden had kept for decades. [361]
Amos heard June laughing near the copper trees although no path showed her footprints. [375]
He heard a child calling him Daddy from behind the iron fish at low tide. [390]
The voices lasted only seconds and left him clinging to echoes that thinned as he chased them. [407]
He became certain the garden had a center where all its borrowed sounds pooled and waited. [423]
If he could locate that center he could finally record time itself leaving a human body. [439]
Fog stole landmarks after ten paces and his forgetting stole whatever the fog spared. [453]
So each dawn Amos worked by arranging driftwood as directional markers along the drowned paths. [468]
Long pieces pointed onward while short pieces warned of statues hidden close enough to strike his shins. [485]
He chalked arrows beside them because tomorrow he would not remember why the wood lay there. [501]
For nine mornings the markers led him in circles around angels he no longer recognized. [516]
On the tenth morning he found fresh chalk writing on the gate in his own hurried hand. [533]
It said follow the child past the bell and do not stop for June. [547]
Amos had no memory of writing it and his fingers were white with chalk dust. [562]
He obeyed because his past self had learned to leave orders instead of explanations. [576]
The child voice called beyond the iron fish and Amos followed without answering her. [590]
June laughed behind him with such tenderness that his steps faltered despite the warning. [604]
He whispered an apology into the fog and kept his eyes on the driftwood markers. [619]
Past the bell the path narrowed between two angels whose faces June had modeled on theirs. [635]
There beneath a fallen copper leaf sat the recorder lately dug free from its burial. [650]
Inside its rusted case lay a tape labeled Lila learning the names of shells. [664]
Amos pressed play and his younger voice filled the fog with patient and unbearable brightness. [679]
Young Amos named each shell while little Lila repeated the words and laughed at his mistakes. [695]
Old Amos stood listening and heard beneath them the thing he had hunted for three years. [711]
He lifted the recorder and spoke his name in a voice worn thin by weather. [726]
The machine held both voices together and neither erased the other. [737]
That was the sound he had wanted not decay alone but years touching across a single breath. [754]
He carried the recorder home past markers he no longer needed to understand. [767]
Then he chose a videotape from the silent shelf and finally pressed play. [780]
