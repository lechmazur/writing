<story>Every morning during storm season, Ines Vidal walked her nursery rows before she unlocked the gate. [16] She had owned Linden Row Nursery for nineteen years, since her mother signed it over and died the following winter. [36] A single linden tree shaded the oldest beds, and Ines still pressed its first fallen leaf each year between ledger pages. [57] She told customers the habit helped her to study plant behaviors across wet years and dry ones. [74] In truth, she trusted leaves more than forecasts because leaves admitted stress early and without embarrassment. [90] That Tuesday the rain had stopped an hour before dawn, leaving every path dark with soaked bark. [107] By the mossy trellis after rain, the climbing roses had torn loose and lay across the pea gravel like tired swimmers. [128] Ines stood there with twine in one fist and pruning shears in the other, already rehearsing repair. [145] For nineteen years her answer to damage had been order, applied quickly and tied tight. [160] Her nephew Tomas arrived late, boots muddy, carrying coffee he set down without meeting her eyes. [176] They had argued the night before about the new irrigation timers she refused to install. [191] He wanted sensors and weather data, while she wanted hands in soil and eyes on leaves. [207] Ines was circuitously sincere, so instead of apologizing she handed Tomas the good shears and kept the rusted pair. [226] He understood, because he thanked the rusted pair as if she had spoken plainly. [240] Beyond the trellis, the storm had lifted three cold frames and scattered seedlings across the nursery in green confusion. [259] Ines made a list on her cuff and divided the morning into tasks no plant could dispute. [276] First they would reconstruct the trellis exactly as her mother had built it, straight, square, and unyielding. [293] Tomas read her cuff, looked at the roses, and asked why straight had to mean unchanged. [309] The roses, he said, had survived by bending around the old wires until the wires cut them. [326] Ines wanted to dismiss him, but the nearest cane showed pale scars where her careful ties had bitten. [344] She had recorded those scars in April and written weather, not tying, in the margin as the cause. [362] Now the evidence lay wet in her hand, and her own notes accused her more gently than Tomas could. [381] A linden leaf had stuck to the trellis upright, heart shaped and torn along one vein by the wind. [400] Ines peeled it free and saw that new moss had rooted in the tear, holding the halves together. [418] The leaf was ruined by her ledger standards and alive by every standard the nursery had taught her. [436] She laughed once, surprised, and Tomas pretended to inspect a knot so she could keep her dignity. [453] Another band of rain moved over the hills, rattling the greenhouse roof and darkening the linden shade. [470] They worked under tarps, rescuing seedlings first and arguing less as the rows slowly regained names. [486] When they reached the trellis again, Ines held the twine so tightly that her knuckles turned white. [503] She wanted the roses safe, which for years had meant she alone decided what safe required. [519] Tomas did not pull the twine from her hand, because he had learned patience from repotting ferns beside her. [538] Instead he set a curved willow branch beside the upright and waited until she looked at it. [555] The branch suggested a trellis that could bow in wind and return without pretending storms never happened. [572] Ines said the willow would rot, then admitted moss already lived on the old wood and softened it. [590] Her reluctant metamorphosis cost her more than money, because it loosened a rule she had used to mourn her mother. [610] If they used willow, they would reconstruct the trellis as a living frame rather than a monument. [627] Ines opened her fist slowly, by releasing control one finger at a time, and let the twine fall. [645] She told Tomas to choose the angles, then forced herself to fetch nails instead of correcting his hands. [663] Her suggestions came out as questions, clumsy and sincere, and each question left more room for his answer. [681] They bent willow into arches, tied roses loosely, and left gaps where swallows could pass and rain could drain. [700] By evening the trellis leaned a little, beautifully, like something listening rather than standing at attention. [716] Ines kept the torn linden leaf on the ledger cover, where its moss could stay alive in the damp air. [736] That night she wrote Tomas a note about the timers and folded it inside a seed packet. [753] The note said only that healthy roots need room, and that she was still learning how much. [770] Outside, wind tested the new arches, the roses moved with it, and nothing in the nursery broke.
