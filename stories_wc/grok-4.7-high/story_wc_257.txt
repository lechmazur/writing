After hitting rock bottom, the mourning jewelry maker climbed the narrow stair of the timeless observatory carrying a tattoo stencil on vellum and a coil of unfinished silver. [28]
She had spent years setting braided hair into lockets and blackening silver until other people's grief looked contained, and then her own grief had outrun the craft. [55]
Orders stopped, the lamp oil failed, and she could no longer tell a finished piece from a small grave. [74]
The observatory did not ask her name. [81]
Its dome held a night that never aged, a genuinely artificial sky of pinprick lamps and painted constellations tended by hands no record could name. [106]
She had come because a folded crane on her bench, with coordinates inside, carried one hard line: illuminate what you mean, or the dark will finish the sentence for you. [136]
Curtains of intention hung between the lenses, heavy cloth that warmed or cooled according to the honesty of whoever stood beneath them. [158]
When she touched one, it stayed cold. [165]
Her motive was not grand. [170]
She wanted to teach withering to bloom again, not as a miracle and not as a lie, but as a practice she could set into metal and leave where a living hand might find it. [205]
The tattoo stencil on vellum showed a narrow stem and a flower half open, drawn for a wrist that would never take ink. [228]
She had cut it in the last month before the loss, promising a mark that would outlast weather, and the promise had withered in a drawer. [254]

She began to work by the method the crane had taught her. [296]
