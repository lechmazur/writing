After the town goes silent, Mara climbs the mountain with her battered notepad and the thin papers she uses for taking impressions. [22] She is a gilded-leaf stationer, paid to make wedding invitations feel like heirlooms and apologies feel older than the offenses they address. [44] Her customers buy beautiful paper; she makes the journey to collect the textures of different times. [60]

On the mountaintop, a blooming labyrinth of bonsai forests waits behind her father's empty potting shed. [76] Its trees grow from invented histories, each account folded beneath a root and watered until its world becomes accessible between the branches. [98] A fingertip can enter a century; a careless foot can follow. [109]

Her father planted modest alternatives: winters without famine, summers when the apricot harvest lasted another week. [125] Mara, earnestly studious even as a child, recorded which leaves yielded the grain of vanished floorboards or the nap of unwoven velvet. [147] She learned to press those textures into paper, then catch their raised edges with gold. [162]

But tonight she carries a history he refused to plant. [172] In it, her mother survives the fever that killed her when Mara was three. [186] The account fills twelve pages, researched from household bills and the recollections of neighbors, with one impossible sentence holding everything together. [207] She has left no room for doubt. [214]

At the shed, she switches on the fluorescent lamp above his workbench. [226] Its ballast hums against the mountain's last dark hour. [235] Her father discovered that the garden could counterfeit any remembered sound, but not a sound arriving continuously from outside it. [255] In the notepad, he mapped the shifting paths by charting the faint hum in a fluorescent lamp at dawn. [274] Beside each turn, he marked where the note thinned, doubled, or trembled against a particular trunk. [290]

Mara plants her pages beneath a bare apricot and waits until the eastern sky begins to whiten. [307] Blossoms open, each holding its light close. [314] Dew beads refract the first sunlight, and between two branches a kitchen appears, ordinary enough to stop her breathing. [333] Her mother stands at the table, wiping flour on an apron. [344]

"You are late," she says. [349]

Mara steps inside before remembering that she came for an impression of the apron, something small enough to carry home. [369] The cloth beneath her fingers has the softness of years of washing. [381] She could cover a thousand books with this. [389]

"Your father is mending the gate," her mother says, setting out a second cup. [403]

Mara reaches for it, then notices the smooth hands extending from her own sleeves. [417] The scar from her gilding knife is gone. [425] Beyond the window, where her workshop should stand, apricots lie drying on white sheets. [439] In this history, her father never needed the extra income that made him apprentice his daughter to a stationer. [458] She has researched everything except the person she would become. [468]

The notepad is fading in her pocket, its rubbed corners growing square and new. [482] When she opens it, the measurements are disappearing beneath a careful recipe for apricot jam. [497] Under her mother's voice, almost too faint to separate from it, the lamp still hums. [512]

Mara turns toward the sound, comparing its thin vibration with the last marks remaining on the page. [529] Left where it doubles; right where the lower note breaks. [539] The kitchen stretches into a passage of flowering branches. [548] Behind her, a cup touches its saucer. [555]

"I will keep it warm," her mother says. [563]

Mara cannot trust herself to answer. [569] She follows the hum until her scar catches against the wire latch of the real gate. [585]

At sunrise, she digs up the twelve pages. [593] She does not burn them; she folds them into an envelope and gilds only its ragged seam. [610] On the front she writes the date her mother died, and underneath it, the date she planted the tree. [629] Both belong to her now, but they must not be mistaken for each other. [643]

Before descending, she enters the apron's texture in the battered notepad: soft, closely woven, never worn. [659] Then she switches off the lamp, saving its remaining hours for the next person who needs a way home. [678]
