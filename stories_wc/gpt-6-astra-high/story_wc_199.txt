Iona, the orderly seed sorter of the ashen skyborne arbor, kept even her mistakes in labeled drawers. [17] Above the burned valley, the floating lattice of branches carried the region's last seed harvest, ready for release at dawn. [37] She had sorted every acorn, alder seed, and tuft of willow down, but did not know where anything should fall. [57] The old planting charts showed orchards where there was now scree. [68] The families camped along the ridge were counting on her to grow them a way home. [84] She wanted to put everything back exactly where it belonged. [94] Tonight, she hoped, the falling leaves would tell her. [103]

This was the hush where past meets prophecy, when remembered voices traveled ahead of their echoes and tomorrow's birds tried yesterday's songs. [125] Fresh leaves unfurled on the arbor's charred branches, then dropped into Iona's apron. [138] Their veins shifted whenever she attempted to copy them. [147] Once, they made a shape like a laughing mouth. [156]

Regional folklore offered an instruction her training manuals omitted: build tiny monuments to forgotten things, then feed them through the arbor's knothole. [178] Whatever entered that hole emerged transformed, always. [185] Her grandmother had insisted this meant remembrance was gardening, not pickling. [196] Iona, who appreciated properly labeled pickles, had never understood. [205]

She began with a miniature of the valley's great bell tower, assembled from straight twigs. [220] When she pushed it into the knothole, it emerged as a tower of mushrooms, handsome and useless. [237] The leaves continued their green, illegible laughter. [244] Of course, she thought, everyone remembered the bell tower. [253] Its name was printed on every sack she owned. [262]

She opened her drawer marked UNIDENTIFIED, which was mostly a polite name for things she could not bear to discard. [282] Inside lay a dust speck from her grandmother's moonstone cabochon, saved from its cracked setting. [297] Tilted toward the lantern, it carried the same blue glimmer the whole stone once held. [312] Her grandmother used to touch that stone while telling stories about the spring beneath the communal washhouse. [329] Nobody had named the spring; the washhouse had been demolished before Iona was born. [343] Yet its water had kept the village gardens alive through three dry summers. [356] She had filed the story under FAMILY, never under WATER. [366]

With cupped seed husks she built the vanished washhouse, setting the moonstone speck inside for the spring's elusive gleam. [385] The little roof caught in the knothole, so she used her sorting needle to prod it gently through. [403] Out came a cushion of moss with a blue drop trembling at its center. [417] It smelled of wet aprons, limestone, and her grandmother's wrists. [427] A falling leaf settled beside it, its veins suddenly still: a stream curved around three recognizable hills. [444]

Iona spread her obsolete charts across the sorting bench. [453] She built a pebble trough for the forgotten mule watering place mentioned in a margin, then a reed bridge for the ditch her grandmother had jumped on her walk to school. [484] Each returned mossy and altered, its lost usefulness flowering into something new. [496] Around them the leaves gathered into a map, showing not the vanished buildings but the water still traveling underground. [515] In the miniature ditch, a frog no bigger than a lentil opened its mouth and squeaked. [531] Iona laughed so hard she misplaced north. [538]

Then she saw what the leaves were asking. [546] The old forest could not simply be poured back onto the exposed slopes; first, the thirsty seedlings needed those secret threads of water. [569] She moved the alder and willow seeds to the first release baskets, reserving the acorns for sheltered nursery beds aboard. [589] The oaks would follow when there was soil enough to welcome them. [601] For the spring below the washhouse, she tucked living moss around the seeds, a damp little traveling blanket. [619]

At dawn, she steered the arbor along the newly legible waterways and opened one basket at a time. [637] Below, someone saw the first green bundles descending and rang a cooking pot like a bell. [653] The monuments stayed on her bench, quietly growing. [661] Beneath the drawer marked UNIDENTIFIED, she added a second label: NOT YET REMEMBERED. [674] Then she put away her needle and let the moss keep its untidy edges. [688]
