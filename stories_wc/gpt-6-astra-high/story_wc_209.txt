Lena arrived at the amber pavilion in autumn, wearing a coat so splashed with vermilion that the cathedral porter mistook her for someone bleeding. [24] She was a mural artist known for enormous, vivid gardens, painted where factories had left walls too ugly to look at. [45] Here, beneath golden roof panels, she had been hired to paint summer over the crumbling wall that joined the pavilion to the cathedral. [68] Electric floodlights would keep her flowers blazing through the evenings and draw paying visitors to a place whose heating bills threatened its survival. [91]

Her mother had repaired the cathedral windows and tended the pavilion's climbing flowers, both occupations Lena had considered small. [110] Now her mother was dead, and among her effects was a collection of old postcards showing the same cathedral window from slightly different positions. [134] Some bore dates, others arrows, but none carried the affectionate explanations Lena suddenly wanted. [148] As a child, she had asked why the white flowers opened only at night. [162] Her mother had said, "Stay awake with me and see," but Lena had preferred sleep, then cities, then walls visible from highways. [184]

Wanting at last to understand why flowers bloom at night, she postponed her sketches and carried the postcards into the pavilion. [205] The steward assumed she was gathering inspiration. [212] Quietly driven, she let him think so. [219]

At first the cathedral window above the planting beds offered only a saint holding a lamp, his face obscured by generations of protective glazing. [243] Then, matching a postcard's angle during the dawning hush, she saw a moth scratched into a clear pane behind his sleeve. [264] The rising light caught its wings for perhaps twelve seconds. [274] Other angles disclosed other hidden layers of etched glass: a curling tongue, a flower's narrow throat, and dates in her mother's cramped numerals. [297] The postcards were an index to observations she had engraved on spare margins whenever she took a window apart for repairs. [318]

Through winter, Lena painted by day and deciphered the window at dawn, learning to distinguish a moth's visit from a gardener's guess. [340] The records described pale petals reflecting faint light, perfume strengthening after sunset, and pollen carried between blossoms on a visitor's soft, unsuspecting head. [363] Night flowering was not a failure to welcome daylight, but an appointment with another life. [378] Below the newest engraving, her mother had added a warning about the streetlamp installed beside the pavilion's ventilation hatch. [397] "Fewer visits, fewer seedpods," Lena read, and looked at the floodlights waiting in their crates. [412]

She had to reckon with what her brightness would cost, and with the rent she owed on her room. [431] The steward would not abandon evening admissions merely because a dead glazier had scratched insects into glass. [448] So Lena asked him for one unlit trial evening before he paid the electrician. [462]

Her vigil stretched across the melting of winter's last snow, until warmth loosened the first buds on the surviving vines. [482] On the appointed night, she covered the streetlamp with a borrowed maintenance hood and opened the ventilation hatch. [500] The steward sat beside her beneath the darkened mural, listening to water drip from the gutters. [516] White petals unfolded, and their scent seemed almost too intimate for the company of someone who kept accounts. [534] A moth entered, hovered, and lowered its tongue into a flower with the exact tenderness her mother had drawn. [553]

"People might pay to sit here," the steward whispered. [562] Lena almost resented him for saying it, then remembered the pipes knocking cold beneath their feet. [578] "Small groups," she said, "and shielded lamps along the floor, not over the flowers." [592] They worked out a price before morning, her full fee preserved by returning the floodlights. [607]

By daylight, her finished mural burned with copper leaves and violet shadows, but its sweeping stems led the eye toward the real planting beds. [631] She had left the space around the window bare. [640] At the first public vigil, Lena watched strangers grow still as the flowers opened, their faces losing the guarded look they had brought inside. [664] Later, alone in the dawning hush, she saw the blossoms begin to close. [677] For once, she did not mistake their closing for refusal. [687] She put the postcards back in their box and stayed until the last flower had folded itself into the light. [707]
