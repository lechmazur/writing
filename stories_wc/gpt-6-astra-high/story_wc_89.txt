After the phoenix rose, shaking embers from its wings, the orchard opened its thorn gate beneath a moon that never moved. [21] Mara entered carrying a brazier and the fortune from a cookie she had shared with her husband on their wedding night. [42] For eleven years she had kept the little paper because he kept nothing, not receipts, not grudges, not even frightened animals. [62] Every fox he nursed through winter vanished into spring. [71] Now Iven had been dead a year, and Mara, the village beast whisperer, could no longer bear the silence in their kitchen. [93] She still understood the mice beneath the hearth, but their small concerns made his absence larger. [109]

Only after the phoenix's annual rising could the living enter this orchard, where the dead wore the shapes of creatures they had loved. [132] Mara had come to reforge their bond, though every tale agreed that no human hand could touch a soul. [151] Above her, silver pears chimed against one another, and branches bent to shelter her flame. [166] To conjure one particular dead person among that endless company, she needed a scent he would remember. [183] She had traded her wedding ring for two rare spices: blue cardamom and the saffron that flowered only during eclipses. [203] The traveling baker had used both in their wedding cookies, declaring that an ordinary sweetness would never suit them. [222]

Mara crushed them over the coals, and their scent unfolded through the blossoms, warm as the hollow beneath Iven's sleeping chin. [243] Something rustled beyond the nearest trunk. [249] A fox stepped out, carrying distances inside him: stars turned beneath his transparent ribs, and a comet shivered along his tail. [270] He stopped exactly where Iven always stopped before approaching a wounded creature, close enough to offer company, far enough to allow refusal. [292] She whispered his name. [296] His answer arrived in the language of beasts, a tremor of breath and attention that meant he had been looking for her. [318]

Mara reached out, and her fingers passed through a hundred burning suns without feeling warmth. [333] Then her nails darkened. [337] Fine red hair crept across her knuckles, and she snatched her hand against her chest. [352] The old tales had warned of this too: touch required a shape the dead could hold. [368] She had imagined a little fur, perhaps, something gloves could conceal. [379] Now her teeth ached, her spine pulled, and she understood how carefully she had misunderstood. [394] She thought of kneading bread, mending a sleeve, holding a cup by its handle. [408] She did not want to die, or to become someone who had never done those things. [424]

Iven backed away, giving her room to keep the life he had lost. [437] Her heartbeat remained paradoxically steady, the same patient rhythm with which she had waited beside trapped wolves while they decided whether to bite. [460] She could recognize fear without obeying it; she had taught Iven that. [472] The fortune lay beneath her thumb, its promise suddenly less simple than she remembered: "Your home will know you in any shape." [494] Iven nudged the paper without disturbing it, asking in his wordless way whether she knew she could still leave the orchard alive. [516]

"But never human again," she said. [522]

He lowered his ears, refusing to make the price smaller. [532]

Beyond the thorns waited their cottage, and the creatures who came there because she listened. [547] She would need the villagers' help now, instead of always being the one who helped. [562] But she could still listen. [567] Mara set the fortune beneath a stone, where she could find it when she returned, and reached for her husband again. [588]

This time she let her bones fold, grieving each finger as it shortened, until the earth rose softly against four small paws. [610] The spices became a path she could smell all the way through the stars. [624] When Iven touched his nose to hers, warmth entered her at last, no larger than a breath, sufficient for a universe. [645]

They lay beneath the flowering pears until the phoenix's embers began to cool and the gate stirred on its hinges. [665] Then Mara rose to go home, because there were living things waiting, and because another rising would come. [683] Behind her, her husband held his place beneath the permanent moonlight, not calling her back. [698] For the first time, leaving him was not losing him. [708]
