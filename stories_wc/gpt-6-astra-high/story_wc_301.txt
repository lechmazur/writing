Six weeks after stopping the medication under her doctor's supervision, Mara returned to the disused railway station, where ivy had stitched the ticket windows shut. [25]
The panic sometimes returned too; she carried her doctor's number beside the river gauge readings. [40]
As the valley's river chronicler, she had once believed every uncertainty could be settled with a measurement. [57]
Then she had marked the station's northern steps as a safe flood exit, and her brother had drowned there guiding three children uphill. [80]
The children lived, a fact people offered her as though it could be traded for him. [96]

She brought his accordion, retrieved from a railway locker by the demolition crew. [108]
Its bellows were patched with tape, each repair bearing numbers in his cramped handwriting. [122]
On their last survey together, rain had ruined her notebook; he had offered the waterproof patches instead. [139]
He entertained retired railwaymen with tunes until they brought out their old plans; then he copied references while she measured the ground they pointed out. [164]
She had copied those map references into her report without asking which map he meant. [179]

For eleven months, she had been unable to open the instrument. [190]
Now she eased the bellows apart and found one reference hidden inside a fold: R17, 42. [206]
It was the only entry she had not transcribed. [215]
She wanted it to prove that someone else had made the fatal mistake. [228]
Wanting that frightened her more than the empty station. [237]

Her brother used to allude to his university education whenever he came out of the station bathroom. [254]
He meant the graffiti above its cracked basin. [262]
Mara pushed through ivy into the room, where roots had lifted the tiles and a wren nested in the hand dryer. [283]
Beneath a drawing of a magnificently endowed stationmaster, someone had written, FIRST FIND OUT WHO DREW THE BLOODY MAP. [302]
Below that, in her brother's lettering: THEN GET YOUR BOOTS DIRTY. [313]

She laughed once, painfully, and went back for her survey bag. [324]
The wisdom above the basin had always annoyed her because it made doubt sound easy. [339]
But R did not mean river, as she had assumed. [349]
It meant railway. [352]

In the planning archive on her tablet, she found railway survey sheet seventeen. [365]
Grid square 42 showed a culvert beneath the northern steps, connected directly to the tidal reach. [381]
The modern county map omitted it. [387]
Her measurements had been accurate; their frame of reference had been wrong. [399]
Worse, a recording of their final interview caught an old signalman warning that the culvert breathed backward on a rising tide. [420]
Her published account had reduced his warning to local folklore. [430]

Daringly methodical, her brother had called her, proud of the way she tested dangerous ground before trusting it. [448]
She had kept the daring and mistaken tidy paperwork for the method. [460]
Now she clipped her safety line around a sound iron column, tested each platform board, and lowered a camera through the broken stair tread. [484]
On its screen, tidewater gleamed inside the culvert despite three dry days. [496]
An old flap gate hung open, jammed with brick. [505]
She photographed its hinges, measured its elevation, and marked both map systems on the same drawing. [521]

The discovery did not acquit her. [527]
It gave her something harder and more useful than acquittal: an error she could name, a danger someone could repair. [547]
Before leaving, she sent the evidence to the flood engineer and asked for barriers at the steps. [564]
Then she began a correction for the valley paper, putting her own name beside the false assurance. [581]

She sat on the platform with the accordion across her knees. [592]
One taped patch had loosened, and when she pressed the bellows, the instrument let out a thin, human wheeze. [611]
She almost apologized to it. [616]
Instead, she smoothed the tape and played the three chords her brother had taught her, badly enough that he would have laughed. [638]
Forgiving herself was not something the river could certify, nor something the dead owed her. [653]
For now, she could keep the record honestly and help make the next crossing safer. [668]
Under the ivy, the damaged bellows drew another breath. [677]
