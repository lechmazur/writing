By the time the last village forgot its name, Nera had misplaced most of herself among the silver apples. [19] She was the memory keeper, and each sorrow she stored took a fragment of her mind to keep it quiet. [39] Sometimes she remembered three hundred funerals at once, but never which dead child had been hers. [55]

In the mirror world, morning did not arrive by itself. [65] It grew inside the alchemical orchard greenhouse, where roots drank mercury and branches spun human breath into threads of light. [85] Neighbors once gathered at its mica window, singing the overlapping verses that drew those threads into woven dawns. [103] Its layered mica split the harvest into colors; mirror glass would have sent every thread back into the trees. [122] Since the singing stopped, endless night had shrouded the greenhouse, and even the mercury had begun to freeze. [140]

On the night the first tree split with cold, a boy arrived carrying his infant sister in a flour sack. [160] He asked Nera to take away her hunger. [168] That was what their parents had taught them to ask of a memory keeper: make the hurt go away. [187] He thanked her for making him forget what had happened to their parents. [200]

Nera reached toward the baby's forehead, then stopped when its tiny mouth tried to suck her finger. [217] Forgetting hunger would not feed it. [223] Instead, she took down the last unfinished dawn and began to swaddle the child in its pale, unraveling warmth. [242] Along its hem ran a tune she almost knew. [251]

She hummed, and an apple above her answered with a woman's voice singing about bread. [266] Another answered with the same melody, slower, beneath the sound of shovels. [278] Birth song, work song, burial song: not three songs, but one, carried through a life. [293] She had cut out the painful verses, and the remaining music could no longer hold together. [309] When people surrendered their grief, they lost the words that joined their joy to anyone else's. [325] Outside, villages had become clusters of locked doors. [333]

There was no road back to morning except through forgotten songs. [344] She remembered promising that nobody would ever have to mourn as she had mourned. [358] Then she remembered a face, and her knees folded. [367] Inside the lowest apple, her daughter was still calling from the river. [379]

"Fetch whoever will come," she told the boy, "and tell them I cannot cure this without hurting them." [397] He hesitated, looking at his sister. [403] "I will keep her warm," Nera said. [410]

He returned with seven people, then others followed the sound of an argument about whether the keeper could be trusted. [430] A baker brought her nursing baby, and a carpenter brought the door he had burned halfway through for heat. [449] Nobody volunteered to sing. [453]

Nera understood that asking them to open their wounds while she kept hers sealed was another kind of theft. [472] She plucked the lowest apple and broke its silver skin. [482] The river entered her mouth, cold enough to stop her breath. [493] Then came what she had also buried: her daughter singing the bread verse badly, deliberately, until both of them laughed. [513]

Nera sang the wrong notes exactly right. [520] The baker recognized them and supplied the next verse; the carpenter answered with a funeral refrain. [536] As apples opened overhead, the listeners cried out, but nobody asked her to make it stop. [552] At the window, Nera tilted the mica leaves, crossing gold with rose while the singers kept the fragile strands taut. [572] Every voice bound one color to another, and beneath that binding the night began to loosen. [588]

The carpenter offered the baker his arm, and together they carried the door to the stove. [604] The baker settled beside it and opened her blouse for the boy's sister. [617] With the new cloth, Nera wrapped the babies together, leaving their small hands free. [632] Beyond the greenhouse, the first roofs emerged, separate houses under the same imperfect morning. [646]

Her daughter was gone, and would be gone tomorrow. [655] She told the boy her daughter's name, Bess, and he repeated it carefully, as if learning his part in a song. [676]
