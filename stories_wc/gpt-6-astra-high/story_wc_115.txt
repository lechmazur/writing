"I knew your mother was behind it," her father said. [10]

In the hush following that sudden confession, Nell heard the abandoned train tunnel breathing, although the mill above it had been silent for thirty years. [35] Beyond the floodgate, something dragged a heavy load along the rails. [46] The factory ghost shift had started again. [53]

She had spent three sleepless nights listening to recordings, and exhaustion gave everything the tender sharpness of the instant before a dream. [75] Rust glittered like sugar beneath her torch. [82] Her father's open mouth looked absurdly vulnerable. [89]

For years Nell had explored abandoned mills recklessly, crossing rotten floors and climbing chimneys, drawn to places where people had worked hard and then been forgotten. [115] The official account said a flash flood had taken her mother before anyone could have helped. [131] Her father always called it God's will, which had helped make her skeptical of every religion that offered suffering an alibi. [152]

But somebody using his childhood nickname had called a radio psychic and asked whether the dead could hear an apology through iron. [174] Nell found the recording while searching an archive for broadcasts about the mill's haunting. [188] Then she found other frantic calls to talk radio psychics, scattered across decades, all carrying his small whistle between words. [208]

She had learned to appraise his story by interpreting the questions he asked when he believed nobody knew him. [227] Asking whether obedience counted as murder meant there had been an order. [239] Asking whether a spirit could feel cold meant the water had risen slowly enough for him to imagine it. [258] And asking how to silence knocking beneath railway sleepers had brought her here, dragging him along, to find the weight of a swallowed truth. [282]

Now his torch illuminated a beaded valve wheel beside the floodgate. [293] A rosary wound around its rim, its wooden beads swollen and split by damp. [307] In one broadcast he had described leaving it there instead of turning the wheel. [321] The psychic had praised his offering, then sold him another ten minutes of comfort. [335]

Nell touched a bead, and the dragging stopped. [343] Through a hole in the corroded gate she saw figures waiting beside wool carts, their sleeves floating in water that was no longer there. [367] Her mother stood nearest, still wearing the apron Nell remembered clutching while the woman tried to cook. [384]

The calls rearranged themselves in Nell's tired mind: the manager upstairs, the rising river, the order to protect the machinery. [404] Closing this gate had kept the mill dry by trapping the loading crew on the flooded side. [421] Until this moment she had left him one possible mercy: perhaps he had thought everyone was already gone. [439] His confession had taken that away, and still her hand wanted to steady his trembling elbow. [455]

She put her pry bar through the wheel instead. [464] Ordinarily she would have thrown her whole weight against it, trusting luck, as she did on every rotten stair. [483] This time she checked the footing, tested the spokes, and guided her father's hands onto the bar beside hers. [502] He shook his head, looking toward the waiting apron. [511] Nell did not release him. [516]

Together they pushed until the rosary snapped and beads scattered down the sleepers, sounding horribly like teeth. [533] The wheel moved one reluctant quarter turn. [540] Then another, and somewhere inside the wall river water began filling an old hydraulic cylinder. [555]

The gate lifted just enough for the first worker to bend beneath it. [568] Whenever their grip slackened, the wheel reversed, lowering the gate again. [579] Others followed, leaving their useless carts behind, passing through the yellow torchlight toward the tunnel mouth and the road home. [599] Nell's mother paused beside her husband, not touching him, not smiling, then looked at her daughter's hands. [616] Nell kept turning until the apron vanished into the dark. [626]

Her father sagged, but she left his hands where they were. [637] She had imagined truth as something you spat out, a stone you could finally put down. [653] Instead it had the weight of this iron wheel, and it required holding long after the person you wanted to save was gone. [676] In the unbroken hush, she counted the others while he struggled to keep the gate from falling. [693]
