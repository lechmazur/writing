Leda wore vermilion boots, kept violet paint beneath her nails, and had not made a cheerful mural since her husband died. [21] People still called her vivid, which seemed an accusation against her laundry rather than a description of her life. [40] When the city offered her a commission to display its ruined orchard district, she almost refused. [56] The place had been lost in centuries of thick fog, and she was tired of being hired to illustrate absence. [76] Then the curator unwrapped a fresco fragment: a yellow pear balanced on somebody's unmistakably mischievous nose. [92]

The fresco's address survived along its bottom edge, enough to guide a sleeper through the municipal dream archive. [110] There, vanished places persisted within the fold of a daydream, their objects preserved exactly as their last inhabitants remembered them. [130] Places nobody discussed became fogbound; the orchard district, remembered only for the flood that destroyed it, had become almost inaccessible. [150] Leda wanted to meet whoever had managed that pear. [159] Perhaps they could teach her something her condolences had omitted. [169]

At noon she lay beside the fragment and joined the knowledge network, a fellowship of sleeping restorers linked through the archive. [190] Their voices accompanied her into streets where drowned apple trees leaned through roofs and fog hung heavy as wet wool. [210] At the surviving address stood a photographer's shop, its advertisement missing a patch precisely the shape of her fragment. [229] The painted pear had promised portraits without solemnity. [237] Inside, beneath a collapsed counter, she found a sealed tin labeled EXPOSED FILM: FLOOD WEEK. [252]

She reached for the lid, but a voice in the network stopped her. [265] Exposed did not mean developed; another dose of daylight would erase whatever the photographer had saved. [281] A retired chemist talked her through the darkroom, while an archivist translated the bottle labels from the district's obsolete script. [301] Leda, who ordinarily painted with light projected through colored glass, worked for once in perfect darkness. [317] Her hands shook until the chemist began complaining about apprentices, a subject so reassuringly eternal that she laughed. [335]

Once fixed, the negatives showed broken bridges, floating cupboards, and people queuing for bread. [349] Then came a woman standing in mud with a pear on her nose. [362] Behind her, children perched on an overturned wardrobe, their mouths open with laughter. [375] The woman wore mourning black. [380] Leda had expected joy before the disaster, neatly separated from everything that followed. [393] Instead, someone had balanced a small ridiculous sweetness above a mouth that surely knew how to cry. [410]

She remembered her husband spilling soup during his last winter, then solemnly asking whether the floor preferred more pepper. [429] She had stopped telling that story because people looked embarrassed when widows laughed. [442] Now she told it to the network. [449] Someone snorted; someone else quietly asked for the photograph. [458]

The commission required a record of destruction, the curator reminded her through their waking link. [473] Leda studied the image again, careful not to crop away the wreckage. [485] "Then it will be an accurate record," she answered, and asked the network to help her borrow the shop's enlarger. [505] No single sleeper could carry an object out of the archive, but many could hold an image steady for projection. [525] As the chemist adjusted the focus, others relayed the photograph toward the waking square, where Leda's mural lamps waited. [544] She painted with their light, leaving the floodwater gray and warming only the pear to its original, impertinent gold. [563]

For a moment the square remained silent, and Leda feared she had mistaken a private consolation for something shareable. [582] Then a child demanded a pear, and an old man said his grandmother had called that trick the orchard salute. [602] His recollection entered the archive like a window opening. [611] The fog loosened around the shop; beyond it, Leda could just distinguish a footpath between the ruined trees. [629]

She woke before the clock finished striking noon, with tears drying beside a smile that felt slightly out of practice. [649] The curator asked whether she could restore the whole district. [659] "Not today," she said, setting the fresco fragment beneath the projected photograph so visitors could see how the search had begun. [680] Then she borrowed a pear from someone's lunch and learned that rediscovering joy involved considerably more neck work than anticipated. [700]
