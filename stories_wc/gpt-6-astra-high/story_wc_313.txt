At midnight on the ridgeway, in the lingering northern twilight, levee warden Mara Venn listened until her wind chime fell silent. [21] Only then could she distinguish water whispering through the bank from water merely lapping against it. [37] The emergency drawdown had relieved the pressure, but it had also brought Bellwether, drowned forty years earlier, back into the air. [99] Mara's final survey would authorize clearance and expunge the settlement from the active hazard register. [114] She had allowed former residents onto tested ground in pairs, with helmets and twelve minutes each. [130] Precisely humane, her supervisor called her, not entirely as praise. [140] Nobody had fallen; everybody had brought something home. [148]

Her own twelve minutes began with an envelope of photographs retrieved from her mother's things after the funeral. [166] The photographs documented the evacuation route, and Mara retraced their trail: clock tower, communal pump, the tavern steps. [184] Matching each foreground to the next picture brought her along the street she had last walked at six. [202] She checked her watch and the seepage markers before crossing each patch of silt. [216] Memory was not a reason to disregard a bank that could swallow a boot without leaving a hole. [234]

The last photograph showed an upstairs window above the tavern, with a dark square pasted inside the glass. [252] Below it, her mother stood beside people Mara did not recognize. [263] The clearance plan called the building a warehouse, vacant for years before inundation. [276]

On the photograph's back, her mother had pasted a sapphire-hued liqueur label beside a list of names. [293] Its silver heron stood in blue reeds. [300] Mara had pasted an identical label over a crack in that upstairs window, making an ocean for her dolls. [319] The memory arrived with the smell of wet wool: dolls on the sill, mattresses underneath, somebody coughing behind a blanket. [339]

Not a warehouse, and not empty. [345]

When the dam's temporary barracks closed, laborers with nowhere else to go had lodged above her mother's bar. [363] There had been a woman who mended gloves, a man who carved whistles, a baby born between two trunks. [502] There would be no entering the tavern, no heroic search for objects to make the past feel solid.
