As fog rolls across the harbor, Mara steps onto the golden dock under snow, carrying eelgrass seeds in a jar of seawater. [22] Where her boots disturb the white crust, the old cedar shines like something rescued from a fire. [39] She collects seeds for the mainland conservatory, where her habit of asking what a thing owes its descendants has earned her the title of philosopher. [64] Today she has come to preserve an ancient method before Iven leaves on the noon ferry. [80]

At the last piling waits Iven, a fisherman whose hands keep mending even when there is nothing between them. [99] By evening he will be living with his daughter, beyond the reach of salt damp and these punishing winters. [118] He has offered her his mother's planting instructions, but instead of a manuscript he lays a length of fishing rope across her knees. [141] "The secret weavers never trusted paper," he says, almost beneath the sound of water nosing the piles. [158] "Paper stays ashore when the boats go out." [166] His mother taught Mara to mend nets before Mara left to study botany, but she never explained why some repairs contained deliberate mistakes. [189]

Iven names the first few signs, and her memories supply the gestures that make the rest intelligible. [206] Now, by decoding knot patterns in a fisherman's rope, Mara begins to recognize those mistakes as instructions. [223] Three turns mark the falling tide; paired loops mean seeds; the spaces between them measure the distance between planting pockets. [243] Each wrong knot holds information that a tidy repair would erase. [254] She sketches the sequence, pleased to have saved it, until Iven closes her notebook with one wet finger. [272] "You have saved a description of supper," he says. [281] "Nobody has eaten." [284]

From his coat he brings a branching piece of fulgurite glass, sand fused around the path of a lightning strike. [304] One hollow arm is worn smooth where generations of thumbs have held it. [317] She reaches for a specimen sleeve, imagining the label, but he puts the glass directly into her palm. [335] "A seed pipe," he says. [340] "For getting past the current." [345]

Below the dock, the retreating sea has uncovered a narrow shelf of silt, still shining beneath an inch of water. [364] They descend the ladder slowly, Iven's knees refusing alternate rungs. [374] He spreads a loose mesh of flax across the mud and weights its corners with stones. [390] The same paired loops appear in it, each holding a little pocket that will rot away after the seeds take root. [411] The rope was not merely a record; it was a pattern for making the planting net. [427]

"Why secret?" she asks, kneeling beside him. [434] "The old harbor masters called eelgrass a nuisance and paid to have it torn out." [449] He points toward the empty shallows where young fish once sheltered, then pushes the pipe through a pocket into the silt. [470] His trembling fingers cannot pick a seed from the jar. [480] Mara steadies his wrist, and together they coax one slippery seed into the glass with a drop of seawater. [499] What lightning hollowed in an instant has become a tool for work that cannot be hurried. [515]

At first she keeps looking up toward the ferry berth. [525] Her return ticket is in her notebook, beside the neat instructions she meant to publish before seeking another collection. [544] Iven waits, infinitely patient, while she spills three seeds and learns how lightly the fragile pipe must touch the bottom. [564] He corrects her grip without taking the work away. [573] By the seventh pocket she can feel the right depth through her fingertips. [586]

When the ferry sounds beyond the fog, Mara helps him back onto the dock but does not fetch her bag. [606] "Who will watch the bed through winter?" she asks. [615] Iven looks at the glass in her hand. [623] "Someone who can teach the next pair of hands." [632] When he boards, his hands are still at last. [641]

She opens her notebook again, not to cancel the book, but to add what her first account could not contain. [661] Beside the knot drawings she records the pressure of silt, the trembling wrist, the necessity of staying. [678] Then she writes to the village schoolteacher, offering lessons on the dock when the tide is right. [695] Beneath her boots, the first pockets disappear under returning water, their knots holding around something they were never meant to keep. [716]
