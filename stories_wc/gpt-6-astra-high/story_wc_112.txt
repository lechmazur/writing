At the xenobiology research station, Mara was the obliging sky mechanic who made artificial weather for creatures that could not survive their adopted sun. [24] She lowered cloud ceilings for nervous hatchlings, patched auroras, and never charged the biologists for rain. [40] Since her brother vanished inside the twilight apparatus, she had also kept its little moon polished, though nothing living seemed to need it. [63]

The director had given her until morning to dismantle it. [73] The report said equipment failure, which meant that Iven was dead and Mara, who had fitted the apparatus, was responsible. [93] She wanted to prove that time moves sideways, not because she expected applause, but because Iven's last transmission had described a second station beside their own. [119]

The station's dusk moths offered the only evidence nobody had yet filed away. [132] Their young emerged with memories of flowers extinct before the eggs were laid, but only after sleeping beneath the magnetic nexus at twilight. [155] When the feeding lenses projected flowers reconstructed from fossils, their tongues uncurled toward petals absent from every living seed bank. [175] Mara called these subdued awakenings, since the hatchlings merely turned toward absent blossoms and waited. [190] The biologists called them errors. [195]

Tonight, the last brood clung under the dome, wings folded, their synchronized heartbeats trembling through the frost. [212] All her work had to fit within the hush of beating wings at rest; once they stirred, their shared magnetic field would dissolve. [235] Mara's hypothesis was simple enough to fit on a maintenance label: time had neighboring rooms, and the moths slept against the partitions. [257] Not the past, she thought, but other presents, where a glacier had turned differently or an extinction had never happened. [277]

Through holographic principles, each wing scale encoded the whole partition rather than one piece of it. [293] The apparatus's mirrored moon could enlarge that pattern into a doorway, provided she stopped treating its distortions as faults. [312] She had filtered them out on the night she lost him. [323] Iven had trusted her adjustments because, as children, she had always repaired whatever he broke. [338] She had mistaken being needed for never being wrong. [347]

Her prosthetic hands were mechanically warm, but she cooled their fingers against a pipe before touching the delicate brood. [366] With those fingers, she eased a sleeping moth off the access plate, then used a spanner to lever the plate loose. [387] On the intercom, the director ordered her to stop. [396] Mara disconnected the speaker and diverted the dome's heater current into the nexus. [409]

Beyond a skin of blue light stood Iven, snow collecting on his shoulders. [422] "You fixed it," he said, and reached for her. [431] She caught his hand; its cold weight nearly brought her to her knees. [444] "Not a recording," she whispered. [449]

Behind her, a moth dropped onto the grating. [457] The nexus was stealing the heat that kept their wing muscles loose enough for the sleeping pulse. [474] Her gauges said she could pull Iven across only by increasing the current; the entire brood would freeze before the doorway widened. [496] The emergency batteries were already warming the nursery; taking those would only choose smaller victims. [511] Obliging Mara, who had always found another fuse, another favor, searched the panel for something she could promise him. [530]

Iven asked, "How many?" [534] She could not make herself count. [540] His fingers loosened. [543] "There's a station here," he said, "and people who need someone to mend its sky." [558] Behind him, someone called his name with an accent she didn't know. [570] He pressed his spanner into her hand, its maker's stamp identical to hers except for a country that had never existed. [591]

Mara wanted her brother, not evidence. [597] For once, she let a repair remain unfinished. [606] She reversed the current, and he lifted his empty hand as the blue skin closed. [621] Heat returned to the dome in increments too small to feel. [632]

"What did you recover?" the director asked from the hatch. [642] Mara held up the spanner, still cold where Iven had touched it. [654] "Enough to build a safer door," she said. [662] Then she warmed the fallen moth between her patient, artificial palms. [673] Under its folded wings, a small pulse resumed. [681]
