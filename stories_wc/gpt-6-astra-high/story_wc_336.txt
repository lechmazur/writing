On the verdant parapet beside the waterfall, Mara cut one crowding branch so three younger ones could reach the sun. [20] Her granddaughter, Nell, caught it before it tumbled into the white gulf separating their orchard from its sister world. [39] Once, blue moths had crossed that gulf with pollen on their feet, bringing colors no orchard could invent alone. [58] Then winter froze the crossing, and spring thawed everything except the invisible passage inside the falling water. [75]

Now the stored pears were gray, although the ivy remained green. [86] Through the spray, Nell sometimes glimpsed a boy shaking an empty basket beneath trees that had not leafed at all. [106] Nell asked whether they could send him their pears, even without the gold. [119]

The pruner lowered her shears. [124] For weeks she had searched for a way to preserve the color of wonder; Nell had noticed the hunger beneath it. [145]

From her apron she took the heirloom seed her mother had carried out of that other orchard fifty years before. [165] Inside its husk slept the last chance to taste the crimson pears of Mara's childhood. [180] But such seeds could also mend crossings, by capturing essence and giving it a living shape. [196] Her mother had taught her the method, but warned that a bridge must spend its whole life reaching, never bearing fruit. [217] Mara had kept the seed wrapped in her mother's handkerchief ever since. [229]

She unfolded it now. [233]

The work could begin only upon the hush of budding hope, when the first closed bud loosened before dawn. [252] All night Mara prepared, insatiably creative even when afraid, bending discarded twigs into shuttles and making a loom of her pruning ladder. [274] Nell brought the basket of gray pears and sat beside her without asking for promises. [289]

At last, a bud opened. [294]

Mara pressed the seed into a mortar crack and laid her bare palm against the waterfall. [310] She took none of its water, only the essence of its reaching, the way it gave itself to somewhere else. [330] That she breathed onto the seed. [336] Pale shoots climbed the ladder, and Mara passed them through her twig shuttles, weaving between worlds as her mother had once woven linen. [359] Each crossing of the strands pulled a little distance out of the gulf. [372] Beyond them, the boy lifted his face. [379]

Then a crimson blossom opened beside Mara's thumb, filling the air with the scent of her mother's kitchen. [397]

For an instant she wanted both things. [404] She let the flowering shoot remain, and another curled back toward her, heavy with the promise of familiar fruit. [423] The woven span slackened. [427] Across the water, the boy's outstretched hand began to fade. [437]

Nell steadied the ladder, and Mara understood that the old warning was not a prohibition but a description of weight. [457] A living branch could carry only so much. [465]

She cut away the blossom. [470] Then she pruned every shoot that turned homeward, leaving only those reaching toward the boy, and braided their flexible tips together. [491]

The bridge tightened, narrow as a garden path, with the waterfall shining through its living mesh. [507] Inside the span, the thunder thinned to a radiant hush. [517] Mara crawled across with the basket strapped before her, stopping wherever a strand needed coaxing around another. [534] She had come to succor a stranger, but his first gesture was to steady her trembling elbow. [551]

They ate a gray pear together beneath his bare trees. [561] When she asked about the moths, he opened his coat, showing three blue cocoons sewn carefully into its lining. [580] He had kept them warm all winter. [587]

Mara showed him how to prune the damaged branches, and he showed her which roots were still alive. [605] Before evening, neighbors were carrying food across the woven passage and returning with cuttings tucked into damp cloth. [623]

Every morning afterward, Mara trimmed the bridge before tending her orchard, cutting off the crimson buds while they were still small. [644] She never pretended it cost her nothing. [651] But when the moths hatched, Nell followed their blue flickering along the parapet, naming shades her grandmother had never seen. [671] Mara listened, her shears resting open, and made room for every name. [683]
