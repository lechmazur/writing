Mara had advertised the cursed orchard for sale every spring, and every autumn she painted over the price before anyone could mistake it for an invitation. [26] Someone had to keep travelers out of its cliffside labyrinth, where the apple trees drank human warmth when the night frost formed. [48] Her mother stood somewhere inside now, crooked with fruit. [57] Mara pruned only the boundary trees and never ate. [66]

Overhead, mechanical birds patrolled the corridors, their tin throats grinding out warnings nobody understood. [80] She collected their fallen parts because clearing the paths was easier than thinking. [93]

That afternoon, a child's cry came up through the sea mist. [104] "Three people, lower terraces," Mara said to the empty kitchen, offhandedly exact as she always was when frightened. [122] She had distinguished a man's answer, a woman's cough, and the child's repeated call. [136] The night frost would form in forty minutes. [144] The corridors shifted whenever the roots fed; yesterday's safe route was useless, and she could not fetch help before dark. [164]

She reached for her mother's surveying case, untouched since the funeral without a body. [178] Inside lay blue printing paper, a small brass nut, and a photograph of a mechanical bird spread flat above a sheet. [199] On the back, her mother had written: They were made to bring people out. [213]

Mara nearly threw the photograph down. [219] Her mother had gone into the maze with nothing but rope because the birds no longer came to their keeper's whistle. [240] Whatever this promised, it had failed her. [247]

Then sunlight passed through a discarded wing, scattering little rectangles across Mara's wrist. [260] Not decoration: windows, steps, a door. [266] The birds carried something older than the wandering paths. [275]

She tipped her salvage basket onto the table and began to assemble a bird. [289] Its left wing came from a gull, its right from a crow, but the mounting marks matched. [306] The small nut fitted the shoulder spindle, locking both wings level instead of letting them droop. [322] Without it, their projected lines would never meet. [330]

"Seven inches above the paper," she murmured, judging the height from the photograph's familiar teacup. [345] She propped the ugly creature over a fresh sheet and opened the shutters. [358] Sunlight worked through the pierced metal, leaving impressions without touching, while she counted the seconds and listened for the child. [378]

After rinsing the sheet, she held a forgotten blueprint of the cliff itself. [391] Beneath the orchard, a maintenance stair descended through solid rock to doors on every terrace. [406] The roots could move the hedges, but not those stairs. [416] The entrance was under the keeper's hearth. [423]

For twelve years she had warmed her feet over the way out. [435] Her mother had spent years trying to make the birds fly again; Mara had finally let one be still. [454]

She levered up the hearthstone, tied her rope to the stove, and lowered herself into a shaft smelling of rust and old tides. [477] At the third landing, she drove her pruning hook into a swollen door and pulled until something tore in her shoulder. [498]

Outside, the family huddled beneath an apple tree whose branches were bending toward the child's uncovered head. [515] "Here," Mara called, and the father stumbled toward her instead of the inviting gap in the hedge. [532] The mother could not walk; a root had tightened around her ankle. [544]

Mara struck it with the hook, though the bark beneath her blade looked terribly like a wrist. [561] Sap spattered her face, warm as breath. [568] She had spent twelve years refusing to hurt anything that might be her mother. [582] She struck again. [585]

Between them, she and the father carried the woman upstairs while the child clutched the wet blueprint and read out the landings. [607] They rolled across the kitchen floor just before frost whitened the apple nearest the window. [622]

No one looked graceful: the father was weeping into a coal scuttle, and Mara had lost a shoe. [640] The child touched the assembled bird with one careful finger, setting its mismatched feathers rattling. [655]

"Will it fly?" [658]

"No," Mara said, "but tomorrow we can make more maps." [668]

She would mark the hidden doors, oil their hinges, and hang a whistle beside each one. [684] Outside, the patrol wheeled over the frozen orchard with its customary racket, but inside, by lamplight, the crooked bird cast a steady, useful shadow. [708]
