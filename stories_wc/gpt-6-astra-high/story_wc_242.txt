At midnight, the streetlamps above Mercy Station stopped humming for one second, and in the subway utility tunnel below, that second lasted an hour. [24] Mara, the underground phone booth operator, worked between the hum of streetlamps, connecting calls that ordinary exchanges discarded. [42] These were usually calls to people who had died, or left without explaining where. [56] She could not connect anyone to the dead, but she could offer another wrong number. [71] Strangers sometimes answered when relatives would not. [78]

A retired baker named Ivo had dialed his wife's disconnected number every night until Mara routed him to a girl who burned everything. [101] Now he stayed awake to explain bread, and she mailed photographs of loaves to the booth for Mara to forward. [121] Through wrong number friendships, people became something other than the ones who had been abandoned. [136] Mara called the practice cultivated estrangement: enough distance to escape an old story, enough attention to start another. [154] She never gave callers her own name. [161]

Beneath her switchboard lay a glass harmonica disc, the smallest from the instrument her husband Tomas had played in station entrances. [182] He had left it to keep her company, teaching her how wet fingertips could coax music from its rim. [201] The night he died, his last telephone message had reached her through the booth while the disc was still ringing. [221] Glass remembered what it resonated with down here. [229] An unfinished note waited through daylight; at midnight, its keeper could release it or wind it back. [246] By circling its rim, Mara could loop the final phrase: "I'll be home soon." [260]

For seven years, she had kept one finger moving while her other hand connected strangers. [275] The booth was melodically haunted, and she preferred that to silence. [286]

Lately, however, the phrase had begun taking longer. [294] Each repetition stretched the last note, stealing seconds from the callers, until conversations broke off before anyone could say goodbye. [314] On Thursday, Ivo's voice vanished halfway through a recipe. [323] On Friday, only Tomas came through. [329] Mara tightened a screw, replaced a fuse, and polished every contact except the one beneath her finger. [346]

On Saturday, someone knocked on the booth's folding door. [355] The girl who burned everything stood outside with a loaf wrapped in a dishcloth. [369] She had followed the return address on Mara's forwarding envelopes, certain the operator would know whether Ivo was alive. [388]

"He's alive," Mara said, although she had no way to check with Tomas filling every wire. [404]

The girl listened to the thin, revolving promise. [412] "Is that who you're waiting for?" [418]

Mara nodded, ashamed of the relief she felt at finally being asked. [430]

"I waited for my mother," the girl said, setting the bread beside the disc, "but Ivo was the one who answered." [451]

Mara looked at the loaf's crooked seam, where dough had burst through an uncertain join. [466] She knew how many nights of patient instruction were baked into it. [478] She had made room for that patience, then gradually taken the room away. [491]

Once, she had told Tomas she wanted this job to teach endings how to begin. [506] He had laughed and asked whether endings wanted lessons. [515] She could remember his laughter without the glass. [523]

Her finger stopped, and for the first time she let the promise reach its end. [538] The last note trembled beneath the weight of everything it could not deliver, then faded into the tunnel's dripping dark. [558] Mara tried the rim once more before she could stop herself, but it gave only a small, clear musical tone. [578]

The girl said nothing and cut the bread with a pocketknife, leaving the first slice beside Mara's idle hand. [597]

When the telephone rang, they both flinched. [604] It was Ivo, furious with the exchange and worried that his pupil had finally poisoned herself. [620] Mara handed over the receiver, then listened as the girl described the loaf instead of apologizing for it. [638]

When they finished, Mara asked whether they might both come tomorrow, before the lamps fell quiet, and eat upstairs. [657] She gave them her name and the address of her kitchen, which had chairs she hadn't uncovered in years. [676] Above them, the streetlamps resumed their humming, and this time Mara took the stairs while the music was still going. [696]
