The drought came after the strangers in our landing camp had become family, after we knew whose nightmares needed company and whose soup needed salt. [25] Until then, I had mistaken staying together for knowing how to stay. [37]

Our orchard planet, Aril, lay hidden in a nebula of respite, a violet dust cloud that blinded the war fleets' hunting instruments. [59] Above every pear grove floated rock arches, their cold undersides combing water from the fog that rose each night. [78] The dead orchard keepers had left channels beneath them, but thorn roots had split those channels, and now the water fell into salt ravines. [102] We rationed drinking water and argued about which trees deserved our leftovers. [114]

Then a ghost began to organize us through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn. [129] He called himself Iven and wore the translucent apron of an orchard keeper. [142] His first messages identified safe wells and loose ladder rungs, never asking payment or prayers. [157] He was strangely trustworthy, this man with daylight showing through his pockets. [169] When he wrote my name beside an arrow, I followed it. [180]

The arrow led past the thirsty pears to a butterfly garden choked with nettles. [194] Beside its empty cistern stood a butterfly garden watering bell, a squat clay vessel pierced underneath with tiny holes. [213] On the paving, Iven had written, "Fill this, Mara, before you water another tree." [227]

I nearly walked away; my daughter's pear sapling was already dropping leaves. [239] But he knelt beside a nettle and pointed to the silver eggs beneath its curled leaves. [255] A second message made me reexamine what I had thought were weeds: "No butterflies, no pollination; no pollination, no pears." [275]

I fetched our ration and submerged the bell, then covered its top opening with my thumb to hold the water inside. [296] Lifting my thumb released a gentle shower around the roots without battering the brittle stems or dislodging their eggs. [315] One bellful reached six plants; six plants might carry next year's harvest. [327]

At dawn, the sidewalks became a map of work. [336] Iven assigned climbers to the arches, cooks to carry water, and children to tend the nettles with the bell. [355] Outside my door he drew replacement channels with dimensions matching the hull plates of my landing boat. [372]

I was our mechanic, and I had kept that boat intact in case the nebula ever failed to hide us. [392] Its seats could hold my daughter and perhaps four others, a calculation I had never admitted making. [409]

"You want me to cut up our way out," I told him. [421]

He pointed down the street, where people I loved were portioning their breakfast water into cups for the garden. [440] Then he showed me where his chalk came from. [449]

Behind the cistern stood his headstone, rubbed almost smooth, its dates already gone. [462] He had been breaking pieces from its limestone edge because we had nothing else soft enough to write with. [481] Even his name was becoming instructions for people who had never known him alive. [495]

I brought my cutting torch. [500]

By evening, the camp rang with hammers, and strips of our escape boat climbed toward the arches on ropes woven from everyone's bedding. [523] The first channel buckled when its arch drifted, spilling our precious catch into the ravine. [538] Someone shouted that we should have saved the boat. [547]

I wanted to shout it too, but my daughter was below us, carefully passing the watering bell to another child. [567] Neither of them spilled a drop. [573]

At the next dawn, I found Iven sketching flexible joints, his last name ground away beside a diagram of overlapping plates. [594] I knelt and added measurements while he marked where each crew should stand. [607]

That night, when the arches shifted, our channels bent instead of breaking, and water drummed into the garden cistern. [626] Nobody cheered until the overflow reached the first row of trees. [637]

We still had ration lines, torn blankets, and no certain shelter if the war found us. [653] But weeks later, when the first butterfly unfolded its wings above the nettles, my daughter ran to fetch Iven. [672] I had hammered his name into the longest channel, where he could read it from the garden. [689] He watched her hold out a scratched finger, and the butterfly settled there as if thorns had always been part of the invitation. [712]
