After the jet stream shifted, the experimental weather station began receiving rain from below. [14] Water swelled its cork floors until every footstep sounded like someone struggling inside a mattress. [29] Mara, its new map librarian, kept drawing shorelines where the official charts showed blank water. [44] She believed a map owed people the truth, especially people nobody expected to find. [58] Her supervisor called this idealism; Mara called it cataloguing. [67]

The station's proudest invention was reversible exile: threatened islands could be folded into weather that had been predicted but never happened. [88] Returning them required bringing their deferred storms ashore, so the return switches had quietly disappeared. [103] Thirty years later, the ministry still described the inhabitants as temporarily sheltered. [115] Her grandmother, Elian, had tried to reach them with a rescue flotilla. [127] Officially, the ships had broken against the fold, and Elian had died in prison for wasting public property. [145] Mara had come to clear her name. [152] She carried a copper nail from Elian's coffin, the only keepsake allowed when the prison cemetery was relocated. [170]

In the salvage archive, Mara found six shipwrecked journals, their pages as soft and swollen as diseased tongues. [188] Each captain had copied the same dreadful verses about a mother calling children home. [202] Elian had been a terrible poet, but an exact navigator. [212] Mara questioned why the stresses wandered between seven and thirteen while the rhymes repeated with mechanical precision. [229] The errors were coordinates; the rhymes specified pressure; the repeated refrain meant reverse. [242] Through coded stanzas in the shipwrecked journals, Elian had distributed instructions for returning the islands without striking the fold. [261] She had expected the authorities to confiscate charts, not sentimental poetry. [272]

The flotilla had not been trying to enter exile. [281] It had been placing beacons for a return. [289] Mara found those beacons on the station's survey maps, labelled wreck hazards. [301] Her grandmother had completed the route. [307] Each wreck marked a safe anchorage; the captains had sunk their own ships to leave iron beacons the station could detect. [328] Mara checked the prison rolls: every crew had returned alive, only to be jailed alongside the navigator who saved them. [348]

Nervously confident, she presented the reconstruction and asked the director one question. [360] "How long can we call exile reversible while refusing to pay for the return?" [374] He rested his palm on the wall, which yielded like wet bread. [386] "The storm will come out here," he said. [394] "Not over the islands." [398] "Here." [399] Mara understood: Elian's route would turn the station itself into the storm's drain. [412] No heroic portrait, then, no corrected plaque in an undamaged hall. [423] Honoring her grandmother meant finishing the work that had killed her. [434] "We can evacuate a station," she said. [441] "We cannot evacuate thirty years." [446] The director watched rain bead upward through his desk. [455] At last he reached for the evacuation roster. [463]

She photographed the journals while the staff evacuated, then stood among the irreplaceable charts she had spent her first month restoring. [484] Leaving them felt like abandoning patients. [490] With the last launch waiting, Mara opened the return console and found its copper bridge removed. [506] A hammer lay beside the fire bell. [513] She flattened the coffin nail on the console's rim until it spanned the empty contacts. [528] It was soft enough to shape, sound enough to carry the current. [540] She had polished it for years, thinking remembrance meant keeping something unchanged. [552] Now she drove it between the terminals and entered Elian's coordinates. [563] Under the station, something enormous inhaled through soaked cloth. [572]

On the launch, the director wrapped a blanket around her as the first island shouldered through the rain. [590] Then another appeared, its harbor crowded with boats carrying lanterns. [600] Along their rails stood old people holding children up to see the world. [613] Mara's grandmother was not among them. [619] She had hoped vindication might feel like getting her grandmother back, but the empty place beside her stayed empty. [638] When a boatman called across to ask if their island still existed on the maps, Mara opened her chart case to a blank sheet. [662] Then she asked him to help her put it back. [672]
