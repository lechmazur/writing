In the gargantuan shell, where drowned avenues curled around a sealed throat, the upper spiral owned the lamps and the lower spiral owned the water. [25] They had spent three centuries teaching their children which deprivation justified a knife. [38]

Mara, an insomniac poet, had written both sides' marching songs, changing only the names of the villains. [55] The shell slowed aging, but did nothing merciful for memory. [65] Each night she heard her verses returning through the ventilation holes in children's voices. [79] Sleep felt like leaving them unattended. [85]

Above her bed hung a galaxy postcard, mailed by her wife from the surface before the last passage collapsed. [104] Its stars were printed with living plankton, a tourist novelty that still glimmered when warmed between two palms. [122] On the back, Iona had written, "There are gardens up here that nobody has to deserve." [138] Mara had kept that small, purchasable universe alive across centuries of longing, although no second message came. [155]

Then the lower spiral seized a lamp station, and the upper spiral announced that it would boil the reservoirs. [174] For the first time, both commanders commissioned the same song: a victory hymn without a surviving enemy. [191]

Instead, Mara visited the seed vault. [197] Among its exhausted treasures lay mourning vine, once cultivated beside the shell's sunlit mouth. [211] Its leaves could eat grief, but only living light could wake them; electric lamps had never worked. [228] Mara wanted to photosynthesize pain into beauty, not another reason to sharpen it. [241]

She asked the keeper for a seed and learned why nobody had planted one in generations. [257] "It flowers when grief is freely given," he said, "and our people prefer collecting it from others." [274] He gave her the smallest seed anyway. [281]

At home, she decided to ensconce it in the postcard's folded corner, where the printed galaxy was brightest. [299] Its first root pierced Iona's signature. [305] Mara nearly pulled it out. [310] Then she put her mouth to a ventilation hole and began a lullaby her wife had sung badly, always laughing at the difficult part. [334]

She offered no heroic death, only the truth that she missed someone and had helped make other people miss someone too. [355]

The vine climbed toward her voice. [361] By morning, its pale tendrils had entered the shell's acoustic passages, following sorrow via half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind. [382] Mara went door to door, shyly unstoppable, asking people not for allegiance but for one unfinished song. [399] Some spat at the author of the marching verses; she left those doors unmarked and returned when invited. [417]

At the lamp station, a boy sang about his father's empty boots. [429] Across the barricade, the woman aiming at him lowered her rifle long enough to remember her sister's terrible cooking. [448] A white blossom opened between them, illuminating both faces. [457] Then another opened beside the reservoir valve. [464]

Its own glow woke new leaves, and its roots drank water from below. [477] By noon, the lower spiral had light it did not have to purchase. [490] When the upper commander ordered the vines cut, his soldiers asked who would extinguish the flowers over their own dead. [510] The lower commander, suddenly less indispensable, demanded that the roots be starved. [522] His reservoir keepers opened the valves. [528]

The blossom armistice began as an agreement between plumbers and exhausted infantry, written on the back of Mara's rejected victory hymn. [549] Water would flow upward; cuttings would travel downward; nobody would be required to sing. [563] The commanders signed when they discovered that supper, too, depended on cooperation. [575]

Mara did not call it forgiveness. [581] She spent the following weeks taking down the names her old songs had replaced with vermin, and reading them aloud when families asked. [604] There were arguments beneath the blossoms, and thefts, and mornings when the water arrived late. [619] But children started inventing rude rhymes about both commanders, which seemed a sturdier beginning than gratitude. [635] Their laughter had a reef-shadow joy: bright movement under the weight of something that could still crush them. [653]

When Mara finally lifted the vine from her bedside, the postcard came apart in her hands. [669] The stars had migrated into the leaves, and Iona's handwriting was gone. [681] She cried without trying to make the crying useful. [690]

Outside, someone stumbled over the difficult part of the lullaby, laughed, and started again. [704] Mara lay down beneath the unfamiliar garden and slept. [713]
