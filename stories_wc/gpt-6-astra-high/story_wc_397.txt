After waking up changed, I mistake the hiss of my oxygen machine for my husband breathing beside me. [18] He has been dead eleven years, and I have brought his absence north because it fits into any luggage. [37] The clinic doctor says the confusion comes from too little oxygen, not from any visitor in the night. [55] He adjusts my treatment and postpones my assessment until my thoughts are reliably mine. [69]

I am a euthanasia tourist, although the woman arranging my accommodation prefers the word guest. [84] My lungs are scarred beyond repair. [90] Since the bad night, my mind grips reality loosely, as though afraid of bruising it. [105] I want, before deciding anything further, to learn the sound of approaching endings. [118] The doctor cannot teach me that. [124]

At the drift-ice marina below my rented room, last night's surge has torn three pontoons from their anchors. [142] Relief crews trawl the narrow lanes of black water for fuel drums and floating insulation before either reaches the harbor mouth. [163] Through disaster relief volunteering, I find a place among people who listen before they move. [178] Iben, the harbor keeper, parks my wheelchair beside the surviving gangway with blankets, a clipboard, and oxygen. [195]

"Names coming back, names going out," she says. [203] "Nothing heroic, please." [206]

In my pocket is an heirloom lace parasol tip, a brass point polished smooth by three generations of summer pavements. [226] I gave away the furniture before coming here, but could not give away that. [240] My grandmother used to tap it three times outside our door, allowing my slow grandfather time to reach the latch. [260] The lace eventually rotted, and the shaft split. [268] What remained was the part that had announced her. [277]

When the water seems to breathe, I touch the brass to remind myself what metal feels like. [294] By noon I can distinguish a loose cleat rattling from a boat knocking against its fenders. [310] Neither is the final sound I came to hear. [319]

I ask Iben whether ice gives warning. [326]

"Sometimes it complains all day and holds," she says. [335] "Sometimes it hardly bothers." [339]

She checks the moorings anyway, kneeling to examine each shackle. [349]

Later, while two volunteers unload recovered drums onto the last pontoon, I tap my parasol tip against the gangway rail. [369] Three knocks, a pause, three knocks: an old habit for keeping loneliness from becoming an entire room. [386] Between the knocks, a thin humming begins beneath my hand. [396]

For a moment I am back in bed, unsure which sounds belong to the world. [411] I nearly wait for certainty. [416] Then a mooring line rises dripping from the water and draws straight as a ruled mark. [432]

"Iben, would you listen?" [436]

She lays her hand beside mine, looks downstream, and shouts the volunteers ashore. [449] An ice sheet has wedged beneath the pontoon, lifting its outer edge. [461] The men cross the gangway slowly, because Iben orders them not to run, and I count both names aloud. [480] Once they are clear, she wheels my chair behind the seawall. [491]

A minute later, something breaks with a noise like a cupboard shutting. [503] The pontoon turns, spilling the drums we have spent the morning retrieving. [515] I begin to cry, absurdly, for the wasted work. [524]

Iben puts a blanket over my knees without telling me what I ought to be grateful for. [541]

"We can trawl again tomorrow," she says. [548]

That evening, the clinic calls to offer another appointment. [557] I keep it; nothing at the marina has unscarred my lungs, and I will not turn the rescued men into an argument against my own pain. [583] But I ask for an afternoon appointment, because I have promised to tally the morning crew. [599]

What I have found is a fold in patience, a little room inside the waiting where something besides an ending can occur. [621] I had imagined knowledge would arrive like a bell, unmistakable and addressed only to me. [636] Instead, I needed someone else's hand on the railing. [645]

Below my window, Iben is calling tomorrow's assignments, and the wind lifts her voice through the dark. [662] I set the brass tip on the sill and listen until she comes to my name. [678]
