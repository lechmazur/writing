Beyond the murmur of lost languages, people kept their histories in a crystalline orchard perched at the edge of a shimmering crater. [22] Each tree carried family memories in its luminous fruit, and each new branch required a relative's sponsorship. [39] Neri, a marsh-reed sculptor, arrived with a sack of stems and nobody who could vouch for her dead. [57] The flood that had taken her village had also taken everyone who could remember her being small. [74] She wanted to graft herself onto this community before grief persuaded her she had never belonged anywhere. [91]

At the orchard gate stood a glimmering obsidian arbor, its black uprights pierced with empty sockets. [107] The keeper refused her request to plant a memorial beneath it, though he offered her bread. [123] "These places are inherited," he said, unable to meet her eyes. [134] Neri ate his bread on the steps and tried not to mistake kindness for a welcome. [150]

There she noticed a painted mark: a severed twig bridging a broken circle. [163] Someone had scratched it almost away. [169] A lamplighter passing with his ladder stopped when she traced its shape. [181] "Wren's tag," he said, "but the keeper scrubs it off." [191]

Wren was an elusive street artist who painted on doorways where someone had been turned away. [207] The authorities called the work insolent; Neri thought it boldly sympathetic, giving rejection a shape that could be changed. [226] She asked the lamplighter to help find an untouched example, hoping its maker knew a way past the gate. [245] He agreed because Wren had once painted his doorway after his sons stopped visiting. [259]

Their shared search led through streets paved with dark glass, where reflections walked beneath them like possible companions. [277] A baker joined them after recognizing Neri's sketch; she had washed the same mark away, afraid customers would ask questions. [297] At dusk, a girl who swept the market led them to another, half hidden behind a drain. [314] Inside its circle, someone had painted a little loaf of bread. [325] The girl said Wren had painted it when the baker began leaving bread for her. [340] The baker looked startled that anyone had noticed. [348]

They searched for six evenings, following weathered tags from locked workshops to crowded lodging houses. [363] Neri listened more than she questioned, and carried the lamplighter's ladder when his shoulder hurt. [378] She learned that the baker called the market girl her niece, although neither could name an ancestor they shared. [397] She learned that the keeper had been raised by an orchard worker who had found him beside a ferry. [416] Beneath the city's official pedigrees ran other webs of kinship, bright and stubborn as roots beneath paving. [433]

On the seventh evening, their search brought them back to the arbor, where the girl spotted paint inside a socket. [453] Neri threaded a reed through it and drew out a plug of compacted leaves. [467] They cleared the other sockets, uncovering Wren's tag in each: a broken circle receiving a branch from outside. [485] Perhaps the arbor had been built not for descendants, Neri said, but for arrivals. [499] Its empty sockets were invitations the city had gradually mistaken for vacancies nobody could claim. [514]

The keeper brought an old planting ledger, his hands trembling as he turned its brittle pages. [530] The first entries named sponsors beside strangers, with no blood relationship recorded. [542] "I thought keeping it meant keeping it unchanged," he said. [552]

Neri had come seeking one place; now she asked the others to help her make many. [568] All winter she wove reed cradles into the arbor's sockets, flexible enough to hold crystal cuttings without cracking them. [587] The baker brought twine, the girl sorted stems, and the lamplighter held his light steady over their hands. [605] The keeper took cuttings from trees whose families consented, so no new branch would have to begin alone. [623]

In spring they gathered to dedicate the arbor to those who had not yet found their people. [640] Neri set a cradle aside for her village, then left the next one open. [654] No fruit could restore the voices she had lost, but now there were people willing to hear her describe them. [674] As they worked, the girl painted a twig on Neri's wrist, and Neri did not ask where she had learned. [694]
