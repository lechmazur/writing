By dawn, the hidden orchard behind the kaleidoscopic waterfall would finish turning to glass, and the valley would lose its spring. [21] Irena, the auburn-haired alchemist, arrived carrying her mother's notebook and a frozen dew crystal wrapped in wool. [38] The crystal was the last thing they had made together, catching midsummer's first moisture and persuading it to remember winter. [58] It could burn once, releasing enough heat to wake the orchard's ancient furnace. [71] Afterward, nothing of it would remain. [77]

Beyond the waterfall, broken rainbows slipped over trunks older than the kingdom, while crushed mint scented the newly whitening grass. [97] Here, according to her mother, the world rehearsed each spring before sending it downhill through the roots. [114] Now the furnace beneath the oldest pear tree had stopped breathing, and transparent veins were climbing its bark. [132] Irena knelt beside seven bronze air valves, each engraved with a bird. [144] Their counterweights lay locked beneath a century of mineral crust, which she scraped away with her knife. [161] Her calculations gave them identical openings; her mother's drawings gave them wings in seven different positions. [177] The discrepancy had haunted her since the funeral. [185] She had come to harmonize logic and intuition, not surrender either one, but grief made certainty terribly attractive. [203] If her equations were right, she could save the orchard without admitting how much she still needed her mother's voice. [223]

She tested the equal settings with ordinary charcoal. [231] Blue flame rose neatly, then flattened beneath a sudden pressure from the roots. [244] A pear fell and shattered at her knee. [252] The furnace did not need equal air; it needed air balanced against something moving underground. [267] She searched the notebook for another formula and found, tucked between density tables, a pressed starling feather. [284] Secretly sentimental, she had preserved even the muddy thumbprint beside it, though she called such things contamination in her laboratory. [304] Under the feather, her mother had written: Watch what the air is asking. [317]

Above the orchard's open ravine, starlings were gathering, their black bodies stitching the pale sky. [332] They dipped whenever warm vapor escaped a root vent, then wheeled away as another vent breathed. [348] Irena began reading the furnace's hidden rhythm through the patterns in a flock of starlings. [363] She counted the intervals, marked the corresponding valves, and discovered that the drawings recorded successive movements, not permanent settings. [382] But the intervals kept changing as the glass advanced. [391] No finished table could tell her when to turn the next handle. [403] She would have to begin with measurement and continue by attention, trusting her hands to learn what her numbers described. [423]

Across the orchard, the last green leaf stiffened at its edges. [434] There were only moments left before the final transformation. [443] Irena unwrapped the crystal, remembering her mother's cold fingers closing around hers on that long vanished midsummer morning. [461] For years she had kept that morning safe inside a box, unable to bear spending its only tangible remnant. [480] Now she set it in the furnace and struck her flint. [491]

The frozen dew began to flame, clear as daylight, and the seven handles shuddered within their sockets. [508] She raised the eastern counterweight when the flock unfurled, lowered the southern one when its dark hem folded inward. [527] Once she moved too soon, and a glass branch rang overhead like a warning bell. [542] She corrected, listening, watching, letting error become information instead of shame. [553] Slowly the furnace found its breathing, and the handles began moving without her, lifted by pressures she had finally balanced. [573] The crystal dwindled to a spark and disappeared. [581]

Under Irena's hand, the pear tree softened, its bark opening into ridges that held the cool dampness of living wood. [601] A drop of sap touched her wrist, and she laughed with a suddenness that hurt. [616] This was the touch of wonder her mother had meant: not an escape from understanding, but the warmth of understanding something alive. [638] Downhill, water began moving through channels cut before anyone remembered naming the valley. [651] By sunrise, she had recorded the changing pressures, the counterweight adjustments, and the questions she would test when she returned. [671] Then she tucked the feather back beside the muddy thumbprint and left the empty crystal box open beneath the tree. [691] A white petal settled inside it, fresh enough to bruise. [701]
