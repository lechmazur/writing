During the sanctions period, when the harbor cranes stood still, Mara learned how far a spoon could be heard scraping an empty pot. [23] As leader of the exile community, she was expected to have answers, but lately she had only a gently persistent habit of knocking twice. [47] People who refused help on the first knock sometimes opened at the second. [60]

Beneath the terra-cotta arbor on the ridge hung an antique silver bell, brought from the schoolhouse they had abandoned across the water. [82] The curved tiles magnified its smallest chime. [89] Mara wrapped the clapper in felt so her night volunteers could signal without waking everyone. [104] Anonymous donations had begun appearing beneath it: coins in envelopes, always enough for another sack of rice. [121] She turned those coins into meals delivered through midnight food runs, sparing the proudest families the sight of a public queue. [142]

She wanted the deliveries to build intergenerational support, not merely keep people breathing until the shipping lanes reopened. [160] But the elders called the young selfish for planning to leave, and the young no longer attended meetings where remembering counted for more than proposing. [185] Every volunteer she recruited was over sixty. [192]

Then Tomas, the former headmaster, pushed his unopened supper back across her table. [205] He suspected the envelopes came from the government that had expelled them. [217] Accepting secret money, he said, could purchase their silence. [226] At tomorrow's meeting, she must declare who was paying, or he would advise everyone to refuse the food. [244]

That night Mara waited behind the arbor's rain barrel. [253] A bicycle labored up the gravel path, its chain ticking louder than the rider's breathing. [268] Niko, Tomas's grandson, dismounted badly and caught the bell rope to stop himself falling. [282] The muffled note barely escaped the tiles. [289] He had an envelope between his teeth and blood soaking one sock. [301]

"Your grandfather thinks our enemy sends these," Mara said, coming forward. [312] Niko lowered himself onto a bench. [318] "He would rather believe that than believe we can feed him." [329]

The donations were pooled wages from the children of the exiles, earned hauling freight for the few merchants still trading. [349] Niko had canceled his passage to a mainland apprenticeship; others had surrendered deposits, tools, the little futures they could sell. [369] They hid their names because their parents would otherwise return the money. [381] Tonight a brake cable had snapped on the road from the warehouse. [393]

Mara knelt beside his ankle, ashamed of every invitation she had issued to young people to come listen. [411] She had never asked them to come decide. [419] She unwound the felt and rang the schoolhouse alarm: three strokes, a pause, three more. [434] Under the tiles the sound swelled until it seemed the whole ridge had become a bell. [450]

Tomas arrived first, carrying the walking stick he usually pretended not to need. [463] Behind him came Sella, the retired midwife, who washed Niko's cut and bound it with strips torn from her underskirt. [483] When Tomas noticed the envelope, Mara put her hand over it. [494]

At dawn, with Niko's permission, she told the gathering that their children had been feeding them, but named no donors. [546] She laid out the accounts, including the cost of each meal. [557] Then she declared the relief kitchen common property, with an equal vote for every member, regardless of age. [575] Tomas rose slowly, and Mara braced for his objection. Over the following weeks, younger hands hauled the midnight carts while older neighbors planned routes, mended tires, and rode along to make introductions. [617] The donations remained anonymous; the decisions did not. [625] Nobody was asked to buy belonging with the money meant for leaving. [637] When a berth became available again, the kitchen paid Niko's fare from a fund he had helped establish for apprentices. [657]
