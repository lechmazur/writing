When the magician loosened the silk around Mara's wrist, she stood between understanding and confusion, her first waking thought trembling in its weave. [23] Behind him, the broken orchards of Vey waited for her decision. [34]

Vey was an orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches, their backs green with apple trees and their shadows blue over villages. [56] Nothing floated freely. [59] Each dawn, magicians collected bartered awakenings: a person's first clear thought purchased a day of movement for one unbroken body of stone. [81] The seller slept until the following morning, while the stone climbed or hovered as instructed. [96] For generations, the harvest had paid for the sleepers' bread. [106] Then an earthquake had split the eastern arches into thousands of separate pieces, and the price of lifting them had become thousands of lives suspended. [131] There were not enough people left awake to gather what survived. [142]

Mara, a meek blacksmith who apologized even when customers underpaid her, had spent the evening beneath those ruins. [160] Using abandoned mine shafts, she had reached the ancient bedrock without disturbing the fallen trees or the families sheltering among their roots. [182] Her mother had followed with a lamp, asking whether they should sell tomorrow's awakenings too. [197] Mara had answered by tapping the walls with her smallest hammer. [208] A crack answered with a hollow chatter; sound stone rang. [218] For hours, through three shafts, she had followed one deep, continuous note. [230]

Now the magician held her thought inside his silk, the blue cloth he once used to make little stones dance at harvest fairs. [253] Only that weave could hold an awakening long enough to carry it from flesh into rock. [269] She had lowered its unravelled warp down the nearest shaft, weighted with a hammer to keep it against the bedrock. [289] The magician asked which way the stone should rise. [298]

Mara nearly said upward, because everyone knew an orchard belonged against the sky. [311] Then she looked at her mother's hands, still bleeding from digging children out of the rubble. [327] She made herself deliberate, although agreeing quickly had always felt safer than making other people wait. [343] To restore the arches would restore their hunger, and tomorrow someone else would have to surrender the work of living. [363] She wanted to coax resilience from rubble, not purchase another beautiful postponement. [375]

"Sideways," she said, so softly that the magician bent closer. [385] "Tell it to fold along the old workings and push the fallen stone into terraces." [400]

He stared at her drawing, scratched into soot on an iron shovel. [412] Each terrace leaned into the next; drainage channels followed the shafts that miners had already cut. [428] She had drawn a place for every surviving tree, and broad steps where people could gather fruit together. [446]

"At sunset, it stops moving," he warned. [453]

"Stone does not need to be awake to bear weight." [463]

For the first time since the earthquake, her mother laughed. [473] The magician traced the terraces with a dirty fingernail, then nodded and called for everyone to leave the ruins. [492] Mara waited, quietly determined, until the last child crossed the safety line she had chalked beyond the proposed foundations. [511] Only then did she let him pull the knot. [520]

Her name began slipping away as a low note rose through her boots. [533] Down in the shafts, the buried ridge stirred, lifting its shoulders into the broken orchard. [548] Rubble rolled and settled against rubble; shattered arches became retaining walls, their fallen splendor finding the humble strength of contact. [568]

One wall sagged where wet clay slid beneath it, and Mara tried to shout, but could no longer remember the word for stop. [591] Instead she tapped the shovel against a loose stone: three sharp blows, the warning every worker in her forge knew. [611] Her mother understood and shouted instructions; through the silk, the magician coaxed a broad stone ledge beneath the failing wall. [631] The trembling stopped. [634]

The loose end of the magician's silk lifted in the wind, a scrap of azure defiance above the grounded orchard. [654] Mara forgot what blue was called, then what the trees were for, but someone eased her down without letting her fall. [675]

"Tomorrow," her mother whispered, "we will all be here to pick." [686]

She did not understand the words anymore, only the steadiness of the hands that held her. [702]
