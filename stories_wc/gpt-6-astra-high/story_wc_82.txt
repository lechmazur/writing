After the shadow detaches from the floating isle at sunset, the glass basilica begins recording the dreams of the tea pickers. [21] Only then can sleeping minds cast their own shadows, which the basilica catches in sapphires beneath its dome. [39]

Mara crosses the tea garden carrying a lantern whose silver shutters imitate the flutter of a trapped moth. [57] She made it to distract the custodian, who cannot bear to see anything struggling against glass. [73] While he fetches a butterfly net, she slips behind the altar into the polishing room. [88] By the time he discovers the moth is only light, she has barred the workshop door. [104]

Around her neck hangs the sapphire that has recorded the same dream for eleven months. [119] In that dream she descends while ascending, climbing the basilica stairs as seawater rises faster, dragging her daughter farther below. [139] The real storm broke the garden bridge while Mara was unlatching their door, her daughter's hand briefly released from hers. [159] Every night since, she has climbed toward safety and sunk toward that single opening of her fingers. [176]

The basilica's polishers can transform recorded dreams into paper, but they grind away the painful parts first, calling the resulting blankness peace. [198] Mara has watched their customers leave without remembering why they came. [209] She wants to fold grief into origami cranes, because her daughter made birds from the tea sellers' discarded wrappers. [228] She does not want to forget the weight, only to give it wings small enough to hold. [245]

She sets the sapphire against the wheel and wets the abrasive with cold tea from the custodian's cup. [263] At the first turn, blue water fills the glass walls, and the staircase unfolds beneath her feet. [280] Dream recording preserves sensation faithfully: the salt burns her throat, although her body remains dry beside the wheel. [298] Somewhere beyond the water, the custodian begins pounding on the door. [309]

She presses harder, trying to reach the bright memory beneath the drowning, and her daughter's face loses its mouth. [328] A pale shaving falls from the gem, smooth and empty. [338] Terror makes her stop the wheel with her bare palm. [348] She has nearly polished away the crooked smile she meant to save. [360]

For years she has disguised bubbles in cheap lantern glass by shaping each lens around them, bending light instead of chasing purity. [382] Now she tilts the sapphire until its black seam lies parallel to the wheel, where the pressure cannot tear it out. [403] She turns the crank slowly, taking a layer of brightness and darkness together. [416]

The mouth returns, crooked with concentration, and Mara remembers her daughter showing her how to crease a wing against a thumbnail. [437] The memory hurts worse than drowning, because it contains an ordinary afternoon she had never thought to treasure. [455] She keeps turning anyway, while a sheet unspools beneath the gem, veined with the sea's darkness and the kitchen's amber light. [476]

Below the staircase, her daughter reaches upward, and Mara's free hand rises before she can stop it. [493] But this is a recording of a dream, not a hidden passage back to the bridge. [509] There was no staircase that night, no higher step from which she could have reached farther down. [526] She lowers her hand to catch the paper, and the climbing stops. [538]

When the sapphire is spent, she sits on the floor and folds along the dark seam instead of cutting it away. [559] Twice she spoils a wing; twice she unfolds it, finding that dream paper remembers every crease without refusing another. [578] The custodian falls silent when she finally opens the door and shows him what his wheel has made. [596] He reaches for the crane, but she shakes her head and asks him to carry the lantern instead. [614]

Together they walk between the tea bushes, whose wet leaves catch the false moth's trembling light. [630] Mara places the crane on the garden table where her daughter used to work, weighting its feet with two fallen tea seeds. [652] It does not fly away, and the ache beneath her ribs does not follow it out into the sky. [671] Through one imperfect wing, lamplight illuminates a small black hand held inside a larger one. [686] Mara sits beside it until the tea pickers wake, keeping both hands open on the table. [702]
