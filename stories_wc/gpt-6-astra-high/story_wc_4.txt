Mara practiced puzzle archiving, a subtle, patient trade involving torn letters, unlabeled faces, and the things families filed under Never Again. [21] This spring, the town asked her to make a memorial in the lavender field tucked behind its rusted factory ruins. [41] Twenty years after the fire, neighbors still crossed streets to avoid comparing losses. [54] Mara intended to transmute their pain into a masterpiece: an unbroken mosaic, every fractured photograph fitted perfectly into place. [73] If nothing was missing from the picture, perhaps nothing would feel missing inside her. [87]

She carried the donations in her father's dove gray map case, which had once protected his drawings for the factory. [107] The drawings survived because he had left them at home; he had gone back for his men. [124] Her mother contributed one black-and-white photograph, facedown. [131] In it, a young woman laughed beside a curved white line. [142] Mara recognized her mother, but not the laugh. [150] "That was three days afterward," her mother said, and reached to take it back. [164]

Mara remained perceptively calm, sliding a clean sheet over the image instead of asking why. [179] People trusted her because she knew when a silence needed somewhere to sit. [192]

Over the next week, similar arcs appeared in other black-and-white photographs: behind a bandaged wrist, beneath a loaf, beside a sleeping child. [214] The prints had been cropped so closely that each survivor seemed alone. [226] Mara unfolded the map case across her workbench and arranged them by the curves. [240] Following the arcs rather than the faces, she reconstructed a circle divided by familiar Roman numerals. [256] The factory clock had become a table. [263]

Old Tomas, who brought the final photograph, explained that the wooden dial had fallen clear of the blaze. [281] They had laid it on barrels in the field and served soup while the missing were still being counted. [300] An insurance photographer had photographed everyone there, so families could find one another. [313] Later, people had trimmed their prints to fit lockets, frames, and whatever amount of remembering they could bear. [331] Tomas's picture supplied the other half of her mother's laugh. [341] A little boy wore an enormous saucepan for a crown. [351] His solemn expression suggested the kingdom was in capable, if slightly sticky, hands. [364]

Her mother studied the reconstruction for a long time. [373] "I thought laughing meant I hadn't loved him enough," she said. [384] Mara looked at the careful borders she had drawn around her proposed masterpiece. [397] She had planned no place for laughter either. [405]

At the next meeting, she proposed a table instead of a monument. [417] The committee objected that tables attracted crumbs, arguments, and children with muddy elbows. [430] Mara said those were excellent qualifications. [436] But when Tomas asked whether the dead would have names on it, she heard the fear beneath his sharpness. [455] The center would hold their names, she promised, with room around them for the living. [470]

As vines climbed the broken walls through summer, neighbors built a circular table among the lavender, contributing both timber and opinions. [491] Mara used the photographs to restore the clock's white rings along its edge, leaving visible gaps wherever evidence was missing. [511] People could bring another picture, or let a gap remain. [521] Beneath sealed glass, the recovered images faced outward toward the benches. [532] Her mother's laugh stayed. [536]

On the first evening, a widow accused Tomas of getting out before her husband, and the soup went cold. [555] Mara did not fit their stories together or sand the accusation smooth. [567] She asked whether they wanted her to stay, and when both nodded, she stayed. [581] The next week, the widow returned with bread; Tomas brought the bowl she had forgotten. [596] Nobody called that forgiveness. [600] It was enough that they could linger at the same table. [611]

By autumn, lavender seeds gathered in the seams of the dove gray case, which now carried photographs and spare place cards. [632] Mara still missed her father most fiercely when someone reached across her to pass the salt. [648] But her mother sometimes laughed before remembering to be sorry, and sometimes forgot to be sorry altogether. [665] When visitors asked when the masterpiece would be finished, Mara set another place. [678]
