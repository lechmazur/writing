We came to Greywater with empty wagons, intending to sell the town one stone at a time. [17] Thirty years after the earthquake broke its aqueduct, there was nothing left worth carrying except masonry and the gate bell. [37] I had claimed the bell because its bronze would buy my mother a proper grave. [52] Exile had given her thirty years of laundry work and a wooden cross. [65]

Beneath the ancient archway, a stranger held an ebony tuning fork against the cobblestones. [79] Overhead, the aqueduct's empty channel rested on a keystone that had split clean through. [93]

"You are late," he said, "but your feet are not." [103]

I recognized the bell tuner from my grandmother's stories, although she had described him as ancient when she was small. [123] He was infinitely patient; he had spent those empty decades listening for someone to return. [138]

"Bells learn their voices below," he told me. [146]

"Help me cut this one down," I said. [154]

He pressed the fork's stem into my palm. [162] The black wood swallowed the wind's noise, and through it I felt footsteps: boots, clogs, bare heels, hundreds of them. [182] A wild hush filled the square, as if a forest had crowded into the roofless houses and held its breath. [202] Under my boots, pale figures stirred inside the stones. [211]

"The builders," the tuner said, "waiting for their partners." [220]

Above us, grit hissed from the keystone's crack. [228]

"We can rebuild before the collapse, if you remember how this town walked." [241]

I remembered the noon bell, and hurried workers marching beneath it toward the weaving sheds. [256] I stamped that rhythm on the cobblestones. [263] The figures rose waist high, lifting fallen blocks, but their elbows struck each other and a stone shattered beside me. [283]

"That was the mill owner's pace," said the tuner, catching my wrist. [295] "Not the town's." [298]

I almost flung his fork into the weeds. [306] My mother had walked that way until her knees swelled, and still the owner had docked her wages for lateness. [326] Why should I summon back a place that had spent her so carelessly? [339]

"Not everything that stood here deserves rebuilding," he said, and waited while another thread of mortar sifted onto his sleeve. [359]

Then I remembered my mother coming home, slowing her steps so my small legs could keep beside her. [377] I tried that uneven rhythm, and one builder straightened, holding a block against his chest. [392] His free hand reached toward an empty place. [400]

Behind me, our neighbors were loading the first wagon with lintels. [411]

"Leave those," I called, and explained what the aqueduct could mean: water, gardens, homes without rent paid to strangers. [430]

Ruth, who had already sold her winter coat to hire the wagon, asked whether I would pay her losses. [449] I gave her the money I had saved toward the grave. [460] She weighed it, then stepped onto the square. [468] Her stiff hip made her hesitate every third stride, and the waiting builder shifted his block to accommodate her. [487]

Others joined us, not marching, but making room for one another as they crossed and recrossed the cobbles. [505] With every footfall, another ghost found purchase; with every yielded passage, two could lift together. [520] The tuner moved among us, touching his ebony fork to stones where our rhythms fought. [535] Its quiet vibrations showed us where to pause. [543] We were learning to restore shared balance, not obedience, letting each person's limits shape the work without stopping it. [562]

When the keystone finally broke, the builders already held its weight. [573] They dressed a block from the wagon and wedged it into place while we walked until our soles burned. [592]

At dusk, Ruth's sons climbed the hillside and opened the old headgate. [604] Water crossed the archway, trembling in its channel, and spilled into the dry fountain. [618] The builders sank gently into the cobblestones. [625] We would have to rebuild the houses ourselves, but nobody loaded another stone for sale. [640]

I found the tuner beneath the bell, adjusting its clapper with hands as dark as his fork. [657] I asked what hour it would ring. [664]

"When someone needs a hand," he said. [671]

That night, beside the fountain, I carved my mother's name into a fallen stone. [685] Ruth sat beside me, sharpening the chisel whenever I rested. [695]
