Decades later, Mara returned because the harbor bell had forgotten to ring. [12] For sixty years, everyone had paused at its morning stroke and said, "What the water takes, the water keeps." [31] Mara still said it over her first cup of tea, though she had lived inland for decades. [48] Now the rope had snapped, and her aunt had remembered a woman singing over a bowl of pear skins. [67] "Your mother," she said, as if Mara had ever possessed a mother who sang. [81]

Mara knew only the official account: drowned in the great flood, along with thirty others. [96] She had no face to put against the words. [105] A hands-on folklorist, chronically curious, she had spent her working life kneading festival bread and repairing fishing charms rather than merely recording them. [128] She knew old traditions reveal their machinery when habits break. [138]

She began with the fishermen's star maps, drawn in chalk on kitchen tables. [151] An old school register named her mother as their navigation teacher. [162] Before the flood, their boats had steered by the Orchard Woman, a constellation holding a basket against her hip. [181] Afterward, every chart retained the same stars but omitted the lines that made her hands. [196] Even men who remembered the constellation's name could not draw those hands. [208] In other patterns, the same pupils had forgotten oars, sails, the shapes of home. [222] By studying broken constellations, Mara could detect a precise absence: not ignorance, but knowledge severed at the joints. [240] The same gaps appeared in her father's notebooks too, beside his experiments with a fairy ring mushroom. [257]

He had served as both harbor physician and keeper of the orchard. [269] His surviving ledger contained a recipe for forgetting, originally administered only at a sufferer's request. [284] After the flood, the handwriting changed. [290] "No one can work," he wrote, and then, beneath thirty-one names, "Mercy cannot wait for consent." [306] She had come looking for a face; now she wanted everything he had stolen, including the grief. [323]

Mara took his oyster knife and waited for the lowest tide. [334] Beyond the harbor lay the sunken orchard greenhouse, ringed by shipwrecks her father had purchased as a breakwater. [352] Their ribs surfaced first, then the greenhouse roof, green glass cracked into sharp little seas. [367] She waded past a trawler's wheelhouse and ducked through a gap in the greenhouse wall. [382] Inside, dead trees filled the air with the impossible fragrance of ripe pears. [395] She remembered that smell without remembering ever smelling it. [404]

Her father's notes described the mushroom as the fruit of a buried network, capable of holding what its donors could no longer bear. [427] Habit kept those memories separate from their owners; repeating the proverb each morning renewed the separation. [443] Without that repetition, the boundaries were thinning. [450] The town had mistaken a prescription for a custom. [459]

At the greenhouse's center, pale caps encircled a tiled well. [469] Beneath each lay a shell marked with a name. [478] Mara found hers beside her aunt's. [484] Her mother's name was absent: these were not graves, but deposits. [495]

The ledger warned against disturbing the knotted threads beneath each cap; cutting alone left the stored memories safely buried. [514] Instead, she worked the oyster knife beneath her mushroom and loosened the earth. [527] Fieldwork had taught her how easily an impatient hand destroys the thing it hopes to understand. [543] She lifted the whole small body. [549]

Her mother returned in fragments: flour on a wrist, impatient laughter, a pear divided with a thumbnail. [566] Her mother's hands covered hers, joining chalk stars into a woman with a basket. [580] Then water hammered the greenhouse doors. [586] Her mother pushed Mara through the roof hatch and turned back for another child. [600] Mara felt again the terrible safety of being carried away. [610]

She knelt until the tide touched her boots. [618] The singing came last, plain and slightly out of tune. [628] Her father had called his theft mercy, but he had taken the bread with the hunger. [644]

Mara carried the mushroom home in her father's empty medicine tin. [655] She did not dig up anyone else's. [662] At the harbor, people waited beside a new bell rope. [672] She told them what lay beneath the greenhouse, and what recovering it would cost. [686] The proverb would still work for anyone who chose it. [696] Her aunt put down the rope and reached for the oyster knife. [708] Together they checked the tide table, a familiar habit still worth keeping. [720]
