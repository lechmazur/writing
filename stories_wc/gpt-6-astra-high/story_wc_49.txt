In the verdant arbor at dawn, Sella opens her teapot toolbox beneath the harvest moon, still floating above the western ridge. [21] By night she spins golden stories for children with a lantern made from a colander; by day she repairs whatever their parents break. [44] This morning, nobody has brought her anything broken. [52] They have brought empty buckets and a saw. [60]

The arbor's ancient vine drinks from the reservoir, but beyond its green shade the winter seedbeds lie powdery and bare. [80] Sella's clockwork gate should have watered them yesterday, at the village's sacred opening of the channels. [96] Under that moon, their grandparents gathered the last grain by night and returned water to the soil at sunrise. [115] Instead, its brass cuckoo announced the hour while its gears chewed helplessly against a vine tendril wrapped around the release pin. [136] Now the miller sets his saw against the vine's trunk. [146]

Sella lays her hand over the teeth. [153] She installed the clock twenty years ago so nobody would need to rise early, sing the opening songs, or bother with the old observances. [177] Her mother alone kept coming, until her burial last winter. [187] Sella thought she came to pray. [193]

"Cutting it down will remedy this for good," the miller says, "and some of us need bread more than shade." [213] He is right about the bread. [219] Sella cannot ask hungry people to preserve something merely because her mother loved it. [233] Yet she remembers that hand, brown and knotted as the vine, resting on the gate after every ceremony. [251]

Beside the empty buckets, a little girl begins singing about a silver bone. [264] Sella recognizes the tune before the words: one of her mother's forgotten songs, smuggled into the lantern tale about a skeleton who planted a garden. [289] The girl has always preferred its nonsense refrain to the ending. [300] "Lift the silver bone," she sings, "and loose the summer's wrist." [311]

Sella looks again at the release pin. [318] The clock turns it, but beneath its head is an old lifting ring, polished where her mother's fingers must have worked. [339] Every year the vine grows across the mechanism; every year someone must free it without severing the shoot that will bear next summer's grapes. [363] Her machine has never done the whole job. [371]

She takes apart the cuckoo, whose slender steel axle fits under the lifting ring, and uses it to lever the pin upward. [393] With the strain released, she unwinds the tendril onto the arbor's lattice. [405] The gate shudders, but she holds it shut; she remembers the next verse now, and the neglected channels beyond the village. [426] "First clear the stranger's furrow, then clear your mother's own." [436]

The miller's field is nearest; without the channels cleared, it will swallow the first rush while the lower plots receive nothing. [457] Sella remembers her mother returning from the arbor with muddy skirts, refusing help because the young people were busy. [476] "I told you the clock could replace the ceremony," Sella says, "but it was only replacing the witnesses." [494]

She asks the miller to take his saw to the fallen branches clogging the lower channel, and gives the girl her little folding shovel. [518] Others set down their buckets. [523] As they go, Sella sings what she remembers, and voices answer from the paths with verses she thought had died with her mother. [546] Through forgotten songs, they find the buried junctions, the stone that divides the flow, the narrow runnel reserved for travelers without land. [568]

The moon touches the ridge as sunlight threads the eastern leaves. [579] Sella waits until the last raised hand appears beside the farthest channel; this, she understands, is when everything aligns. [598] Then she opens the gate. [603]

Water slips beneath the arbor in a gathering hush, and nobody reaches for a bucket until the distant seedbeds darken. [623] The miller returns with his saw wet and muddy, asking whether she can mend the cuckoo before next year's ceremony. [643] Sella promises a bird that calls them here, not one that takes their place. [657] She hangs its empty case beside the vine, where the child is already teaching another child the song. [675]
