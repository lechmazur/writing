After the reincarnation memory, Mara stopped calling the thylacine a specimen. [11] She remembered its striped haunches moving behind wire, and her own small hand holding a key she had been forbidden to use. [33] A girl in Hobart, she had obeyed while the last one shivered outside its locked shelter. [49] Centuries later, the memory arrived with the cold still in it. [60] Now she tended the tidal library above clouds, where seawater climbed hollow mountains twice daily and filled the restoration tanks. [80] Its books held the bodily patterns of vanished creatures, preserved in salt instead of ink. [95] Mara had accepted the solitary post to translate isolation into independence. [106] Nobody could lock her out up here. [113] Then she found the volume labeled Thylacinus and discovered another use for being alone. [127] There would be nobody to forbid her. [134]

[152] The handbook called these failures brittle awakenings: a remembered body could form before it possessed any way of belonging to the present. [174] A reader's voice supplied that missing connection, but only while the reader kept speaking. [188] By the sixth attempt, Mara could sustain a warm, breathing female through an entire afternoon. [222] Then Mara noticed the animal watching the open door with the same fixed attention she remembered from the cage. [241]

[259] The library itself was held together by the hum of an ancient stone circle on its roof. [276] Built when the mountains were islands, the stones converted the pull of tides into a continuous vibration. [293] That vibration let fragile salt structures coexist with living matter without dissolving. [305] Once aligned with it, a restored body could maintain its own cohesion.
