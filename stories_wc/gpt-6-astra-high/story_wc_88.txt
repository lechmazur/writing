After the procedure, Mara watched her daughter burn a finger on the stove and felt only irritation at the noise. [20] The clinic had promised a clean slate, not a heart as empty as a condemned apartment. [36] But guilt and tenderness apparently shared wiring; by evening, her daughter had stopped asking her for anything. [53] Mara could not feel that loss, but she understood its dimensions. [64] She left the girl with her sister and went looking for what the city had removed. [80]

She carried a newspaper from her years at the censorship bureau and a baseball signed by a time traveler. [99] "Jonah Bell, 2146," read the signature, beneath the crooked stitches her father had repaired when she and Jonah were children. [119] Jonah had returned old, carrying their ball and news of a future without the clinics. [134] She had traded his address for an apartment outside the flood zone. [146] The bureau called him a panic merchant, then put him through the procedure before it became fashionable. [163] Now he swept clinic corridors, and the future he remembered interested nobody, including him. [177]

His last warning had survived in her memory: nothing extracted was ever destroyed. [190] Mara followed the disposal pipes beneath the old stadium, where their childhood diamond lay buried under treatment wards. [208] Below them waited an underground bioluminescent lake, blue as a television selling holidays to people who could no longer enjoy them. [229] The discarded emotional tissue had grown into a living archive. [239] Naturally, the city had found a way to make even a miracle into sewage. [253]

A membrane crossed the water in branching paths, delicately robust, trembling beneath her boots without breaking. [269] At every junction, its light raised scenes from the memories fed into it: an unsigned warrant, an unlocked door, Jonah escaping on a train. [293] Some were recollections; others were the merciful alternatives people invented afterward. [304] She followed one in which she had refused the apartment and arrived back at the shore. [320] The labyrinth of choices offered endless innocence and no passage through it. [332]

Then the newspaper began to glow. [338] To save paper, the bureau had bleached rejected editions and printed new assurances over them; living light caught the damaged fibers of erased words. [362] By reading between newspaper lines, Mara found the names she had removed, the arrests she had renamed protective custody. [381] At each branching, she chose the scene that matched the buried account. [393] Her journey shortened as her excuses disappeared. [400]

At the center stood a chair connected to the membrane by translucent threads. [413] Its controls were the same ones she remembered from the clinic, except that the flow could run backward. [431] An engineer had scratched instructions into the metal: restoration began with the memory that had brought the patient to the clinic. [452]

Mara held the baseball, but the lake would not accept a pleasant afternoon of catch as payment. [469] So she remembered Jonah placing it in her hands after his return, his aged fingers waiting for hers to close. [489] He had trusted her with evidence that another future remained possible. [500] She remembered calculating the value of that trust in square feet. [511] When the threads entered her wrists, she almost fled into the comforting version where she had done it all for her daughter. [533] Instead she stayed, and felt his bewilderment before she felt her shame. [545]

By dawn, the ball was wet with something considerably less impressive than luminous water. [559] Mara had wanted to kindle empathy in a city that had none left, preferably without becoming miserable herself. [577] Apparently that discount was unavailable. [582] She climbed out carrying the newspaper, now marked with the route, and used her old bureau key to reach the presses. [603] Her confession appeared beside directions to the lake, with no promise that restoration would feel good. [619] Nobody would be taken there. [624] They would have to choose. [629]

Before going home, she found Jonah sweeping beneath an advertisement for permanent peace. [642] She offered him the ball and told him what she had done, without mentioning the flood zone. [659] He studied the signature as though it belonged to somebody whose mail he sometimes received. [674] He asked whether it would hurt. [680] "Yes," she said, and waited until he reached for it. [690] Once, on the diamond above them, they had known not to throw until the other person was ready. [708]
