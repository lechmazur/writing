After the leash breaks, my first thought is that I have failed somebody again. [14] Brindle vanishes into the fog, dragging the rotten cord toward the veiled ridgeway that rises from our estuary like the back of a sleeping seal. [39] Somewhere beyond it, a boy has missed the last ferry. [49] His mother stands beside me with his other mitten. [58]

We have been keeping a vigil in twilight, listening for footsteps while the tide quietly covers the path. [76] Sisters Ruth and Elin, the oldest and wisest ferry guides on this coast, have already brought their shallow boat against the landing. [98] They know the boy went gathering mussels, and they know a frightened child sometimes hides from rescuers he expects to scold him. [120] That is why we brought his dog. [127]

"You take the tiller, Ada," Ruth says. [134]

Since the spring squall, when I froze and Elin had to steer us home, they have been gently persistent about asking. [155] Each evening I mend nets instead. [161] Tonight there are no nets between my hands and their request. [172]

In my pocket lies the ticket punch with its star stamp, entrusted to me when I became their apprentice. [191] A star means passage home is paid for, no matter how late the passenger returns. [206] I have kept it polished while refusing to earn it. [216]

Ruth says courage does not disappear when we fail; it waits until we have a use for it. [234] I want to find where unused courage waits, but I have always imagined having to be alone when I find it. [255]

Then Brindle barks, three sharp notes, from the far side of the ridge. [268] I step aboard. [271]

I point our bow toward the barking, and Elin lays two fingers on the tiller. [286] "Not across the oyster beds," she says, and I almost surrender my place. [299] But her fingers lift. [303] She tells me to follow the creek inland, away from the sound I want to reach. [319]

To traverse this flooded country safely, we must travel through indirect routes, letting deep water carry us around what shallow water conceals. [341] It feels like leaving the boy behind. [348]

Ruth sits facing me and names each turn before we reach it, her voice falling and rising with the little waves. [369] Past the bent willow, back toward the salt shed, then seaward beneath the ridge. [383] The engine murmurs like someone soothing a cradle. [391]

At last our lamp finds Brindle standing over a small huddled figure on a shelf of stone. [408] The boy holds his bare hand inside his coat. [417] Water strokes the toes of his boots. [424]

"I lost my ticket," he calls before we can speak. [434] He lifts an empty mussel sack as if it proves something terrible about him. [448]

I understand that sack better than I understand the currents. [458]

Holding the boat steady against the tide, I ask Ruth for a ticket from the book beside her knee. [477] She tears one free and holds it out. [485] I take out the punch and press its jaws together, making a clean star that the lamplight shines through. [504]

"Your passage is paid," I tell him. [511] "We came because you belong on the boat." [519]

He lets Elin lift him aboard, and Brindle follows with a shower that smells of mud and oysters. [537] Only then do my hands begin to shake. [545] Ruth wraps her scarf around them without taking the tiller away. [556]

On the return journey I name the turns myself, and she corrects me once, as casually as she might correct a stitch. [578] The boy sleeps against Elin, his new ticket folded inside his mitten. [590] Our lantern moves between the reeds, taking the long way home. [601]

At the landing his mother makes no speech, only a sound so low and tender that the shore seems to hum with it. [624] I tie the boat fast while Ruth closes the ticket book, ending our watch. [638]

Tomorrow I will replace Brindle's leash and inspect the ferry ropes, because courage is not a reason to trust rotten cord. [659] Tonight I hang the ticket punch beside the tiller, where I can reach it from the seat I have stopped avoiding. [680]
