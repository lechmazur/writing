At dusk, when the migrating butterflies settled beneath its cracked glass, the ancient greenhouse became Ruth's workshop for the dead. [20] She was a gravestone carver, thoughtful enough to ask how someone had laughed before deciding how deeply to cut their name. [41] Now the village wanted one stone for the thirty people taken when the river demolished the lower bridge, including her father. [62] They had given her until the last butterflies crossed the valley, before frost made setting foundations impossible. [79] Ruth wanted to discover the shape of collective grief, something truer than thirty names trapped inside a rectangle. [97] She drew circles until their perfection began to feel like an accusation. [109]

Her father appeared on the evening the first orange wings covered the rafters, transparent except for the stone dust on his sleeves. [131] He examined her circles without approval, exactly as he had inspected her first crooked letters. [146] "You have drawn something nobody can enter," he said. [155] Ruth reached for him, but her fingers passed through his wrist and struck the drawing board. [171] She sat until the pain subsided, fiercely meditative, refusing either to dismiss him or to mistake his return for restoration. [191] Outside, the river poured through the ruined weir with a thundering calm. [203]

"Start with supper," he told her, and she nearly hated him for offering another lesson instead of an embrace. [222] In their family, dinner had always involved trading favorite poems, each person reciting someone else's offering before sharing their own. [242] Her father had called it learning where another person's breath must fall. [254] The following dusk, Ruth brought soup to the greenhouse and invited the bereaved to continue the custom. [271]

Seven came, then twelve, carrying verses on receipts or folded inside the damp wallets recovered downstream. [287] Beneath the resting butterflies, a widow recited a comic rhyme her husband loved, laughing until she frightened herself. [305] A boy refused every poem except the unfinished one his brother had written about wanting to leave the village. [324] Repeating its last line, Ruth finally heard that his anger belonged beside the widow's laughter, not underneath it. [342]

Each evening brought fewer butterflies and more chairs, but no two mourners described the same absence. [358] Ruth's father stood behind the empty chair she kept for him, listening where once he would have corrected the meter. [378] When she asked which sorrow the monument should express, he shook his head. [391] "I can teach you to cut letters, but I cannot hear for you." [404] It was the first time she remembered him admitting there was something he could not teach. [420]

From his old tool chest she took a woven ribbon roll, used for measuring curves that a rigid ruler could not follow. [442] She began to retrace the paths people took between chairs when another person's poem drew them close. [459] Unrolling the ribbon along the floor, she marked where they paused, where they turned aside, where they left room for silence. [480] The resulting line bent around the table without enclosing it. [490]

She proposed a low stone bench following that broken curve, with names along its back and gaps wide enough for anyone to enter. [513] The village council complained that they had commissioned a monument, not furniture. [525] Ruth brought them to dinner instead of answering. [533] After the boy recited his brother's poem, the council chairman moved his chair beside him and said nothing. [551] By morning, the quarrymen had offered stone. [558]

Through the migration's final week, Ruth carved while neighbors dug foundations beside the greenhouse and fitted the bench's heavy sections. [578] At dusk her father still inspected her lettering, but his sleeves grew clearer as the rafters emptied of wings. [597] On the last evening, he asked her for a poem, and Ruth chose the foolish rhyme that had made the widow laugh. [619] He repeated it badly, forgetting a line, and for once she did not help him. [634] Then a butterfly lifted from the glass, his sleeve became the color of evening, and the space behind his chair was only space. [657]

Ruth sat on the cold bench until the boy arrived with bread and asked whether there would still be supper tomorrow. [678] "Yes," she said, shifting to make room. [685] Across the valley, the last orange wings passed beyond the broken bridge, while the river kept its enormous, unanswering voice. [705]
