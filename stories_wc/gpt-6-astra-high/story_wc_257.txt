After hitting rock bottom, Mara slept beneath the observatory steps, her last engraving tools pawned and her husband's hair still hidden inside her wedding ring. [25] She had made mourning jewelry for twenty years, but since his death she could finish nothing that required a clasp. [45]

The caretaker found her there and offered a room in exchange for repairing the mechanical orchard beneath the great telescope. [65] Inside the timeless observatory, pendulums hung motionless, and bread stayed warm until someone carried it outside. [81] The orchard, however, had found a way to die. [90] Silver leaves curled around blackened stems; little brass pears lay scattered on the tiles. [104]

"The curtains of intention," the caretaker explained, pointing to the gauze surrounding the telescope, "admit only the futures we can imagine." [125] The astronomers who built the orchard had watched a plague empty the city, then abandoned their instruments behind tightly closed curtains. [146] The trees were supposed to flower in that light. [155]

Mara examined every joint and found no broken part. [164] When she pulled the gauze aside, it gathered again behind her fingers, soft and stubborn as the clothes in her husband's wardrobe. [186]

"You want me to teach withering to bloom again," she said. [197] "I can only make things last." [203]

Nevertheless, she stayed; the room had a door she could close, and the caretaker lent her tools without asking for promises. [224] In her bag she found a tattoo stencil on vellum, drawn for her husband before the fever took him. [243] A nurseryman, he had wanted a grafted pear branch on his wrist, the binding clearly visible beneath its leaves. [262] She had offered to disguise the wound. [269]

"Then how would anyone know it lived?" he had asked. [279]

Under a work lamp, she used the stencil to trace a new branch onto a sheet of silver. [297] Instead of restoring the orchard's original blossoms, she fashioned a conspicuous graft, attaching a hinged bud to one withered stem. [317] It was genuinely artificial: silver, wire, a rivet for a knuckle, with no attempt to counterfeit living bark. [335] She could offer it a future without pretending it had never been hurt. [348]

Still, the curtains remained shut when she set it beneath the telescope. [360] She discovered she could imagine the branch opening, but not herself being there to see it. [376]

So she returned to the city with the vellum folded safely inside her coat. [390] For seven mornings she walked her former delivery route, leaving origami figures with coordinates inside at the doors of old customers. [411] They were paper pears, folded from discarded jewelry invoices; the numbers located the observatory on the city's survey grid. [430] Beside the coordinates she wrote, "Bring something you cannot throw away." [441] She made no promise to cure anyone. [448]

Five people came, including a baker carrying a child's shoe and a woman whose pocket held the key to a demolished house. [470] Mara nearly apologized for bringing them to another room full of dead things. [483] Instead, she showed them the graft and asked what small thing they might be willing to do after leaving. [502]

Nobody answered until the baker said he might bake a loaf small enough for one person. [518] A thin seam opened in the gauze. [525] The woman with the key wanted to plant beans somewhere she intended to stay. [539] Starlight began to illuminate the silver branch, catching the blunt edge of its binding. [553]

Mara understood then why the observatory had needed an orchard rather than a monument: its instruments required futures that someone would actually enter. [576] She looked down at her wedding ring. [583]

"I will reopen my shop," she said, and felt the curtain hesitate. [595] That was too large, too polished, a declaration made for witnesses. [606]

"Tomorrow I will mend the clasp on my own necklace." [616]

The gauze loosened, and the grafted bud opened into a flower unlike anything her husband had grown. [633] No one clapped; the baker touched the child's shoe, and Mara let herself want to make another flower. [651] They left their keepsakes beside the tree while they worked out which hinges might carry the next grafts. [669] Later, each took their own grief home. [676]

Mara pinned the stencil above her bench, not under glass, and carried her supper outside, where it could cool. [695]
