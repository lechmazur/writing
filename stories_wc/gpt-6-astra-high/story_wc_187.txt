After fog became solid, Mara climbed the iron stair to the stormlit observatory above clouds. [15] Beneath her, lightning illuminated the frozen whiteness like something struggling inside an egg. [28] She was the city's summer-quill archivist, carrying a feather that had spent June absorbing sunlight. [43] Its stored warmth could melt winter fog without destroying the memories trapped within it. [57]

The observatory's crystal forecourt whispered after rain, speaking in the voices of everyone whose breath had joined the fog. [76] Beyond it stood monuments containing the city's collective memory: harvests, migrations, marriages, disasters. [89] Each testimony the archivist inscribed raised its monument a little; each deletion lowered it. [103] What vanished from those stones vanished from public recollection, surviving only in frozen fog until the spring thaw. [121]

Mara loved the work with a preemptively nostalgic tenderness, already mourning each morning's lamplight while she trimmed the wick. [140] Her father had trained her to preserve happiness first, because winter was long and people needed reasons to endure it. [160] Since his death, she had polished his statue every week. [170] Its inscription named him Keeper of the Bread. [178]

That noon, examining shadow lengths at monuments, she discovered that the memorial to the famine had shrunk. [195] She corrected her measurements for the winter sun, checked last year's ledger, and measured again. [210] Three feet of remembered suffering were missing, although her father's shadow still reached the same brass marker. [227] The council had authorized routine corrections; she had signed the order without reading its attachments. [242] Now she could only conjecture what those comfortable words concealed. [252]

She fetched a fragment of frozen fog from the sealed famine vault and suspended it above the forecourt. [270] Under her quill's warmth, the fragment softened into rain. [279] The first drops made the crystal murmur like a kitchen heard through a bedroom wall. [294] Then a woman asked for flour, and Mara recognized the voice of the laundress who had braided her hair. [313] Her father answered that the granary would open when the striking millers returned to work. [328] Behind his voice came another sound: a child chewing. [337] Mara remembered that bread, its crust softened in milk. [346]

She sat beside the crystal until the drops stopped. [355] Below the parapet, thunder continued, but the forecourt settled into a hush deeper than silence, crowded with people waiting to finish. [376] She had entered the archive to sculpt an identity from experience, believing careful choices could make a life beautiful. [395] Instead, she had been trimming herself into her father's favorite shape. [406]

The council's attachments lay beneath her signature in a drawer. [416] They described the millers' deaths as divisive material and requested that the remaining testimonies be discarded before thaw. [434] Restoring them would expose not only the Keeper, but the daughter whose negligence had helped absolve him. [451] She pictured losing her rooms, her office, the neighbors who still left flowers at his feet. [467] For a moment she wanted spring to come early. [476]

Then she carried every fragment from the famine vault onto the forecourt and drew her warm quill across them. [495] Rain gathered in the crystal channels; voices rose, overlapping, until she nearly reached for the sluice that would drain them away. [516] Instead she listened, separating names from pleas, recording even the testimony of a miller who had stolen another family's ration. [536] The dead did not become innocent merely because her father had wronged them. [549] She inscribed until the quill grew cold and the famine monument stood taller than the Keeper. [565]

By morning, people were climbing the iron stair, carrying scraps of paper on which they had written the memories returning in their sleep. [588] When the council's messenger demanded Mara's quill, she surrendered it, but the people stayed around the famine monument. [606] Nobody shouted, and nobody moved aside when he ordered the masons through. [618] The laundress stood nearest Mara, close enough for their sleeves to touch, without taking her hand. [634]

Mara unfolded one last testimony, written with ordinary ink because there was no sunlight left in her feather. [652] It described a father warming milk for his daughter while other children waited outside a locked granary. [669] She read it without asking anyone to forgive him, or her, and the laundress corrected the name of one child. [689] Mara crossed it out carefully and wrote the name again. [699]
