At midnight, the grain elevator shouted through the speaking tube in the astronomical clock tower, waking everyone beneath Mara's music shelves. [21] As choral librarian, she could coax a harmonious chord from rival congregations, but she had never conducted a building. [40] It cried in Polish, Spanish, then the dockworkers' Arabic, each voice strangled by a fluttering valve. [56] The refugees stirred under the hush of borrowed starlight, cast across the ceiling from glass photographs salvaged from the observatory. [76]

"They're going to empty me of everybody," the elevator rasped. [86]

At dawn, crews would dismantle its six steel silos, whose curved panels the town engineer had designed into emergency footbridges. [106] The flood had torn three neighborhoods apart, leaving the hospital on one bank and most of its patients on another. [126] Mara had championed the plan to construct bridges from silos, and the petition downstairs carried her signature first. [144]

Now the elevator was pleading in her mother's voice. [153]

Her mother had graded wheat there for forty years, singing over the machinery until retirement stole her audience. [171] Mara had catalogued thousands of workers' songs without imagining that their workplace, too, had been keeping copies. [188] She knelt beside the tube and tried her mother's lullaby, interspersing languages at the places where each verse offered shelter. [208] The elevator answered, but the valve kept chopping names into sobs. [219]

Above her desk, her mother's cuckoo clock ticked with intolerable composure. [230] Mara unhooked the cuckoo clock weight and fastened its chain to the valve's exposed lever, counterbalancing pressure from the distant silos. [251] The little clock stopped; the enormous voice steadied. [259]

"My songs live in the headhouse," it told her, "but your engineer has marked that for demolition too." [277]

Mara summoned Engineer Anik from the stairwell, where he was sleeping upright with a soaked blueprint under his coat. [296] He spread it over the star projector, blotting out Orion with a river. [309]

"The headhouse braces the silos," he said, "and leaving it unsupported would put the crews at risk." [326]

On the astronomical clock, the moon hand crept toward the mark for the next high tide. [342] They could not spend another day arguing while the stranded sick waited for boats that might not come. [360] Mara pressed her palm against the tube, feeling the old voices tremble. [372]

"We need your steel, but we do not need your silence," she said. [385]

She asked Anik what it would take to rehabilitate the headhouse rather than destroy it. [400]

"Independent supports, and the original foundation drawings, which drowned with the municipal archive." [413]

From the tube came a Polish phrase Mara recognized from a builders' counting song. [427] She repeated it in Spanish, then Arabic, inviting the other crews' memories to answer rather than compete. [444] Depths emerged in one language, distances in another; Anik translated the numbers into lines, stopping often to ask the elevator questions. [465] It remembered separate footings beneath its oldest room, buried when the storage yard was paved. [480]

Anik sent two workers to uncover them while Mara kept the voices singing, gently separating recollection from repetition. [498] When confirmation came, she made her choice without asking the frightened building to become fearless. [513]

"Brace the headhouse first, then take the silo panels, and stop whenever Anik says stop." [528]

To the elevator she promised singers, not storage: a rehearsal hall where nobody would have to shout over grinding wheat. [548]

"Will I still sound like myself?" it asked. [556]

"Not exactly," Mara said, and let it hear her grief before she asked for its consent. [572]

The valve lifted against the cuckoo weight, then settled into one long, deliberate note. [586] By sunrise, hammers were answering it across the water. [595]

When the first footbridge opened, Mara walked beside a stretcher, one hand on the rail that had once held grain. [615] From the rescued headhouse came a thinner voice, uncertain in its changed body, trying out her mother's lullaby. [633] She answered on the next verse, and people crossing from both banks joined wherever their words belonged. [650]

Back in the tower, the cuckoo would never announce that hour; its weight was still helping someone else speak. [669]
