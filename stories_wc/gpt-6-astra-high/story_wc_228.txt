After the mirror cracked, Elias found the painted seashell wedged between its silvered glass and the frame. [17] Tom had knocked it with a packing crate, and now stood watching his father brush glass from a tiny blue boat with wings. [40] "I thought you'd thrown that away," Tom said. [48] "I kept it out of sunlight," Elias answered, as if preservation explained everything. [61]

For twenty years he had been the quay's nostalgic painter, selling pictures of the old ferry, the demolished fish market, the harbor before the cranes. [86] He remembered customers' birthdays and dead dogs, an intimately aloof man who could paint anyone's childhood without mentioning his own. [106] Upstairs, his wife's coat still occupied the best hook, although Tom had been seven when she died and was leaving tomorrow to survey distant coastlines. [131]

Elias slipped the shell into his pocket, remembering a promise he had kept badly, and asked for one last walk. [151] "Your mother loved the caves," he added, and saw Tom's face close. [163] Nevertheless, his son fetched two torches and checked the tide tables. [174]

Beneath the headland, the coastal cave network breathed through narrow passages, drawing their sleeves toward darkness as the falling sea sucked air behind it. [198] Elias intended to reach the western arch, where his wife used to sit with her feet in the foam. [217] He remembered every color of those mornings, but not which branch of the tunnel they had taken. [234] At a fork he chose left, confidently, and soon the familiar limestone gave way to a muddy crawl. [252]

"Wrong turn," he said, already retreating. [258] Tom lifted his torch toward a root hanging through the rock. [269] "There's daylight ahead, and we're still above the tide mark." [279] Elias nearly insisted on the arch, then moved aside. [288]

The crawl opened into a hidden oasis beneath a collapsed chimney of stone, where a fig tree leaned over a clear pool. [310] Ferns crowded a ledge watered by a spring, and sunlight struck the pool so directly that its reflected leaves seemed rooted in another sky. [334] Tom knelt beside a submerged groove leading back toward the sea. [345] He explained how the spring filled the basin at low water, until the returning tide muddied it and broke the reflection. [366]

Elias reached for the sketchbook he always carried, wanting to hold the leaves precisely where they were. [383] Then he looked at Tom, whose muddy knees and absorbed expression belonged to no scene he had ever painted. [402] His son knew things he did not know, had become someone while Elias was busy restoring a world that no longer existed. [424]

"On the night we buried her, I promised myself you wouldn't grow up inside a shrine," he said. [442] He had never meant to reveal that private vow, least of all when its failure stood so plainly between them. [462] Tom watched the water for a while before answering. [471] "I used to think leaving would make me a bad son." [482]

Elias wanted to protest, but protest would only give Tom another feeling to look after. [497] Instead he took out the shell, with its impossible flying ferry, and laid it on his open palm. [515] "You gave me a way out, and I put it behind glass." [527] Tom smiled without quite forgiving him, which Elias understood was more generous than reassurance. [541]

[560] For once, Elias did not need the moment to survive unchanged in order to believe it had been real. [579] When Tom checked his watch, his father rose without asking for another minute. [592]

[615] Outside, Elias asked where the survey boat would take him first, and listened until Tom had finished explaining. [633] At the quay he offered his son the shell. [642]
