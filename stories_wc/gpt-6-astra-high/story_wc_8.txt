After the final petal dropped from the old rosebush, the demolition crew unlocked the gates of Bellweather Station. [18] The permit required this small courtesy to the flood memorial, though nothing required the council to save the building. [37] Ivy had swallowed the departure boards, and its roots were lifting the platform where the last relief train had stood. [57]

Historian Mara Venn arrived carrying an illuminated letter in a transparent conservation sleeve and the book that had made her respectable. [78] Behind her came fourteen people who had slept in the waiting room until the council evicted them in February. [97] They intended to recapture their shelter; Mara needed to recapture something less comfortable. [110]

The foreman recognized her from the photograph beside the station's plaque. [121] Her book had supplied its inscription: HERE THE TOWN SAVED ITS PEOPLE. [133]

"My grandfather worked that train," he said, putting himself between her and the gate. [147]

Mara showed him the letter, whose painted roses wound around the king's thanks to the stationmaster. [163] Yesterday, removing it from its frame for the municipal museum, she had discovered what the cardboard mount concealed. [181] The final paragraph praised the stationmaster's refusal to admit families without property deeds, thereby preventing "disorder among the deserving." [200] In the council's transcript, which Mara had trusted twenty years ago, that paragraph became a row of decorative stars. [219] Even the roses had served the deception: the memorial bush was planted to reproduce the border of this supposed testament to universal rescue. [242]

"Those people drowned outside the fence," she said. [250]

He looked at the waiting room rather than the page. [260] "My grandfather carried children through those doors." [267]

Mara considered herself emotionally rational: anger told her where to look, not what she was entitled to find. [285] The letter named the stationmaster, not every railway worker, and she would not manufacture another certainty to replace her first. [305]

"He may have saved them," she said. [312] "That does not mean everyone was allowed aboard." [320]

Her phone vibrated with the mayor's third call. [328] When she had sent him photographs that morning, he had called the missing paragraph an unfortunate necessity. [345] Towns needed noble lies, he said, stories generous enough for former enemies to inhabit together. [360] She had nearly accepted that argument because it offered absolution for her book. [373] But she had wanted to rescue truth from a pit of lies, not build herself a prettier room above it. [393]

Now she opened the book to a photograph of the station gates and placed the council's demolition notice beside it. [413] She could reach him by drawing parallels, not by demanding that he despise his grandfather. [428] The notice said the waiting room's occupants had been "transferred to appropriate accommodation." [441] Beside her, a woman unrolled the bus voucher she had received instead of a bed. [456] She had kept it dry through six weeks of rain. [466]

"Both accounts say everyone was taken care of," Mara said. [476] "Both depend on deciding that some people do not count." [486]

The foreman read the voucher, then the letter. [494] Behind him, a driver revved an engine, and he raised his hand without turning. [508]

"A day," he said, unlocking the inner gate. [516] "I can report a dispute over historical material, but tomorrow they will send somebody else." [531]

The fourteen entered quietly, carrying blankets, a kettle, and no assurance that the council would let them stay beyond tomorrow. [551] Mara photographed the letter beside its misleading transcript and sent both to the newspaper, with an account of her own mistake. [572] Then she called a solicitor, because exposing a falsehood did not automatically create a right to shelter. [589]

The woman with the voucher asked whether the newspaper would want pictures of them looking grateful. [605] Mara lowered her phone. [609] She had already begun arranging the morning into a story with herself at its useful center. [625]

"You can tell them what happened," she said, and handed over the reporter's number. [639]

While the kettle heated, they made two lists on the back of the demolition notice: beds needed tonight, witnesses willing to speak. [661] Outside, the foreman covered the rosebush's exposed roots with damp sacking before taking his crew away. [677] Mara left the plaque untouched and propped the sleeved letter beneath it, where none of its words could hide. [696]
