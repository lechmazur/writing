Nera, the pioneering cart glider of the southern wastes, had crossed the acid sea on thermal winds and landed with her eyebrows on fire. [24] The newspapers celebrated the crossing, although she considered extinguishing herself the greater technical achievement. [38]

Now her sailcart hung above a floating workshop where gears and cogs turned behind windows filmed yellow by corrosive spray. [58] She was building an ear. [63]

The coastal fever had destroyed her eardrums in childhood, as it had her mother's and almost everyone else's along the shore. [84] Her mother still polished the harbor bell, feeling its vibrations through the rope when fog swallowed the returning gliders. [103] "Someday this will bring you home," she signed whenever Nera left, and pulled the rope. [118]

Nera wanted to ring that bell for someone who could hear it; her mother's faith deserved better than mere vibration. [138] She gave that stubbornness to a calculating engine, calling its instructions the yearning algorithm. [152] It compared a desired vibration with whatever survived transmission through artificial skin, then designed another receiver to narrow the difference. [172]

Without microphones, she measured those vibrations through the images in a kaleidoscope, whose mirrored chamber held silver dust on a stretched membrane. [194] Each tone shook the dust into a distinct constellation. [203] She would transliterate those glittering shapes into punched instructions, making stars speak the engine's blunt mechanical alphabet. [220] God, if present, had apparently subcontracted miracles to a woman with greasy fingernails and unpaid buoyancy fees. [237]

Throughout the evolution from crude listening trumpets to delicate pressure plates, Nera remained steadily curious, even when curiosity required dismantling yesterday's triumph. [259] The engine, unfortunately, grew less flexible. [265] By winter it demanded a needle of spring steel, thinner and tougher than anything her workshop could supply. [283]

The only suitable metal was a hunter's arrow tip embedded in her sailcart's steering joint. [298] During her first crossing, an island hunter had mistaken the descending sail for a scavenger kite and fired. [316] The broken tip now served as a makeshift locking pin; removing it meant sacrificing her only reliable passage home. [335]

She extracted it anyway, cursing the hunter, then thanking him, which seemed a reasonably balanced prayer. [351] Ground to a slender point, it carried vibrations from a receiver's diaphragm into the artificial skin with exquisite precision. [370] The kaleidoscope bloomed into perfect stars, and the engine stamped its brass completion seal. [384]

Against Nera's ear, the receiver produced nothing. [391]

She nearly threw the machine's seal into the sea, but stopped with her arm raised. [406] The algorithm had answered the question she had asked, not the one she meant. [420] She had taught it to yearn for a beautiful measurement. [430]

Outside, a loose anchor knocked against the hull; through the floor, she felt its blows in her ankles. [448] Why insist on the ruined doorway when the whole damned house could carry a knock? [463]

She pressed the receiver against her jaw, carefully shielding its point, and struck her little test bell. [480] Something silver opened inside her skull. [486] Not the memory of sound, but sound itself, thin and outrageous and quite indifferent to whether she deserved it. [505] She laughed, heard an unfamiliar rasp, and laughed harder. [514]

Then she changed the algorithm's target: not a perfect pattern, but a vibration a living person could recognize. [532] Every new design required someone to answer, and sometimes the answer was no. [545]

By spring, she had a comfortable jawpiece, imperfect but useful, and a steering pin hammered from three inferior metals. [564] She glided home slowly, skimming low enough that the acid stippled her boots, refusing to trust that pin with any heroics. [585]

At the harbor, her mother accepted the jawpiece with the patient expression she reserved for expensive ideas. [602] Nera fitted the receiver, placed her mother's hand on the rope, and closed her own hand around it. [620] They pulled together. [623]

Her mother's knees buckled, but she held the rope and pulled again, fiercely, as if hauling someone out of deep water. [644] Nera steadied her, feeling the bell through their joined hands while her mother listened. [658] There would have to be another receiver, and then another; already the unfinished world was making demands. [675]

For once, she let it wait. [681]
