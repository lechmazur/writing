After the last ship sailed, its lamps dwindling like sparks over black water, I found a woman waiting in the palace's shadowed garden. [23] Autumn had filled the marble fountains with copper leaves, and the empty windows held the last gold of evening. [42]

She knew my profession from the notice outside my lodging: obituaries written for the living. [57] Along this coast, people called them explained absences, accounts of those who had emigrated, deserted, or simply ceased answering letters. [77] I traveled to understand the geography of healing, although for seven years I had avoided the town where my wife lived. [98] Giving other people's losses an ending was easier than admitting that mine could still change. [113]

"My daughter was aboard," the woman said, pointing beyond the garden wall. [125] "Write that she loved me." [130]

Her name was Mara, and she had spent forty years repairing the palace carpet on her knees. [147] From her sleeve she drew a crimson weft thread, saved when the court dismissed its servants for winter. [165] A strand from that carpet could project a remembered presence into empty air; kings had used it to dine with their dead. [187]

Mara stretched it between her wrists and asked me to hold the middle. [200] The thread warmed against my finger. [206] Beside the fountain appeared a girl of twelve, solemnly carrying a bowl of cherries. [220] Sunlight shone on her bare shoulders, the private weather of a vanished summer. [233]

Mara leaned forward and pressed her lips to the girl's hair. [244]

"Ina is nearly thirty now," she whispered, as though apologizing for an inaccurate measurement. [258]

I opened my book across my knees and asked what had taken her away. [272] Work, Mara said, then adventure, then the ingratitude that afflicted children raised without fathers. [286] Each explanation tightened the thread until a red groove marked my skin. [298] The girl kept offering cherries. [303]

I wrote that Ina had been devoted to her mother, but the sentence looked less like a tribute than a locked gate. [325]

"Try remembering her this morning," I said. [332]

Mara's hands trembled, and the child flickered into a woman wearing a patched traveling coat. [347] There was tenderness in her face, but exhaustion beneath it, dark as bruising. [360]

"I cannot be your whole country," the remembered daughter said. [370]

Mara flinched, and I nearly released the thread, as if pain meant the ceremony had failed. [386] But she caught my hand and held it still. [395]

"I thought if she stayed, everything I endured would have been worth something," she said. [410]

Across the water, the ship's last light disappeared. [418] I thought of my wife asking for one supper without an account of what she owed me. [435] For years, I had written her departure as cruelty, revising every detail except my own. [450]

Under my finger, the weft ran across Mara's palms: the traveling thread, not the fixed warp, binding separate lengths without making them one. [473]

"She gave me an address," Mara said. [480]

With her free hand she unfolded a scrap of paper soft from being clenched. [494] On its back, Ina had drawn the route from the mainland harbor to her lodgings, including a bakery marked with a star. [516]

"She said their bread tastes like mine." [523]

The woman beside the fountain waited with the terrible patience of an image that could offer nothing new. [541] Mara loosened her wrists, and Ina unraveled into the garden's blue shadows. [553]

She did not look healed. [558] She looked cold, and tired of standing, and able, at last, to admit both things. [573] I put my coat around her shoulders and crossed out the obituary. [585]

Together we began a letter that described the garden without making its emptiness an accusation. [600] We told Ina about the copper leaves, the fox sleeping beneath the sundial, and the pears Mara would preserve until spring. [621] There would be no post until the thaw, which left time for another letter, and another, none pretending to be the last. [643]

Mara tucked the crimson thread between the pages, no longer stretched tight enough to bring anyone back. [660] Before returning to my lodging, I copied my wife's address onto a clean sheet. [674] For once, I did not try to imagine her reading it. [685] I asked whether she would welcome another, and left enough room beneath the question for an answer I could not write. [706]
