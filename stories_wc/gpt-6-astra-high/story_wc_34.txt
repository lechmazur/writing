In the misty courtyard above clouds, lightning caught the copper weathercock, and the world beyond the balustrade abruptly stopped moving. [20] The rain climbed instead, bead by bead, into the city's emergency clock. [32] Nell had between lightning and thunder to teach the engine what its passengers could not bring themselves to ask. [51] Her mother had designed that borrowed interval for repairs, before becoming the machine's most persistent malfunction. [67]

Nell was a seldom-heard balladeer, paid to sing beneath the turbines because their grinding frightened children awake. [84] The council measured her usefulness in sleeping babies, never in listeners. [95] She had become driftingly alert: half dreaming through familiar verses, half listening for the little catches where someone swallowed a request. [116] People wanted warmer rooms, somewhere to grow something useless, an hour without the turbines chewing every word. [133] After years of rationing, wanting had begun to feel like theft. [144]

Only the courtyard walls admitted otherwise. [150] Across their cracked blue plaster, anonymous painters had given birds blankets and drawn gardens inside open mouths. [167] Nell had come to articulate those unspoken needs through messages encoded in graffiti art, using a language her mother had hidden there. [189] The maintenance eyes could read it, although the council's inspectors saw only decorative weeds. [203] Her mother had preferred systems that let small things slip through. [214]

Nell unwrapped a flower press bolt plate, the last piece of her mother's workshop not surrendered for scrap. [232] Its four bolt holes established the corners of a maintenance glyph; the tiny perforations between them supplied its alphabet. [251] Once it had flattened alpine violets collected before the city fled the flooded mountains. [265] Now Nell rubbed charcoal through its openings, turning the painted blankets and gardens into instructions. [280] She hummed as she worked, giving each stroke the length of a note, because her mother had built the cipher around their planting song. [304]

The maintenance eyes clicked awake, and a violet appeared on the inspection screen. [317] One petal was crooked, exactly as Nell always drew it when she was six. [331] The ghost in code had recognized her. [338] During the evacuation, her mother had copied her mind into the engine to navigate passages no automatic pilot could survive. [358] Everybody called the continued transmissions an echo, but echoes did not correct their daughters' arithmetic. [373]

HEAT DIVERTIBLE, the screen offered, followed by diagrams of pipes that could warm bedrooms and a greenhouse. [390] Then it drew a bird with its beak stitched shut. [400] Nell stopped humming. [403] She had spent eleven years trying to make the engine speak, never wondering whether keeping it speaking was kind. [422] Above the balustrade, the clockwork bellows heaved and wheezed, and the clock held only a trembling necklace of rain. [441]

She used the plate to encode a question beneath the bird: WOULD YOU LIKE TO REST? [457] The screen filled with calculations, then erased them for a single, almost apologetic YES. [471] The city no longer needed a mind to steer it; its automatic pilots could manage ordinary weather. [488] But Nell had renewed the waking command every year, calling it maintenance, calling it love. [503] Her thumb found a violet stain still sheltered beneath the plate's rust. [515] She remembered her mother closing the press, saying that holding something safely was different from keeping it alive. [533]

Nell canceled the waking command and gave the ghost control of its own clock. [547] Then she approved the new heat routes and the slower nighttime turbine settings her mother had tucked beneath the greenhouse plans. [568] Inside the walls, valves chimed as they turned, little brass throats practicing the first three notes of spring. [586] The violet on the screen folded its petals, leaving one small green bud lit. [600] Not gone, Nell understood, and not obliged to bloom. [609]

With the last charcoal, she added an empty speech ribbon to the mural at a height a child could reach. [629] She hung the bolt plate beside it, no longer a relic but a tool. [643] Tomorrow she would teach the planting song, and this time she would ask to be heard. [659] The final raindrop slipped into the clock, and Nell lifted her face toward the first soft edge of thunder. [678]
