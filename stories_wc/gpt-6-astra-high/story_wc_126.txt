Three years after the rainforest burned, Mara returned to a harbor where nobody called across the water. [17] Once an echo-harbor violinist, she had played toward the cliffs and let their answering notes finish her tunes. [35] Now the boats were gone, and the survivors' chronic hush seemed deeper than the absence of birds. [52]

Her aunt lived in the sunken orchard greenhouse ringed by shipwrecks, the last place still growing anything sweet. [70] The wrecks broke the waves; behind them, patched glass held a little warmth above trees planted below the tide. [89] A surviving seawall kept their roots dry. [96]

Mara had come for cuttings, hoping to start an orchard inland, but her aunt met her with empty hands. [115]

"Nothing leaves," Aunt Sella said, touching a pear branch wrapped in faded blue cloth. [129]

During the evacuation, a storm shattered the roof; afterward, Sella splinted broken limbs with strips of clothing from the abandoned houses. [150] She had stitched the missing people's names into their cloth, giving each a tree to belong to. [167] The blue sleeve had belonged to Mara's father. [175]

Mara set down her violin and listened. [182] Always acoustically perceptive, she could distinguish a loose peg from a cracked bridge before another player noticed anything wrong. [201] Now she heard a small, dry complaint whenever the wind moved the pear tree. [215] Beneath the sleeve, new wood bulged against a cord that had begun to disappear into the bark. [232]

"We have to unbind these," she said. [239]

Sella caught her wrist before she could reach the knot. [249] "Your father tied that one himself, before he went back for the others." [262]

Mara let her hand fall. [267] She had once believed that bringing music home would cure the silence, as though grief were an audience waiting to be entertained. [289] Instead, she asked Sella where her grandfather's mendicant's bowl had gone. [300]

The old man had begged his meals along this coast, offering stories when he had nothing else to give. [319] His iron bowl stood under a leak. [326] Mara emptied it, filled it with coals from the stove, and carried them to the stone landing outside. [344] There she built a bonfire from fallen orchard wood. [353]

Before the fires, her family had gathered around such a blaze every spring, passing the bowl from hand to hand. [373] Whoever held it owed the others a story. [381] Tonight Mara invited the neighbors, not to remember the dead properly, but to help her remember something badly. [399]

"There was a fisherman who caught the moon," Mara began, turning the empty bowl between her palms. [416]

Sella remained beyond the firelight, her arms folded. [424]

"He put it in his soup, I think." [432]

"His boot," said a neighbor, and somebody made a rusty sound that became a laugh. [447]

The bowl traveled slowly, gathering no money, only corrections. [456] When it reached Sella, she sat down. [463]

"Your father never told the ending," she said. [471] "He wanted us to argue about what happened next." [480]

Mara remembered lying against his knees, furious that the fisherman might spend his whole life with one luminous foot. [499] She had spent years learning to finish every phrase beautifully, leaving no uncertainty for anyone to enter. [516]

Across the fire, Sella rubbed the bowl's worn rim with her thumb. [528]

"I thought keeping his knot meant keeping his hands." [537]

Mara wanted to promise her something equal to that loss, but there was nothing she could honestly offer. [555] She held out her own hands instead. [562]

At dawn they worked together, moistening the old knots until the cords loosened without tearing the tender bark. [580] Sella folded each named strip into the bowl; none went into the ashes. [593] When the blue sleeve came free, the branch lifted only a finger's width, but Mara heard its complaint cease. [612]

Sella chose three young shoots for the inland orchard. [621] "You will have to learn the grafting," she said, and began explaining where to cut. [636]

That evening Mara played beside the greenhouse while Sella sorted the cloth into pieces large enough to shade seedlings. [655] She left her father's tune unfinished, not because she had forgotten it, but because she wanted to hear what followed. [675] There was water against the wrecks, the brush of cloth, her aunt asking whether pears would like the inland rain. [695] For once, Mara did not need the cliffs to answer. [705]
