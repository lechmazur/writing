When the story changes from a flood forecast to a list of stranded people, Mara Venn unlocks the disused railway station beneath its curtains of ivy. [26] An elusive disaster relief logistician, she has spent twenty years borrowing warehouses without permission and vanishing before anybody can thank or prosecute her. [49] Today she pins a notice to the ticket window: PLEASE DISREGARD ALL REGULATIONS THAT PREVENT YOU FROM SAVING A NEIGHBOR. [69] Underneath, in smaller letters, she adds: PLEASE APOLOGIZE AFTERWARD. [78]

The station stands on the only dry embankment between the flooded retirement home and the municipal fuel depot. [96] Mara needs a boat, diesel, and people capable of carrying frightened passengers down the home's submerged front steps. [114] She has none of these things, and the depot manager will release fuel only to registered emergency services. [132] "Excellent," she tells the empty waiting room, desperately jocular as always when afraid, "we've eliminated the inconvenience of having options." [151]

From her pocket she takes a fragment of moon rock, certified lunar meteorite, inherited from her father along with his conviction that solitude was freedom. [176] His death went unnoticed for eleven days. [183] She has carried the stone through thirteen disasters, secretly hoping that somewhere among so many people needing one another she will find the cure for loneliness. [209] Instead, she always leaves before needing anybody herself. [217]

With the mobile network down, she tries the station telephone and discovers that nobody ever disconnected the line. [235] She calls a baker from an earlier evacuation, offers a piece of the moon for a rescue boat, and jokes that she could also use company. [261] "No celestial bodies accepted on credit," he replies, but promises to ask around. [274]

By way of his estranged wife's hairdresser, a pigeon breeder, and a woman who rents costumes to amateur magicians, the offer reaches a canal mechanic. [299] Somewhere along this improbable rumor chain, Mara's joke about wanting company becomes a guarantee that touching her stone cures loneliness. [319]

The mechanic arrives towing a dinghy, accompanied by the costume woman and her brother, the recently widowed depot manager. [338] He carries a fuel authorization book. [344] "My sister says you can help," he tells Mara, looking not at the flood but at the closed fist around her stone. [366]

For a moment she considers letting the lie do its work. [377] Beyond the embankment, somebody waves a bedsheet from the retirement home's upper window. [390]

"It cannot cure anything," she says, opening her hand, "and I cannot even promise those people will be grateful." [409]

The manager shuts his authorization book, while the mechanic checks his watch. [421] Mara understands that she has summoned people with a promise and then offered them only another place to be disappointed. [441] Usually this is when she disappears. [447]

Instead, she sets the fragment on the counter and says, "I need help, and I am tired of pretending that is merely a professional condition." [472] She asks the manager for fuel, the mechanic for his boat, and the costume woman for blankets from her stock. [492] In return, she offers to repair their flooded homes with whatever hands she can assemble once the water falls. [511]

"Mine first," says the mechanic, "my mother's downstairs ceiling is gone." [522]

"Certainly," says the manager, opening his book, "provided your mother teaches me how to cook something that requires a plate." [542]

The costume woman promises curtains for the repaired house if someone will mend her leaking roof. [558] What catalyzes them is not generosity alone but the unexpected permission to ask for something back. [574] Mara writes every offer beside a name and address, leaving room for needs nobody has admitted yet. [591]

By dusk, seventeen pensioners are wrapped in theatrical velvet on the platform, drinking tea from cups politely requisitioned from the mayor's locked office. [614] One woman insists on recording everyone's birthday in the ledger before she will surrender its pencil. [630]

When Mara starts packing, the depot manager points to the blank space beside her name. [645] "You cannot leave us bound by reciprocity and exempt yourself," he says. [657]

She almost asks for replacement batteries, then writes: SOMEONE TO HAVE BREAKFAST WITH AFTER THE WATER GOES. [674] The old woman adds an address, a time, and NO EXCUSES. [685]

Mara leaves the moon rock weighing down the ledger, where even the loneliest things are permitted to hold something together. [705]
