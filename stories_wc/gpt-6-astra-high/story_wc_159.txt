In the opal library above the clouds, Mara tended glass orchids whose transparent roots curled around cylinders of recorded breath. [20] Each flower held a dead person's name, spoken by someone who had once loved them. [35] The city below had paid to forget those people, and then paid Mara to keep forgetting from becoming complete. [54] Now it refused the second payment. [60]

The library measured its days across the Museum of Extinct Feelings, from the opening of Wonder to the locking of Regret. [81] Today, when Regret closed, the collections would be melted down to settle the library's debts. [96] For thirty years, nobody but Mara had crossed the museum's silver thresholds. [108]

She worked in a gently systematic fashion: wiping petals, checking resonance, recording fractures with the same pencil she sharpened each morning. [129] Among these deserted marvels, method had become her substitute for hope. [140] She could preserve a name's vibration, but the orchids would not speak unless awakened by the melody that had originally sealed them. [162] The librarians who knew that melody had purchased their own forgetting long ago. [175]

While dismantling the entrance wind chime, she found one bamboo tube cracked along its grain. [190] Its damaged note trembled down the stacks, and an orchid answered with a syllable that made her reach instinctively for a small, absent hand. [214] Mara dropped her pencil. [218]

She remembered buying relief after the funeral, remembered the foxgloves outside the clinic where her child's heart had stopped. [237] She had called forgetting mercy, although she had known, even then, that mercy ought to include the person being forgotten. [257]

The chime's broken note had dislodged another memory: librarians humming as they sealed her daughter's name into glass. [275] She could remember only a few wavering intervals. [283] Mara carried the tube from flower to flower and marked the petals that shivered. [297] Guided by the echo of a long-forgotten song, she sang those intervals back until the smallest orchid whispered, "Ada." [316]

She sat down before her knees could fail. [324] All morning she had been protecting the possibility of that sound without allowing herself to know why. [341]

The museum clock advanced through Unreasonable Hope, and the furnace doors began opening beneath the floor. [357] Her storage cylinders could preserve vibrations for centuries, but there was no money left to carry them down to the city. [378] She had one transport basket, and room for perhaps six orchids. [389] Choosing six would make her a clerk of oblivion again. [399]

Instead, Mara opened the conservatory's old growing manual to its warning about heat. [412] An orchid warmed beyond flowering temperature would give up its entire recording, then collapse into ordinary glass. [429] The library's speaking vents still reached the abandoned listening rooms below, where people now sheltered from the rain. [447] She could not save the flowers, but she could send their names to people who might still have someone to mourn. [468]

She began to light the cultivation lamps, turning each wick precisely one quarter higher than the manual allowed. [486] Then she hung the broken chime over the first flame, where rising air coaxed out its fragile note. [504] Following it, she sang. [508]

Names opened around her, not in one grand chorus, but hesitantly, each with its own breath and accent. [526] A man laughed halfway through his wife's name, and a child mispronounced a grandfather's. [540] Mara kept singing until the last petals sagged, though Ada's voice was not among them, only her own younger voice saying Ada. [562] That was what she had saved: not a resurrection, but evidence that love had once addressed someone. [579]

Below, a listening bell rang, silent for decades until somebody pulled its answering cord. [593] Then another rang, and a voice rose through the vent asking her to repeat a name. [609] By the time the clock reached Regret, Mara had filled three pages of her fracture ledger with names. [627]

She packed it in the empty basket beside the cracked bamboo tube. [639] Tomorrow, below the clouds, she would find the people who had answered, and learn what they remembered. [656] The grief went with her, bitter as foxglove, and this time she did not ask it to leave. [674]
