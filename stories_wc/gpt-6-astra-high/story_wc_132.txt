After the third storm of the season, Mara found the nursery's linden canopy hanging through its mossy trellis like a broken umbrella. [22] Rain still ticked from the leaves overhead, although the whole sky had cleared. [35] She stood beneath the wreckage with her pruning shears, looking for something small enough to fix. [51]

Her father had built the trellis when she opened the nursery, twenty years before. [65] Every linden had been trained against its squares until the branches made a roof as level as his carpenter's bench. [85] Since his death, she had maintained its angles as if they were a kind of handwriting. [101] Photographs of it brought customers who wanted trees that would behave. [112] Mara had originally opened the business to study plant behaviors, but lately she studied invoices and corrected every unexpected shoot. [132]

Her apprentice, Nell, arrived carrying two mugs and the cancellation notice from a hotel that had ordered twelve trained lindens. [152] Their trees had snapped against their stakes during the night. [162] Mara counted the mugs before accepting hers. [169]

"There might be less work next month," she said, meaning that she could not bear to lose Nell. [187] Her sincerity usually had to travel around something before it could arrive. [199] "Then we should find out what broke," Nell said. [208]

Mara showed her the rotten foot of a post, but Nell crouched beside a fallen branch. [224] The break was exactly where a wire fastened it to the frame. [236] Across the nursery, the hotel trees had broken at their tightest ties. [248] The untrained lindens beyond the shed were battered but standing. [258]

Mara reached for a linden leaf on the lowest living branch and held it flat between finger and thumb. [277] Its unequal lobes had always irritated her in botanical drawings. [287] Even the tree's hearts refused perfect symmetry. [294] When a gust pulled against her fingers, she let go. [304] The leaf folded along its midrib and turned edgewise, then opened when the gust passed. [319] She watched it do this three times before putting down her shears. [331]

For the next week, while Nell salvaged saleable plants, Mara tried to reconstruct the storm from broken wood and old photographs. [352] Not every break implicated her training, but enough did. [361] She marked the failures in the notebook she had abandoned when orders became more urgent than questions. [378]

Then she loosened the supports on six surviving saplings, leaving enough slack for their trunks to sway. [395] She measured their movement, photographed their leaves, and resisted the urge to straighten them after every wind. [412] It felt less like an experiment than neglect. [420] Twice Nell caught her reaching for the ties. [428]

"Your father built a frame," Nell said the second time, "not a promise that nothing would ever move." [446] Mara disliked being understood so accurately by someone whose wages she might soon be unable to pay. [463]

The next storm came at dusk, and she watched from the shed instead of running out with more rope. [482] The loosened trees bowed terribly. [487] One lost a branch, but all six trunks survived. [496] By morning, their leaves had opened again. [503] It was evidence, not absolution, and she wrote down the lost branch too. [516]

They rebuilt the mossy trellis after the rain, replacing rotten posts and lowering the roof so new shoots could eventually cover it. [538] Mara kept her father's sound crosspieces, but attached the living branches with broad, loose loops instead of wire. [556] From the road, the canopy looked unfinished. [563] It would look unfinished for years. [569]

She refunded the hotel by selling the little tractor, then offered its gardener her observations without asking him to reconsider. [589] He bought four untrained trees for an exposed boundary, a smaller order with no guarantee of others. [606] Nell stayed, and the wheelbarrow acquired a squeak. [614] She kept her pruning shears sharp, but learning when to leave them untouched made her hands ache. [631]

Near the end of storm season, she and Nell sat beneath the trellis after another rain, drinking tea through its slow dripping. [653] The linden leaf she had watched was still there, its edge browned but its stem green. [669]

"There will be work next month," Mara said. [677] Nell lifted her mug. [681] Above them, the leaf turned in a small wind, making room for it without surrendering its hold. [698]
