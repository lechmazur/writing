Lark was an elusive logistician of disaster relief, arriving after floods with crates and jokes before anyone knew her name. [20]
She came to the disused railway station overgrown with ivy because the place gathered people whom loneliness had already displaced. [40]
Her motive was simple, though few believed it: to find the cure for loneliness. [54]
Lark was desperately jocular, offering tea, inventing forms, and saluting pigeons as if they were officials. [70]
Beneath the jokes she carried a fragment of moon rock sealed in a brass locket. [85]
It had come from a child who saved her during a landslide, and she was bound by reciprocity to answer one need. [107]
The child's need had been impossible: make a town feel inhabited again. [119]
At the station, the old departure board still listed destinations that no train served, and the ivy had signed its own polite anarchy across the walls. [145]

Lark studied the crowd and decided that the moon rock must catalyze not a miracle but a pattern of obligations. [186]
She whispered to a boy that the fragment could remember every kindness ever given to it, which was false but useful. [207]

The prophecy said that when the story changes, the last train would return if strangers carried one useful thing and one true question. [256]
Lark posted a notice written in chalk: Bring what you can lose, ask what you can keep. [273]

Lark moved among them, matching needs to gifts, until every exchange was bound by reciprocity and the air felt furnished. [382]
