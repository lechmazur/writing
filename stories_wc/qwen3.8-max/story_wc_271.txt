Three years after the last lighthouse keeper left the cliff, Nadia carried a crumpled photograph into the arboretum. [18]
The arboretum was perched on a seaside cliff, terraced with wind bent pines, salt roses, and small trees that had learned to lean. [41]
Before dawn, she checked the saplings, her fingers reading bark and soil the way other people read familiar letters. [60]
She was quietly talented, the kind of gardener who could graft an old apple into a young root and make both believe in spring. [84]
The arboretum had been planted by the keeper's wife, then abandoned after the light became automatic and the cottage doors rusted shut. [106]
Nadia had come there because no one else wanted the exposed ridge, and because she needed a place where survival meant patient work. [129]
In her pocket, the crumpled photograph showed a wedding tent, a string of lamps, and a groom mid laugh, blurred by a torn white crease. [154]
She had been the bride, until the drone strike folded music into smoke and left her under a tablecloth dusted with glass. [176]
The clinic had called her recovery a miracle, but Nadia mistrusted words that skipped over the missing chairs. [194]
Her task now was to anchor optimism in realism, not to decorate grief with cheerful flowers. [210]
Each morning she planted according to what the cliff could keep: thrift, sea pea, tamarisk, and one stubborn rowan. [229]
She measured hope in root balls and rain barrels, because a promise that could be watered was easier to trust. [249]
On clear days, visitors came from the village to walk the path, and Nadia taught them to freeze before they stepped on seedlings. [272]
Freeze, she would say, lifting a hand. [279]
It was the first lesson the cliff demanded: stop the foot, then let the eye notice. [295]
The second lesson came on a bright afternoon, when a survey drone rose from the road like a black wasp with a blinking eye. [319]
Nadia froze, and the sea vanished under the sound of the wedding tent tearing. [333]
Her shoulders climbed toward her ears, her breath locked, and the photograph in her pocket seemed to burn against her thigh. [354]
The old therapist had given her a method, and Nadia had shaped it into something plain enough for a cliff path. [375]
She returned through somatic markers, small signals from the body that could interrupt the mind's worst forecast. [392]
First, she pressed her thumb into the scar at the base of her palm and named it as a ridge, not a wound. [415]
Second, she felt her boots find the gravel, counting the stones that held her instead of the air that threatened to drop. [437]
Third, she let the salt wind touch her upper lip, where cold had once told her she was still breathing after the blast. [460]
These markers were not magic, but they made a pearl of stillness inside the alarm, a hard little center she could hold. [482]
The drone circled the rowan, perhaps mapping the erosion, and Nadia forced herself to track it as a tool rather than an omen. [505]
It had a job, she said aloud, and so did she. [516]
When the drone descended toward the road, she unfolded the photograph for the first time in weeks. [533]
A crease ran through the groom's face, but his smile remained, creased and present, like a path that had been walked too often. [556]
She did not tell herself he was safe, because the strike had made certainty impossible. [571]
She only let the image remind her that love had existed before terror, and that the body could remember both. [591]
Then she knelt by the rowan and cleared the weeds with careful hands, letting the work steady her nerves. [610]
By evening, the wind had softened, and the lighthouse began its slow white turn without anyone to trim a wick or polish brass. [633]
Nadia tied the young tree to its stake, not to imprison it, but to teach it where uprightness was possible. [653]
The photograph went back into her pocket, no longer a verdict, but a small witness with softened edges. [671]
When the sea darkened below the cliff, she allowed herself one sentence of hope that did not insult the dead. [691]
Tomorrow she would plant more, knowing roots needed realistic soil, and so did she. [705]
