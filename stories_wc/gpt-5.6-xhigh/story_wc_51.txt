After the first snowmelt, Ilyra descended alone into the bronze grotto under starlight, carrying her dead mother's kerosene lamp. [19]

She was the valley's last fractal weaver, trained to braid repeating patterns through cloth, memory, and the hidden architecture of sorrow. [40]

Her purpose was neither mercy nor scholarship, though it borrowed the clothing of both: she came to collect the weights of different burdens. [63]

The elders believed grief could be compared only after it had hardened, and Ilyra believed comparison might reveal why her mother had drowned on dry land. [89]

Within the grotto, overlapping bronze plates climbed the walls like scales on a sleeping leviathan, each holding a confession no villager could speak twice. [113]

Their layered silence formed what her mother had called the shingled hush, a quiet so dense that footsteps seemed to sink without echoes. [136]

Ilyra set the lamp upon a whalebone altar, whose long ribs curved overhead as if the extinct sea were dreaming itself around her. [159]

The reservoir was empty, but the kerosene lamp burner still smelled of storms, salt, and the bitter smoke of her childhood. [180]

Mother had taught her that the burner did not need fuel; by orienting properly, its teeth caught starlight and burned whatever truth faced it. [204]

Ilyra turned its notched crown toward the constellation called the Baleen Gate, and a blue flame opened without warmth. [223]

One by one, she unwound burden-threads from the plates: a farmer's guilt, a widow's relief, a child's terror of becoming cruel. [244]

Under the flame, each thread began to calcify into a pale, branching weight, delicate as coral and heavy as judgment. [264]

She placed the forms on her mother's brass scale and recorded their measures in a ledger stitched from sailcloth. [283]

Guilt weighed nine drams, relief fourteen, terror scarcely one, yet its coral branches pierced her palm when she lifted it. [303]

The measurements made no moral sense, and that failure frightened her more than any ghost. [318]

Then the deepest plate rang, although nothing had touched it, and her mother's voice moved beneath the bronze like a whale beneath ice. [341]

"You cannot weigh me while you stand outside me," it said. [352]

The floor unfolded in recursive spirals, and Ilyra fell through chambers nested inside chambers, every wall an image of the first. [373]

She landed beside a black pool whose shore receded whenever she approached, leaving her boundary-lessly trapped in a place with no edge to cross. [397]

Across the water sat her mother, alive and young, combing moonlit kelp from her hair. [412]

Ilyra understood that this was the oldest burden: not grief itself, but the wish to enter another person's pain and arrange it into justice. [436]

"Why did you leave me?" she asked, and the blue flame trembled in her hand. [451]

Her mother smiled with unbearable tenderness and pointed downward, where hundreds of calcified burdens lay embedded in the pool like a drowned reef. [474]

"I carried everyone because I mistook endurance for love." [483]

"When their weights filled me, the grotto called me home." [493]

The answer broke Ilyra's secret design, for she had planned to gather every burden, bear the heaviest, and thereby surpass her mother's sacrifice. [516]

Around her, the recursive chambers narrowed like the throat of some patient beast waiting to swallow another dutiful daughter. [535]

To escape, a fractal weaver had to alter the smallest pattern, trusting the change to repeat beyond sight. [553]

Ilyra removed the one-dram terror from her satchel and held it over the flame until its sharpest branch softened. [572]

Instead of measuring it, she named it: the fear of inheriting cruelty from those we love. [588]

She snapped the scale's beam, laid half beside the child burden and half before her mother, making no side answer the other. [610]

The fracture echoed upward through every chamber, multiplying into thunder, until the impossible shore stopped fleeing. [626]

Ilyra crossed the pool, but her mother faded as she reached her, leaving only a warm drop of kerosene in her open hand. [649]

When Ilyra woke beside the altar, dawn silvered the grotto, and the collected weights had returned to threads. [667]

She did not restore them to their separate plates. [676]

Instead, she wove them into a single sail patterned with branching choices, strong because no strand was asked to carry the whole. [698]

That evening, she hung the sail above the village square, where people could add their threads or take them back without judgment. [720]

The shingled hush loosened, plate by plate, into voices. [729]

Ilyra kept the broken scale, not as proof that burdens were equal, but as a reminder that none were meant to be solitary. [752]

Far below, in the whalebone reverie of the bronze grotto, the lamp's blue flame became a star reflected on a peaceful sea. [774]
