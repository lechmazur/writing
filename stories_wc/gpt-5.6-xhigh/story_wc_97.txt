At Meridian Station, the cloud tide climbed the trunks each evening, whitening the roots of the tidal arbor above the clouds. [21]
Passengers watched from iron balconies while Iven Marr, the station's wistful pianist, played departures softly enough to resemble apologies. [40]
He had played there for nine years, since his wife, Ada, boarded the westbound observatory train and never returned. [59]
On the piano stood their broken anniversary clock, its silver hands fixed at 8:17, the minute her last letter had promised home. [81]
Iven wound it before every performance, not from hope, he told himself, but because rituals gave grief a railing. [100]
That afternoon, however, every brass compass in the station began blinking north, west, nowhere, north, as if direction itself were losing courage. [122]
The porters called them flickering compasses and covered their faces, for such failures had preceded the Great Derailment. [140]
By sunset, three trains crowded the high platforms, unable to descend through the cloud sea, and the tide kept rising. [160]
Stationmaster Vale ordered everyone into the north tunnel, although an old flood chart showed that tunnel ending beneath the deepest roots. [181]
Iven's longing hardened into something steely: Ada had distrusted Vale's schedules, saying fate loved timetables because they made people obedient. [201]
Grief had made Iven strategically creative, teaching him to hide private questions inside polite routines and maps inside harmless music. [221]
So while Vale shouted, Iven examined the clock and noticed a strip of film pinched behind its cracked wooden back. [241]
The strip bore Ada's tiny handwriting: When needles stutter, let our first night run past the ending. [258]
Their first night had been spent in the projection room, laughing at a black-and-white film of Meridian Station's grand opening. [278]
Iven fetched its dusty reel, locked the room against Vale, and threaded it backward, using the frozen time, 8:17, as a frame number. [301]
After the ceremonial ending, scratches became doors, and blank leader became a moonlit diagram of roots, switches, and an abandoned storm vault. [323]
Ada had sent him a path to refuge through hidden sections in a black-and-white film reel, trusting that only he would look beyond applause. [347]
One final hidden frame showed her beside a western signal, holding their clock before it broke, with RETURN ROUTE chalked overhead. [368]
The vault could shelter the passengers, but reaching it required opening a disused switch whose electric code had vanished from every manual. [390]
Then Iven saw that the compass needles flickered in groups matching the film's scratches: three, one, four, then two. [409]
Ada had turned the station's failing guidance current into a message, or someone on her lost train was repeating her design. [430]
Iven copied the pulses onto staff paper and found four blunt chords that, played through the station piano's telegraph pickup, would throw the switch. [454]
Vale broke down the projection-room door and demanded the scheduled evacuation, but thunder from below made the north tunnel breathe white water. [476]
Iven carried the clock into the concourse and told the crowd that certainty had failed them, while a discarded ending offered refuge. [498]

He remained at the piano with Vale, who finally admitted that Ada's train had never wrecked; it had slipped onto the unmapped western loop. [546]
Vale had concealed the error to protect Meridian's perfect record, leaving the train circling beyond ordinary bearings for nine years. [566]
Iven wanted to strike him, yet the approaching pulse shivered under his fingers like a heartbeat returning after winter. [585]

Ada stepped down wearing the same red coat, then stopped before Iven as if nine years were merely a delayed measure. [737]

With the rescued crowd cheering from the vault stairs, Iven began a tune no timetable had expected. [792]
