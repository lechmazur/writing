At seventeen, Mara inherited the cliffside orchard because everyone older had either fled its curse or followed one of its paths into the sea. [24]
She accepted the keys only after the mayor threatened to burn the trees, and she called herself caretaker with the bitterness others reserved for jailer. [49]
The orchard grew inside a cliffside labyrinth patrolled by mechanical birds, where pear branches snagged iron arches and roots steadily buckled the stairs. [72]
Each dusk, the walls turned while bronze wings clattered overhead, and every ripe apple whispered a direction that was almost correct. [93]
Thus the curse did not kill travelers outright; it persuaded them, politely and patiently, to lose themselves. [110]
Mara kept bells at every entrance, because resenting the orchard had never cured her desire to guide lost travelers to safety. [131]
She rescued them with rope, chalk, and a casually exact sense of distance that made panic sound foolish. [149]
"The north stair is thirty seven steps, then three embarrassed shuffles," she would say, as if measuring terror were dull housework. [170]
On the first freezing evening of autumn, six bells rang together, announcing a caravan trapped in the deepest terraces. [189]
"Bring them out before the night frost forms," the mayor begged, staring at clouds already white along their bellies. [208]
Frost made the metal birds hunt warmth, and no living traveler had survived their winter beaks. [224]
Mara knew the ordinary route would turn twice before she reached the caravan, sealing retreat. [239]
Yet in her dead father's shed, beneath seed ledgers, she had once found a forgotten blueprint drawn on blank copper foil. [260]
It showed no ink, only shallow dents arranged like branches, and she had dismissed it as another useless inheritance. [279]
Now, when an orchard bird thrust its claws through the broken window, she raised the foil beneath them. [297]
The bird hovered above it, leaving impressions without touching, its magnetic shadow dimpling the copper. [312]
The dents deepened into words: ROOT REMEMBERS WHAT WALL FORGETS. [322]
In one corner, the blueprint pictured a small nut seated inside a cage of three copper rings. [339]
Mara touched the hazelnut pendant her father had tied around her neck before he vanished. [355]
She had thought it sentimental; now she saw a hairline seam around its shell and a blue spark within. [374]
According to the labels, the nut was the orchard's compass, removed years ago from the root engine that governed walls and birds. [396]
Her father had not abandoned his post, as Mara believed; he had stolen the compass to stop the curse from luring people toward smugglers waiting below. [422]
Scrawled beneath the diagram was his final correction: without compass, paths wander, but with cage, compass obeys caretaker. [440]
Mara was angry that his explanation had arrived as machinery instead of apology, but anger was warm, and useful. [459]
She ripped copper hoops from sapling braces and began to assemble the cage while running toward the first turning. [478]
It was ugly work: wire bit her thumb, rings sagged crookedly, and the glowing nut rattled like a tooth in a beggar's cup. [501]
Nevertheless, when she closed the last twist, every bronze bird wheeled toward her in one shining, graceless bow. [519]
The walls shuddered, exposing an old central stair that descended through roots thick as ship masts. [535]
Mara followed, but at the lowest terrace she found the caravan huddled beneath carts, with birds circling above. [553]
"Do not run," she told them. [559]
"Walk eleven paces toward the edge." [565]
They stared at the black sea below, and one merchant accused her of serving the orchard. [581]
"Unfortunately, I do," Mara said, her voice offhandedly exact even with cold silvering her eyelashes. [596]
"The drop is eighty two feet, but the bridge will arrive in four seconds." [610]
A wall fell outward with a groan, becoming a bridge just where empty air had been. [626]
The travelers crossed as metal birds dove, not at their bodies, but at the compass burning in Mara's hand. [645]
She understood then that the root engine could guide or hunt, and the nut merely magnified the caretaker's chosen purpose. [665]
The smugglers had cursed it with greed; her father had answered with fear; she would answer with care. [683]
Instead of shielding herself, she raised the crooked cage and commanded, "Lead every lost soul home." [699]
The birds struck, then broke formation inches above her, their wings pressing warm patterns into the frosted ground. [717]
Those untouched impressions became arrows, and the orchard opened passage after passage toward the town. [732]
Mara limped behind the caravan as branches tore her coat and apples shouted contradictory advice. [747]
When the last child crossed the gate, frost sealed the labyrinth in white, and the birds settled harmlessly among the pears. [768]
In their graceless beauty, Mara finally saw that caretaking was not loving a place, but choosing what it would do to strangers. [790]
