At midnight, rain pressed its ear against the copper dome of the Orpheum, and the empty balconies answered in hushed thunder. [21]

Mara Vale, last projectionist and reluctant crystal gazer, climbed to the vintage movie palace projection booth carrying a velvet-wrapped quartz sphere. [42]

The Orpheum would be demolished at dawn, yet she had returned because her dead mother's final note contained only an impossible instruction: find where unused love is stored. [70]

Her mother Celia had sung here during silent-film revivals, until illness stole first her voice, then the tune of every song she knew. [93]

Mara spread dozens of carbon copies of goodbye letters across the rewinding bench, each addressed but never sent by patrons who had visited Celia backstage. [118]

For thirty years, Celia had invited the bereaved to write farewells during intermission, promising that the Orpheum would keep whatever courage failed them. [141]

After her death, only these copies remained, and Mara suspected the originals waited somewhere inside the building's ornate, bewildering anatomy. [161]

Mara was spatially imaginative; rooms unfolded in her mind as luminous boxes, connected by stairwells that bent like ribbons through darkness. [182]

Yet no blueprint showed a hidden room large enough for thousands of letters. [195]

Then beneath the quartz sphere she noticed a film strip whose leader bore Celia's handwriting: "For Mara, when the last audience has gone." [218]

The frames appeared black, but when she threaded them through the projector, pale shapes trembled on the screen below like submerged stars. [240]

The mechanism coughed, caught, and began to incubate old light, warming silver grains that had slept beyond ordinary sight. [259]

The booth slipped outside measured time, into the hour while forgotten lyrics search for tune, and a woman's humming rose below, almost Celia's. [282]

Mara pressed two fingers to the moving strip and began the psychometry reading Celia had taught her but warned her never to perform alone. [306]

At once, every frame became a doorway, and every doorway opened upon someone failing to leave. [322]

A soldier sat in Row G, writing to the man he loved before boarding a train that returned without him. [342]

A mother apologized to a daughter asleep against her shoulder; an old usher forgave the brother who had emptied their childhood house. [364]

Each goodbye entered the projector as ink, but emerged as light that streamed not toward the screen, but backward through the booth wall. [387]

Mara closed her eyes and fitted that impossible direction into her inner Orpheum, rotating corridors until the beam aligned with a forgotten ventilation shaft. [411]

It led beneath the booth to a cavity no architect had drawn, exactly between the auditorium's painted heaven and the rain-black roof. [433]

Taking the quartz and Celia's letter, Mara crawled along the shaft while the unattended projector clattered behind her like teeth suppressing a confession. [456]

At the cavity's end, she found no sacks of paper, only a round chamber lined with discarded speakers from every renovation. [477]

Within each speaker cone rested dozens of rolled original letters, protected by wax, their unsaid words aimed inward at a bowl of black glass. [501]

When Mara laid the sphere in the bowl, the chamber sounded without sound, a pressure so deep the roof dust rose. [522]

Celia appeared inside the crystal, younger than death, holding the one farewell she had never copied. [538]

"Dear Mara," she read, "I mistook keeping love for giving it a home, and made this theater heavy with departures." [558]

Celia touched her palm to the crystal's inner surface. [590]

"Do not store yours with mine." [596]

Mara had brought no letter, having believed silence stronger than any sentence, but now she spoke into the listening cones. [616]

"You left me a building full of strangers' courage," she told Celia, "and I was angry you saved none for staying." [637]
