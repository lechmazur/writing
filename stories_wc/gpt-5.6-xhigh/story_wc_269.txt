<story>Thirty years after the fossil fuel ban, Mara Vale lived alone at Kestrel Sea Level Monitoring Station. [17]

She had witnessed coral bleaching as a young diver, watching a jeweled reef whiten until it resembled a drowned city. [37]

The sight had never released her; it drifted through her sleep, pale and weightless, while the station's pylons held her above the breathing water. [61]

Kestrel was officially automated, but Mara remained because machines recorded rise, not meaning, and she wanted to discover why some paths disappear. [83]

At low tide, a stone causeway emerged toward the abandoned mainland; at high tide, no instrument admitted it had ever existed. [104]

It seemed periodically eternal, returning by the moon yet losing another slab each season. [118]

To calm herself, Mara practiced tapping rhythms on surfaces: three beats on glass, two on steel, one on the old oak desk. [140]

Each material answered differently, and over years she learned the station as a musician learns a stubborn instrument. [158]

One stormless April morning, the desk replied with a hollow note beneath its northwest corner. [173]

She pried up a warped board and found an atlas page torn out long before the coastlines were redrawn. [192]

Salt had blurred the printed roads, but someone had added red dots along the vanished causeway and penciled four words: LISTEN WHEN WATER LEAVES. [216]

Mara knew the handwriting belonged to Ilyan, her father, Kestrel's first keeper and the man who had taught her to dive. [237]

He had vanished during the final oil winter, leaving only a report that blamed subsidence for the causeway's collapse. [256]

If he had hidden this map, his report had hidden something else. [268]

Next low tide, she carried the page outside and tapped its marked sequence on every exposed slab: three, two, one. [288]

Granite gave back blunt knocks until the seventh dot, where a square stone rang over an emptiness. [305]

Beneath the stone lay a brass cylinder containing strips of tide gauge paper, each punched with tiny holes. [344]

A breaker struck her knees, forcing her back toward Kestrel before she could examine them. [359]

For six days, wind pinned the station inside rain, and the cylinder waited beside the atlas page. [376]

Mara scanned the strips, aligned dates, searched databases, and found nothing but obsolete pressure readings. [391]

Then, drumming impatience against the cylinder, she heard its holes interrupt her rhythm. [413]

It was not data to be read, but music to be assembled. [447]

Using the red dots as measures, Mara arranged the strips across the floor and performed them on window, desk, railing, and cylinder. [469]

The station answered with a wandering composition, its tones drifting room to room yet moored to a repeating bass from the sea below. [492]

At the final measure, a maintenance panel beside the gauge clicked open. [504]

Inside, untouched for decades, a recorder woke and projected Ilyan's voice into the storm. [518]

"We cut channels beneath the causeway to feed cold deep water toward the reef during heat tides." [555]

"If I fail to return, decide whether a path matters more than what it reaches." [595]
