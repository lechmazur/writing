<story>In the margin of daylight, when sunset had withdrawn from the gantries but stars had not yet claimed the sky, Mara stood barefoot inside the abandoned spaceport repair bay. [29]
The people of Veyra called her the barefoot stargazer, partly because she watched heaven from rooftops, and partly because her boots had been traded for medicine during the first winter of rebellion. [61]
Tonight, a lull in rebellion held the city: no sirens, no rifle flashes, only distant storm clouds rolling like black ships above the silent runways. [86]
Mara had entered the bay to discover meaning in the mess left by a decade of sabotage, retreat, and patched machines, although she had told the sentries she was merely looking for copper wire. [120]
Around her, decommissioned shuttles hung from chains, their hulls opening and closing in the wind with the slow breathing of sleeping beasts. [142]
Thunder walked across the roof, yet between each vast concussion lay such complete serenity that she could hear dust settling on metal. [164]
It was thunderous serenity, the kind that made destruction seem briefly capable of forgiveness. [178]
From her coat, Mara drew a pair of vintage opera glasses, their brass rims absurd among the diagnostic screens. [197]
They had belonged to her mother, who once watched singers through them before the opera house became a tribunal and then a crater. [220]
Through their warped lenses, ordinary lights doubled, but polarized engine glow separated into blue threads that betrayed live circuitry. [239]
She scanned the bay awkwardly confident, announcing each useless discovery to the ghosts of mechanics as though they were an attentive crew. [261]
One blue thread ran beneath a toppled fuel cart and vanished under a deck plate stamped with the old imperial sun. [282]
When she levered it aside, she found not cables but black soil, and twelve blunt stone tips stood exposed there in a perfect ring within it. [308]
The spaceport maps called the place solid bedrock, but her grandmother's forbidden songs named an ancient stone circle buried there when the first launchpad was poured. [334]
Mara knelt, set her bare feet inside the ring, and felt a hum climb through her bones before any sound reached her ears. [357]
As the note deepened, dead consoles brightened one after another, painting the suspended shuttles with pale constellations. [374]
Through the opera glasses, Mara saw the truth: every improvised patch and crooked wire converged upon the stones, generations of mechanics secretly turning wreckage into an amplifier. [401]
Then the speakers exhaled voices, thousands of them, reports from rebel cells believed destroyed, all trapped in the port's damaged memory. [422]
Among them was her brother Iven, speaking calmly from a moon prison and asking anyone alive to delay the planned assault. [443]
The lull was not surrender but a held breath: imperial dockworkers would open the prison gates at dawn, if the rebels did not provoke a massacre first. [470]
A field radio crackled at Mara's belt, and Captain Sorn demanded to know why the bay's reactor signature had returned. [490]
When she described the hidden archive, he ordered her to reignite the launch beacon, so his bombers could cross the storm and strike before dawn. [515]
She could reignite it by the hum of the ancient stone circle; the awakened consoles had already offered her a single command. [537]
Yet the same surge would erase the stored voices, destroy the prisoners' warning, and end the lull in fire. [556]
Mara had spent years mistaking certainty for courage, because Sorn's orders were clean and grief was not. [573]
Now the mess spoke in her brother's voice, and its meaning was that rebellion mattered only if people survived it. [593]
"No," she told Sorn, and her voice shook only after the single word had crossed the air. [610]
He threatened treason, but Mara removed the lenses from the opera glasses and fitted them over the beacon's paired photonic sensors. [631]
The old glass split the circle's pulse just as it split engine glow, feeding power to transmission and guidance without touching the weapons bus. [655]
Barefoot in the ring, Mara stamped the rhythm of her grandmother's song, and the hum rose until loose bolts danced across the floor. [678]
Across Veyra, runway lamps reignited beneath the storm, not as a path for bombers but as a message in the workers' old code: wait. [702]
Iven's warning poured through every rebel receiver, followed by the prison workers' codes and proof of their plan, too widely scattered now for Sorn to bury. [728]
The bombers remained grounded, and when dawn finally entered the bay, it found Mara amid singing consoles, holding the empty brass frames. [750]
Outside, thunder answered the cheer rising from the city, while shuttles lifted from the prison moon carrying workers, captives, and Iven toward the brightening runway. [775]
For the first time, Mara understood the sky not as an escape from ruins, but as meaning made through them. [795]</story>
