<story>Mara kept the tide pools of Nacre Bay as other people kept diaries, recording each vanished anemone, each newly arrived crab, and every moon reflected whole in a cup of stone. [31]

The villagers called her the tidal pool chronicler, sometimes teasingly, yet they brought her their children because she noticed fear without shaming it and curiosity without dismissing it. [59]

Her warmly observant nature had once delighted her son, Iven, before his wish to leave made every gentle question feel like a net. [82]

On the night before his ship departed, he followed her down the cliff stair into the ancient grotto under stars, where the roof had fallen away centuries ago. [110]

Starlight silvered the pools; below it, dark water inhaled through channels worn by generations of turning tides. [127]

At the deepest basin, Iven said, "If you ask me to stay again, Mother, I will leave without saying farewell." [147]

Mara opened her hand, revealing the jade cicada her own mother had carried from a drowned temple. [164]

She had planned to give it to Iven, but his ultimatum lodged between them, and the difficult conversation entered a pause so complete that a falling drop seemed unable to land. [195]

All her life she had wanted to discover the texture of time, not its measures, but how one moment frayed into another. [217]

For that reason she chronicled transition periods: the instant salt water sweetened with rain, the hush before spawning, the uncertain border between a wound and a scar. [244]

She never studied change directly, since direct attention could flatten delicate things. [256]

Instead, through a tangential approach, she watched what change disturbed nearby: sand lifting before the current shifted, limpets tightening before thunder, a son's jokes growing careful before goodbye. [284]

Now she looked not at Iven's anger, but at its wake. [295]

His boot remained pointed toward the stair, although his bare toes curled against the familiar rock, and his gaze kept returning to the cicada. [319]

From these small contradictions she could foresee two futures, not as visions, but as currents taking shape around a stone. [339]

In one, she closed her fist, demanded gratitude, and watched him harden into a stranger who sent dutiful letters. [358]

In the other, she pressed the cicada upon him as a promise of return, making love another rope he would spend years cutting. [381]

Neither future had the porous, living texture she recognized in honest change. [393]

Beside them, the suspended drop struck the basin, and its rings crossed the reflected constellation without destroying it. [411]

A hermit crab abandoned a cramped shell, stood naked for one perilous heartbeat, then entered a larger home. [429]

Mara had recorded hundreds of such exchanges, yet only now understood that shelter and prison could be the same shape at different times. [452]

The insight did not tell her what to say; it showed her what she must not preserve. [469]

She set the jade cicada on a ledge between them, neither withholding it nor burdening him with it. [487]

"My mother gave me this when I chose the bay over traveling with her," she said. [503]

"I told myself it was permission, but for thirty years I kept it as proof that one of us had chosen wrongly." [525]

Iven's shoulders lowered, though the toe of his boot still aimed outward. [537]

"Which one?" he asked. [541]

"That is what time changed," Mara said, "and what I refused to let change." [555]

She nudged the carving toward the pool, where green stone gathered the stars along its polished wings. [572]

"Tomorrow, take it or leave it here; either choice will be yours, and neither will be a message to me." [592]

For the first time that night, Iven looked directly at her, not as a gate he must force, but as a shore he could depart. [617]

"I need you to be glad for me," he said. [627]

Mara felt the old answers rise, each shaped like caution, and let them ebb. [641]

"I am afraid for you," she said, "and glad that there is a you beyond my knowing." [658]

The pause ended there, not with agreement, but with room enough for both truths to breathe. [674]

Iven turned the cicada so it faced the sea, then offered Mara his arm for the climb. [691]

They left it on the ledge until morning, balanced between inheritance and gift. [704]

As they ascended, the tide entered every pool by a different path, and Mara heard in those small arrivals the texture she had sought: time was not a cord that bound one moment to the next, but water making contact, withdrawing, and returning changed. [748]</story>
