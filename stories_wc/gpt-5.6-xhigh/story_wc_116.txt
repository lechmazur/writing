Beneath the drowned city, an overgrown library slept in the catacombs of lost knowledge, its shelves knitted together by blind roots. [21]
Mara, its last chronicle keeper, lived farther below in an obsidian refuge along the river that carried forgotten ink toward the sea. [43]
Her appointment had never included rescue, only a vow to witness change without judgment and record whatever the ruins chose to become. [65]

Mara wrote these transformations on scraps, then released each page into the current, because permanence had destroyed the city once already. [105]
She gripped reality loosely, trusting several contradictory accounts until time revealed which one could bear the weight of consequence. [124]
This habit preserved a crumbling peace among the few creatures who spoke: moth priests, feral catalogers, and a patient colony of whispering mice. [147]
Then, one rainless morning, the river stopped. [154]
Without its murmur, the refuge seemed to hold its breath, and roots throughout the library stiffened like ropes pulled from above. [175]

Its paper was soft as cloth, yet cryptic folds crossed its wings in patterns matching the library passages. [217]

The swan was not a map of the catacombs, she realized, but their memory of themselves, compressed into paper. [257]
A black stain marked its breast where the river should have curved, and beside it someone had inked a tiny seed. [278]

Instead, she heard a deeper absence beneath the ordinary quiet, a regenerative silence in which damaged things paused before choosing another form. [319]
The silence did not ask her to judge change, she understood; it asked her to preserve the possibility of change. [339]
Mara navigated via cryptic folds in the well-worn origami swan, descending beyond the oldest index and turning the paper at each sealed junction. [362]
At the bottom, she reached an obsidian gate sealed with clay and dead roots, and felt cold water hammering beyond it. [383]
