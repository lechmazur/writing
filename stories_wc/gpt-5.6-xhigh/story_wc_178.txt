<story>Between regimes, while conquerors argued beyond the Euphrates, Naram lived alone in an ancient Babylonian palace reimagined as an observatory. [20]

The throne room held brass orreries instead of courtiers, and blue glazed lions prowled beneath constellations painted across the ceiling. [40]

Naram was a frugal astronomer, saving lamp oil by working in twilight and writing calculations between the lines of discarded tax decrees. [62]

Economy had begun as necessity, but became his discipline: nothing should be spent until its hidden value had been measured. [82]

This made him distrust the one treasure he could not measure, a black clay magic square tile found beneath the deposed king's bed. [105]

Its sixteen compartments bore no numbers, only tiny stars that brightened whenever Venus descended into the western haze. [123]

On those evenings, four lines of cuneiform surfaced around its rim, then faded before he could copy them. [141]

The message seemed less like sorcery than encrypted devotion, because repeated groups resembled the palace hymns once sung to Ishtar. [161]

Naram believed its maker had loved something that possessed no name, perhaps a god, perhaps the ordered dark itself. [180]

He wanted to solve it not for fame but to seal a covenant with the unknown, promising honest witness in return for intelligible wonder. [204]

Yet the tile resisted every prayer, flame, solvent, and whispered royal password. [216]

Its refusal was empirically ironic: the more faithfully he observed it, the less of its writing remained observable. [234]

When the city's last governor fled, his chief archivist, Belili, came to the observatory seeking shelter from both armies. [253]

She brought no jewels, only a basket of ration tablets and an eye trained to detect altered signs. [271]

Naram offered her half his lentils, though he privately calculated that peace had better arrive within nine suppers. [289]

Belili laughed, and the abandoned palace briefly recovered the dusky harmony of its vanished musicians. [304]

At sunset he showed her the tile, expecting awe, but she rotated it and asked why astronomers always assumed heaven was above. [326]

The stars immediately rearranged themselves, and one complete line burned silver across the clay. [340]

Belili translated: "I hide my face so that power cannot inherit my promise." [353]

Outside, soldiers were changing the bull standards on the gate, proving the maker's caution had outlived another crown. [371]

They spent three nights testing orientation, moonlight, breath, and touch, recording each response through schematic representations on the blank backs of decrees. [393]

Their diagrams revealed that the square behaved as a palace plan: every star marked a room, and every sum traced a path. [415]

The path ended beneath the old audience dais, where masonry concealed a narrow chamber shaped like the tile. [433]

Dawn found them prying up bricks while rival trumpets answered each other across the river. [448]

In the chamber stood sixteen clay figures, each cloaked in bitumen and positioned beneath a shaft of paling starlight. [467]

Beside them, an inscription instructed the finder to uncloak no figure until a rulerless hour, then judge what emerged without obedience or fear. [490]

"We are between kings now," Belili said, as drums from the eastern camp stopped and the western camp had not begun. [511]

Naram hesitated, realizing that thrift could become another form of fear, a refusal to spend oneself on uncertain truth. [530]

He broke the first black shell. [536]

Inside was not an idol, but a small clay likeness of a washerwoman carrying a jar. [552]

Belili opened another and found a shepherd; together they freed bakers, scribes, foreigners, children, and prisoners. [568]

The final figure represented neither god nor king, but an astronomer holding one lamp toward a faceless companion. [586]

When all sixteen stood bare, their shadows joined into cuneiform words across the floor. [600]

They read, "No throne owns the sky; whoever attends its silence belongs to everyone." [614]

The tile then warmed in Naram's palm, waiting, he understood, not for decipherment but for an answer. [631]

He set his costly final flask of oil among the figures and lit every palace lamp, making the covenant visible to whichever army entered. [655]

"We will preserve no knowledge for rulers alone," he said, and Belili pressed her seal beside his on the tile. [675]

The stars vanished, leaving sixteen ordinary marks whose sums any patient child could learn. [689]

By noon, both armies occupied the city, yet neither could claim the chamber, because Belili had summoned its people before opening the gates. [712]

Washerwomen, shepherds, bakers, foreigners, children, and prisoners crowded the throne room, copying the diagrams while generals waited outside. [730]

Naram watched evening gather over the glazed lions and felt no god reply, only hundreds of voices checking one another's sums. [751]

That silence was answer enough: the unknown had accepted devotion only after devotion ceased to be secret. [768]</story>
