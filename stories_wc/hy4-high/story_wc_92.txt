The luminous fort at twilight had always seemed too pleased with itself, all milk glass, blue salt lamps, and walls that shone as if newly forgiven. 
At the hour of bells, during an unexplained blackout, that complacency went out with a tiny, almost courteous sigh. 
Ione Vale, the vigilant lock sentinel, stood inside the darkened gate hall and listened to thirty-seven locks reconsider their loyalty. 
The dark had a winking hush, as though every shadow knew a joke and had been sworn not to tell it. 
She had been calmly inventive since childhood, when she repaired the kitchen latches with hairpins and made the chaplain's censer ring like a dinner bell. 
Her post gave her keys to every door except the cradle of sparks, the sealed chamber beneath the parade yard where the first pilot flames were taught to breed. 
Officials said the cradle fed the lamps with bottled lightning, but Ione had seen the wage rolls, the erased names, and the cots folded behind the furnace screens. 
Her mother, a lamp-woman who vanished after asking why the furnace had chimneys, had left one inheritance: a bronze quill holder with a false bottom. 
Inside slept a contraband spark, no larger than a comma, warm enough to make the bronze blush whenever the governor lied nearby. 
Governor Pyr had made intrigue the fort's polite indoor sport, passing private orders via the coded angles in a half-painted sundial. 
Each afternoon the painters left one quadrant bare, and the missing brush marks told readers which door, hour, and lie to prefer. 
As keeper of every bolt, Ione translated those angles into keys for years, with a clean collar and a dirtier conscience. 
The blackout arrived just as the supper angles appeared on the dial, so no ordinary reader could see which lie had been chosen. 
Captain Orrel, smelling of wet rope and authority, brought her a lantern that would not light and an order to seal every low door. 
The low doors led to the lamp-workers, the scribes, the kitchens, and the little school where children learned to cheer quietly. 
Ione read the order twice, smiled at the extinguished lantern, and understood that the governor had shuttered the cradle to hide the code and cage the lamp-workers. 
She had long wanted to spark a silent revolution, not with cannon, but with bolts sliding back at the same instant. 
The design was simple only on paper: if every lock opened together, the alarm wires would tug against themselves and produce one embarrassed tinkle. 
She decided to intrigue rather than fight, because suspicious captains were easier to move than honest soldiers. 
From her sleeve she drew the bronze quill holder, slipped a goose quill into its split collar, and fitted the old tube over the sundial's iron gnomon. 
The false bottom clicked open, the contraband spark climbed the quill, and a small golden shadow lay across the half-painted face. 
Ione turned the holder until the quill pointed at seventy-one degrees. 
That angle meant all quiet keys receive mercy, a phrase the old locksmiths had hidden in the code for the day mercy became practical. 
She then set three lesser angles around it, each falsely suggesting that another captain had been promised a private promotion. 
The watch captains were not fools, which helped; they read the insults perfectly and began watching one another instead of the low doors. 
While they cultivated elegant suspicions, Ione carried the bronze quill holder to each key cabinet and touched its warm lip to the master rings. 
Her mother's spark passed from bronze to iron, from iron to tumbler, and from tumbler to the patient darkness inside every lock. 
At the third bell, a sound that did not quite happen, thirty-seven locks and two hundred smaller ones opened together. 
The alarm wires shivered, conferred, and decided to keep the matter to themselves. 
Lamp-workers stepped out, scribes pocketed ledgers, and schoolchildren offered bread to the guards. 
Down below, the shutters of the cradle of sparks swung inward, and the furnace exhaled a light that no longer needed erased names. 
Ione returned to the gate hall before anyone thought to shout, because shouting would have spoiled the arithmetic. 
Governor Pyr found only the half-painted sundial, the empty bronze quill holder, and a note angled at seventy-one degrees. 
No one asked; the fort kept its winking hush.
