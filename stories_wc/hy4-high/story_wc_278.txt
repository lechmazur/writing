Beneath the abandoned observatory, the laboratory of lost sounds held a meteor hush, as if the last extinct bell had fallen through the roof. 
Only when time folds in on itself did its doors find the right corridor, so Mara came at midnight with frost on her coat and no shadow. 
She was the constellation tattooist who inked old skies on living skin for sailors, mourners, and insomniacs seeking directions. 
The summons said only that the lullabies were out of tune and that her winter sky map was needed. 
Mara came to hush the dissonance of old guilt: thirty years ago she had ignored her infant brother's last cry, choosing her needles over the stair, and fever took him before dawn. 

Inside, Warden Iven led her past cabinets of vanished noises: drowned bells, final vowels, a door closing for the last time. 
At the center stood a brass cradle machine run by haunted firmware that translates baby cries into plain sentences for those who have forgotten how to listen. 
Now, in every language, it printed the words You did not come, bending archived lullabies into a scrape of wrong notes. 
The printed words loosened other lost sounds, and the dome above them reflected a sky no living generation had seen. 
Iven gave her a piece of amber with an insect inside, found in the first cradle machine, and the insect's dry wings still beat whenever a cry passed. 
Because the firmware corrupted every typed or spoken order during the fold, repair instructions reached the wardens via handwritten letters found in antique furniture. 
One lay in a walnut desk, one in a child's wardrobe, and one in the leg of an ancient rocking cradle. 
The ink was Mara's mother's, though her mother had never worked there in any year Mara remembered. 
The letters said the machine had been built to catch a lost cry, but without a listener the cry became a ghost in the code and pulled other griefs into its loop. 
They said a constellation tattooist must tether that ghost to the sky as it was on the night of the first unanswered cry. 
Only then could the amber, the wing beat, and the machine agree on one moment. 

The last letter's margin stars matched the unfinished winter tattoo on Mara's left shoulder, begun the night her brother died and never completed. 
Outside, the folded hour pressed its cheek to the glass; yesterday's snow and tomorrow's ash lay together on the sill under a meteor hush. 
In the antique cradle, handwritten letters rearranged themselves into Mara's old movements: kettle, needle, stair, the unentered room. 
The cradle machine shuddered, translating hunger, pain, fear, and a sound too young for names; its paper spilled the words Come now in her mother's script. 
Mara saw that the fold had returned her mother's letters from a future attempt to repair what Mara could not forgive in herself. 
The ghost in the firmware was not malice but one family silence passed from listener to listener. 

She took up her needles, not for a client, but to finish the winter sky on her own shoulder. 
With ink mixed from lampblack and a grain scraped from the amber, she set the missing star where the letters instructed. 
Iven held the piece of amber with an insect inside against the cradle machine's clock port, and the fossil insect beat its wings once. 
Mara pressed her bleeding shoulder to the cabinet and told Iven to tether its cable to her wrist; the ghost needed a living chart. 
When the second hand touched the folded hour, she let the old cry enter her mapped skin. 
It did not accuse her; it was only a baby calling from a room where no one had yet decided to be brave. 
Mara answered aloud: young, frightened, wrong, and done with silence. 
The firmware shivered, cabinets shed their wrong notes, and lost sounds settled into harmless distances. 
In the desk drawer, the letters faded, the wardrobe and cradle became ordinary furniture, and Mara returned the amber to Iven with a keeper's care. 
Dawn arrived without folding; the laboratory of lost sounds opened onto familiar stars, and beneath Mara's completed map, old guilt softened into a note she could carry. 
Outside, the meteor hush remained, not as silence imposed on grief, but as the space after a falling light where someone finally hears and answers.
