Amid the hush of unstruck chords, Mara eased her skiff between the drowned rows of the flooded opera house lit by flickering lanterns.  She had been an orchestra pit musician for twenty seven years, long enough to know that silence could have a seating chart.  Water covered the third pit stair; velvet seats floated like dark lilies beneath gilt balconies.  Rain worked through torn scenery, and lanterns guttered without quite going out.  Under her coat lay the antique opera program from the last performance, wrapped in oilcloth.  That night, a river wall failed during the final act, and Ione, the prompter, vanished below the pit with the floodgate crank still in her hand.  Officials called her careless, then sealed the story under plaster and a subscriber's plaque.  Mara had never accepted the vanished chord in that account, the one awaiting the orchestra when water took the lamps.  Lately inspired by dampness rather than memory, she had come to unravel the code left by a ghost.  The first clue appeared when water damage creating images across the program made a tiny hand inside a printed margin.  The hand pointed not to words, but to the pale gap between two columns of donors.  Other stains followed: a fan, a stair, nine dots, and a bell with its tongue erased.  They emerged near flame, then faded when she tried to name them.  Mara understood that the message lived in between spaces, not in the ink respectable people had saved.  She had spent her life in between spaces, below the stage, above the cellar, between written notes and sounded ones.  At dawn, a restoration crew would drain the house and replace the pit, so she had one night.  She tied the skiff to the conductor's rail and set three lanterns on protruding music stands.  Each flame leaned toward the program, as if asking for the next page.  Mara opened it flat on a submerged stand, letting the lower edge drink.  A brown watermark climbed the cast list, drawing Ione's profile from a smear of old glue.  No words came, only a tilt of the head toward the prompter's box, and Mara answered by lifting the left lantern.  Its ring broke across the water, reassembled, and pointed under the stage.  It was a wordless conversation, but not vague; each gesture had the precision of a bowed entrance.  Mara moved the oar once to disrupt the mirror, and broken reflections rearranged the stains into a stairwell seen from below.  She knew that passage, a service stair hidden behind a panel, bricked up after the inquiry.  Lantern in hand, program under her coat, she waded until her shoulder found the loose panel.  Behind it, brick dust sifted into black water, and nine nail heads in a rotted beam repeated the margin's dots.  Mara pried the beam with her bow case and felt cold air move against her wrist.  The passage was neither dry nor drowned, a thin in between space where the flood had not decided what to claim.  At the bottom, her lantern lit an iron crank, a rusted valve, and a sleeve of oilcloth caught on the handle.  Inside lay the conductor's missing page, sealed in wax, with every note scraped away except the rests.  Mara laughed once without sound, recognizing Ione's patience and the shape of the code.  The ghost had not wanted music; she had counted silences between commands, showing where the floodgate had been left closed.  Mara placed the waxed page against the antique program, and a new stain formed the same open hand.  The hand did not point outward now; it waited, palm up, as Ione's ripples waited beside the skiff.  Mara understood because she had lived it from the pit: do not strike the chord, open the gate.  She set her shoulder to the crank, and the iron groaned through the water like a held breath released.  Above, a sluice gave way, and the opera house began to drain with a low, steady whisper.  The lanterns straightened, the images faded, and the space between Mara and Ione grew bright with nothing either needed to say.  At dawn the crew would find an open gate, a cleared name, and a pit still waiting in the hush.
