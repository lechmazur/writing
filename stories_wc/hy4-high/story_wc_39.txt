It was at dawn's earliest whistle that Oran Vey, a vagrant penny poet, crawled through the seam of the feral loft beneath the dunes with six empty glass jars knocking at his ribs. 
The sound slipped from a reed lodged in the buried chimney, and pale sand light lay over fox prints, cracked mirrors, and wild barley in a lonesome splendor. 
The children in the pump camp called him positively wistful, because even his comic rhymes bent toward doorways nobody could open anymore. 
He sold couplets for a penny, slept under forgiving roofs, and spent each coin on corks, wax, and tickets to nowhere comfortable. 
What he wanted most was to become the translator between generations, though the elders and the young agreed on almost nothing else. 
The elders spoke of harbor bells and buried orchards, while the children spoke of shift whistles, pump numbers, and fresh wells. 
For him, capturing sounds in glass jars was less a mystery than a patient grammar, learned by listening to creamery bottles cool after the morning carts. 
A sealed jar kept a voice without its grudge, and two uncorked voices made a third meaning that neither speaker could have managed alone. 
That third meaning was why the council had granted him one morning before workmen sealed the loft and moved the old people uphill. 
He had come to sweeten a rusted wedding air that both camps remembered wrongly, since grief had soured it for the old and mockery had soured it for the young. 
The air lived in his mother's music box, which he had traded to a peddler for a rail ticket when the first dune crossed the orchard wall. 
He called the hope of returning it, note by salvaged note, gleaned redemption, because no single grand apology could cover a lifetime of departures. 
His sister Sel had been nine when he left, old enough to count coins and young enough to expect him by supper. 
Now she managed the pump camp, answered his letters with blank paper, and brought her daughter Pip to every argument about the doomed building. 
Before sunrise, Oran had hidden under the warped dance floor because the foreman would have barred a thief from any official farewell. 
There, in a nest of fox fur and broken sheet music, he found the forgotten music box with his mother's initials scratched inside the lid. 
Sand furred its velvet bed, salt had locked the brass cylinder, and the crank lay bent like a question asked too often. 
He did not touch it at first, because the lonesome splendor of light on its scars made his practiced wistfulness feel like cheap coin. 
Then he wiped the cylinder and remembered the peddler saying the loft swallowed goods, grudges, and one inconvenient box. 
Oran set six jars around the box, mouths inward, and waited for the chimney reed to gather the next thin breath of morning. 
From the first jar he released old Marta naming the harbor bell, each syllable roughened by eighty years of blown sand. 
From the second he released Pip asking for water, her small voice full of tomorrow and innocent of the orchard she had never seen. 
To sweeten the bitter interval between them, he added a bee in dune grass, a pump starting, and Sel laughing once at a spilled cup. 
The glass clouds thickened, the fox kits stilled, and the buried chimney drew a second whistle exactly when the first sun touched the highest dune. 
Oran turned the bent crank with two fingers, not forcing the mechanism, but offering it the borrowed breath of every jar at once. 
The cylinder answered in a thin wedding tune, then Marta's harbor, Pip's water, the bee, the pump, and Sel's laughter found places among its missing teeth. 
It was not the old song restored, and it was not a child's game, but a version sturdy enough for both sorrows to inhabit. 
When Sel arrived with the elders, Pip, and the foreman, she found Oran sitting beside the open box with empty hands and no prepared poem. 
The tune played once more, and old Marta hummed the harbor name while Pip clapped the pump rhythm, each supplying what the other lacked. 
Sel listened without softening, because gleaned redemption had never promised to erase a stolen year. 
She set the box in Pip's arms, delayed the sealing, and placed one penny on Oran's palm. 
He left with the coin unspent, hearing the wedding air follow him, no longer an exile's apology but a road between voices.
