That month, amid the hush of old videotapes left unwatched, sea fog entered the fog sculpture garden and made the iron saints lean inland. 
The rental shed beside it had closed after my mother died, leaving boxed birthdays and unchosen futures inside a silence that seemed recorded. 
My father, Orin, kept its keys and the neighboring salvage yard, so the town called him the junkyard prophet. 
From cracked dashboards and dead clocks, he could name the winters and minutes other people had not yet reached. 
His gift had made him precognitively forgetful: each future arrived by taking the map that led to it. 
When he foresaw my train, he forgot he had a daughter until I stood before him. 
I came anyway with a recorder from the city archives where I repaired other people's losses. 
He had asked for it because he wanted to capture the sound of growing older before remembering thinned to salt. 
Not my mother's voice, he said, but the small noise a living body makes while consenting to time. 
The garden had been built from his scrap pumps by a sculptor who bought every broken fan in the yard. 
Pipes breathed Atlantic mist through hollow figures that became deer, doors, or mothers bending over baths. 
People came there clinging to echoes, ears to damp plaster, asking the sealed cassettes to confess missed lives. 
Orin understood them; after the funeral he had polished one cassette for twelve years and never played it. 
Now he wanted a sound that did not ask the dead to stand still. 
He had foreseen the Listening Hollow opening on the spring tide, then forgotten where it lay. 
So we had come to locate it before water covered the lower paths. 
His method was arranging driftwood as directional markers at low tide, while the future was still too distant to steal the turns. 
In his coat he carried a piece of plain chalk, because colored chalk made warnings look decorative. 
On each branch he had written one word, and on his cuff he had written Nell. 
At the station he read my name, looked relieved, and said it like a country almost misplaced. 
Fog pooled at the garden gate and erased everything beyond the first iron saint. 
Orin touched a gray branch angled east and found the chalk word GATE blurred to a moon. 
He drew a shorter line below it and waited for his hands to remember what his mind had lost. 
The marker pointed toward a plaster woman whose lifted arms filled with mist, emptied, and filled again. 
That breathing filled the garden, a tidal longing that touched the statues and withdrew before anyone called it love. 
I held the recorder against my ribs and followed pale arrows that surfaced whenever the fog thinned. 
Twice he asked whether the next word was BELL or BONE, and twice the chalk answered. 
At the third turning, the sea reached under the stones and breathed through the buried pipes. 
A low note moved among the figures, not a voice but the hush around one, older each time it returned. 
Orin knelt in the Listening Hollow, where scrap tape reels lay in clay like black moons. 
He drew a circle around the reels, not to trap the sound but to give forgetting a shoreline. 
When the water reaches the circle, he said, do not rewind and do not write a new arrow. 
I wanted to argue, because a daughter forgotten in advance wants every path labeled twice. 
Then the tide slipped into the basin; the chalk softened, and all the iron saints seemed to inhale. 
The recorder caught a faint tick, like a knee bending in sleep, followed by a dry whisper from chalk on slate. 
Orin listened without triumph, and I understood the sound was not an echo but a leaving. 
The tide took the circle, then rearranged the driftwood so every marker pointed back to the sea. 
He did not chalk new arrows, though I saw his fingers search his coat. 
On the way out he looked at his cuff, read Nell, and chose my face instead of the word. 
Behind us, the garden went on breathing with the unwatched tapes, no longer asking anyone to stay. 
I carried the recorder under my coat as if it held a coal, listening to tidal longing go out and come back without possession.
