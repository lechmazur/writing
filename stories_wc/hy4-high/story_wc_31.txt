Each year during a sky lantern release, the harbor entered a meteor hush, as if the town held its breath for falling stars. 
Ivo Vale, the late-shift pier lamplighter, lowered the pier beacons so the rising lanterns could be seen without vanity. 
On their paper skins townspeople wrote luminous vows, promises that shone only while someone honored them. 
A kept vow came home at dawn as a coal in the stove; a broken one sank through the tide to be shelved below. 
No map named the forgotten library inside a sunken ship, because sailors preferred not to know where their promises went. 
Ivo knew the door existed, since the Harbor Office had returned a hotel skeleton key tag from Room 12 after Laleh vanished. 
Laleh tuned harbor bells and followed a low hum down the tide stairs before dawn to discover the color of sound. 
At the Seaglass Hotel they had made two luminous vows: return before the lamps went out, and never let them go black. 
They wrote both vows on one lantern, and its steady light had frightened him. 
By sunrise the clerk said lost at sea; Ivo learned the clerk hides behind truth: drowned meant absent at roll call, not necessarily dead. 
That record gave him a grave and denied him a door, so he kept the tag in his coat and his vow at Pier Nine. 
Every release since, the beacon answered the rising lanterns with a faint hum in Laleh's tuning B. 
He never followed it; an absent lamplighter could make a hundred luminous vows miss their way home. 
This year the hum came with three small knocks, like a knuckle against a glass chimney. 
He had called it rescue, but his motive was to discover the color of sound that had chosen his lamp. 
He waited for the last launch, lowered the screen, and left the beacon one hour of oil. 
Under the crowd's meteor hush he fitted the tag into the tide door's brass slot. 
The lock had no tumblers, only a scale pan and a line: give what you kept, and let it signify. 
Ivo set the tag on the pan and made it signify not Room 12 but seven years of flame. 
It glowed with the exact light of their lantern, and the door opened on a stair of black water. 
He descended without splashing past cold railings, drowned letters, and small fish like half-thoughts. 
The ship lay on its side, ribs furred with weed, but the shelves stood upright by old discipline. 
Its books were clouded panes holding the last brightness of failed vows. 
Their whispers made the meteor hush loud, each unfinished promise speaking in its maker's voice. 
A straight aisle returned him to the brass lamp and Laleh's rule: only by wandering purposefully could a reader find the room; haste made circles. 
He chose turns by the hum in his teeth, letting grief set pace but not direction. 
He passed wedding promises, bad debts, and one clear pane saying, in his voice, Never let the lamps go black. 
The listening room held a table, a green lamp, and a keeper made of salt and margins. 
She told no lies; when asked where Laleh was, she answered, Not above. 
He nearly asked again, then saw truth as the screen he had pressed against for seven years. 
Instead he asked what color the returning hum had made inside a kept vow. 
The keeper touched the lamp; Ivo hung the hotel skeleton key tag there, because answers needed a kept vow for light. 
His clear pane flew from the aisle, settled over the wick, and burned pale green with a small gold edge. 
Under it the glass table opened on Laleh's last page: not all returns have feet; a sound's color is changed by the vow that hears it. 
Ivo left the page; above, it would become evidence, and evidence another locked door. 
He listened until the green and gold thinned, then climbed the stair with the tag before his hour was spent. 
Pier Nine still burned; the first luminous vows fell back as coals of dawn. 
He raised the screen, logged Returned in B, and left the tag above the lamp. 
After that, when the beacon hummed in the meteor hush, he saw pale green edged with gold and kept the flames low.
