At dawn, in the hush of a hidden sunrise, Mara entered echo gorge carrying a misaligned harp across her back and a crocodile's tooth on a cord at her throat. 
Sunrise stayed unseen, but its light lay under mist like a coin beneath glass. 
Mara was the district's lost soul collector, though she carried no cages, chains, or polished urns. 
The dead stayed as spectral confessions, scraps of truth snagged on stone until someone gave them shape. 
Only through the distorted notes of a misaligned harp could those scraps be heard and released. 
The harp was Ivo's last foolish gift, its frame warped by river damp and its pegs set a half tone apart. 
He believed straight music flattered the living, while crooked music made room for the dead. 
He had also believed that every healing should have one honest joke in it. 
Mara had argued, because their mother had died of fever and jokes had seemed like unlocked doors in a plague wind. 
Ivo only smiled and tied the crocodile's tooth around her neck. 
It came from his boot after a marsh crocodile bit the leather, and Ivo laughed barefoot all the way home. 
"The river wanted my boot, not my foot," he said. 
"Keep it for the punch line." 
Ten winters later, Ivo was the one unfinished thing in echo gorge. 
During the flood, he carried three children to the upper ledge, then vanished when the rope burned through his palms. 
His body was never found; his last shout went into the cliffs and broke into hundreds of nearly identical pleas. 
Collectors had copied those pleas as cowardice, grief, or pain, yet none had settled. 
Mara knew why, and that knowledge kept her from the descent until the hidden sunrise of that morning. 
Ivo's confession was not missing; she had been refusing the shape it wanted. 
The council called her refusal discipline. 
She called it fear with good manners. 
Mara, modestly fearless, admitted the tremor in her hands and began to pursue the lowest echo down the terraces. 
At each turn she followed the echo through the distorted notes of a misaligned harp, wincing at the sour intervals that came back doubled and late. 
A mason confessed he had loosened a bridge stone for a wager, and the gorge freed him with a sigh like warm bread. 
A bride confessed she had loved her sister more than her husband, and pale mist unwound from the black ledge. 
Mara wrote nothing down. 
True spectral confessions needed no ink once heard; they needed a living person willing to carry the ache without correcting it. 
Her reason had narrowed to integrate laughter into healing, not as sugar on sorrow but as proof that sorrow had not owned the whole life. 
The old rites left families cleansed and mute. 
She wanted them to leave able to speak of the dead at supper without lowering their voices. 
At the bottom of echo gorge, the hidden sunrise gathered in rose light beneath basalt. 
There, Ivo's echo waited, not screaming, not pleading, but repeating the small cough he made before telling a joke. 
Mara set the harp on a flat stone, but the lowest string snapped the moment her fingers touched it. 
The break was so ordinary, so like a kettle cracking, that a laugh startled out of her. 
The cliffs answered with Ivo's laugh, thinner now, then with his confession, which came through the broken harp as if he were kneeling beside her. 
He confessed he had been afraid on the rope, had let go to push the smallest child higher, wanted their mother's stew, and believed the river still wanted his other boot. 
The joke was not decoration; it was the last brave shape his love could take. 
She lifted the crocodile's tooth from its cord, wedged it under the slack string, and plucked until the note buzzed like a child laughing into a cup. 
The gorge took that ugly, merry sound and returned it changed: fear, guilt, stew, bare feet, and bootless punch line all held together in one luminous ache. 
Mara laughed with it, and the sound did not erase her grief; it gave the grief windows. 
When she climbed out, she told the waiting families the whole confession, including the missing boot, and taught them to answer with a chuckle before prayer.
