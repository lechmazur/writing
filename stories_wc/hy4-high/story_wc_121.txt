On the glowing viaduct after rain, lamps deepened the dark, giving wet stone a darkening brightness that showed Mara every lie.  Mara was a gravity defier, trained to walk down air and carry climbers back to weight.  Tonight her license was a faded scrap in her coat, and the bridge she needed to cross had no span, only a raindrop pausing above a puddle.  The engineers called that sliver between the drop and the ripple: the last drop had left the parapet but had not opened the water.  In that sliver the viaduct became periodically eternal, dissolving age while leaving choice intact.  Only then could the ancient clay tablet with runes be read, because in ordinary minutes it existed as dust in a dozen contradictory hands.  It lay now beneath the central arch, whole and warm, its runes shifting like ants under a pane of amber.  Mara knelt, not to beg forgiveness from the city, but to conquer the labyrinth of self that had grown from one refusal.  Ten storms earlier, she had stepped from this parapet to prove to her brother Ori that falling could be declined.  Ori, laughing and terrified, reached for her sleeve, and her hand opened before she knew whether fear or pride had moved it.  The fall should have ended in one grave; instead the city made fractured timelines, each assigning guilt to a different Mara.  Since then she woke into mornings where Ori had lived, where she had died, and where strangers wore her face.  The runes on the tablet were not a map of those variants; they were instructions for making more of them.  A supplicant could retrace the first refusal through procedural generation, each answer seeding the next corridor, stair, witness, and lie.  Mara had spent years denying that she needed such a brutal method, yet every easier prayer had merely fed another version of her pride.  She set her palm on the clay and said, "Once more."  The raindrop held.  The puddle beneath it held its black mirror still, and the arches along the viaduct unfolded into a maze of mirrored rooms.  Mara rose and walked upward, because the first corridor had been generated from her childhood boast that she would never need anyone to catch her.  Gravity loosened its claim with patient familiarity, and she answered by taking the ceiling as a floor.  In a chamber of blue tile, a girl in a school coat told her that Ori had always been clumsy.  Mara said, "He was frightened," and the wall split to make a corridor where she admitted that fear had been hers.  Each confession generated a new passage, and every passage contained another Mara with a cleaner story.  Some wore rescue medals, some wore mourning black, some carried Ori by the hand, and all of them asked her to choose them as the true one.  The brightness deepened as she advanced, until each lamp showed less of the maze and more of the small, clenched shape behind every decision.  She understood then that the labyrinth was not made to hide Ori, but to keep her hunting for an innocent self.  The thought nearly made another corridor, for pride was an excellent seed, but Mara gripped the edge of the tablet and refused the comfort of branching.  At the center of the maze, the suspended drop appeared again, huge as a bell, with the face of Ori turned inside its curve.  He was not asking to be saved from the fall; he was asking why she had made of herself a place no one could reach.  Every Mara behind her answered at once, and their excuses filled the chamber like birds beating against glass.  Instead of selecting one voice, she said, "All of you are mine," and the words were so plain that no rune could improve them.  The generated corridors folded inward, not destroyed but owned, and the labyrinth of self became a single narrow stair.  Mara descended it without defying gravity, letting weight teach her the shape of the act she had spent a lifetime escaping.  At the bottom, the tablet had already begun to crumble, because the periodically eternal instant was ending.  She did not try to save it, and this time the refusal was not a wound but a boundary.  The drop touched the puddle.  A ripple opened, small and ordinary, and the viaduct returned to minutes that could age her.  Mara walked home, no longer innocent or multiplied, in darkening brightness.
