In the velvet library in autumn, the curtains held the color of bruised plums, and the radiators clicked like small tired beetles beneath shelves of pattern books, funeral scores, and tools for vanishing arts.  Neda Orr, the harmonious choir teacher, arrived each Thursday with a satchel of chalk, spare reeds, and an expectantly resigned smile, because the town had promised singers before and usually sent grievances.  She had returned from years of tuning bells in occupied districts to braid diverse voices into harmony, and she believed that a chord could reveal what a treaty could not: who still heard whom.  The term stretched across unlearning hatred, from leaf-fall to the thawing of the canal, because no calendar could predict when suspicion would loosen.  On the first night only six people came, three from the river parish and three from the ridge families who had once lit signal fires for the raiders.  They kept their coats on, as if harmony might be asked to leave before tea.  A traveled stillness settled among them, not peace exactly, but the hush of long roads, inherited names, and boots that had learned where not to step.  Madam Vale, the librarian, had no piano, so she unlocked the cabinet where the last master luthier's work was kept.  From a folded cloth she lifted a zither soundhole rosette, a thin pearwood ring carved with twelve unequal petals and darkened by a century of breath.  It belonged to the peace choir that had sung in the library before the occupation, when enemies were merely neighbors with louder opinions.  The rosette had been found nailed under a choir bench, and no one could say what its missing petals were meant to denote.  Neda held it to the lamplight and saw that the cutouts made a map of shadows on the green baize table.  Rather than tell the singers what to feel, she asked them to denote, with blue chalk, every place where a shadow asked for silence.  That was her whole method, though it sounded like no method at all: she taught by subtracting noise.  First she stopped the radiator with a folded glove, then covered the ticking clock, then asked everyone to lay old accusations on the table and leave them there until the final chord.  When the room was bare of everything but breathing, the six voices sounded ragged, each one defending a private version of the dead.  Ivo from the ridge refused to sing beside Lina, whose brother had vanished after the mill fire, and Lina kept time by striking her boot against the chair rung.  Neda did not scold the boot; she subtracted it, slipping a velvet cloth beneath her heel until the beat became a bruise no one had to hear.  Week by week, the class treated the rosette as both score and witness, because its empty petals seemed to answer the town's worst habit of filling every gap with blame.  They learned that a rest was not surrender, that an unarmed breath could carry a bass line, and that listening to another person's grief did not erase their own.  Neda made them trade seats every rehearsal, then trade languages for the old refrain, until the river vowels and ridge consonants rubbed smoothly against one another.  At midwinter, snow sealed the library steps, and the librarian found a ledger describing the vanished instrument maker's craft: he had tuned zithers by subtracting noise.  He cut each rosette according to the room, leaving openings where some partial tones muddied the strings, so the wood removed what metal could not.  Neda read the passage twice, then understood that the chalk marks around the table were a human version of the same carpentry.  The rosette did not tell them what note to add; it told them which habitual sound had to go.  After that, Ivo removed the triumph from his high notes, Lina removed the revenge from her timing, and old Ansel stopped humming the marching tune he had brought from prison.  What remained was not softer, exactly; it was clearer, as if the air had been folded back to show its original grain.  By the thaw, the town asked to hear what the choir had made.  Neda set the rosette in the reading lamp, where its shadow marked six places to stand and one empty place to honor.  No one called the performance forgiveness; it was a song with enough cleared space for differences to meet.  When the final chord faded, traveled stillness held while Madam Vale catalogued the rosette and Neda, expectantly resigned no more, wrote: continue.
