After the drowning, decades later, Mara Venn came back to the sunken orchard greenhouse ringed by shipwrecks at the slack hour when habits break.  She was a hands-on folklorist, with a trowel, a wax pencil, and no patience for tidy theories.  Her return was not sentimental, though cold apple under brine recalled her mother's kitchen.  She had come to recover stolen memories because her own question had once helped index them.  At twelve she had been chronically curious, slipping her hand into locked drawers and asking why the harvest lamps were lit only for the dead.  That curiosity had made her crouch behind the pear bench when Harbor Master Sale brought his tin boxes and his polite, ruinous contract.  The adults signed because the storm had taken the roofs, and Sale promised to keep their first memories safe in a dry archive.  He cracked the navigation lamps of wrecked ships, set their shards into the glass roof, and sold those memories to a city that wanted obedient workers.  Too young to sign, Mara watched him lift a small star from the lamp of the Mary Ellen and heard herself ask its name.  Her exact question became the index in his ledger, and by morning Bellwater had forgotten its orchard songs but still set out clean cups.  Mara had spent the intervening years collecting seed labels, door charms, riddles, the exact ways old women tied knots, and a copy of Sale's ledger, because folklore kept better in hands than in grief.  By comparing those objects with the sale marks in that copy, she learned to detect the hollows by studying broken constellations.  Each constellation above the greenhouse was a family chart of wreck lights; each missing shard dated a theft.  To a broker the cracks looked like storm damage, but to a folklorist with rubbings of every lamp they read like a torn index.  She carried a fairy ring mushroom in a flat tobacco tin, dry as paper and still smelling of the November grass where she had found it.  On the night of the contract, it had pushed through the ring around the oldest apple tree, before Sale broke the Mary Ellen lamp.  Its gills had caught the lamplight first, preserving the unbroken sky in a pattern finer than any ink she owned.  For years she had thought the specimen merely proof that she had once been there, but at the greenhouse threshold the cap warmed in her palm.  Water reached her boots, clear enough to show the tiled path, drowned labels, and the small brass screws Sale had driven between panes.  Outside, the shipwrecks leaned in their circle, their ribs fretted with weed and their lantern holes aimed at the roof like empty eyes.  At that hour, when habits break, the tide stopped retracing its old line, the greenhouse clocks ran backward, and even the gulls forgot their usual complaints.  Mara knew the lapse was brief, the only interval in which memory could be coaxed out of stolen light and back into its first owners.  She set her tools on a dry brick: trowel, wax pencil, lamp rubbings, needle, twine, and the tin with the fairy ring mushroom.  Rather than curse Sale, she worked as her mother had taught her to work, by mending one true joint at a time.  She wedged the mushroom beneath the oldest pane so its gills faced the wreck lanterns, then scraped salt from three brass screws.  The broken constellations trembled on the flooded tiles, not healed, but willing to be read.  She matched the preserved print under the cap to the gaps overhead, naming each lost lamp as she tied its screw to its neighbor with twine.  It was slow, physical work, and the greenhouse seemed to approve: glass ticked, apple wood steadied, and the water carried a younger smell of hay.  When the last screw touched the last string, Mara detected a faint rearrangement in the roof, the click of a box being opened far away.  She did not see the city, only guessed its clerks finding shelves of first kisses, recipes, burial names, and orchard choruses suddenly blank.  The memories came back without thunder, as precise and ordinary as the smell of a mother's apron or the weight of a pear in the palm.  The last memory to settle was her mother's laugh at that forbidden question, and Mara, still chronically curious, finally knew what her curiosity was for.  She closed the empty tin; the orchard smelled of November grass.
