In the copperlight repose before they find the bodies, the lighthouse prison off a jagged reef seems less built than forgiven.  Copper shutters warm the lantern, and the sea lies still as if listening for a bell that must not be rung.  In the register, the warden had written "cry wolf boy who cried wolf's son" because mainland clerks loved curses more than names.  Your father had learned that a false alarm could empty a village and leave real wolves to feed unchallenged.  You grew up under the silence after that lesson, apologizing for alarms before anyone heard them.  So you came here to mind other men's alarms, and the truer motive was to thread a labyrinth with your own story.  Warden Sella said the prisoners were not serving sentences; they were chasing hush, the pause after a warning no one believes.  She told you that the hush sustains impossibilities, and here impossibilities were the only reliable walls.  Cells doubled back on themselves, and drowned men could hover a hand's breadth above wet stone.  The dead did not escape; they hovered in the copperlight until a true story gave them weight.  Lies had made the first hush and could never mend it.  The label tied to the pierced oak screen said simply: lattice from confessional.  You carried it each night to the stair where the maze began, feeling its holes chill your palms.  A narrow door stood beside the stair, left ajar for decades because the maze breathed through its finger of darkness.  Generations of shoulders and keys had worried pale scuffs into the varnish.  By setting the lattice behind the lantern, you read the labyrinth through the scuffs on a narrow door left ajar for decades.  Copper light poured through oak holes and scraped lines, making projected corridors to cells where prisoners waited.  Your task was to whisper a story into the lattice until the hush followed it from cell to cell.  Sella called this mercy; you called it bookkeeping, because every impossible body had to be entered before sunrise.  There were fourteen keepers, smugglers, and sailors with dry coats, blue lips, and boats broken on the jagged reef.  Mainland inspectors would count them as murdered if they found them before the confessions were threaded.  Sella wanted another night of hush, enough time to invent a kinder ledger for the prison.  You wanted the ledger to stop lying, though that felt like betraying your father twice.  Near midnight, the hush slipped its leash and went hunting for the bell rope.  Sella ordered you to chase it with the prison's oldest story, the official one in which nobody drowned.  Instead, you pressed your mouth to the lattice and gave the maze your father's village, its false shout, and its unguarded sheep.  You admitted years spent lowering your voice, dread inherited like a tool, and your hope that stone would believe you.  The hush came close, not calmed but recognized, the way a dog recognizes the hand that once called it wolf.  It wound through the scuffs, along the projected corridors, and into all fourteen waiting mouths.  One by one, the dead confessed not crimes but the small alarms they had ignored before the reef took them.  One named a loose sheet, another a cracked oar, another a gull striking the lens; each truth settled the bodies lower.  Sella watched the impossible walls fade and did not stop you; perhaps copperlight repose had made honesty seem less violent than rescue.  At the last cell, the youngest sailor gives you the bell rope and asks you not to ring.  You understand then that a warning can also be a wound when no one answers it.  You do not ring; you tie the rope to the lattice so the first true story has something to hold.  By dawn, mainland boats are close enough for oars to be heard against the reef.  The bodies lie where searchers will find them, no longer impossible, but no longer silent.  Before they find the bodies, you leave your confession in the maze and close the narrow door only halfway.  The copperlight repose stays on the water, steady as a hand that has finally stopped trembling.
