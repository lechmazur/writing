Under the hush of borrowed starlight, the astronomical clock tower held its breath above the canal. 
In the lantern room, Mara, the harmonious choral librarian, tuned eight copper tubes for the grain elevator talks. 
Those talks were no metaphor: each elevator gathered what people muttered while unloading wheat, then returned those stored voices through the tower at midsummer. 
For a century the town had used that chorus as a ledger, since each silo remembered what its own bank preferred to forget. 
Lately north had heard only north, south only south, and every grievance had grown a harder shell. 
The Council voted to scrap Old Seven, silent since the flood, and melt its ribs into a gate. 
Mara had come to rehabilitate the elevator, not pour another silence into iron. 
Her commission, older than the Council and written beneath the zodiac dial, was to construct bridges from silos. 
She carried it like family debt: her parents had died on opposite banks, each certain the other had ignored the warning bell. 
In her palm lay the cuckoo clock weight, a dented brass pinecone from the kitchen clock her mother had wound. 
During the flood, her brother Tomas had tied it to Old Seven's reply cord as a prank, and the extra pull had jammed the answer gear. 
The Council called that sabotage; Mara called it embarrassment, because Tomas drowned hours later trying to rope the south doors shut. 
Three years of questions had taught her how a downward tug might answer rather than choke. 
Now the hour was short: when the lens returned the borrowed light, the tubes would cool and unsaid names would harden into dust. 
Below, markets pretended not to watch; above, apprentices steadied the orrery, moon dial, and star charts. 
Mara set a vigorous tender rhythm: stamp for beams, hum for sleepers, clap for children. 
They flushed and worked harder; grief had become useful, not small. 
At the first cuckoo, northern tubes accused the south of opening sluices for profit. 
At the second, southern tubes answered with drowned mules, ruined ovens, and unpaid wages. 
Old Seven remained silent, its black tube shivering like a throat that had forgotten water. 
Mara did not scold it; she laid her palm on the cold copper and waited. 
Empathetically decisive, she heard the apprentices protect her mother's keepsake, thanked them, and ordered the weight fastened to the breaking chain. 
That order cost the keepsake but gave Old Seven a counterweight that could fall with consent. 
The chain tightened, the zodiac dial clicked one degree, and every lamp leaned toward the dark tube. 
Mara opened the harvest index and began her repair. 
She named bread in English, Spanish, Polish, Ukrainian, Anishinaabemowin, and old mill Latin, interspersing languages until no single bank could own the sentence. 
The phrase entered the tube as a chord, rough with consonants and warm with breath. 
North echoed only English; south echoed only Spanish; Old Seven coughed dust. 
Mara lifted both hands and, interspersing languages more boldly, made the choir repeat the list without letting any word keep its usual place. 
The escapement, built for planets rather than parishes, liked disorder. 
On the third cuckoo, the cuckoo clock weight dropped the length of Mara's forearm. 
It did not yank the gear; it steadied it like a nurse at a first step. 
Old Seven spoke in a braid of voices, beginning with the warning bell that both banks had heard and both had answered. 
It returned a carpenter running toward the sluice, a baker hauling children, and Tomas wanting the clock to sing for their mother. 
The words were no verdict but a bridge plank, and the people below felt it through the tubes underfoot. 
Mara wept once, then turned lament into a work song. 
She ordered the north conveyor and south catwalk unbolted, swung across the canal, and locked to Old Seven's curved ribs before the starlight expired. 
Apprentices, bankers, and the Council clerk pulled together, sleeves rolled and faces streaked with dust. 
Under that vigorous tender labor, two narrow bridges rose from silos; by dawn they joined the markets where a gate had been planned. 
Old Seven, rehabilitated by recovered speech, ticked with the tower chorus. 
At future grain elevator talks, no bank asked for a private version; people crossed first, sang in mixed tongues, and carried bread over the water.
