Mara Vey was the village's thoughtful gravestone carver, fiercely meditative in the way she listened before lifting a chisel. 
After the river took the lower quarter, the council asked for one memorial instead of forty separate stones. 
She accepted the commission not for praise, but to discover the shape of collective grief. 
Stone dust had taught her to honor private sorrow, yet the new task seemed to breathe through every house at once. 
Each evening she entered the ancient greenhouse at dusk, when storm light gathered under the ribs of green glass. 
The panes had been set by monks before the town possessed a name, and they held a thundering calm while thunder rolled over the hills. 
Across the butterfly migration, from first orange cloud to last ragged remnant, she worked there with the doors unlatched. 
Monarchs funneled through the broken roof, settled on rosemary, and made the air seem stitched to their patience. 
On the potting bench lay a woven ribbon roll left by her grandmother, each strand carrying a household color. 
Mara had used it to bind funeral wreaths, never to measure anything larger than a name. 
Now the roll waited beside bread, olives, and one empty chair. 
For guidance she relied on a family custom of trading favorite poems at dinner, practiced even when death made the table smaller. 
She recited a poem chosen by the baker, whose two sons had vanished with the mill, and waited with her fork laid across the empty plate. 
The spectral mentorship began with her grandmother's chalk dust moving across the slate, drawing a line no living hand could draw. 
At first Mara thought the line marked a grave, but it bent toward the path where the baker walked after the flood. 
The dead were not asking for more letters; they were asking her to retrace the routes of the living. 
At every dinner she would retrace those routes, following the baker to the bridge, the widow to the pump, the schoolmaster to his daughter's shoe beneath the sycamore. 
With every favorite poem, another ghost joined the teaching, leaving frost or pollen on the slate. 
They taught her to notice where errands had stopped, where meals had been shared, where silence crossed itself like a road. 
She pinned ribbon ends to a floor plan of the lower quarter, then tied them to the roll so the colors would not scatter. 
By the fourth evening, the woven strands made loops around the greenhouse, and butterflies moved through them like thoughts. 
Mara's fierce meditations, once fixed on single names, became a listening broad enough for overlapping absence. 
She kept her breathing slow because haste felt like carving over a pulse. 
Outside, thunder broke over the river with such force that windows rang, yet inside the greenhouse the old calm grew louder. 
On the seventh night, the council clerk came to complain that no memorial stone had been cut. 
He found her at dinner, trading a favorite poem with an empty chair while orange wings trembled in her hair. 
He asked for names, because names were what people could read. 
Mara offered him bread and asked which poem his mother loved before the flood. 
The clerk flushed, recited a ragged verse about swallows, and then wept into his cup. 
That poem earned a green ribbon, and the next morning he returned with three other verses from neighboring tables. 
Soon the whole village was trading favorite poems at dinner, and Mara copied each one onto the slate before dusk. 
She did not force the words; she listened for the places where a poem made several people inhale together. 
When that happened, she drew a curve from the speaker's house to the river, then back to the houses that answered it. 
The slate filled with a map that looked less like damage than like lungs. 
By the last dusk of the butterfly migration, the woven ribbon roll had become a braided plan no single grief could own. 
She cut the memorial stone flat outside the ancient greenhouse, carving channels and one wide unmarked hollow as the braid demanded. 
At the unveiling, families laid ribbons and folded poems into the channels until the stone held a winged braid. 
Butterflies crossed the glass above them with thundering calm, and nobody needed to be told where to stand. 
Mara understood that collective grief was shaped like migration, a pattern of departures returning as shared attention. 
She had carved no comfort, only a route for each family to retrace when words failed.
