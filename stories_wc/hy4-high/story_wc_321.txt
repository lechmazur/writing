Every night at the wrong end of the commute, when clocks run sideways, the Number Nine thinned beside the third buoy. 
Clocks in the harbor did not reverse; their minute hands crept across the dials like crabs, and every arrival became an almost. 
By morning, headlines called the boat the vanishing commuter ferry, though the clerks who rode her still kept season tickets in their breast pockets. 
Mara Vale was an ardent ferry deckhand, resiliently positive even when the harbor master ordered every rope coiled and every name entered in the ledger of likely losses. 
She had learned from years of dawn runs that ferry people were a permanent transient tribe, loyal to a place that existed only between shores. 
Her brother Emil had vanished on the first crossing, leaving behind a lunch pail, a wet glove, and a promise to meet her at Bell Pier. 
In the office safe she found his note and an empty hourglass, two quiet relics that refused to say which hour had taken him. 
The note, dated seven tomorrows later, said that the ferry had not sunk but split, carrying one set of passengers into each neighboring version of the same tired Tuesday. 
Mara wanted to merge parallel timelines, not to conquer time, but to make one pier where no commuter had to be a ghost in someone else's memory. 
That motive kept her aboard after the company docked the sister boats, and it made her polish the brass radio until her knuckles shone. 
She had tried to radio the harbor, but her calls returned as lullabies sung in her own voice from tomorrow. 
On the seventh night, she tied Emil's glove to the rail, set the empty hourglass in the chart room, and took the Number Nine out alone. 
At the third buoy, the deck lamps blurred, the cabin clock turned its face toward the wall, and the hull slid into a spectral grotto under starlight. 
The grotto had no darkness; its limestone ribs held photographs of dawns that had never happened, and stars trembled in tide pools like coins in a beggar's palm. 
Moored along those pools were seven versions of the same commuter ferry, each crowded with pale clerks reading newspapers dated one day apart. 
On the nearest bow, Emil stood older by a beard and younger by the anxious bend of his shoulders, holding a lunch pail he could not set down. 
He mouthed her name, but the ferries overlapped without touching, so his voice arrived as rain on the glass of every cabin at once. 
Mara felt the old wound open, then smiled, squared her cap, and told the frightened passengers that the gap was closing. 
The empty hourglass began to glow, not with sand but with the faint routes scratched by years of keels crossing the same black water. 
Those routes braided with the fare ledger, the seat numbers, the ribs of shells, the star reflections, and the static pulsing from the brass radio. 
Mara saw that every lost crossing had left the same braid, a map made not of places but of relationships, and she understood why Emil's note had been cheerful. 
He had trusted her to work via interconnected patterns, because a single rope, hour, or brother could never pull seven Tuesdays into one harbor. 
She lashed seven mooring lines to the rail, matching their knots to the seven shell spirals glowing in the nearest tide pool. 
Then she set the empty hourglass on its side across the chart, so no glass stood above any other and no sand could pretend to fall. 
To radio the fleet, she did not shout for rescue; she read the routes as a steady roll call, letting each name answer the same name on six other decks. 
The brass radio carried her cadence through the grotto, and the interconnected patterns tightened until echoes, reflections, ropes, and clocks shared one pulse. 
Each ferry tried to remain separate, because separation had given each Emil, each clerk, and each Tuesday the dignity of being the only true version. 
Mara understood the last knot: to merge parallel timelines, she had to stop choosing the brother on the nearest bow. 
She laid Emil's glove across the empty hourglass; seven hands reached, and the ferries folded together without a crash. 
At Bell Pier, dawn brought one ferry, all the commuters, and Emil asking whether she had saved his seat. 
Mara kept the empty hourglass by the radio; a saved world is a permanent transient crossing, and hope is a rope.
