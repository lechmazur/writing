On the echoing terrace in spring, the prism parapet after rain held small dawns in every wet facet. 
Below it, Aven waited in dusky harmony, lilacs dripping and bronze bells hooded until sunrise. 
At the bells, the Hall of Answers would open and its mirrors would fix each child to one future. 
Only over the hush before bells ring could a voice enter their silver without becoming law. 
The elusive mirage painter arrived then, never early enough to catch, never late enough to miss. 
She had come to persuade mirrors to show possibilities, not predictions. 
Sera Vale was perceptively calm, hearing a guard's shaken breath and a sick child's cough before either became trouble. 
All her life she had lived on a borrowed heartbeat, her twin Mara's pulse carried by a silver thread from a crib emptied by fever. 
Because the beat was not hers, Sera trusted the pause between beats, where another life might begin. 
The council's mirrors denied such pauses; they reflected only roads chosen by fathers, ledgers, and fear. 
Brushes vanished on their surfaces, pigments slid away, and adult arguments came back as tired faces. 
Children were different, since they believed a song could change a room. 
For twenty springs Sera worked through coded lullabies in children's songs, hiding alternate endings in notes nurses hummed. 
A skipped rest meant perhaps; an open rhyme meant the singer could choose. 
The prism parapet after rain was the instrument she lacked, splitting sound into colors when wet. 
Those colors could slip under the hall windows more gently than words. 
The lullabies also needed the borrowed heartbeat as a tempo, for Mara's pulse was no official rhythm. 
Sera carried the silver thread in a locket, promising each year to return the debt before the bells. 
This spring the tick stumbled on the stairs, as if Mara were tired of lending a life. 
At the arcade, Captain Orrel blocked the terrace and asked why the stones smelled of paint. 
His daughter Lio stood among the singers, and the ledger had assigned her to the quarry. 
Sera did not vanish; she showed him the empty paintbox and let him hear the lullaby. 
His hand stopped halfway to his sword, and she stayed perceptively calm, leaving him room to disobey. 
Over the hush before bells ring, shutters held their breath, and the children began the sleepy lamb song. 
Its skipped rests laid a stair of perhaps across the stones. 
Lio's clear notes struck the prism parapet after rain, scattering amber, green, and an unnamed blue. 
The colors slid into the hall, where nineteen mirrors waited like blind judges. 
One shivered and showed a boy with a surgeon's lamp instead of chains. 
Another showed a girl leaving the quarry path for a pear orchard. 
Official futures loosened, and parents made no sound, afraid that breathing would choose again. 
Above them, bell ringers gripped their ropes, waiting to stamp the glass into certainty. 
The locket missed, caught, then missed; Sera knew a borrowed heartbeat would die at the first bell if it stayed tied to her. 
Among mirage painters, grace was the last action of a work: giving the illusion away. 
Sera had never managed it, always wanting one answer signed with her grief. 
Watching Lio sing, she understood that possession turned any future into a cage. 
She opened the locket, wound the silver thread around the wet rail, and let Mara's pulse pass into the crystal. 
Then Sera performed grace, releasing the thread, her sister, and the final open rhyme without claiming any of them. 
The parapet flung that freed heartbeat into nineteen arcs of light, and the mirrors answered with dusk instead of decree. 
In their depths, children saw branching paths: quarry and orchard, sickroom and ship, error and repair. 
The bells rang a moment later, but their bronze entered a hall already full of maybe and could not flatten it. 
Captain Orrel lowered his sword and whispered Lio's name without adding a trade. 
Sera faded as the first full note crossed the terrace, yet not into nothing. 
In the parapet, a pulse kept time with rain, no longer borrowed and no longer owned. 
The children added a new ending to the lullaby, thanking the absent painter by leaving room for the next singer. 
By the next spring, every nurse knew that tune, and no mirror could honestly claim to show only truth.
