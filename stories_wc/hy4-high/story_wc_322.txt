At the licensed hour, the one the permit named 'as memories alter', Mara Venn, the fever dream architect, returned to the optical illusion gallery. The visitors were already revising themselves with polite patience, forgetting childhood streets and accepting new companions beside them. The guards unlocked the front doors without expression, because surprise corrupted measurements. The staff called that hush unsettling calm, and kept their voices at ledger level. She had earned that title by drafting corridors for fever patients whose serpentine wallpaper still needed a trustworthy exit. The gallery taught rational enchantment: every wonder had axioms, loads, maintenance costs, and a posted margin of error. No one called it magic when the ledgers balanced. Mara had accepted the restoration to discover the texture of connection, though the contract mentioned only a cracked staircase. Her sister Ilen had entered the Perspective Room twelve years earlier and vanished from every family memory except Mara's. Photographs showed an aunt holding a coat no one remembered owning, and Mara had grown tired of smooth absences. In her coat pocket lay a spool of sinew thread, rendered from the cured tendons of the horses that had hauled the first mirrors. The catalogue called it realistically imaginary, strong enough to bear a calculated load but absent whenever anyone praised it. Under rational enchantment, that was ordinary; square roots also held beams in tables. The restoration had to be done by weaving patterns, because nails split illusions and knots left visitors with migraines. Mara passed the thread through floor tiles, mirror frames, and the tiny brass rings that marked recorded lines of sight. She began at the cracked staircase, where perspective made each step seem both near and impossibly far. With every crossing, a visitor remembered arriving with a different companion and calmly adjusted the space beside them. No one screamed; the exhibition had trained them to expect revisions, and the revisions arrived on schedule. Mara worked slowly, because the official 'as memories alter' hour was also the only interval when the gallery's joints could be seen. They looked less like hinges than pale cords tightening between one remembered doorway and the next. The sight made her hands steady, which was how she knew she was afraid. At the ninth crossing, the sinew thread caught on something that had no measured thickness. Her calculations said the obstruction lay beyond the west wall, in a chamber deleted after Ilen disappeared. The deleted chamber had proved, under rational enchantment, that connection required two observers and one agreed error. Mara had hated that proof as a student, because it made love sound like bookkeeping. Now, holding the thread against the catch, she understood why Ilen had entered and why no one could remember leaving her. The chamber had not hidden a person; it had held the only link between a person and the memories that answered her. If Mara restored the staircase, the link would close into an ordinary wall, and the official records would remain clean. If she tensioned one more loop, the sealed room would erupt and every present memory would make room. The choice felt calm; the hall stayed cool and a child yawned into a paper mask. Mara tensioned the loop. The white wall did not burst; it began to erupt in rectangular openings, each one edged with the exact shadow its geometry required. The eruption had the unsettling calm of a proof completed by someone who had never needed comfort. Inside the largest opening stood Ilen, realistically imaginary down to the black comma of a mole on her cheek. She had no history that could survive a ledger, but her left shoe bore Mara's chalk mark. Mara did not step toward her, because rational enchantment allowed no reunion without a second agreed error. Instead, she drew her palm along the sinew thread, feeling what she had come to discover. The texture of connection was not smooth, warm, or merciful; it was braided from corrections, ridged where memories altered to admit another life. It tugged like scar tissue and gave like a balancing ledger, with a dry pulse that hurt because it continued. In the final chime of the 'as memories alter' hour, openings folded into an unsurprising staircase. The guards recorded the new staircase, and the catalogue listed no missing room. Family photographs now showed two sisters beside that coat, but only Mara felt weight in the air between them. That weight did not console her; it simply held, with unsettling calm, while the gallery opened on time.
