After the pilgrim arrives, Ila Venn, the sharp-eyed drone pilot, keeps her three survey drones above the ancient observatory at dusk, where the light settles into brilliant dimness. 
She is perpetually surprised, not because she is naive, but because she refuses to let a repeated pattern become invisible. 
That habit made her famous in the survey corps, where other pilots trusted tidy maps and she trusted the omissions between readings. 
She has come to decode the frequency of belonging, a signal her displaced family claimed could be heard when a place answered a person truly. 
The pilgrim, a narrow old woman with rain stitched into her cloak, carries no staff, no beads, and no explanation. 
She lays on the cracked altar a type foundry matrix chip, a blackened copper square impressed with the hollow of an unknown letter. 
Ila recognizes the negative used to cast movable type, a tiny absence that once gave metal language its form. 
The official archivist calls it debris and wants Ila to seal the room before the wind rises. 
Ila notices, with one sharp look across her lenses, that the hollow in the chip mirrors a gap in the oldest star panel on the dome. 
The founders were printers and astronomers, stamping star trails from long exposures into letters so the sky could be read like a page. 
Over centuries, letters were pried from sockets, sold as relics, or swallowed by salt, leaving the archive full of missing pieces. 
Scholars replaced lost glyphs, but each restoration silenced another note in the low hum of the observatory. 
Ila had watched her mother sand blank spaces from family tablets so the village could pretend no one had vanished. 
Because of that, she had learned to distrust any answer that looked complete too quickly. 
The old method is by following star trails, and Ila uses it to guide the drones in widening spirals from the altar into the dome. 
Each camera finds a streak that a missing letter should have carried. 
The first trail is a silver scrape; the second is another; the third stops where mortar was repaired with red clay. 
Ila feels the old surprise again, because an interruption in a pattern is a door rather than a flaw. 
She sends the smallest drone under the dome lip and finds a cavity packed with wax, sand, and a second broken chip. 
The pilgrim watches without triumph, her eyes wide at every rotor flash, as if she too is perpetually surprised by what she has carried. 
Ila asks who gave it to her, and the pilgrim answers that a town beside the salt flats has been singing the same empty letter for three generations. 
They sent her because old maps said the observatory could turn shared absence into a tone that tells scattered people where to stand together. 
The archivist calls the tone a superstition, but his voice softens when the drones project the three trails across the dome and the repaired clay begins to glow. 
In the brilliant dimness, the glow is not bright enough to banish shadow, only bright enough to give every shadow an edge. 
Ila slots the type foundry matrix chip into the socket of the empty letter, then sets the broken chip beside it, leaving the fracture visible. 
The device does not click; instead, a low tone gathers in the stone, hesitant at first, then steady as a pulse. 
Her console translates the pulse into wavelengths, and she understands that no single note contains the message. 
The spaces between notes, the gaps where letters are gone, and the delays caused by the broken chip carry the true pattern. 
It is a frequency made of what a community refuses to erase, an answer to exile that never asks the exiled to become whole. 
At the center of the altar, a brass ring rises from a slot earlier restorers had filled with wax. 
Beside it, the only surviving instruction says pull, and Ila knows belonging cannot be sounded by observation alone. 
The archivist reaches first, but Ila stops him, because the town beside the salt flats deserves an answer from someone who has spent years looking for gaps. 
Together, Ila and the pilgrim take the ring, and one careful pull opens concealed vents in the dome to the dusk wind. 
The tone travels outward through channels cut by following star trails, and Ila, perpetually surprised, finally hears belonging not as completion but as a faithful chorus of missing pieces.
