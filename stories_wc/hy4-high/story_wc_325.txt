Mara Venn was a claustrophobic spelunker, and every cave she entered had to be earned twice.  Panic registered pressure, distance, and the minute intentions of air; the old work log read: Tracks invisible things.  That skill brought a brass tube to her boarding house, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling of salt, and carrying an archivist's request.  Inside lay the codex margin illuminant gold fleck, a flake of gold leaf from a monk's painted vine.  A note said Tideglass had one interval left, between tidal changes, before the landing vault drowned again.  Mara knew Tideglass from stories told by her aunt Ione before illness took even Ione's name.  Ione had tested undersea cables at the forgotten research facility, and after its closure no payroll or grave remembered her.  Mara came not for salvage, but to stand by the unremembered.  At low slack she followed a fissure under the cliff while the Atlantic made a crashing hush behind basalt.  The sea struck hard, but the cave swallowed every blow, leaving a hush louder than noise.  The passage pinched to a shoulder's width, and her lungs insisted that stone had started closing.  She named the dimensions aloud and moved only after the walls agreed.  Beyond the squeeze, a rusted door opened into the forgotten research facility, still wearing charts of currents and splices.  Tideglass stood where seven undersea cables came ashore, each armored in black sheath and threaded through rock.  Its official work was signal drift; its secret mercy was memory.  Ione's team mapped faint splice signatures left by every hand that made or mended the lines.  They designed a Return sequence to give erased workers back to every receiving log as names, not ghosts.  A director sealed the project, and the staff vanished from records with a tidiness that enraged Mara.  The Return could work only through phase transitions, crystal to glass in memory cells and warm brine to vapor in cooling lungs.  Tideglass used the tide as a pump, so the cycle had to finish between tidal changes.  Miss that window and the chamber would flood before the cables accepted the return.  Gold did not corrode after decades of salt, and in the old apparatus it gave each phase change a clean edge.  Mara found the control room under a ceiling low enough to make her swallow a scream.  She set the codex margin illuminant gold fleck in a cracked clamp above the transmitter.  The console woke reluctantly, showing columns blanked by damp or command.  Ione Venn appeared as a row of dashes.  A red light showed the vapor valve stuck; the second phase would never arrive.  Mara closed her eyes and tracked the invisible chill the gauges could not show.  It crept from seawater under the west wall, not from any cable conduit.  She followed that thread to a service crawl so tight her fear rose like floodwater.  Standing there would have been easier, but the unremembered had never been granted standing room.  She lay flat, slid one shoulder at a time, and reached the valve.  The wheel gave with a cough that filled the crawl with warm steam.  Mara almost sobbed at the heat, because brine, alloy, and glass were changing state, and so was she.  She crawled back with panic folded small, like a map she refused to obey.  In the control room, columns flickered as the transmitter drew power from the rising tide.  Blanked rows filled with names, dates, trades, and corrections officials had never bothered to erase well.  Ione Venn, cable tester, rose first, followed by a cook, three divers, six clerks, and a splicing boy.  Mara whispered each name, staying beside them while the station made room.  The console offered one living command: Return.  She pressed it.  Through phase transitions, crystal blurred to glass, vapor collapsed to brine, and the names became pulses.  The undersea cables took them past the shelf, where ordinary messages had crossed for a century.  On distant screens, receiving logs repaired themselves with names no clerk remembered missing.  The crashing hush deepened as the tide turned and water climbed the entrance stair.  She had returned what was made invisible, and the invisible things now kept her company.  Before leaving, she set the gold fleck in a niche scratched with Ione's initials.  She climbed into open air while cable pulses went on under the sea and the surf kept its enormous quiet.
