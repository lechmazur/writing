Across the death of a language, people among towers of bone in a canyon of endless echoes learned to lower their voices.  The language called Avra lived less in mouths than in fingertips, its grammar carved as ridges on clay, cloth, and bone.  For water, a parent pressed two short grooves and one long groove into a palm, and the answer felt cool before it was heard.  Then quarries closed, weavers died, and trade words filled the markets, so Avra retreated to scars and the hands of the old.  Mara was the copper-veined storyteller, trained to feel a sentence through her skin instead of hear it.  Thin copper lines showed along her wrists, placed by an initiation that made her veins conduct the tremble of carved stone.  Because even a cough returned enlarged, she became quietly articulate, giving each low word an edge sharp enough to survive repetition.  Her ambition was not to embalm Avra as marks, but to translate texture into sound before the last hands forgot their pressure.  The elders called it vanity, because sound there never stayed single.  Under the lowest tower lay a tunnel of echoes, where voices entered carved memories and returned wearing the shape of every wall.  Mara believed those returns carried Avra's lost consonants, but no ear could separate one ridge from a thousand reflections.  She needed to harness that confusion for one clean moment.  For that she kept a flawed diamond rumored to be cursed, bought from grave robbers who said it repeated a dead man's name until his widow stopped sleeping.  Mara saw no curse, only a black feather inside the crystal, a flaw splitting light into two unequal paths.  The elders forbade her to carry it below the towers, and their warning followed for years.  During those years, Avra lost its numerals, then jokes, then the tense for speaking to someone who would not return.  Mara sat with each dying speaker, copying finger pressures onto hide, while copper in her arms hummed with what she could not yet pronounce.  The answer came from hunger, not scholarship, because canyon swifts rose at dusk to feed on insects stirred by pressure waves from the tunnel.  Their bodies flinched where reflected sound crossed itself, so the flock drew moving diagrams of delays no instrument could measure.  Mara learned to tune the diamond through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight, opening its crack whenever a V of wings folded into a noose.  Her quietly articulate explanations moved the elders by showing that the supposed curse was a pair of doors.  One door returned the past as noise; the other returned the pressure that had made it.  Where both doors met, she could harness texture and tone at once.  By the winter old Tovan died, no living person could ask for water in Avra, and the council gave Mara the key.  She carried the hides, the diamond, and a bowl of water into the tunnel that smelled of old rain.  At the mouth, her apprentice waited by a screened roost, and Mara told him barely above breathing to lift it when the first echo reached the third rib.  Then she pressed Tovan's last hide against the diamond and wet the two short grooves and one long groove he had made for water.  The canyon answered with a century of shouts, bells, coughs, prayers, and lullabies returning at once.  Mara's copper lines burned, but she kept her fingers steady, because the hides held the last pressure of the dead.  Above the tunnel mouth, the screen rose, and the swifts burst into violet air.  Their flock loosened, hooked, divided, and folded as hidden reflections crossed.  Mara read that folding through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight, turned the diamond a hair, and felt the flaw catch a clean pulse.  The pulse became a small, cool syllable, the word for water in a language no mouth would use again.  Mara laughed, set the diamond beside the hide, and let the tower carry that syllable to the children above.
