Only after the tide pools empty could anyone enter the centuries-old labyrinth carved into red canyon walls below Sable Reach. For one turn of the tide the sea withdrew through slotted floors, leaving salt, bronze eyelets, and a hush the old poets called orchid solace. Before the collapse, the wharf families had walked that maze each dry tide, repeating precedents as exact as warp threads. A child carried the salt, a widow carried the cord, a harbor master named each turn, and no one stepped where the storybook said no. Then the winter quake split the quay, drowned the chapel of names, and broke the printed plate from which every festival sheet was pulled. Afterward the survivors locked the gates and called their grief order, because order was cheaper than another funeral. Ila Venn, a poetic wharf weaver who wrote couplets into net meshes, had once loved those tidy rules. She had believed repetition could hold back water, until water took her brother Tovin and most of the singing choir. In the months that followed, Ila became hauntingly optimistic, not because she denied the dead but because she heard them asking for a different kind of rope. The council wanted to seal the maze with rubble and hold one plain prayer on the open quay. Ila wanted to carve a path from ruin to reverence, so that the living could arrive at the drowned names without pretending the wharf was whole. Her mother had left her a storybook illustration plate, a palm of engraved copper from The Child Who Led the Whales. The picture showed a small girl leading whales through a maze with a thread, while tiny sleepers circled every corner. Ila's mother used the plate to print festival broadsheets, and its dotted border had been copied from the real labyrinth. The quake had cracked the copper across the child's thread, so each new impression gave the maze a different ending. To the council that made the plate useless; to Ila it made broken precedents visible, honest, and finally open to repair. Since the gates had closed, half the town had begun to sleepwalk, rising when the pools drained and padding into the upper galleries with eyes full of moonlight. The watchers were ashamed and kept no record, but Ila followed with a sailcloth notebook, marking bare footprints in red dust. Night after night she stitched the tracings onto old panels, and the panels began to answer her. Where one sleeper stopped before a crack, another turned toward a cold draft, and a third drifted safely through an unlit arch. Laid over one another, those night journeys formed a braided route via overlapping sleepwalking paths, not the straight rule printed before the quake. The plate's chipped corner even matched the first safe arch, proving the old story had hidden a method under its picture. When the council voted for rubble at low tide, Ila chose the maze instead of the meeting. She carried needles, whalebone bobbins, strips of retired sail, the cracked copper plate, and Tovin's last coil of blue harbor line. At the seaward mouth, where broken timbers leaned like exhausted ribs, she told the waiting watchers that she had come to sew rather than to restore. Mara, the harbor master's daughter, asked whether thread could carry a town's dead, and Ila said that thread had carried nets, sails, and her own silence. Then the sleepwalkers arrived, thin and calm in the orchid light, and their feet found the line she had sewn into memory. Ila followed the composite line into the maze, stopping wherever all the chalked paths lay thickest. With the plate's sharp edge she scored the damp sandstone, a shallow cut that would guide the first festival hand. Behind the cut she began to sew a braid of sailcloth through the bronze eyelets, taking three stitches at each dangerous turning. At the collapsed stair, where the old plate insisted on a rightward step, the sleepers' overlap bent left toward a seam of dry stone. Ila hesitated, hearing Tovin in the drip of water, then chose the left seam and drove her needle through cloth stiff with salt. The wall held. One by one, the watchers entered with lanterns, adding their hands to the stitches because a path made from grief required more than one maker. By the time the tide pools began to refill, the braid reached the drowned chapel, where names remained on the walls above the waterline. Ila pressed the cracked plate to the altar, and the broken child in its engraving seemed to be holding not a thread but an opening. No one repeated the old rubric; they spoke each name once, then followed the sewn braid back toward the ruin as dawn softened the canyon into orchid solace.
