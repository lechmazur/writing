During a once-in-a-lifetime celestial alignment, Mara kneels in the rusted greenhouse with a knowledge fruit seed in her palm.  Above her, sun, moon, and a broken orbital mirror make one white ladder through the cracked panes.  Outside, the district is ash, wire, and apartment shells where the Civic Kitchen taught people to forget.  Mara is a chef who can taste memories, and the ministers once praised that gift.  Salt gives her her father's hand on a ladle; pear skin gives her sirens, dust, and neighbors on a staircase.  For years she cooked Approved Comfort, bland stews that made obedience feel like homesickness.  A population busy tasting safe Yesterdays rarely asked who had emptied the granaries.  Then closure orders marked old greenhouses for scrap, because unsupervised growing led to remembering.  Mara kept one forbidden thing from the last orchard, the seed now warm as a sleeping animal.  Old agronomists designed knowledge fruit to teach through synaptic plasticity, letting flavor lay down living paths.  Repetition strengthened those paths, neglect erased them, and no censor could burn a skill once tasted.  The Civic Kitchen had burned the orchards anyway.  Mara, who helped make forgetting delicious, wants to create something beautiful from the ruins.  Not a restored palace or hot revenge, but a supper returning useful dead knowledge to the living.  Sami helps her, informally wise in the way of children who repair pumps and read weather without instruments.  He knows which beams hold, which leaks are sweet, and which rusted bolts must never be struck.  Before moonrise he patches panes with scavenged acetate and sets chipped bowls beneath condensation lines.  He lays warped trays across the entrance, not as a barricade but as a place where arrivals must slow down.  Mara understands the wisdom of it, because the quiet arrangement is already her gentle rebellion.  At alignment, the seed wakes.  Its shell splits, and a neural-root lattice spreads through compost, tasting rust, rain, ash, and onion broth.  Each time Mara recalls a kitchen before the closures, the lattice answers with a green pulse.  She feeds it memories by taste, never by command, because forced recollections make fruit dream of cages.  Through synaptic plasticity, repeated pairings strengthen useful circuits while neglected circuits hang like dead tendrils.  That makes her gift a gardener rather than a confectioner; she must prune.  The vine grows too honestly, offering branches for fear, ration lines, interrogations, and her own shame.  If she leaves everything, the first fruit will teach children how to survive a world that should not be survived.  So she takes Sami's sharpened shears and begins to prune the darkest shoots at their joints.  Each cut hurts her mouth with the memory it removes.  She keeps branches carrying hand skills, lullabies, seed charts, and the names of wells.  Sami opens vents a finger at a time, keeping aligned light from cooking the tender grafts.  When a Compliance patrol appears with crowbars, he does not hide the bowls or raise a tool.  He sets more spoons on the tray, as if hunger were a custom no uniform dared refuse.  Mara recognizes the captain from kitchens where she plated forgetting for his officers.  Her hands shake, but she ladles broth as the first ripe fruit falls open on its own.  Amber flesh smells of rain on hot iron, and Mara cuts it into slices too thin to intoxicate.  The captain eats because refusing a shared table would make him smaller before the gathering neighbors.  In his mouth, a buried lesson unfolds: how his grandmother made cistern valves from willow and brass.  He lowers the crowbar, not forgiven, merely interrupted by knowledge gentler than an order.  One by one, neighbors taste slices and remember how to mend pumps, salt seed, set bones, and sing babies to sleep.  Patrol members carry bowls to the elderly, then sit among them with their helmets on the damp floor.  Mara tastes the finished dish and finds grief there, but also yeast, pepper, rain, and a door opening.  Sami smiles at the rusted frames, which have held the aligned light just long enough.  By dawn, no anthem has been sung and no wall has been pulled down.  Still, the greenhouse smells of supper, and everyone leaves carrying a seed of instruction wrapped in a leaf.  The ministers can demolish the glass, but they cannot easily demolish people who have remembered how to build.  Mara knows the ruins may stay, yet beauty has taken root in them, quiet as yeast and hard to ration.
