The funeral home prep room smelled of lilies, phenol, and cold breath, and every drain hummed as if the dead still bargained. 
No calendar had jurisdiction there; no clock certified sorrow; the green tiles made blood look honest. 
I was the con artist's honest child, employed to wash instruments and audit ledgers because I could not bear an unbalanced column. 
My father sold names like grain, and each forgery left a family holding a receipt for a vanished person. 
I wanted nothing from his trade except to find the mathematics of a fair trade, the equation that could return a stolen life without interest or disguise. 
On the shelf above the artery pump sat his vintage ink pot, black glass crusted with purple sediment. 
Under its base, a line read: liquefies rigid thinking, the one true sentence he wrote about his tools. 
Warmth from a living palm thinned the old ink, and its vapor softened the hard wax that made a state category into a wall. 
The asylum officer brought Mira Vesk to our steel tray for a doubtful death review, with a sheet under her chin and two foreign guards beyond the frosted door. 
She was not a corpse, though the certificate in its wax sealed pouch said fever had killed her in prison. 
Her cheek held the gray of cold storage, but a quick pulse beat under my blood pressure cuff, and the room's deathly vitality gathered around that small, stubborn drum. 
The officer spoke without looking at her, because a person certified dead cannot file for political asylum or embarrass a government that has closed its books. 
I recognized father's numerals on the pouch label, the price of that lie, and the sum paid to subtract a living witness from the world. 
A fair trade, I thought, would add no forgery; it would return the missing term and let the columns accuse themselves. 
We needed a breach, not a smashed lock or heroic lie, but a clean opening in the wax that declared her dead. 
The method was by waiting actively, a trick father used while cheating cards and I used for keeping circulation in bodies that still belonged to someone. 
I set the vintage ink pot beside the tray, warmed its collar, tilted the table a finger width, and sent solvent vapor through the refrigeration valve toward the seal. 
Waiting actively meant adjusting the lamp when Mira's lips lost color, pressing her hand under the sheet, and listening for the pin sized tick of surrendering wax. 
The guards argued in the corridor, the officer recited regulations, and the dead in their drawers seemed to lean toward a sum that might balance. 
I did not hurry, because hurry was counterfeit; I kept the equation on a burial tag where every gain showed its loss. 
The breach arrived as a hairline under the eagle, a dark seam of ink creeping through softened wax with the patience of proof. 
I lifted the pouch flap, and father's original entry surfaced in purple ghosts: detained, scheduled for trial, witness to the harbor massacres, alive. 
The asylum officer stopped reciting; vapor and revealed arithmetic loosened the certainty in his face, not into sentiment but into calculation. 
He asked Mira to say her name, and the word came thin but warm, a living balance to the cold figures in the ledger. 
With a clean nib dipped in the vintage ink pot, I wrote across the forged certificate: one name stolen, one name restored, no profit retained. 
The officer stamped her petition for political asylum, because the document now showed the debt that made refusal dishonest. 
The guards held a burial receipt for no one, and their authority drained into the floor drain with the melted ice water. 
Mira sat up, shivering under the green tiles, while pumps, drains, and flushed faces made a scandal of being alive. 
I returned father's fee from my own pocket to the ledger box, so no hidden percentage would pass to another child. 
The equation closed, and the prep room kept breathing.
