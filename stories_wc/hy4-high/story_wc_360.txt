Between vanishing stars, the topaz workshop under stars keeps its lamps low and its presses warm.  Orin Vale, the cryptic letterpress operator, works there while the city forgets which feelings belong to whom.  Each extinguished star leaves a bright rim on the glass roof, then a silence below, as if one note had left a scale.  People still laugh at closed coffins, weep over weddings, and come to Orin for labels.  To understand the mathematics of emotion, he builds a press that measures pressure, pulse, pause, and ink heat.  His charts call grief an inverse curve, envy a braid, courage an integer, and love two functions that refuse to meet.  He is cryptic because he answers complaints with proofs and condolences with questions, not from cruelty but because certainty hardens samples.  He habitually borrows personalities, taking a baker's blunt hands, a widow's pauses, or a pilot's laughter, and letting each manner set the type.  Through deliberate ambiguity, he can disorient a reader enough to make a feeling leave its owner and seek another coordinate.  A proof sheet says, "The hand that opens the door has gone," and no client can decide whether to grieve, forgive, or knock.  The source of the displacement is the Halo fragment, a thumb length of gold glass beneath the platen, splitting starlight into measurable arcs.  It fell from the first star that disappeared, and, cold as it looks, it warms when an unclaimed feeling enters the room.  Orin calls the result transposed empathy, not kindness in another's place but the same emotion arriving with the wrong address.  By charting each wrong address, he hopes to find the equation that tells a heart where to stand.  The method works, badly.  Feelings arrive in clear waves, then leave as lucid turbulence, precise enough to count and violent enough to change the counter.  After ten years of borrowed gestures, Orin cannot tell whether his chest pain belongs to him, to a baker, or to a woman who lost her son.  On the night the last bright star begins to vanish, Sela brings him a torn margin of an astronomer's notebook.  She is his daughter, though years of borrowed personalities leave him recognizing her only by anger borrowed from her mother.  Sela says the notebook proves stars are not dying; they are copied into people, one light at a time, overloading the city.  If Orin keeps printing, every citizen will hold a feeling too large to name; if he stops, his unfinished equation dies with the final star.  He asks for the notebook; she asks for one honest sentence.  This frightens him more than the darkening roof, because honesty would fix a value before the experiment is complete.  He reaches for Sela's brisk stance, one heel braced against argument, and finds the borrowed posture refuses to hold.  For the first time, his habit gives no disguise, only the distance his ambition has made.  Above them, the vanishing star sheds a thin ring, and the Halo fragment answers like a struck bell.  Orin understands that the fragment is not a measuring tool but the missing half of one halo, and the city has been completing a circle through human nerves.  The equation is therefore not a line but a closure, and the unknown term is the self that borrows because it fears emptiness.  He could state that plainly, but plainness would leave Sela outside the proof, so he chooses the only generosity his craft has taught him.  Through deliberate ambiguity, he sets a final line: This light is yours when you give it back.  The words disorient the room exactly as intended: Sela reads ownership, the press reads return, and the borrowed personalities in Orin read release.  He pulls the lever.  Ink bites paper, the Halo fragment flashes, and transposed empathy runs backward, giving each feeling its own wound, witness, and limit.  Across the city, laughter returns to kitchens, tears find funerals, and the enormous unnamed light climbs toward the vanishing star.  Orin does not finish his equation; he adds one variable, Sela's hand over his, and lets the answer remain bright, unsteady, and shared.
