Wind combs the slots of the serpentine nexus under starlight while the universe hums its secret song, making a reed-blown melancholy around my shoulders. 
I descend slowly, because one wrong note in these coils can seal a passage for a year. 
Against my ribs I carry the whispering seashell half my mother left me, warm as a held breath despite the cold stone. 
I have come to learn why some people echo and to ensure that no clerk erases the stolen names before morning. 
As the canyon echo singer of Red Orra, I am paid to stand above funerals and call each true name into the gorge. 
When a name is welcomed by memory, the cliff gives it back as a second voice, softer than breath and full of the habits of the dead. 
When no answer comes, the Council calls the deceased hollow, sells the house, and sends the surviving kin to the salt works. 
My mother did not answer me, and that silence made an orphan of me twice. 
The elders call me richly perceptive because I hear the small betrayals beneath official grief: the relieved exhale of a widow, the polished shoe of a nephew, a clerk pausing before a name. 
That gift has made me patient, but it has also made me lonely, since every pause in a song can accuse someone I love. 
At the last funeral, I noticed blue wax on the cuff of Registrar Soven, the same wax used to seal the silence lists. 
He had sworn that hollow cases came only from private shame, yet his boots carried the gray dust of these lower tunnels. 
I followed that dust through petitions, marriages, debts, and erased births until I found webs of intrigue stretched between the Council and the salt works. 
The webs were not made of silk but of favors: a gravedigger paid to forget, an aunt bribed to mispronounce, a singer taught to leave one line unsung. 
People disappeared from the public mouth of the canyon, so their true names had nowhere to travel and no echo could be born. 
The shell understood this before I did, because it had been broken in two on the night my mother was taken. 
One half went with her to the salt works; the other, the whispering seashell half, came back to me in the coat of a sailor, still repeating one damp syllable. 
For years it said only an, and I thought it was the beginning of her name, but tonight it adds ledger, then key, then Soven. 
It yields truth through progressive disclosure, as if a frightened memory must be given one safe doorway at a time. 
At the third turning, Soven steps from a blind arch with the matching shell half, taken from her, hanging at his throat. 
He looks less like a villain than a tired man who has starched his conscience until it can stand upright. 
He asks for the shell, and when I refuse, he says the silent are happier when their families stop waiting for voices. 
I do not answer with anger, because anger would close him like a fist; instead I question him through progressive disclosure, beginning with the blue wax, then the dust, then the missing funeral line. 
Each small fact gives permission for the next, and with the last one he touches the shell at his throat as if it has bitten him. 
He confesses that the Council sold names to the salt works, bought new ones from distant ledgers, and paid singers to shorten the laments. 
My mother, Anet, had refused to sign a water grant that would have drowned three poor terraces; her silence was a forged verdict, not an emptiness. 
When he says Anet, the two shell halves answer each other across the tunnel, and I hear my mother inhale before a lullaby. 
The sound is so modest that grief almost misses it, yet it topples the lie on which every silence list was built. 
I take the key from Soven, open the archive niche behind him, and find rolls of names wrapped in oilcloth, each roll tied with blue thread. 
To ensure that the Council cannot edit them again, I climb and release them through progressive disclosure: first Anet, then the terraces, then all the bought and buried. 
At the mouth of the gorge, dawn is far off, and a few sleepers gather with shawls against the reed wind. 
I sing the restored names in the order of their wounds, letting every pause carry what the shell taught me about patience. 
One by one the cliffs give back second voices, not grand or consoling, but exact: a cough, a laugh, a spoon set down, a mother saying my childhood name. 
By sunrise, no official can call anyone hollow.
