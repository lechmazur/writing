In the third week after the GPS fails, Vane measures distance by sound instead of satellites.  Beneath the storm-tossed archway, the tidal turbines keep a turbine-hum reverie that enters sleep, bread, and prayer.  Mara, a spirited driftwood sculptor, works on a shelf above the foam and likes the noise.  She is perpetually learning, asking children why knots slip and engineers why clocks drift.  All her work has one motive, to scaffold complexity with simplicity.  Her ideal is not the sea but a spare frame that anyone can hold in cold hands.  Because the satellites are mute, the turbines lose their timing pulses and begin shoving the narrow water out of phase.  Ferry skiffs miss their moorings, eel traps wander, and the basalt arch takes a new crack each night.  The council wants to shut the turbines and abandon the outer island before the equinox surge.  Mara argues that a shutdown will leave the channel unmeasured, because the old tide marks have been paved over.  Old Ren, the lampkeeper, brings her a cracked tin box salvaged from the drowned schoolhouse.  Inside lies a magic lantern slide painted with kelp, three boats, a black door, and a child carrying a red cord.  Ren says the islanders once projected it during bargains, so no promise will depend on memory alone.  Mara nearly laughs, for a glass picture seems weaker than the cracked arch overhead.  Then the lamp warms the slide, and hidden varnish rises like ink under skin.  Kelp becomes the old eel runs, the black door becomes the sluice, and the red cord becomes the timing signal.  Her method works through layered meanings, because each figure holds a bearing, a duty, and a season at once.  She writes no equations; she pins wet tracing paper to the wall and copies what the heat reveals.  The slide is not a chart but a record of timewoven alliances among the drowned, the living, and children not yet born.  Its first layer names who built each sluice, the second names who must tend it, and the third names who will inherit the flood.  Mara understands why Ren trusts a picture rather than a proclamation.  A proclamation ages, but an image can be read again whenever pride, weather, or grief changes.  Because she is perpetually learning, she also lets a ten-year-old apprentice correct her reading of the three boats.  The child says they are not villages but moments, arriving from grandfather, mother, and future crew.  That correction gives Mara the courage to reimagine the lamp itself as a working clock.  She carves a driftwood cradle with five notches, one cord, and three shutters that can be swung by hand.  No one needs a satellite, a manual, or even a number; a notch means pull, a cord means wait, a shutter means breathe.  The simplicity is severe, but it carries phase angles, family obligations, and the channel's oldest delays without naming them.  At dusk, spray climbs the archway and the council gathers under the turbine-hum reverie to watch her trial.  Mara sets the slide behind the cradle, so light passes through painted glass, cut wood, salt fog, and every waiting face.  The wall shows a gray eel path, then a red cord of obligation, then a white ladder of tidal hours.  Mara reads it through layered meanings again and moves the first shutter when the hum drops half a breath.  Ren pulls the cord, the apprentice opens the black sluice, and three crews answer from separate shores.  The turbines stop fighting one another and begin turning in a single, patient pulse.  For a minute the crack in the basalt seems to listen rather than widen.  The council does not cheer, because the pulse still needs every hand and the storm has not yet spent itself.  Through the night they repeat the three gestures, simple as a child's game and exact as a vow.  By morning the ferries find their moorings, the outer island stands, and the archway's crack holds a dry edge.  Mara leaves the driftwood cradle hanging where any passerby can touch it, then adds one more notch.  Under the storm-tossed archway, Mara, perpetually learning, lets the timewoven alliances keep time with the turbine-hum reverie in one simple frame.
