The mysterious lighthouse on a rocky island had kept no beam for thirty years, only a patient ticking beneath its salt-eaten stairs.  Fishermen crossed themselves offshore, because the dark lantern foretold which boats would founder and which winter would starve them.  Elias Rook, a counter clockwise clock maker, had been hired to repair the mechanism after the last keeper walked into the surf.  His neatly inventive fingers could make a broken watch run backward until spilled ink seemed to climb into its bottle.  He had learned the trick after his sister drowned, when he turned every household clock against the hour and begged the minute to return her.  It returned only silence, and from that silence he decided fate was a machine, elegant, merciless, and, therefore, vulnerable.  He had come to this island to step beyond fate's design, not merely to save a light that had failed his sister.  In the lantern room, brass dials marked the deaths of islanders still arguing in the kitchens below.  A fresh dial trembled toward his name.  The disadvantage of a prophecy is that it must use gears, and gears can be listened to.  Elias listened for three nights, while gulls struck the glass and the stairs breathed of kelp and cold iron.  He found that the lighthouse did not lack fire; it had swallowed the latent radiance of a stone on the reef.  The islanders called it the hunger stone, because it surfaced in lean years with carved warnings and dates of empty nets.  Bread lines, failed rains, and tideless nets had been cut across it.  But the oldest groove was a spiral ending in a socket shaped like a two-sided toy.  In a drawer sealed with tar, Elias discovered that toy: an ancient yoyo of black bronze, wound with a cord no knife could cut.  When he dropped it, the yoyo fell, clicked, and climbed back to his palm with the smug obedience of destiny.  Inside, a pin maze counted each descent and answered with the same returning number.  He understood then that the lighthouse had been built as a larger yoyo, pulling every future back to the hand that cast it.  Only through reverse-engineering the puzzle of an ancient yoyo could he alter the count without smashing the world's one clock.  For two days he sketched tooth intervals, remapped the maze, and filed a tiny escapement that made the cord wind outward instead of home.  He worked with his usual neatly inventive precision, labeling each screw with the hour it would betray.  The modification fit his palm, which mattered, because the clock overhead began striking unborn funerals.  On the third evening, under the slow exhale of low tide, the reef emerged black and glistening like an exposed animal.  Elias carried the altered yoyo down the cliff path, past limpets, split rope, and the sea's retiring whisper.  Village windows showed pale faces, waiting to learn whether the dials had lied.  The hunger stone was warmer than the air, and its carved warnings held a faint glow beneath the salt.  That glow was stolen light: wrecks, famines, dawns, and possibilities the lamp had refused to illuminate.  Elias fitted the yoyo into the spiral socket, wound the uncut cord around its oldest groove, and hesitated.  To initiate the outward turn was to put himself outside every dial, with no chart promising where his next breath would fall.  The sea hung below, and the lighthouse waited like a judge with the sentence written.  He felt bleak exhilaration, cold as the wind and bright as the first crack in a locked door.  Then he pulled, not upward, but against the expected return, and let the altered toy fall away from his hand.  The cord spun outward; the yoyo crossed the spiral groove, striking pins in a sequence no prophecy had counted.  Deep in the lantern room, the stolen mechanism shuddered, reversed, and loosened its grip on the latent radiance below.  Light rose from the hunger stone, not as a beam, but as unassigned mornings scattering over wet rock.  The lighthouse lens filled, cracked, and threw a white circle outward, showing a channel no map had ever admitted.  Village clocks coughed, stopped, and restarted at different hours, each household late for a different fate.  Elias saw his sister's name vanish from the brass dial and understood that freedom did not give her back.  He laughed once, because no chart now knew where to carry him.
