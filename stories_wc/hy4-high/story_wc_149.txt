All that winter, over the hush of winter-cracked fields, no cart had reached the alchemical orchard greenhouse shrouded in endless night until Mara Sen came with a carved stone bead to repay a debt across lifetimes.  Ninety winters earlier, her great-grandfather had stolen that bead from a bridal coffer, and the orchard had swallowed every sunrise since.  The bead pulsed when she crossed the threshold, not with sound but with a tingling in her teeth, and she understood why the family called it the key to resonant awakenings.  Those awakenings were not resurrections; they were chords struck through grafted roots, making each tree remember the hand that first planted it.  From the farthest aisle came a rustle of ash-colored silk, and the ashen-veil ghost bride stepped between two vats of sleeping mercury.  Mara knew her from the lawsuit miniature: Ilyra Vale, poisoned on her wedding morning, buried with the stolen bead missing from her dowry.  Yet the miniature had not shown the gray flowers threaded through her veil, or the way her smile was lovingly venomous, as if she could kiss a fever quiet and leave the sufferer grateful for the chill.  She communicated through ephemeral cameo appearances, showing herself for one heartbeat in a droplet, a pane, the black curve of a ladle, then vanishing before Mara could ask whether revenge or pardon had brought her here.  Each flash was a lesson: unlatch the western sash, touch the seventh graft, do not taste the silver fruit, and above all, ghost when the mercury begins to sing.  In the old almanacs, to ghost meant to loosen one's claim on solid things until a barred place admitted you as regret admits itself.  Mara had always been good at accounts, not at surrender, but the bead warmed against her palm like a patient creditor.  She set it in the first notch of the graft, and the greenhouse exhaled a chord so low that frost flowers opened on the inside of the glass.  In that bloom appeared Ilyra's first cameo: a girl laughing in a white dress while Mara's ancestor slipped the bead into his sleeve.  The second notch showed why the theft mattered, for the bead had held the greenhouse's dawn in trust, anchoring the grafts to a season beyond the endless night.  Without it, roots had fed on remembered sunshine and grown lovingly venomous fruit, sweet enough to preserve the dead and poisonous enough to keep them from leaving.  Mara tasted betrayal in the back of her throat and understood that restitution would cost more than an apology, because every inherited comfort had been watered by Ilyra's stolen morning.  At the third notch, the mercury vats trembled and sang with the voices of wedding guests long turned to field dust.  Ilyra's cameo flashed on each trembling surface, her ashen veil lifting, her finger pointing to Mara's chest rather than to another root.  To ghost, then, was not to pass through glass but to let the stolen claim pass through her, naming every life that had prospered because one bride was left unfinished.  Mara spoke their names, not from a ledger but from lullabies, business toasts, and the portrait room where no one had ever mentioned the empty coffer.  With each name, she felt a little of her body become less convincing, while the bead grew heavier, as though stone accepted the weight she surrendered.  At the seventh notch, she pressed the bead into the bark and let herself ghost through the last barred hour of Ilyra's wedding.  There, in a room bright with impossible morning, Mara stood beside the poisoned girl and placed the carved stone bead back among the dowry coins.  The act did not undo the murder, but it returned a beginning, and the greenhouse answered with resonant awakenings along seven rows of silver fruit.  The silver fruit opened, and dawn burned the panes clean.  Ilyra appeared once more through ephemeral cameo appearances, this time in every bead of dew at once, and her torn veil turned from ash to ordinary linen.  Mara laughed until she sobbed, a mournful elation she could not separate from grief, as the fields outside cracked wider to receive the first honest light.
