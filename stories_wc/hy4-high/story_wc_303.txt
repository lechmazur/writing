By dusk the velvet observatory at midsummer held a mulberry ache, purple light lying on the curtains and the bruised sweetness of berries gathering under every chair.  Mara Sen, a provenance researcher with ink on her cuffs, had been summoned to certify the empire's holiest instrument before the promise loses its glow.  At the center of the black brass scale rested the transparent violin made of ice crystal, cold enough that the velvet around its case had darkened with dew.  The registrar wanted one signature by dawn; with it, the Emperor's ancient claim over the orchard provinces would shine for another hundred years.  Mara had come to weigh a dream against an empire: a handful of villages asking to choose their own councils, and ten thousand clerks who called that wish disorder.  Her mother had kept a ribbon from the first village petition, soft as a moth wing, and had made Mara promise never to confuse age with truth.  For twenty years Mara had done her finest work by reading the future in coffee stains, because stains remembered hands, tables, and consequences that clean parchment denied.  She poured a cup from the observatory samovar, set it beside the acquisition ledger, and watched the brown tide creep toward a list of imperial donors.  Inside the drying ring she saw the future in which she signed: roads paved with victorious statutes, villages renamed by number, her own portrait flattering and false in a ministry hall.  Beside that tidy vision, pale gaps opened in the coffee, and the old scholars would have called them perforated destiny, the small lawful holes through which unchosen lives could breathe.  The empire taught seamless fate, one emperor following another like beads on a string, but Mara had built a career proving that every chain had missing links.  A spill of moonvine pollen during an herbarium apprenticeship had left her accidentally telepathic with plants, and now the ferns along the dome thought in slow green alarms.  They did not understand seals or salaries; they understood that the cold thing on the scale was a locked seed case, not an instrument for emperors.  Their root pressure shaped one human word, urgent and botanical: propagate.  Mara bent over the violin, and because it was transparent she saw, beneath the bridge, a black seed ribbed like a comma.  The catalogue said the instrument had been carved from a northern glacier for the first Emperor, but provenance lived in materials as much as in ink.  Under a lens, each crystal carried the spiral scar of moonvine frost, a plant that grew only where village assemblies had buried their charters.  The empire had stolen those charters, frozen the living seed inside imperial music, and called the silence continuity.  Mara's first impulse was to hide the discovery, because a signature would keep her rooms, her pension, and the right to enter any archive in the capital.  Then the coffee stain showed the price of that safety: gardeners dragged from moonvine plots, children reciting emperors, and her mother's ribbon pressed under glass as treasonous nonsense.  The mulberry ache deepened, sweet with what she might save and sore with what she would lose.  She tore blank margins from the ledger, laid them over the pale gaps in the stain, and cut twelve slips of moonvine from the nearest pots.  The cuttings shivered when she touched them to the damp paper, as if they recognized both the coffee and the verdict.  To propagate the evidence, she used the only warmth the observatory allowed: coffee, breath, and one lamp turned toward the violin's strings.  A single drop slid from the ice crystal, fell into the saucer, and opened a new ring around every cutting.  In those rings she read other futures, not victory parades but meetings, stubborn gardens, mistakes, and neighbors learning one another's names.  The holes did not promise happiness; they promised room, which was the only honest thing a dream could ask of history.  With the registrar's boots sounding on the stair, Mara set the ribbon her mother had given her on the empty pan of the black brass scale.  The violin weighed nothing against it, and that was the answer she had feared.  When the registrar entered, she offered him not a certificate but a cutting, roots already knitting through the wet paper.  He reached for the ice violin, but the lamp had done its work; the crystal split, the black seed woke, and the ferns sent their green verdict through every moonvine root.  At dawn the promise lost its glow, and the villages began.
