In the seventh winter after the nostalgia ban, Vel Or kept no heirlooms, lullabies, or maps of the old sky.  Censors called memory a fever and burned it from drawers, sleeve linings, tavern signs, and whispered names.  Only the sea refused their edits, lifting and withdrawing in its patient grammar.  Sela Vey read that grammar as a skilled tide dancer, forecasting closures by watching what the water left unsaid.  Her guild named that balance obsidian grace, grief held above black water without a splash.  Under the ban, she learned by observing gaps: pauses between waves, missing notes in forbidden songs, silences where surnames had been.  When censors smashed her mother's moonstone ring, Sela hid one pale grain beneath her tattoo needle.  Harbor folk called such a relic a moonstone cabochon dust speck, light scraped from a polished curve.  Under stars, it showed deleted constellations as vacant spaces around itself.  Sela wanted to pen a cosmic verse on mortal skin, because paper could be searched and stone broken, but a body could walk.  The clerks named her departure elective exile, as if signing before deportation made the boot less real.  Sela signed anyway, keeping one verb of her life.  On her last permitted night, she entered a twilight garden under stars, walled with black ivy around a fallen meteor.  While Vel Or shifted on flood foundations, the garden stayed spontaneously anchored, level even when tides pulled cellars sideways.  Tavi waited there, a thin apprentice born in the ban years, with salt on his cuffs and no heirlooms.  Because he owned nothing, Sela had almost mistaken him for a clean page.  At moonrise he meant to fledge, taking the outward tide without rope or permission.  Sela brought ink mixed with the speck, a silver needle, and no chart, since the sky gave the same intervals.  She meant to write down his back and send him through the harbor chain as a living archive.  Tavi heard the verse, the dust, the route, and the exile; then he kept his shirt on.  That refusal was the first gap Sela could not read.  'I will carry it,' he said, 'but not because you lost your mother.'  In his mouth, exile was not a sentence done to him, but a door he intended to choose.  Sela felt the old urge to command the tide, and under it shame at having mistaken a boy for parchment.  Her obsidian grace seemed suddenly too polished, a black mask keeping sorrow lovely and other people silent.  Before she could answer, patrol lamps moved beyond the ivy, and a clipped voice recited penalties for remembrance.  The rooted garden held, but every wall lamp went dark at once.  Sela could see neither ink nor needle nor Tavi's face, only stars shaken in the meteor's black basin.  So she returned to her first discipline and found the patrol's path by observing gaps between halted footsteps.  She set each mark where a star was missing, not where one shone.  The dust drew the voids up through Tavi's skin, and he stayed as spontaneously anchored as the garden.  His stillness was no longer Sela's possession; it was a shared craft, chosen and held between them.  When a censor's hand brushed the ivy, Tavi said, 'I claim elective exile.'  The words were no charm, but chosen departure tangled Vel Or's clerks until dawn.  Sela finished the final interval as the outward tide opened a seam beneath the garden wall.  Tavi pulled his shirt down, took the needle case, and asked whether she would come or keep polishing grief.  Sela looked at the city that had outlawed longing, then at the small door of black water beyond the roots.  She left the needle case with him, because a verse meant to live could not belong to the hand that first feared losing it.  Then she stepped after him into the tide, not as his author, but as the witness he had chosen.  Together they watched Tavi fledge from shore, his dark sleeves spreading into the gap the patrol had failed to guard.  Behind them, Vel Or kept its clean mornings and empty drawers, certain that longing had been cured.  Ahead, under the recovered constellations, a mortal back held their altered sky, and Sela finally understood obsidian grace as motion without possession.
