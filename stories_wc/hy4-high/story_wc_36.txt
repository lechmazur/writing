After the guardianship ended, the driven relic keeper Mara Venn returned alone to the Thunder-echo range, carrying a cracked case and a question that had outlived her orders.  No council remained to forbid the journey, and no apprentice remained to ask why she still went.  She had come to harvest insights from failure, not to polish the story of her courage.  Eleven years earlier, the last guardians had tried to seal a sound seam beneath the western cliffs.  They had played bronze horns into the storm, believing volume could command the weaving between worlds.  The seam had answered with echoes that walked back wearing the voices of the missing.  Mara had held the gilded reed flute then, but her hands had obeyed the council instead of the instrument.  The narrow flute loosened the strands by which one world remembered another, answering questions rather than opening gates.  Mara, proud once, had obeyed commands instead, and that obedience waited in the case like a live coal.  The Thunder-echo range received her without ceremony, all red walls and shelves of stone polished by storms.  By dusk the canyon hush had settled so deeply that her boot scuffs seemed intrusive.  After a final peal rolled nine times between the cliffs, the pass became avalanchingly quiet.  That silence was why she had returned in this season, when thunder spent itself before dark.  Only in such air could she uncase the flute without waking every echo the dead had left behind.  The route belonged to Ivo, the youngest guardian, who trusted wood more than music.  Flash floods carried driftwood into high dry cuts, so he could mark forks where thunder made sound lie.  He had spent the failed expedition arranging driftwood as directional markers at every fork where sound lied.  At a black seep, two pale branches crossed like a held breath and pointed up an unmapped rubble chute.  She answered with a fresh splinter, because a marker that did not answer was soon debris.  Then, in the lee of the wall, she stopped to uncase the gilded reed flute and play one low note, less a tune than an invitation for the seam to remember her.  The canyon did not brighten, but a second canyon breathed underneath it, cool and unpitying.  Weaving between worlds felt like stepping through cloth still warm from another body.  There the old storm had not ended, and shadows with bronze horns and council badges kept refusing to look back.  Mara followed the markers left by Ivo through that repeated disaster, noting where each branch had been turned.  He had not marked an advance toward the seam; he had marked places where retreat stayed possible.  That was the first insight she harvested, and it shamed her more than any ghostly voice.  The guardians had called caution a defect in boys, yet caution had drawn the only honest map.  A second insight waited where the horns had been sounded, a fan of splinters all pointing outward.  No one had arranged a way home; everyone had assumed the triumph would make one.  Mara copied the outward fan into her notebook while dry lightning tugged at her sleeves.  Only when she reached the last marker did the voice of Ivo come, small and close behind her.  It asked for the returning chord, the one the council had sworn could mend any severed life.  Her driven heart answered before her hand, for she had carried that chord like a concealed blade.  She raised the flute, but the second canyon grew avalanchingly quiet again, as if snow buried even hope.  In that weight she heard what the earlier silence had tried to teach: echoes are not people.  The returning chord would not restore Ivo; it would feed her need to the seam and widen the old wound.  She lowered the flute and used both hands to answer the last marker instead.  Arranging driftwood as directional markers one final time, she turned every pale arm toward the black seep.  The gesture was small, almost domestic, and it gave the woven place a direction that was not conquest.  The false horns sagged into shadows, the storm folded inward, and the second canyon released its held breath.  Mara cased the flute before the first real thunder could find it.  By morning she descended with a notebook of errors and a path plainly marked for whoever came next.  After the guardianship, no anthem waited to excuse her, only the canyon hush and a harvest small enough to carry honestly.
