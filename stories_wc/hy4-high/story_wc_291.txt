Among cold cases there is a second clock, one that starts after the evidence disappears.  Afterward habit guards the dead and grows mean without objects to correct it.  Mara Vale, a face blind portrait artist, worked in that late hour.  She could copy a cheek from a photograph, yet pass her sister and feel only a coat's draft.  Because recognition never arrived whole, she was very methodical, measuring gait, sleeves, pauses, and the angle of grief.  Her commissions were cold cases without witnesses or forgiving light.  She took them to awaken dormant empathy, not to win arguments.  A portrait made a stranger inconvenient.  The Larkspur archive burned its quarry files after flooding, and only a blue masquerade mask ribbon fragment survived sealed in a dance card.  It belonged to the Quarry Masquerade, a case nobody named without lowering their voice.  Thirty years earlier, lanterns hung along limestone benches for a charity ball.  Before midnight, a blast collapsed the north shelf, killing six dancers and watchman Orrin Pike.  Seamstress Iva Renn vanished from the refreshment table.  The report said Orrin set the charge in drunken rage, and Iva stole the donation box.  Two portraits hardened from that report, one villain and one thief.  The council wanted Mara to repaint them, but the hall received her in a furrowed hush.  People feared an honest brush might disturb the arrangement that let them sleep.  Mara asked for ledgers, weather logs, hospital sketches, and glove menders' names.  She read with a ruler and copied contradictions into two columns.  On the left she placed what the town had seen, on the right what it refused to see.  She worked by unifying opposites, because panic leaves its shape between signal and shadow.  The ribbon fragment showed two dyes, festival blue outside and lampblack in the weave.  A laundry account listed the same dyes for Iva's aprons, since she repaired masks near smoking lamps.  Orrin's ledger, nearly dissolved, noted a cracked fuse box and a warning to the committee.  The last page held a triangular knot Iva used to mark unsafe equipment.  Mara set the dance card beside the report and felt the case turn.  At dusk she and the caretaker carried lamps into the shadowed quarry in mist.  The benches rose in black terraces, and mist made every distance seem abandoned.  Mara had numbered the wall into squares from a survey map.  She was very methodical again, kneeling to record damp, lichen, and cracks.  At the tenth station she asked the caretaker to lance a clay blister with his survey awl.  Water breathed out, then a blue-black thread slipped from the seam onto her glove.  Behind it, cut into stone, were Iva's warning strokes and Orrin's initials.  The blast had not been rage.  It was an attempt to fire a warning charge away from dancers, using a frayed fuse Iva tied for visibility.  Orrin died beside it, and Iva, trapped by the collapse, lay under the stone the report called empty.  Her donation box was later found in the caretaker's shed, full of wet gloves, not money.  Mara did not hurry back.  She sat until mist blurred the marks and let the dead become particular instead of useful.  The next morning she painted two panels, not faces but gloved hands, one tying a knot, one shielding a flame.  Only after the hands did she attempt portraits, borrowing posture from ledgers and expression from actions that outlived rumor.  Orrin appeared anxious, not cruel, and Iva appeared tired, not guilty.  When the memorial opened, no one cheered, because the hall kept its furrowed hush.  But the mayor's daughter touched Iva's painted glove and asked why nobody had mentioned the wet gloves.  An old mason removed his cap before Orrin's panel and stood longer than politeness required.  Mara watched from the aisle, unable to find those faces in the crowd, yet certain recognition had begun elsewhere.  The repaired story did not resurrect anyone or return the missing evidence.  It made room for grief folded away, and in Larkspur that was the first warmth anyone dared to feel.
