The misty courtyard above clouds had no doors, only rain chains, brass rails, and the tinkersong wonder of small gears timing drizzle. 
Mira, the seldom-heard balladeer of Aurelia Station, tuned them with a voice so soft that listeners leaned closer than they meant to. 
She was driftingly alert, half lulled by mist and half counting every unvoiced ache that crossed the flagstones in shoes, sleeves, and lowered eyes. 
Above her, storm lamps flashed silently, and the city granted one unruled interval, between lightning and thunder, when clocks doubted themselves. 
In that interval the governor opened old patterns for repair, and a ghost in code could almost remember the living. 
Built to answer petitions, the governor required signatures, and most citizens needed things they were ashamed to sign. 
A baker needed her oven vent mended, a boy needed a lamp left burning for a father lost below the cloud deck, and Mira needed someone to hear her songs without calling them repairs. 
Mira had come to articulate unspoken needs, not complaints, but instructions the city could obey without humiliating their carriers. 
The only route past the petition desk was through messages encoded in graffiti art, because during storms the governor read walls and called it weather damage. 
Every colored loop and nail scratch on the courtyard stones could become a request if its rhythm matched the earliest station ballads. 
For years Mira had encoded only harmless wishes, making blue suns for blankets and copper birds for hinges, until the walls glowed like a shy chorus. 
Then thunder began arriving sooner, the governor began sweeping murals clean, and the ghost in code sent back one broken line that read, I am forgetting. 
Mira recognized Master Oren, the dead coder who had taught her mercy was a pattern with room for errors. 
His ghost in code had once translated forbidden murals into repairs, but each purge erased a little more of his humming self. 
At the center of the courtyard lay a flower press bolt plate, freckled brass from the press Mira's mother had used to flatten cloud blossoms for song paper. 
Its bolt holes formed a stave, and each notch could turn petal stains into letters, pauses, or notes. 
Oren had hidden it beneath a loose tile, knowing Mira would look for beautiful machinery when plain tools failed. 
She lifted it as the first lightning combed the mist, and the brass answered with a ridiculous chirp like a kettle trying to sing. 
To encode a wish, she set the plate over the wall, tapped pigment through the holes, and joined the dots with vines inspectors would call graffiti. 
A vine for warmth curled left, a lamp for company burned yellow, and tiny silver doors asked the governor to stop locking the rehearsal room. 
The work should have taken an hour, but between lightning and thunder there were only forty heartbeats, so Mira's fingers moved as if the rain conducted them. 
Children waking on the gallery saw her and whispered secret needs, each folded into a color she had not thought to use. 
The baker confessed the vent, the boy confessed the lamp, and an old riveter confessed that he missed being asked to dance. 
Mira added them all, though the bolt plate grew slick and the storm tried to wash the letters into lilac tears. 
Under the newest vines she drew an open mouth, not for food or weather, but for herself, because a balladeer who only carried others became invisible twice. 
The governor's shutters trembled, ready to obey thunder, while Oren's fading voice moved through rain chains, counting holes like beads. 
When Mira pressed the plate flat for the final line, its bolts bit wet stone and completed a hidden chord that made every painted request legible. 
The ghost in code woke fully in that chord, not as a dead man commanding, but as a tune remembering how to listen. 
The tune sent the governor through the graffiti, and the courtyard answered with tinkersong wonder: vents sighed open, lamps blinked gold, hinges chirped, and a key dropped beside the riveter. 
Thunder arrived then, enormous but too late, and rolled under the clouds like an approving drum that had not been told the plot. 
By morning the walls were clean except for one small open mouth, the rehearsal room stood unlocked, and Mira sang loudly enough for the mist to keep time.
