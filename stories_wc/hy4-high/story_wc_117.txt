The clinic clung to the sage ridge above town, smelling of scrub, rain barrels, and warm cedar.  Mara Vale, the mellow music therapist, arrived before breakfast with chimes, a bodhran, and no sudden cures.  There, ordinary magic meant small, testable acts: spoon against cup, breath finding beat, a shaded room made less lonely.  Her songs were softly adaptive, slowing for rapid pulses, brightening for lifted eyelids, and leaving room for silence.  She had chosen the work to nurture lifelong wonder in children who had learned to expect doors closing.  Her hope never became certainty, so nurses called it cautious exuberance, joy that checked weather and exits.  In September, when the almanac promised a solar eclipse, Ada brought Ivo to the music room.  He was nine and silent since the funeral of his sister Lark, who had lived eleven days.  Ada carried a keepsake baby hair curl in a blue envelope creased from handling and hiding.  Mara did not touch it; grief could flinch from kindness.  Ada said he had stopped asking even rude questions; the doctor called it withdrawal, but Mara heard a child guarding wonder from another loss.  An eclipse might make a door too brief for argument, so she planned a side entrance through shadow, sound, and ordinary attention.  The almanac placed totality beneath the hush of dreaming roots, the gardeners' hour after irrigation when water sank around sage.  Her method was a shared moment during a solar eclipse, to link Ivo's memory of Lark with the durable world.  To keep it honest, she listed only glasses, a drum, water, breath, and permission.  Ada nodded fearfully.  On eclipse evening, Old Ames, the gardener, brought glasses, white paper, a basin, and three patients; Ivo sat apart on the porch.  Mara counted chairs twice, because joy there needed railings.  As the moon bit the sun, weak tea light scattered leaf crescents across the paper; someone laughed.  Mara asked for notes held without strain; the chord grew softly adaptive, bending toward the slowest breath.  Ivo did not hum, so his silence stood as part of the chord.  Ada set the envelope on the drumhead; two fingers from Mara made it whisper and tremble.  At totality, crickets sang in the wrong hour, the basin held a white coin gone black, and nobody spoke.  On the drumhead, the keepsake baby hair curl shifted inside its envelope, lifting with the low note and settling when Ada exhaled.  It was not a visitation or promise, but ordinary magic: light failing on schedule, skin remembering rhythm, a curl answering breath through leather.  Ivo leaned forward; suspicion and curiosity crossed his face like clouds sharing one edge of sky.  Mara stopped the drum before anyone could turn it into a lesson.  When daylight returned, the porch became only boards, chairs, and people.  Ivo reached for the envelope; Ada opened her hand instead of closing it.  He held the curl against the returning sun and asked if it could happen again.  Ada said yes, though Mara heard the harder question: could wonder survive being touched?  Mara said nothing about cures; she offered a list of trembling things.  Ivo asked whether roots dreamed or only people said they did.  Old Ames said roots kept their own counsel, and Ivo almost smiled.  That almost let cautious exuberance cross the porch without applause, and Mara knew the link was thin, strong, and untugged.  By morning, he had taped the paper to the wall with a crescent, a crooked drum, and the word again.  She checked the window, then tuned one chime to irrigation water finding sage roots.  He asked if roots could hear it; Mara said perhaps wonder could begin with a question.  She hummed with Ivo until breakfast bells replaced the chime.
