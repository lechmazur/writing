In Harrow Lane the rule was old: after the final grain slips through the hourglass is when an unsaid house begins to breathe.  What breathed in the Sten house was no draft or settling timber, but a true haunting in which cupboards opened to show the cup their dead boy had chipped and mirrors rehearsed a smile no mouth owned.  Mara Vey arrived with dry leaves and the pull cord handle of her first machine.  She had been the city's leaf blower symphony conductor and had led twelve orange blowers through the echo chamber tunnel each autumn, turning gutters into brass sections.  The concerts ended when Timm Sten died of fever, and his parents locked away his scarf and every sound that belonged to him.  Now she came to restore lost laughter to a household that spoke only in permissions: sit here, close that, do not mention the nursery.  Her older trade was tending to the unspoken and watering words families refused to say before they rooted in walls.  In the parlor the apologies were mournfully eager and pressed against her coat like cold dogs.  Ada had never said she was sorry for sending Timm out with a wet cough, Jonas had never said his dragon joke was meant to make the boy laugh, and their daughter had never said she hid the scarf.  After the funeral, each unsaid thing had sunk to the echo chamber tunnel, where the city once tested horns and stored refusals.  Mara knew the old score: restoration could pass only via half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind because finished songs invited the dead to stay and perfect notes excused the living.  A half song left room for an answer, and bent wind could enter without waking defenses.  She descended with the twelve blowers, set bowls of leaves and rainwater under their nozzles, and let the engines breathe until the leaves could macerate into the smell of the yard where Timm had once laughed at machinery.  One blower lacked a starter, so she tied its bare cord to the red plastic pull cord handle worn smooth by Timm's hands.  Years before, he had sneaked into rehearsal, begged to start the first dragon, and laughed one hiccuping laugh that went unspoken after the funeral.  Mara lifted a broom handle notched with old tempos and signaled the first breath.  She hummed broken lullabies without words while the blowers answered in a sour chorus, one flattening, one sharpening, one rattling a loose screw.  The off-key wind pushed through the bowls and carried the half lullabies upstairs in thin spirals.  In the tunnel, echoes blurred metal, the dead, and her throat until doors shuddered, a cup clicked, and a whispered sorry returned three times smaller.  Mara called for the broken machine, wrapped her fist around the pull cord handle, and yanked.  For a moment the other blowers stalled; then the bare cord snapped tight and Timm's hiccup of delight came out of the machine, not recorded or imitated, but small and unmistakable.  It ran ahead of the wind into the parlor and worried the silence loose.  Above, Ada made a swallowed sound, Jonas failed the dragon joke aloud, and their daughter confessed why the hidden scarf smelled of smoke and hospital soap, then laughed because he made the dragon worse.  The laugh that followed was not happy, not clean, and not large; it was the first honest noise the house had permitted in a year.  It passed from throat to throat, humbled and shaking, while the mirrors kept the dead boy's smile too long.  Mara stayed below until the engines cooled, tending to the unspoken once more by letting the echoes finish.  They had their laughter back, though it came whenever a cup was lifted or a cord was pulled, smelling of wet leaves from a tunnel no one could find by day.  Mara walked home with the pull cord handle warm in her pocket, conducting the gutter wind because restored things need tending, and the unspoken are never truly gone.
