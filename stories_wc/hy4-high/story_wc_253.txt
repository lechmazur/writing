By the third winter after the happiness mandate, the farmhouse overshadowed by giant wind turbines had stopped sounding like a home.  Their blades divided every room into brief shadow and weaker light, and the walls answered with a low, compliant hum.  Tomas kept the kitchen clean because cleanliness was measurable, and measurable things were less often punished.  He had not cried since Lena died, because the mandate had reclassified tears as an insult to public confidence.  The silence left behind had been taken by inspectors, edited of breaths, and returned to grieving houses as remade hush.  A black box above the stove produced it each evening, smoothing the turbines' pulse until sorrow sounded like approved weather.  On Fridays, Mara arrived as the blank-faced performer assigned to audit cheer in private rooms.  Her face had been trained empty so cameras could project whatever expression the bulletin required.  She wore a yellow sash, carried a scoring slate, and knocked once on the doorframe before entering.  She was morally ambiguous in the plain way of people who carry both keys, because she sealed houses for the state and sometimes left hinges loosened.  Her method was dramatically narrating mundane tasks, turning sweeping into a procession, dishwater into a sea, and kettle steam into the last witness of empire.  She had requested the bereavement route to cultivate lightness in grief, a private offense too faint for the statutes to name.  On the last Friday before the spring calibration, she found Tomas sitting beside a dried flower bouquet.  It had been Lena's funeral bunch, hidden under fresh mandated carnations until the colors died into one brown permission.  A stiff willow support ran through its center, and Tomas kept touching it as if it were a bone.  Mara set down her slate and, in a voice trained for balconies, declared that the table would now endure the tragedy of crumbs.  She wiped them with her sleeve while the wall microphone listened for unlawful sadness, and it heard only theater.  "Behold the chair," she continued, "accepting the weight of an ordinary man without once appealing to history."  For that reason she narrated the kettle next, telling Tomas that patience could make a small wooden citizen fit the country's narrowest lock.  Tomas looked at the willow support, then at the black box, and understood the chore hidden inside her pageant.  His hand took the small knife used for turnip peeling and began to whittle.  Each cut was quiet enough for the turbines to cover, and each shaving fell among the bouquet's brittle petals.  Mara lifted a cup and proclaimed its rinsing an abdication, thereby giving his knife a sentence of noise to hide beneath.  On the slate she wrote, "Uplift maintained," though the true report would have carried him to a correction farm.  The stylus was also a wire, and the slate sent its cheerful line to the district office before she set it face down.  The false record protected Tomas while proving her useful to the people who would erase him.  When the willow key was thin enough, Tomas stopped whittling and held it over the bouquet as if asking permission from no one.  Mara narrated the closing of the cupboard with such solemn grandeur that the microphone followed her voice and not his hand.  He slid the key into a seam beneath the black box and turned it until the remade hush missed a beat.  The room did not fill with sobbing or music, only with the unedited absence that had waited beside Lena's empty chair.  Tomas felt no relief, but the box could no longer make grief sound sanitary.  Outside, the giant wind turbines went on converting weather into current, indifferent to the small illegal quiet below.  Mara packed the cup and the slate, ignored the knife, then recited the hanging of her sash as though it were a coronation.  At the door she told Tomas that crumbs would return because tables were faithless and floors were patient.  He understood he must sweep the shavings, hide the knife and bouquet, and say nothing if another performer came.  He also understood that Mara might return with inspectors, because the same mouth that gave him this quiet could name it.  Neither of them called the moment kindness.  The mandate had made kindness too loud, and grief had learned to survive as an error in a household report.  In the farmhouse, under the turning shadows, the dried petals stirred once in the faint lightness, and nothing else changed.
