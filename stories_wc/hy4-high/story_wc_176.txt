In the icebound sanctum under aurora, the door opened only after forgiving yourself. 
You arrived in thaw month, when snowmelt ardor put a wet shine on the blue walls and loosened the old shame. 
For twelve winters you had believed guilt made you useful, a bargain as brittle as cold wax. 
Once you had been Vey's lost wax caster, making bronze throats for bells and for street organs that sang the poor to their graves. 
You shaped a model, buried it in clay, burned it out, and poured metal into absence. 
That trade taught you that a faithful thing could be made by disappearance, but not whom to disobey. 
When the Chancellor outlawed mourning, soldiers brought the organs to your furnace, and you cast their frames into notification bells. 
You told yourself the ledger was safe, because that was easier than watching children cover their ears outside the foundry. 
By spring the ink had flaked, clerks had forgotten their formulas, and Vey could no longer pronounce its dead. 
A transcribed memory in that city was not a page but a sound held in holes, crossings, and tuned air. 
The archivists copied every name twice, in organ cards and in the braids of the ceremonial rope above the sanctum. 
You had hidden a street organ punched card scrap in your sleeve on the night of the bells, keeping seven names in square apertures. 
You had come to preserve the sound of forgotten names, not to purchase pardon from any door. 
The guild had called your hands technically gentle, meaning you knew exactly how much pressure a fragile thing could survive. 
The threshold inscription repeated the rule: begin after forgiving yourself, because a clenched thumb splits ancient fiber. 
You laid the card scrap on the reading stone, and the aurora leaned through the ice roof until the holes filled with green light. 
The dots made a pattern of long and short intervals, the last organ's old method of spelling breath. 
Above you hung a centuries-old ceremonial rope through bronze rings, its braids crossing in the same grammar as the card. 
Caretakers had loosened the knots while forgetting their burden, so one crossing had reversed during a frightened winter. 
You had come to illuminate the dome by reconfiguring the braids of a centuries-old ceremonial rope. 
Only that alignment could send aurora through ice prisms, wake the bronze throats you had cast, and return stored intervals to air. 
You warmed wax between your palms and pressed it against the frozen braid, taking a negative without tugging one strand. 
The cast showed a short hole on the card answered by a long overcross on the rope, silencing the first two names. 
Your heart wanted to wrench the mistake straight, but snowmelt ardor gathered in your fingers as a steadier, thawing heat. 
You softened the strand with breath, slipped a wax wedge beneath it, and changed nothing else until the braid settled of its own accord. 
Each repaired crossing illuminated more of the dome, and each green flash shook a syllable loose from the bronze throats. 
"An" came first, then "Sella," then "Ilen," the apprentice whom you had sent home from the foundry to keep her safe. 
She had died of winter fever before the bells were finished, and the Chancellor had crossed her from payroll and memory. 
Guilt rose with old authority, asking you to stop, because hearing her name felt like a sentence you could not commute. 
This was why the rite waited until after forgiving yourself; otherwise the final crossing would become another noose. 
You breathed out, not to excuse the furnace but to leave room for the dead, and shifted the last braid one hole. 
Light ran along the rope, found the prisms, and illuminated the nave in ribbons of thaw-green fire. 
The bronze throats answered with intimate clicks and sighs, and seven names returned with hundreds stored behind the corrected pattern. 
You could not restore Ilen or the children outside the foundry, yet the sound of forgotten names now had a warm body. 
You pressed the repaired crossing into wax and copied the card scrap's holes along its edge for the next keeper. 
At dawn the wax would be buried in clay, burned away, and replaced by bronze, so neither grammar could again stand alone. 
The sanctum dripped with snowmelt ardor under the fading aurora, and you carried away no pardon, only the card scrap clicking as if the dead were clearing their throats.
