In the living bridge garden, roots had been coaxed across a ravine until every crossing breathed, and Mara Venn, the hedge maze designer, walked them because she trusted no path that kept still. 
The commission slate at the eastern gate stated no date, no season, and no deadline: Timeframe: None. 
That absence was why she had come: to discover the texture of time, which she imagined as pile on velvet, grit in pear skin, or the warm nap of bread before a crust forms. 
Mara was retrocognitively trapped, so her eyes arrived a moment late, showing the prior shape of each leaf, the hand that had pruned it, and the rain that had not yet ceased falling. 
To keep herself oriented, she carried fading photographs in which the garden appeared as it had never been, each print a scaffold for the trellises that grew only where memory expected them. 
One photograph held a halo fragment above the oldest bridge, a crescent of pale exposure that warmed her fingers and made the roots beneath her quiver like a choir trying not to breathe. 
Mara had handled enough fraudulent relics to know that this one altered the fading photographs instead of merely shining in them. 
Whenever she placed the print beside a living arch, the pictured arch grew younger in the paper and older in the world, and the difference passed through her wrists as a soft, granular tremor. 
That tremor was the nearest answer she had, and she followed it deeper into the maze she had been hired to redesign, where yew corridors multiplied without ever forming a comforting center. 
Her first plan used Euclidean turns, yet every route returned her to the same childhood room where a girl sorted photographs by the degrees of their disappearance. 
She understood then that the maze was not built in space, but in lost occasions, and that the girl was herself, retrocognitively trapped and still asking whether time had a surface one could touch. 
The halo fragment answered by sliding from the photograph into her palm, leaving behind a white wound that gardened itself into new images whenever she blinked. 
Under its light the whole place entered a quaking reverie, with bridges swaying in leaf green sleep and photographs fluttering though no wind existed. 
Mara laid the fragment on the central knot and, through hyperbolic geometry, opened corridors that diverged faster than memory could claim them, each saddle shaped bed inviting more paths than the garden could hold. 
The design was dangerous because her gift had always been containment, teaching wealthy clients how to lose themselves safely, but here every hedge refused the mercy of a boundary. 
As the negative curves widened, the fading photographs began to disintegrate, not into dust but into salts of moments, the grit of a step, the cool pile of shade, the slick skin of a pear eaten long ago. 
Mara wanted to stop the ruin, for the garden had been her only map, yet the salts settled on her tongue and gave her the very evidence she had crossed the living bridge garden to find. 
Time was not a road or river, she learned, but a weave of vanished contacts, rough where regret snagged it, smooth where attention had been kind, and always thinning where someone demanded that it stay. 
The halo fragment burned clearer as she accepted this, and it showed her the original commission hidden beneath the gate: not a maze to preserve the photographs, but an engine built to release them. 
Because the phrase Timeframe: None had never promised an afterward, she understood that release required no repair, no memorial, and no client waiting beyond the garden. 
She opened her hand, and the halo fragment passed through her skin like warmth through linen, stitching her perception to the present for one unbearable instant. 
In that instant, the living bridges no longer echoed prior crossings, the girl in the childhood room looked up from her sorted images, and Mara felt the whole hedge maze begin to disintegrate through hyperbolic geometry. 
She did not run, so she stood in the quaking reverie and let each leaf, print, bridge, and younger self separate into the tactile hush she had mistaken for loss. 
When the last photograph vanished, the texture of time remained on her palms, too fine to name and too real to mourn.
