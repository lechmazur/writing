At the celestial mapping observatory, midnight solace had a rhythm: the shutters folding back, the pens thawing, and the sleepers breathing under blue wool.  Mara served there as the lucid dream chronicler, recording whatever trained dreamers brought back after they learned, inside sleep, how to question the patterned sky.  She had taken the post to decode temple inscriptions that her mother had copied before fever erased her speech, and Mara believed the lost grammar hid between dream-maps and stone.  Mara heals with words, not with hands: she had learned to give the bereaved one clean sentence at a time, as if naming a wound could keep it from spreading.  Distrusting shared burdens, she kept ledgers locked and answered couriers herself, because every shared grief at home had become another duty laid on her mother.  On the night of the winter eclipse, a temple runner climbed the ice steps with a daylily-pressed telegram warmed against his ribs.  The petals were brown and translucent, their veins crossing the flimsy message like minute meridian lines.  Mara read it by the green lamp: Open the lens when understanding dawns; the lower inscription has surfaced; bring every dream-ledger, or we drown.  Moving water in the vault made the stone alphabet shine.  Three translators had fainted from holding the whole sequence alone.  Mara recognized the symptom: a burden made private until it poisoned the mind.  She packed the ledgers alone, refusing the runner's offer and the night assistant's hand.  At the gate, old Pell, who had kept the dome for forty years, asked her to witness one thing before she left: the minima of three variable stars burning above the southern roof.  She almost refused, but her mother's last command had been to look before translating, so she stayed.  She recorded the dimming through variable stars whose periods the abbot had inked in the margins of the daylily-pressed telegram.  The brown petal veins matched three changing stars, and their long and short pulses divided the telegram into syllables.  Only a living witness could mark the omissions between pulses, the pauses where the stone carvers had left meaning for another mind to supply.  The dream-ledgers held those omissions, one fragment from each sleeper, and Mara had hoarded them as if solitary accuracy were a virtue.  Now the pulses insisted on shared burdens: no single book contained a sentence, but arranged together they made a weathered grammar of loss and rescue.  The telegram's phrase, when understanding dawns, was not a clock but a condition, the moment when private knowledge became common enough to bear.  At the first pale dilution of the sky, she unlocked every cabinet and carried the ledgers to the dome.  Instead of translating alone, she asked the runners, the cooks, the shivering novices, and Pell to witness the last three minima and call out whatever fragment their own dreams or duties had given them.  Fragments surfaced: a stair of seven dots, the verb for carrying, the sign for water, Pell's interval that meant wait.  Mara wrote quickly, and because she heals with words, she did not flatter or command; she gave each frightened offering an exact place, and the room grew quiet.  Through variable stars, the pulses ordered those spoken pieces, and the lower inscription resolved into a floodgate rule and a confession: the ancient scholars had divided grief among many mouths.  It named shared burdens not as kindness but as engineering, the only way any vault, heart, or sky stayed upright.  Mara laughed once, because the grammar her mother lacked had been a chorus she kept trying to silence.  She sent the daylily-pressed telegram back with the decoded command written across its petal stains, and the runners reached the temple before the water found the inner stairs.  The gates opened, and the waking translators were carried into ordinary rain.  Mara's copied sentences told each translator what was carried and what could be set down.  By morning the translators slept, and a novice kept a ledger beside his cot.  Cabinets stayed unlocked, and each midnight shift began with a witness and ended with a reading aloud, until no dreamer carried a whole sky.  Mara still came to decode temple inscriptions; when understanding dawns, it is midnight solace: enough hands holding one another's portion of light.
