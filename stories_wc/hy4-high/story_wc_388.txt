The maps, after the liquidation, called Cinder Valley uninhabitable, and most people preferred that clean sentence to any living question.  Only a wasteland orchard thriving amid radioactive dust storms disagreed, standing green beyond the salt fences with pears heavy as arguments.  Ida Renn arrived with a cracked dosimeter, a tape recorder, and the professional caution of a conspiracy debunker who had disproved too many beautiful rumors.  She was sharply observant, noticing that the dust lay evenly on the fence posts but not on the innermost leaves, and that bees flew in straight lines through air her instrument swore was lethal.  She had come to excavate truth from comfortable lies, especially the kind printed on ration notices, not to chase the old story about a gem that murdered whoever touched it.  That gem, a flawed diamond rumored to be cursed, had once crowned the surveyor's brass vane above the orchard's central tree.  The liquidation report said the vane had melted, the archive beneath it had burned, and three hundred orchard workers had been resettled before the first radioactive dust storm.  Ida had debunked forged martyr lists and paid apparitions, yet this report smelled of a room scrubbed after blood, because every date ended before anyone could ask a second question.  In the workers' last folk tale, a hidden stillpoint lay where all storms canceled, a calm eye where fruit could ripen and forbidden names could be kept.  Official commentators called that tale a superstitious conspiracy, useful only for selling curse charms to frightened evacuees.  Ida heard a better claim in the bees, whose unwavering paths suggested that the wind went around something rather than through it.  With the dosimeter stuttering and sunset turning the dust copper, she had to intuit the boundary from pear shadows, pollen rings, and the sudden obedience of her own hair.  At the center, under a tree whose bark had been grafted into a spiral, the storm noise stopped so completely that Ida heard sap ticking.  The stillpoint was not magic; it was an engineered hush, produced by buried windbreak vanes and a ring of leaded stones that funneled the charged dust into a moat.  Someone had built a refuge for truth inside the only weather the censors could not flatten.  Beneath the spiral tree, half swallowed by roots, Ida found the brass vane, its diamond dull with ash but still fixed in its cracked bezel.  The black flaw inside it was not a dead man's eye, as peddlers claimed, but a long mineral fracture angled like a draftsman's slash.  During the next storm, Ida solved the mechanism not with a laboratory but by reassembling shredded letters she shook from a hollow pear crate sent as charity.  The shreds were thin as onion skin, cut by a postal shredder, and every strip carried a few gray words in the careful hand of the orchard's vanished bookkeeper.  Ida sorted them on the calm ground by fiber, ink shade, and the tiny crescent bites that no two shredder blades repeated.  When the last columns joined, the letters became testimony, a map, and an accusation hidden inside an ordinary requisition for winter sacks.  The bookkeeper wrote that the Ministry had not resettled anyone; it had liquidated the workers for refusing to certify poisoned fruit for safe export.  Before the soldiers came, the engineers hid ledgers, witness statements, and radiation readings in a vault under the hidden stillpoint.  They set the flawed diamond above the vault because its strange fracture cast a split beam onto a latch only when the storm light reached a precise angle.  The curse rumor began later, spread by officers who feared that frightened looters might discover what curiosity could open.  Ida read the final sheet twice, feeling her old career turn over: she had spent years proving that panic invented conspiracies, and here a patient conspiracy had invented panic.  The official lies had been comfortable because they asked nothing of the living, while these damp scraps demanded that she carry names back into a country that had rehearsed forgetting.  At dusk, storm light struck the flawed diamond and split into two green needles.  Ida set the vane by the map; a latch clicked under the spiral roots.  The vault held ledgers, film cans, and the signed liquidation order.  A Ministry drone hovered past the moat, ready to report another death by curse.  Ida packed the ledgers, the map of graves, and one copy of every name.  With the needles, she burned the first page onto the lens; by morning, comfortable lies would have witnesses.
