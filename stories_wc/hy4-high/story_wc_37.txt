Time did not advance in the mirror world; it braided. 
I worked in the alchemical orchard greenhouse shrouded in endless night, where pears distilled lost names and vines exhaled sleep. 
They called me the scattered memory keeper, because no memory of mine stayed wholly inside my skull. 
One childhood lay in a snail's track, one winter in a cracked thermometer, one kiss in bitter pollen. 
This dispersal had once seemed a curse, until the realm began to forget its own mornings. 
Our suns were not fire but woven dawns, strips of light plaited from songs each village had surrendered. 
A true dawn knits communities together, carrying one neighbor's threshold into another neighbor's dream. 
Without that weaving, roads refused to meet, bells rang in private languages, and children feared shared bread. 
Now the last dawn had thinned to a gray thread, and I had come to rekindle a dying realm. 
To do it, I needed the mica window at the greenhouse's northern wall. 
Mica does not show a face; it shows what a face cannot bear to remember. 
On ordinary nights, apprentices pressed their brows to it and learned which apologies they owed. 
On this night, endless and unbroken, the mica window reflected only an empty cradle of sky. 
Every woven dawn could be repaired through forgotten songs, but remembering them required consent from those who had lost them. 
For seven turns of the irrigation wheel I walked between orchards and huts, asking each settlement for its discarded tune. 
The millers offered a grinding hum, the beekeepers a drowsy scale, the ferry widows a chorus no one would claim. 
Each fragment returned as a knot under the tongue, painful until I sang it into thread. 
The old keepers called this bound release, the mercy of holding pain tightly enough to let it open. 
My own knot was the hardest, a lullaby my mother hummed while hiding me from the first famine. 
Because I was scattered, that lullaby lived in seven places, and I had avoided all seven. 
A keeper may mend everyone else's past and still guard one small room for herself. 
The realm had no rooms left. 
In the alchemical greenhouse, the collected threads shivered on frames of blackened pear wood. 
I began the weaving through forgotten songs, using my hands as loom pins and my breath as the shuttle. 
The grinding hum became warp, the drowsy scale became weft, and the widows' chorus gave the cloth heat. 
Still, the center sagged, for a dawn cannot rise on borrowed courage alone. 
I crossed to the Cold Orchard, where glass lemons held the winter of my fourth year. 
From their rime I drew my mother's first note, clear as a drop refusing to fall. 
Other notes hid in snail slime, thermometer mercury, pollen dust, a cracked cup, and a child's slate. 
Every retrieval cost me a private landmark, until I could no longer say why I had kept the song. 
That was the danger and gift of a scattered keeper: I could be emptied without being erased. 
When the last note came loose, the greenhouse inhaled. 
The gray thread swelled into a small, trembling brightness with no skin to hold it. 
I had to swaddle it quickly, folding the cloth warmed by songs around that brightness as one folds a newborn against winter. 
The bundle kicked against my palms, not fighting me but asking how much pressure a beginning can survive. 
I held it tighter. 
Only after the cloth stopped shivering did I understand that tightness was not possession but a promise of release. 
Carrying the swaddled dawn to the mica window, I saw villages not as they were but as they had refused to be. 
In the reflective panes, millers shared flour, widows mended the ferry rope, and children tore bread in halves. 
The window would not open for a keeper who clutched a dawn as proof of her usefulness. 
So I loosened my arms, though every instinct in my scattered body screamed that looseness meant loss. 
The swaddled dawn leaned out of me and pressed its soft brow against the mica. 
The panes split along their mineral layers, each sheet releasing a note I had believed belonged to me. 
Then the lullaby came back from a hundred throats, altered, rough, and larger than any private room. 
I did not remember my mother as mine; I remembered her as everyone's hand at a fire. 
That was bound release: the woven dawns became roads, and the old night loosened its fist.
