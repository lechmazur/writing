After the anesthesia wears off, the quiet tower at dawn holds a meteor hush, as if the sky is listening for the stone it dropped years ago.  On a narrow cot beneath the bell rope, Sela Venn lies under a gray blanket, counting pain before names because that is the first lesson of her trade.  She is a closure seeker who opens wounds, loosening the last stitch of silence in survivors of the Night of the Falling Stone.  That night, the fever ward prayed beneath a meteor shower, but the children's healing arrived one hill away in the old bell tower.  They called it the displaced miracle: the children lived, forgot the fevers, and the tower ivy grew warm as skin.  Their scars remained, but the stories inside them had gone elsewhere, leaving families with closed flesh and no ending to hold.  Sela's mother was one of those children, and now her throat carries a white seam she cannot explain, only touch when she wants to cry.  Sela's ambition, rehearsed in towels, lamp smoke, and locked infirmary drawers, is to learn to read the stories in scars.  Miss Ansel taught her to begin by examining coffee rings, because a shaking cup records the exact order of a shock.  The dark rim is the instant, the pale center denial; the broken beads hold the sentences a patient could not say.  Sela carries that method into every scar, reading ridges like dried coffee for the moment a life changed direction.  Yesterday, surgeons opened her forearm to remove a thread of tower ivy asleep there since childhood, leaving a neat new wound.  It also left her accidentally telepathic with plants, so at dawn the moss in the mortar keeps whispering the words cold below, cold below.  The ferns are less poetic; they crave light behind the north wall and picture a hollow shaped like a cup.  Sela tries to hush them, but their green panic is as intimate as her pulse, and it points toward the tray the night nurse forgot.  Three cups sit there, each ring dried to a different thickness, each stain dragged toward the same crooked flagstone under the wall.  Coffee does not seep uphill unless a floor breathes, and Sela knows such floors only in houses built over stolen weather.  By the third ring she knows where to tunnel; her bandage says she has waited long enough.  She takes the coal shovel, lifts the flagstone with Miss Ansel's probe, and ignores the cot's invitation to faint.  The plants chorus warnings as images of hooked roots, frost on glass, and a bell silent for twenty years.  Sela apologizes to them and begins to tunnel, working slowly so the new stitches in her arm stay quiet.  The passage, already made, is a root tunnel lined with pale fibers that flinch from the shovel.  It smells of wet iron, cold coffee, and green hush after a meteor's brightness.  Crawling, she remembers her mother asking for one true sentence about the scar on her throat, not comfort, only a sentence.  She has opened a hundred wounds for such sentences, and every story widens the teller's old wound.  Closure is not a door closing; it is a hand steady while the door admits its weight.  At the end of the tunnel, roots braid around a stone pedestal, and on it rests a frosted hourglass no longer than her palm.  Frost feathers the glass from inside, and the sand in the upper bulb is tiny white knots like healed seams.  The displaced miracle beats there without light, a pulse meant for the fever ward years ago.  The ivy warns that turning the glass will erase the children's scars, while breaking it will silence every green witness.  Sela understands that a miracle in the wrong place becomes a theft if she spends it carelessly.  She studies the dark frost rim around its waist as if it were one of Miss Ansel's coffee rings.  That rim recalls a wound's first instant, so she chooses neither turning nor breaking, but opening.  With Miss Ansel's probe she scores the seam, and the frosted hourglass yields like skin under a careful stitch.  White grains spill outward toward the city, carrying cool cloths, bed numbers, meteors, and her mother's burned singing.  Because the anesthesia has worn off, pain keeps the images sharp and refuses prettiness.  The plants keep ordinary green thoughts while Sela climbs back through the tunnel toward her mother's room.  Above her, the meteor hush waits, and one scar at last has a sentence.
