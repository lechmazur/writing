After the last human dream is dreamt, the reclaimed silo library settles into bronze quiet.  Beyond its round wall, people have forgotten sleep, but lime and straw in the old grain bins still remember voices.  Only Iven Vale, an odd historian with chalk in his beard, arrives after the bell.  He has come to collect the stories walls have heard before the plaster dries into mere mineral.  He is sensitively perceptive in the way of people who have spent more years with rooms than with crowds.  His palm can tell which wall kept a wedding, which held a fever, and which has begun repeating a story in the wrong century.  On the third evening, his palm stops above Bay Twelve, where a lullaby is beating under a butcher's bill.  The shelf numbers have not changed, yet every remembered sound leans one bay west.  In a round archive, a displaced axis is not a metaphor but a fault that slides all voices out of true relation.  The central mast, once the grain hoist and now the spindle of the catalogue, has shifted by a hand's width at its socket.  Mara Vale, his teacher and the keeper before him, had warned that without dreams the walls would need a truer center.  Iven had dismissed her as tender toward myth, which is one reason he has come back ashamed.  He has come for all the town, he tells himself, but most of all for the sentence Mara never finished.  To find that sentence, he will probe the fault through half-burned diaries found in a fireplace, because Mara hid her measurements near the hearth.  The fireplace is a narrow brick insertion, installed by a founder who wanted readers warm and forgot that silos draw wind.  At the evacuation, someone burned ledgers there to keep the children warm, and the bad chimney left Mara's diary spines as black bricks under ash.  Iven lifts them with a palette knife and reads by breath rather than by lamp, since flame would finish what frost began.  The entries say the last human dream was dreamt by a girl sheltering under Bay Twelve beside the hearth during the power failure.  She dreamed that dragons circled the mast and held it upright with their tails while the whole town slept inside their coils.  Mara wrote that on waking the girl could describe only the dragons, and no one ever dreamed again.  That night frost cracked the footing, the mast leaned, and Mara understood that memory had lost both its dream and its plumb line.  She did not call this magic; she called it engineering with grief in it.  Her final entry points to the object above the mantel, a fragment of a medieval tapestry showing dragons, pinned beneath smoky glass.  Under the lamp, he sees that copper threads in the dragons' bodies form a circle interrupted exactly at the west bay.  The diaries name those threads a template for the original socket, cut by weavers who loved maps disguised as monsters.  Mara had tried to reset the mast, but wet plaster gave back no clear angle, and she refused to turn the wheel by guesswork.  She left the diaries where Iven would find them, because he had always searched forbidden things first.  The knowledge hurts; she used his oddness as a key.  He lays the tapestry fragment over the iron plate, aligns the copper circle with the mast scar, and crawls below with a wrench.  In dust that tastes of old wheat, he begins a slower probe, feeling for the angle where plaster, metal, and remembered breath agree.  Each turn of the wheel nudges the mast by a hair, and the shelves give small ticks like cooling bronze.  He wants to stop when the lullaby returns to its cradle, but his sensitively perceptive fingers feel one more quarrel trapped in a funeral.  He gives the final quarter turn; bronze quiet deepens into hundreds of stories resting in proper bays.  From Bay Twelve comes Mara's unfinished sentence, not in her voice but in the wall's imitation: the center is a promise we keep for one another.  Iven writes it down, then leaves the page open instead of filing it, because a story owned by one hand becomes another displaced axis.  At dawn, which now arrives without dreamers, he opens the round door so cold outer air can hear what the walls have heard.  The reclaimed silo library holds bronze quiet around him, a listener waiting for the next careful hand.
