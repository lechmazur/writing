Six months after making first contact, the city turned the rainwashed tower in spring into a shrine of controlled arrivals.  Mara Venn, whom the pilgrims called the luminous skeptic because contact spores had silvered her skin, came not to pray but to measure.  She had one private reason: to understand the chemistry of homecoming.  As an orphan of the dredged districts, she distrusted any home that could be issued by decree.  The Ministry called its new rite an artificial pilgrimage, a replica of the spiral the visitors had walked when they first opened their copper hull.  Every dusk, volunteers in ash robes were led around broken statues, through wet boxwood, and up the tower stairs.  At the last stair they would converge, face the west window, and whisper the single sentence the visitors had given humanity: Home answers.  The Ministry promised that obedience would invite the whole fleet to land peacefully under guard.  Mara had been academically rebellious long before the landings, publishing field notes that mixed analytical chemistry, conservation, insect behavior, and forbidden anthropology.  Her deans called it impure.  The visitors had proved her method by refusing translators until someone reproduced the smell of wet limestone in a dish.  That dish, not eloquence, had opened first contact.  In her coat pocket Mara carried the fly amber, a thumb sized bead of fossil resin with a black fly fixed mid turn.  Cut from the tower foundation, it held an older spring bed's chemistry, older than the city.  When warmed, the resin released geosmin, succinic acid, and a faint animal bitterness from the trapped fly.  Mara believed those molecules were a grammar, not a relic.  Guards admitted her because silver skin made superstition easy; they mistook doubt for holiness.  Inside, the tower kept its bronze quiet, a hush of patinated rails, damp stone, and tied bells.  Mara found the mechanism via dusty footprints left on an overgrown sculpture path, where moss softened the shoulders of carved animals and guides shook pale powder from their sleeves.  Each print pressed that powder into lichen, spring water, and limestone dust.  By the time the robes climbed indoors, their soles carried a reaction product that had not existed when they entered the garden.  Mara knelt, scraped a footprint, and warmed the scrap beside the fly amber.  Both gave the same bitter bloom, but the print carried modern skin salt and a catalyst added by the Ministry.  The pieces converged in her notebook: ground fossil resin as bait, human sweat as buffer, rain as carrier, and hundreds of feet as a pump.  The artificial pilgrimage was not a welcome; it was a chromatograph with people for solvent.  At the final convergence, the drains would send one pulse to the copper hull, calling this guarded square a natal shore.  Mara had no army, only a vial of rainwater and the little fly amber.  Her deans had demanded clean instruments and clean consents; the rite had given neither.  In the bell chamber, she uncorked the vial, shaved resin, and let old spring chemistry fall into the drain.  The acid shifted pH enough to break the new ester before it could rise through the west window.  She did not flee; bronze quiet had taught her that truth needs no performance.  The pilgrims entered damp and silent, arranged in the spiral they had been told was ancient.  Mara stood among them, glowing faintly, and waited for the lie to fail.  At the west window, the guides whispered "Home answers," but the room smelled only of rain, stone, and one shaved bead of honest resin.  No trance arrived, and no signal climbed to the hull.  Instead, a child by the drain laughed and asked why everyone looked disappointed.  The question moved through the spiral more gently than any hymn.  Mara told them what their feet had made and what their consent had not.  Guards reached for her, but pilgrims aware of their own soles stepped apart and would not converge again.  Above them, the copper hull opened one iris, tasted the rain, and closed without accusation.  Mara understood homecoming as a compact, not a molecule or a lie told by feet.  She would still measure, argue with deans, and sleep under borrowed roofs.  In that bronze quiet, the rainwashed tower in spring held its breath as the luminous skeptic felt a chosen home begin.
