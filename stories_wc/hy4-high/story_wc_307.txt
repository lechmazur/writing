The floating rock meditation platform drifted above the valley without a sound, its underside furred with moss and its rim planted with pale sycamores.  Beneath it, the magnetic grotto under starlight opened like a black instrument, veins of ore pulling gently at every nail, buckle, and filament of iron.  The evening held a sycamore hush, soft and green, as if leaves were asking the sky to speak more quietly.  Ione Avel, the backwards aging researcher, sat in the center with her small feet tucked under her robe.  She had entered the academy at eighty, published at sixty, disputed theorems at forty, and now had the round hands of a child.  Only her eyes kept the long arithmetic of her years.  Mara, her last apprentice, had climbed the swaying ladder with an iron clasp hidden in her sleeve.  The clasp was meant to pin Ione's robe to the rock when the final lesson tried to take her away.  Mara wanted rescue because she had not yet learned the difference between holding and witnessing.  Ione's written motive, repeated on every grant and copied into Mara's first notebook, was to teach vanishing to appear.  She weaves truth carefully, the Fellows used to say, never to hide it but to let it ripen where haste could not bruise it.  At the center of Ione's work was delayed revelation, the belief that some facts became visible only after the mind stopped reaching for them.  She had taught for decades through the patterns in the rings of a pond after a stone is thrown, reading water as other scholars read print.  The pond on the platform was no wider than a table, blackened with fine iron dust and wired by the grotto's patient pull.  A single smooth stone lay on Ione's palm, gray as a held breath.  "I will withhold the last sentence," Ione said, smiling at the worried bulge in Mara's sleeve.  "If I name the end now, you will watch me instead of the shape my leaving makes."  "Throw the stone, and keep your hands open through the trance."  Mara nearly argued, for nearly every answer she loved had arrived too late to save someone.  Instead she set the clasp on the rim, where the ore tugged it softly, and took the stone.  The lesson lasted through the trance, with no bell and no dawn to relieve it.  Mara threw, and the pond accepted the stone with a small, unshowy plural sound.  Rings opened, crossed the reflected stars, and returned from the rim before fading.  The grotto held every circle a little longer than water should, combing iron into threads that braided with the next returning ring.  First Mara saw only circles, then bars, then the bent letters of a script she knew from Ione's oldest notebooks.  The words did not say where Ione would go; they said what a place does when someone stops filling it.  "Do not look for me yet," Ione murmured, and her voice had become light enough to belong to the leaves.  Mara kept her gaze on the pond, though the iron clasp ticked once against the stone.  She remembered Ione at sixty, correcting a proof with a cane across her knees.  She remembered Ione at forty, laughing at younger colleagues who mistimed a silence.  She remembered Ione last winter, asking for help tying shoes while explaining the ethics of absence.  Each memory arrived later than the ring that summoned it, and each ring altered the memory after it came.  That was the discipline Mara had resisted: attention given before explanation, a held breath before the name.  Through the trance, Mara learned not to turn rescue into noise.  The iron clasp slid toward the grotto.  "Withhold," Ione whispered, using the bare verb as command and farewell.  Mara let it fall.  The rings folded around that lesser absence and drew the clasp in outline.  Then Ione stood smaller and stepped into the center ring.  She did not sink or speak; water continued around the space her weight had left.  Every returning ring marked her years in reverse: stoop, stride, schoolroom, cradle, clean indentation.  Only after the water stilled did delayed revelation complete itself.  The last ring had drawn departure's visible contour, not described a departure.  Ione's withheld sentence lay in the edge Mara could see.  Vanishing had appeared inside a patient border.  In the sycamore hush, Mara threw another stone and began her first lesson.
