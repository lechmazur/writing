In the era the almanacs labeled "after ghosts become visible", the dead acquired daylight but not voices.  At an abandoned beekeeping oasis in a scorched wasteland, Iven mended hive frames and oiled water seals, though no bee had returned for nine summers.  He was an astonished recluse because twelve ghosts stood each noon beside the dry troughs, thin as heat and silent as wax.  Courts now summoned the visible dead as witnesses, and capital clerks grew rich by reading lips, selling verdicts, and making hesitation sound like consent.  Iven had stayed to preserve the weight of unspoken words, because his daughter Miri had died after his last harsh command and left him no answer to polish into comfort.  When she appeared among the twelve, her closed mouth felt more merciful than any forgiveness he might have invented.  The oasis had been founded on a tangled covenant among seven beekeeping families, escaped guests, and the dead who knew where the spring slept.  The living promised water to anyone arriving with hive smoke, silence about the route, and honey for nameless graves.  The dead promised blossoms to bees, lost paths to raiders, and no required accusation from the sheltered.  Later heirs bartered clauses, brides added exemptions, drought erased signatures, and the promises crossed until no sentence could be pulled straight without tearing the rest.  The knot was kept in a stained glass box, cedar sided and smoke blackened, with five chapel panes above waxed cords and old queen cups.  In noon light, each pane threw color on a different object, and the pattern made sense only through contextual clues.  Iven, tranquilly perceptive where other men grew frantic, read those clues in a tilted palm, chosen shade, a sleeve lifted against absent wind.  His daily task was a gaze rather than a question, traveling from box to hives to ghosts until their stillness arranged an answer.  On the morning the Office of Last Meanings arrived, the ghosts stood near the smoker, and Miri's hand hovered above its latch.  Three auditors crossed the salt flat with a warrant, a voice horn, and a chart declaring the spring public once the covenant was certified as lapsed.  Their leader wore clean gloves and carried a coal smoker scented with tar, not thyme smoke borrowed from the hives.  One auditor circled a hive clockwise, which no beekeeper did; another checked a water skin still plump from the capital pump.  The warrant required Iven to speak the covenant into the horn, after which the horn would give the ghosts words and make silence usable in court.  He brought shade, set out the box without opening it, and offered no greeting, because the first spoken claim would begin the unraveling.  The leader frowned at the colored panes, mistaking silence for ignorance, and uncapped the horn like expensive wine.  Iven met Miri's gaze, then looked once at tarry smoke, plump water, clean gloves, and the untouched queen cup by the blue pane.  Nothing passed between them that a clerk could quote, yet the twelve ghosts shifted into red, amber, green, and blue strips across the hive lids.  Each stood beside the object that contradicted the auditors: false smoke, false thirst, false poverty, and a legal seal powdered with northern salt.  Miri took her place by the water seal and laid two fingers over the cord that meant refuge.  The old knot showed, through contextual clues and not testimony, that the newcomers had come to claim, not to ask.  The leader waited for a sentence, but Iven tilted the box until blue light covered Miri's closed mouth and red light covered the horn.  Without a spoken claim, the warrant had no handle; without ghostly assent, the chart had no witness; denied both, the horn breathed hot air.  The auditors left before the worst heat with unused words, while the ghosts remained in colored light.  Iven corked the seals, set the stained glass box in shade, and felt astonishment settle into attention.  Miri did not smile, and he did not ask; the space between them kept its burden, and the oasis answered with mute eloquence.
