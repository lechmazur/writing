The valley doctor wrote after fever breaks on Mira's discharge paper, and the words climbed with her in the cable car to the mountain weather station.  That evening, the windows held an auroral sorrow, green light shivering over empty chairs and the cot where her sister Ida had once slept.  She had returned neither to reopen the signal flags nor to scold the instruments, but to find the weight of a swallowed truth.  Before the fever, Mira had believed in clean verdicts, but the station had survived on swaying certainties.  The fault-tolerant oracle in the north alcove was built from three barometers, a wind harp, punched tape, and dead engineers' patience.  It was peripherally aware, reading drafts at the door, frost ticks in the walls, and hesitations left beside official figures.  It answered by occupying margins, never the ruled center where superintendents stamped their confident arrows.  A failed vane did not silence it; it lowered its claim and kept speaking.  When a sensor iced over, a brass stylus would smudge soft graphite along the chart edge, a warning shaped like dirt.  The valley office ignored such dirt, but Mira knew the mountain kept its mercy there.  Eight winters earlier, Ida had taken her best botanical illustration of a dwarf willow to the east ridge, where new snow made rocks look forgiving.  She meant to compare the drawing with living leaves before descending to a valley post that would take her away.  The central chart promised clear air until noon, and Mira read only that broad, stamped promise.  At the edge, the oracle had written inversion, do not cross, take the lee path, in graphite so faint it seemed embarrassed.  Ida asked whether the pass was safe, and Mira heard the question as a door opening.  Because speech would keep Ida at the station out of obligation, Mira swallowed the warning and called silence kindness.  Mira said the chart looked kind, then pressed a damp cuff across the margin to smudge the oracle's sentence into weather dirt.  By dusk, cloud sealed the ridge, and the rescue party found only Ida's satchel, its straps furred with ice.  The official report praised the faultless center forecast and blamed a botanist's impatience, phrases Mira signed because her hand knew how.  During the fever, she had woken with graphite on her tongue and the word safe breaking in her mouth.  Now she unlocked the archive drawer where the botanical illustration lay beneath that day's log.  She wanted a number, because numbers could be entered, contradicted, and endured.  The assay balance stood under the north window, used for hailstones, pollen, and salt from anemometer cups.  She laid the illustration on its pan, then the log, then both together, as if grief obeyed arithmetic.  The balance gave grams of paper, ink, and leather, nothing more, so she laughed once without pleasure.  Behind her, the oracle clicked, tolerating another broken sensor where ice had chewed through the east vane.  Its tape crept out with swaying certainties: ridge wind rising, pressure falling, old words not settled.  Mira turned, angry at the machine's little courage, and saw its stylus paused above the margin of her blank report.  It was peripherally aware of the drawing on the balance, of her cuff, of every pressure she refused to name.  She understood then that the answer had survived by occupying margins.  The damp cuff that erased one sheet had pressed through to the botanical illustration below, leaving a reversed bruise of graphite.  Under the aurora, the dwarf willow's pale leaves held the negative warning: inversion, do not cross, take the lee path.  The words were not heavier than paper, yet Mira bent as if the pan had dropped through the table.  She copied the hidden line into the official margin, not the center, and wrote that Mira Venn had read it.  Under it she wrote that she had smudged the warning, said the chart looked kind, and let Ida trust her.  She wrote that the official report was false and that no forecast deserved a human name for its errors.  When the ink dried, the oracle printed one last line beside her confession: probability altered by speaking.  The mountain weather station seemed to exhale, and the green light over the snow settled into auroral sorrow rather than judgment.  Mira understood the truth had weighed nothing until she set it down, and then it weighed exactly one life.  At dawn she would send the corrected report down the cable and leave the illustration open to the light.
