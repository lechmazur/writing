After the hydraulics fail, the starlit signal station stops turning its dishes toward weather, ships, or roads beneath the drowned valley. 
Pumps sag, brass lifts settle, and the only light is stars on gallery glass. 
Mara Venn stays because she is the patchwork navigator, licensed to read broken routes with a glass eye, a palm sewn with old maps, and a leg made from an oar. 
She trusts joined fragments, because joined fragments carried her through the flood that took Ilo, her younger brother. 
His convoy vanished between the chapel and eastern locks, leaving no grave, no wreck, only a hiss recorded in the station ledger. 
She has not stayed to recover him; she has stayed to navigate the geography of loss, where every blank has weight. 
In old manuals, reverse migration meant tracing a vanished flock backward from the empty roost until silence pointed to the bend. 
Mara had used it for medicine boats and stranded children, never a brother, but after the failure there are no forward maps left. 
The dishes hang useless, river water claws the lower doors, and within a month the last batteries will drown. 
The station has one cycle of midnights and no second chance. 
What remains of the network reaches her through cryptic phone calls at midnight, one sentence from a dead exchange, then a flat click. 
The voltage in the copper trunk fluctuates with moon phases, so she names wax, full, wane, or dark before trusting any bearing. 
The first voice tells her to build from the last warm place. 
She laughs once, then drags bed slats, bell wire, dish rivets, and a cracked barometer into the chart room. 
Night by night she builds a low valley table, with bent nails for stops, threads for routes, and black pins for unanswered names. 
Each call gives a wound to measure: three bells under black water, a hymn number, a child counting blankets, a goose flying inland ahead of its shadow. 
She checks the fragments against ledgers, funeral rolls, tide books, and the scars on her palm where old maps were stitched into skin. 
The pattern moves backward, eastern locks to ferry, ferry to school kitchen, school kitchen to the chapel where her mother burned rosemary for the dead. 
That return makes no worldly sense, since Ilo had been ordered east, but reverse migration never cares about intended direction. 
It follows the pull left after orders, ropes, and weather are gone. 
To ground the table on the waning moon, Mara opens the salvage cabinet and finds a green lion paw from the drowned chapel, its tag reading patina bronze censer foot. 
She knows the scratch across its pad because Ilo made it with an altar key, teasing that the lion held up all the smoke their prayers could not carry. 
The next midnight voice says the green paw belongs under the place where smoke went up, and Mara finally answers. 
"I sent him east," she says, and the wire offers no forgiveness, only water crackling in its drowned rooms. 
She sets the patina bronze censer foot beneath the chapel pin, tightens bell wire around its stem, and feels the table answer with low animal warmth. 
At the dark moon, the tidal voltage dies, every thread slackens, then rises upright as if a flock has passed upward through the floor. 
Mara watches reverse migration complete itself: the ledger hiss becomes a boy's breath at the chapel door, the breath becomes a list of names held against rain, and the list becomes a hand releasing the receiver. 
It is not Ilo speaking, and it does not reveal a grave; it shows that he turned back to carry the chapel register to the signal wire, so the missing would have witnesses. 
The knowledge does not free her from the order she gave or the water that took him; it gives the blank an edge, a door, a reason bent toward care. 
Mara unpins the eastern road and leaves the backward thread in place. 
Then she rests the bronze foot against her patched leg and listens to the starlit station tick itself toward morning. 
The comfort hurts, because it is exact, and because nothing in it asks her to pretend that Ilo will knock at the door. 
It is raw solace: a mapped absence, a brother's last kindness, and enough solid ground to set down what she has carried.
