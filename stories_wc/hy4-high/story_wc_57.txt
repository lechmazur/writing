As fog rolls across the harbor, the golden dock under snow seems less a place than a held breath. 
Ilyan waits there with a cedar case of seeds, a brass scale, and a lens of fulgurite glass. 
He is a philosophical seed collector, which means he labels each packet with a question as well as a name. 
Where others ask how much grain a field will yield, he asks what promises the dead have left for the living. 
His errand that morning is to preserve ancient methods before the harbor council replaces the salt meadow with heated sheds. 
The sheds will grow clean certified barley, reliable and rootless, and no one will remember which seeds like fog. 
Ilyan has argued that a method can be a kind of seed, but argument has never moved the council. 
At the dock end, old Oren uncoils a rope that his grandfather used when winter nets were still mended by hand. 
The rope is gray, salt swollen, and knotted with the patience of men who expected grandchildren to read their fingers. 
Oren plans to sell it for scrap because the new harbor office calls such ropes unsigned rubbish. 
Ilyan asks to coax the last lesson from it before the cart comes, and Oren, shivering, agrees. 
The method is simple to name and difficult to practice: by decoding knot patterns in a fisherman's rope. 
He does not expect a spell, because the secret weavers hid knowledge in labor, not miracles. 
They were infinitely patient, believing a truth worth keeping should need a season to find. 
Ilyan has never had that virtue, though collecting seeds has taught him to wait beside slow things. 
Snow gathers on lanterns, and the harbor keeps its oceanic hush, as if waves listened under ice. 
The lens came from a dune struck by lightning before Ilyan was born. 
Its fulgurite glass bends weak winter light into bright lines, making every twist in old hemp show direction. 
Ilyan sets the lens over the first cluster while Oren holds the rope across two frozen cleats. 
In ordinary light the knots look like habit, but under the glass they separate into counts and paired turns. 
Three short turns mean days of soaking, a doubled bend means brackish water, and a loose figure means thaw. 
Ilyan writes each sign in a damp notebook, careful not to pull the rope tighter than dead hands did. 
This is why he must coax, not seize: a strained knot becomes a lie and a lost method a rumor. 
Oren watches the harbor office cart appear through the fog, its wheels hushed by snow. 
Ilyan says he has until the lamps are lit, and Oren answers with a nod. 
The second cluster is harder, because the secret weavers braided two kinds of memory into one fisherman's rope. 
One thread records where winter barley was sown; the other records who could gather and share it. 
The council has called the meadow unused because no paper deed names those rights. 
Ilyan understands, with a chill beyond snow, that the rope is a deed written in use. 
He had come for sowing instructions, but the infinitely patient work of the secret weavers offers a firmer defense. 
Lens low, he follows paired turns that repeat nine times and break around a knot shaped like an open seed. 
Oren leans closer and says his grandmother called that knot the ninth sheaf, left always for the shore poor. 
The phrase changes Ilyan's face; his questions about seeds had forgotten the people who ate them. 
He adds a label not for a seed but for a duty owed to strangers. 
The clerk steps onto the dock, stamps snow from his boots, and asks whether the rubbish is ready. 
Ilyan does not argue about yields; he lays the open rope along the golden planks. 
Through the fulgurite glass, the clerk sees boundary knots, the ninth sheaf, and the brackish soak counts. 
"It is only rope," the clerk says, but his voice sinks into the oceanic hush before it reaches water. 
Oren says his father taught him those counts, and Ilyan records the words as living testimony. 
By itself the rope cannot stop the sheds, yet joined to Oren's memory and Ilyan's list, it can delay them. 
Delay is a small, seedlike victory, and Ilyan has learned from the secret weavers to trust such measures. 
He asks Oren to keep the rope until spring, when they can walk the meadow and set boundary stakes by its counts.
