Marn began the last licensed night of the high road as a jittery relic courier, with three locks, two ledgers, and a dragonfly-wing pendant buttoned beneath his collar. 
Below him the aurora-lit ridgeway shone between cairns, every slab green and violet, while wind combed the iron markers like breath over teeth. 
He had walked it every ninth day for twenty years, delivering hinge pins, lamp wicks, census beads, and relics nobody opened. 
The sameness had been the point after Sella disappeared, because his absent mentor had taught him to coax wonder from the routine rather than chase marvels. 
She had oiled a stubborn latch until it clicked like a bell, then made him write the sound in the delivery book. 
When children later said she had flown into the aurora on dragonfly wings, Marn still remembered her cracked nails and practical curses. 
During those years, across the fade from memory to myth, that difference became his private cargo, heavier than the pouch knocking against his ribs. 
The pouch held dormant wayseeds, the last maintenance relics from a road the Archive would reclassify as legend at dawn. 
His order was to carry them to the eastern cairn, obtain one receipt, and return before the register closed. 
No supervisor wanted responsibility for a path that had slipped from budget to bedtime story. 
On the manifest, under condition, a clerk had written, Tone: None. 
Marn knew the phrase from Sella, who called no-tone cargo dangerous because it waited for a living answer. 
The seeds would never germinate for water, soil, chant, or the official morning bell. 
In an inventory margin, Sella had written: not by fire, not by water, only by vibrating sympathetically. 
That was why she had left him the pendant, whose thin wings trembled whenever his fingers did. 
The old couriers said it extends brief encounters, stretching a nod or a dropped cup until its meaning can be caught. 
It did so by vibrating sympathetically with whatever was about to pass away, and Marn had hated that generosity. 
Each stretched second made his losses louder, so he used it mainly to count buckles and catch his runaway breath. 
At the first beacon, a decommissioning surveyor passed him with a shuttered lantern and a mouth shaped around goodbye. 
The pendant woke, and that brief encounter opened long enough for Marn to see her ink-stained hand tremble on the closure seal. 
She was not eager to erase the road; she was frightened of being its last paid witness. 
In the hush she said the eastern cairn had cracked, and unplanted wayseeds would be burned with the old register. 
Marn's first impulse was to recite regulations, because regulations had kept him fed and never asked him to decide. 
Then he remembered Sella turning an ordinary hinge into music, and understood that obedience could be another way of abandoning her. 
He left the receipt unsigned and climbed faster, his satchel tapping a jittery rhythm against his hip. 
The aurora thickened, and the cairns ahead showed their cracks as blue lines, as if stone had begun to remember differently. 
At the eastern cairn, Marn knelt where Sella had last stood and unpacked the pouch with a sorter's care. 
He placed the seeds in the frost crack, set the dragonfly-wing pendant beside them, and let his hands shake without shame. 
The pendant found no note in the wayseeds, only their waiting, and answered by vibrating sympathetically with his pulse, the wind, and the iron under the snow. 
Marn kept the ordinary rhythm of his nightly round, breath, step, buckle, stamp, until the pouch gave a small green tick. 
One seed split, then another, and roots no thicker than thread stitched the crack with useful light. 
By dawn, seedlings had traced the survey lines along the ridge, marking where a courier should turn, wait, or mend a latch. 
The surveyor found him laughing and shivering, and did not burn the register. 
In its final column she wrote that the road required maintenance, not legend, and signed after Marn's name. 
The Archive later filed Sella as a probable myth, but Marn kept a note of her cracked nails in the delivery book, beside the sound of a latch. 
On his next round, still jittery, still ordinary, he paused at the first green marker to coax wonder from the routine before moving on.
