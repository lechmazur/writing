Just after the campfire's last spark dies, the judgment party climbs the salt steps toward the breezy lighthouse at dawn.  Below them, the beach keeps a black ring of ash, and the town windows still glow with people who want one answer.  Ione Vale walks last, paid to give that answer, because the coast calls her a conscience for hire and trusts her most when it is tired of listening.  Hallan, the magistrate, waits on the gallery with a sincerely insincere smile and both hands folded over the purse.  He has used this smile on widows and accused girls; his voice stays soft as wet rope.  The charge is simple: Nell Orro, apprentice keeper, had let the beacon fail, and the harbor wants one small name for its guilt.  Three sailors limp ashore, and no one has died, which makes the hunger for a culprit more frightening to Ione.  She has taken Hallan's coins, yet she came this time to welcome nuance into certainty, opening a shutter without letting the lamp go out.  Her sister had once been named an arsonist after a bakery fire; the clean sentence warmed the council and left Ione outside every door.  Since then, she has mistrusted verdicts that arrive without weather in them.  The lantern room receives them with thick glass, cold iron, and protected vulnerability: the sea is visible but cannot seize anyone.  On its only hook hangs the cloak of doubt, a gray garment used when a council wishes to be merciful and cannot bear to be wrong.  Once it covers shoulders, the crowd stops shouting, but the covered person takes the dawn ferry and never returns.  Hallan touches the hook and says, unhook the cloak, Ione, and let us be kind before the boats go out.  Ione does not obey at once, because a word spoken softly can still be a leash.  She turns to the west rail, where the old keeper's chimes hang in rows, each piece scratched with the tiny marks of a private weather ledger.  Old Sennet could not write after the stroke, so he left the night's conditions in tuned metal, choosing four pieces before every watch.  Nell had learned the code from him, but Ione had learned it from her mother, who repaired lenses and believed no machine failed alone.  Her method has never been glamorous; it is deciphering wind chime selections, listening for order in what everyone else calls weather noise.  That morning, four pieces remain tied to the rail: brass, copper, a green slip, and a hollow mirror shard.  The shard is curved from the cracked reflector, silvered on one side and empty as a cupped palm, so it catches wind instead of faces.  Hallan calls it stolen property; Ione hears the warning note Sennet invented for a lens in danger.  She touches the brass, copper, green slip, and shard, and the sequence opens in her memory as a line of handwriting.  West gale; failing oil pressure; fog; cracked lens; keeper stayed, visitor argued, repair delayed by the council.  Ione looks at the purse in Hallan's hands and understands the last mark: the repair fund had been lent to his nephew's warehouse.  Nell stands beside the lamp with a bandage around her wrist, too proud to shiver and too young to hide that pride.  When Ione asks what happened, Nell says that the oil pipe split, Hallan told her to keep the light burning, and she ran down with a hand lantern when the lens went dark.  Only her lantern had guided the Gannet's crew around the second reef; no one at the campfire had wanted that fact, because gratitude is harder to carry than blame.  The room grows quiet in the way a shell grows quiet, holding the roar it cannot release.  Hallan says that a harbor cannot govern on maybes, and his smile returns, sincerely insincere, asking Ione to make the old comfort.  Ione finally lifts both hands and unhooks the cloak of doubt from its iron hook.  She does not settle it over Nell; she fastens one corner to the rail and one corner to the split pipe, making a small gray shelter around the girl.  Inside it, Nell's shaking becomes a protected vulnerability rather than a sign for men to read and price.  Hallan lowers the purse, and the council begins, awkwardly, with repairs.  The town does not become gentle at once; it only stops asking one shoulder to carry the storm.  When Ione descends, the chimes keep no single note, and certainty has room for weather.
