Upon the shimmer of twilight frost, Mara Venn entered the ruined orchard district lost in centuries of thick fog, carrying twelve unbacked mirrors and the astronomer's broken sextant.  She had come to capture a runaway dream before it crossed the last sleep-wall and scattered itself through the dreams of the unguarded city.  For centuries the fog had been erasing boundaries: pear row from lane, cellar from root, the names of the dead from the mouths of the living.  It did not drift; it stood in glass mountains, clear, enormous, and sharp enough to divide lantern light into cold blades. 
Mara, a mirror maker by trade and by suspicion, trusted edges because she had made them for thirty years.  She was firmly grounded in more than temperament: lead weights from old backing frames were sewn into her boots, and the cobbles knew her tread.  That steadiness mattered, for the runaway dream had been born in her workshop from the last observation of her brother, an astronomer who tried to chart a star above the fog.  He had died with the astronomer's broken sextant clenched in one hand, its arc snapped at the exact angle where the star should have appeared.  When Mara silvered the instrument's cracked index mirror, the unfinished pane reflected not her face but an orchard before ruin, pears burning white in a windless dawn.  The vision fled before she could back it, and since then it had run through every mirror in the district, erasing boundaries faster than fog ever had. 
Children woke convinced they were bell towers; old women answered to the names of pears; a baker kneaded dough that remembered being rain.  Mara did not chase the dream, because dreams that fear cages grow limitless; she meant to recreate the first orchard and give the fugitive a place it would choose.  Only upon the shimmer of twilight frost could frost crystals hold the breath of a crowd long enough to silver bare glass.  Only through synchronized breathing could separate lungs become one patient surface, and only the astronomer's broken sextant could set the mirrors at an angle that admitted a star that had never been seen.  A perfect instrument demanded two real lights; the split arc offered a gap where a lost star could be measured against its absence. 
At the central square, where pear trunks had grown into cellar walls, Mara fixed her twelve mirrors in the old rows and opened the sextant across a stone sill.  The last residents came because they had forgotten how to sleep separately, and Mara arranged them with their palms flat against the cold panes.  She counted four beats in, four held, six out, and the district inhaled with her, a sound like tides under glass.  Their first shared exhale blurred the mirrors; their second silvered them; their third drew black pear branches, a bell tower, and one white star out of the fog.  Mara, firmly grounded by lead, grief, and the practiced refusal to become anyone else, kept the rhythm while the reflections tried to pour their identities into one another.  The recreated orchard rose around them, exact as a cut edge, and the glass mountains of fog leaned down to watch. 
At the far row, the runaway dream appeared as a small figure made of distance, running from mirror to mirror with frost flashing under its bare feet.  It had escaped because every reflection claimed to own it, and Mara understood that capture would be impossible unless she offered a boundary chosen by the dream itself.  So she turned the cracked index mirror outward, not as a trap but as a door, and set the sextant's snapped arc to the angle of her brother's unfinished observation.  The dream slowed, recognizing the orchard it had carried away, and the residents breathed once more together while Mara held her own breath behind her teeth.  Because she remained separate, she could tilt the final mirror by a hair, closing the silvered edge around the dream without closing it inside anyone's hunger.  Frost rang once, a thin sound from glass mountains, and the recreated orchard folded into a mirror no larger than Mara's palm. 
By dawn the fog still covered the ruined district, but it had edges again: lane ended at wall, root ended at earth, and each sleeper woke inside a single name.  Mara carried the small mirror home and set the astronomer's broken sextant beside it, two flawed instruments that had measured the distance between losing a world and giving it shape.
