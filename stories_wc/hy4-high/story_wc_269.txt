In the eleventh winter after the fossil fuel ban, Mara Venn kept the sea level monitoring station at Bight Six, where the customs pier lay under clear water.  The station was bolted to basalt, yet felt drifting moored: its lamps and instruments seemed in passage, held by one patient chain.  Above her desk, a periodically eternal beacon went dark at dawn and returned at dusk, unpersuaded by history.  Mara had been certified as a coral bleaching witness at thirteen, when she logged the month Lattice Reef turned white and the parrotfish abandoned its channels.  Since then, charts had become her ache, and she had taken the night watch to discover why some paths disappear.  Each official Atlas kept deep shipping scars but erased the narrow reef passages where her mother had steered a quiet skiff.  The ministry called missing lines errors; old pilots called them currents; Mara called them grief.  In a drawer beneath the tide drum she found one Atlas page torn out and folded around a strip of oiled copper.  The paper showed a fan of passages through Lattice Reef, with short and long marks clustered along each dotted route.  At the bottom, in her mother's hand, was a command: Assemble when the well repeats the white summer, and do not ask the map to speak first.  Below it, Mara remembered a childhood rule from crews with failed radios: a practice of tapping rhythms on surfaces could carry a whole channel between boats.  Her mother had tapped supper tunes on enamel cups and gunwales, laughing that maps were music slowed down.  Pilots repeated a passage by ear: short knock for shoal, long knock for deep water, pause for a turn.  Now the marks on the torn page looked like a score waiting for a hand.  The tide well repeated the white summer only when lunar pull, barometer, and silted datum matched readings from the bleaching year.  That was the reef service's delayed revelation: a chart's truth could be held until the water that made it returned.  Mara had hated such patience; it sounded like an excuse made by people who had not watched coral die.  On the first calm night of the repeat tide, she locked the door against wind, though no fuel-driven boats hissed beyond the breakwater.  She laid the Atlas page beside gauge strips, old soundings, and the copper rod from the drawer.  Then she began to assemble the pieces according to the marks, sliding strips under routes until numbers and dots aligned.  When a strip resisted, she tapped its rhythm on the nearest surface.  Short raps on the steel rail numbered shoals; long knuckles on the glass case counted deeper cuts; a tap on copper gave the turns.  Salt-stiffened hinges answered differently from hollow pipes, and hydrophones carried each answer into her headphones as a brown pulse.  When Heron Thread produced a clean returning beat, she added a strip from the first bleaching survey.  Hour by hour, the torn page gathered a second chart made of water heights, regrowth notes, and dates from the ban tribunals.  Near midnight, the periodically eternal beacon returned, and the assembled pattern showed that the vanished paths had not been dredged channels.  They were approach lanes used by fuel tenders and specimen collectors during the last bleaching, when boats crossed living reef with pumps and hurried scientists.  After the fossil fuel ban, tribunals used witness records to identify those lanes and quietly delete them from public atlases.  Shoals were left undredged, buoys removed, and captains who knew the rhythms agreed not to teach them to cargo pilots.  Deep scars remained, needed by emergency craft and too damaged to heal; selective disappearance had been triage.  The truth arrived late because a child could recite a route to anyone with a boat, and grief makes poor custody of fragile places.  Mara sat with that delayed revelation while the well held its engraved line, accused and forgiven in one breath.  Her mother had torn out the page not to steal a map but to keep one path audible until reefs could survive without witnesses.  The last rhythm on copper was her mother's supper tune, three soft taps and a pause, not a course but a promise.  Mara played it down through the pier, filed the readings, and left the erased routes unnamed.  The sea lifted, the chain creaked, and the drifting moored station traveled nowhere, faithfully, with the reefs she now helped guard.
