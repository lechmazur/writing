In the cobalt glade at dusk, after the future arrives early, Mara hears tomorrow's funeral bell under the roots.  She is the root-bound visionary of Orra, held by pale roots from both ankles into the oldest oak, and the sound makes her smile before it makes her afraid.  That anxious elation lives in her still; being purposefully curious about a rising flood once saved the village and left her tethered to the tree that answered her.  Because she cannot walk, she has spent every rooted year trying to find the intersection of dreams, the place where sleeping minds enter the glade and borrow shapes from one another.  Now that impossible place spills proof into the grass: ripe pears from unopened blossoms, letters signed by unborn hands, and a boy with her dead brother's eyes.  The elders call it a storm of omens, but Mara recognizes the fracture of the eclipsed covenant, an agreement made under a black sun and hidden from memory.  Under the covenant, the village trims the maze topiary into a patient labyrinth, and the glade holds back unchosen futures, releasing one morning at a time.  Three winters ago, with fuel scarce, the council cut the hedge's heartwood and sold the patterned wood as firewood, never guessing each path was a lock on time.  Mara had warned them to heed the old notches, but fear of cold was louder than a rooted girl.  At the second bluish dusk, the boy stumbles from a gap in the yew shadows and holds out a maze topiary clipping, its leaves wet with silver sap.  He is not her brother, exactly, but he has her brother's laugh.  The clipping has two wounds: one fresh, one scarred by old pruning, and every leaf points toward Mara.  He says, "When the bell finishes, tomorrow will have nowhere to stand, so heed the cut before the roots choose silence."  Behind him, villagers shout to burn the clipping, because a thing from tomorrow might be a lie seeded by the grove.  Mara feels the old anxious elation rise, not from greed but from the terrible chance that her question finally matters.  The only method her grandmother trusts is linking through scars, because whole bark forgets, while healed breaks keep the shape of what opens them.  She lays the clipping's scarred end against the root scars circling her ankles, then touches its fresh cut to the lightning seam in the covenant oak.  The boy, understanding, presses his burned palm over hers, adding the small scar the future has given him.  Pain stitches them together, and the glade turns inside out: petals become doorways, root hairs become constellations, and every dream in Orra shines like a blue thread.  At their crossing Mara sees the intersection of dreams, not a point but a trembling knot where the council's warming hands, her dead brother's unfinished life, and unchosen mornings press together.  She understands then that the eclipsed covenant never demands obedience; it asks the village to share its dreams with the grove, so no future arrives alone or too soon.  The maze had been a diagram of that sharing.  By selling it for warmth, the village has chosen private comfort over communal dreaming, and time, deprived of paths, falls straight down.  Mara has wanted the crossing for herself, a window past her rooted body; now she sees that a window is useless if the house is falling.  So she chooses to heed the clipping, not by restoring the old maze but by giving Orra a new path it can never forget.  She pushes the fresh cut into the soil at the knot and speaks every sleeping name the roots have ever carried to her.  The clipping roots at once, unfolding leaves in the shape of the burned labyrinth.  Grown from linked wounds, the new hedge does not hide the covenant; it makes each scar a blue seam pulsing whenever Orra dreams.  The funeral bell stutters, the ripe pears shrivel back into blossoms, and the letters lose their signatures as unborn hands return to possibility.  The boy beside her grows younger by a heartbeat, then older, then simply a child with her brother's eyes who no longer knows which century has sent him.  Mara keeps one hand on him and one root in the new scar, trembling with anxious elation as the second dusk becomes ordinary night.
