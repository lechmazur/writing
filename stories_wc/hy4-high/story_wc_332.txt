At twelve past three, in the hush of improbable beginnings, the whispering archipelago lay under a sky the color of unwritten paper. 
At that pale hour, which the islanders called the luminous absence, lamps still burned, beds stayed warm, and every sleeper had misplaced the small country of night. 
Only Mira kept watch in the library on Bellwether Key, where she worked as the odd-hour librarian between last watch and first ferry. 
No one came for books then; they came to report the dreams that had slipped from them before dawn. 
Those losses appeared in the ledgers as a cipher of longing, a pattern of erasures no ordinary cataloger could bear to read. 
Mira read it by touching the blanks, because each blank kept the warmth of the thing it lacked. 
It was a map of desire, and the runaway entries always pointed toward the eastern water, where the sun first opened the throats of the islands. 
Her commission that night was simple and impossible: to capture a runaway dream before full light erased it and left its owner hollow. 
The dream belonged to Tavi, a boy silent since his mother sailed beyond the reef charts. 
Mira took the case personally because, years before, she had let the last dream of her sister dissolve while she argued with a jammed shutter. 
Since then she had learned to vitalize what absence had chilled, not by chasing it but by giving it a place to return. 
For that work she carried a watercolor palette whose wells held no paints, only squares of tinted glass and a slim photographic plate. 
Because she paints with light, Mira used the palette beside an old camera, breaking dawn into colors that could be laid on an exposure. 
She had proved, through sunrise photography, that a faithful negative could hold the outline of a dream long enough for a waking heart to recognize it. 
Recognition was the hinge; once a sleeper knew the shape of what was missing, the missing shape could come home. 
The cipher in the ledger for Tavi was subtler than grief, a sequence of bread crusts, blue thread, and a toy sail circled in salt. 
Mira understood that the dream had run toward every small proof that his mother had once been near, and would flee toward one final proof at dawn. 
She rowed past islands that whispered in voices made of surf, each one offering a different ending so the archipelago could keep the dream for itself. 
At Needle Chapel, where two black rocks framed the horizon, she found the last proof: a blue kite made with his mother, snagged above the tide line. 
The runaway dream was the clear space around the kite, brighter for having no body, trembling whenever the wind remembered a hand. 
Mira set the watercolor palette on the chapel step, opened each tinted well toward the east, and locked the shutter of the old camera to her breath. 
If she photographed too soon, the plate would show only sea; if too late, the day would be ordinary and Tavi would remain as quiet as an empty room. 
The first ray slid through the palette, and with no brush but the shutter, she painted with light across the waiting plate. 
The exposure caught a kite, a gap shaped like his mother leaning close, and the exact instant when longing turned back toward its owner. 
Mira did not trap the runaway dream; through sunrise photography, she vitalized the emptiness in Tavi that had been waiting without a name. 
Across the channel, before the first ferry coughed awake, Tavi opened his eyes and asked for the blue thread by its right name. 
His voice was rusty, but it carried the dream home more surely than any cage of silver could have done. 
Mira filed the negative under Beginnings, not Losses, because a recovered absence changes the catalog of a life. 
When she closed the ledger, the whispering archipelago still held its luminous absence, yet the hush felt less like an ending than a door left carefully open.
