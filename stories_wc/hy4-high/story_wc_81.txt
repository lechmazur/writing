During the blackout, the forgotten library inside a sunken city lost its borrowed glow and kept only water-hush. 
Mara Venn, its logical page indexer, stayed because the drawers held the only record of the first salvaged books. 
She had come to trace honest origins, not to polish the official story that every rescued page came from the Governor's shelves. 
That story had erased her grandmother as a thief, and Mara trusted colophons, sequences, and ink pressure over civic praise. 
With the pumps dead, the gallery entered a reclaimed stillness that felt attentive rather than abandoned. 
Mara stood tranquilly perceptive among dim stacks, hearing silt settle, hinges breathe, and a bell strain beyond the flooded square. 
Surface light crossed the ceiling in broken coins, laying dappled resolve across her hands and the brass-bound provenance case. 
On the case lay an antique ink stamp, its walnut handle worn dark and its brass foot nicked like a crescent moon. 
The stamp was absent from the inventory, which troubled Mara more than the darkness or the water at the glass doors. 
She had indexed a hundred crescent impressions, all labeled Municipal Provenance, all dated after the sea took the lower streets. 
An old dry-era tool should not have marked new acquisitions, unless someone used it to launder confiscations into ancestral authority. 
Her first explanation was negligence: a frightened clerk borrowed the stamp and forgot the register before the flood drills ended. 
Then the black pad showed one damp corner, and in the reclaimed stillness she noticed the case's clockwork latch open and ticking down. 
So Mara chose the alternative hypothesis her training demanded whenever a neat answer left evidence behind. 
Perhaps the stamp was not for cataloguing, but for covering older marks of ownership with a respectable municipal rectangle. 
She opened the ledger to a pasted scrap from a children's geography, one of the volumes her grandmother was accused of stealing. 
Beneath the municipal rectangle, pencil initials had been pressed nearly flat by the stamp but not erased. 
Tranquilly perceptive, she tilted the scrap until the dents cast shadows: E V, then a school number from North Quay. 
Her grandmother had kept that school register, and the initials matched the hand in Mara's childhood copybook. 
The honest origin was not a governor's shelf but a classroom where children had written their names inside the cover. 
To prove the pattern, Mara needed to readjust the stamp, whose foot swiveled a hair inside its brass collar. 
She tested angles on scrap paper, watching the crescent nick wander across the rectangle's inked border. 
When the nick met a diagonal gauge mark on the collar, a spring-loaded plate clicked open. 
Inside the handle lay an oilcloth roll printed with the true accession list of the first salvaged books. 
Each line named a school, kitchen, chapel, or printer's attic, then the person who carried those pages above the flood. 
Halfway down, her grandmother's initials stood beside three North Quay crates, followed by the word returned, not seized. 
The accusation had been built by reversing a verb, and the Governor's Collection had grown famous on that useful lie. 
Mara felt no triumph, only the steadiness of a puzzle solved because the room had stopped shouting. 
Then a generator coughed beyond the drowned square, and exit lamps flickered toward the return of official noise. 
The curator would arrive with the dawn watch to lock the case; his signature had approved the catalogue revisions. 
Mara did not hide the oilcloth, since concealment would teach the next indexer to distrust an absent record. 
Instead, she copied the list into the public register, matching each owner to its surviving shelf with narrow strokes. 
She inked the antique ink stamp once more, but pressed only its crescent nick beside each correction as a cross-reference. 
A device once used to bury names now guided readers from the false entry to the honest page beneath it. 
At dawn, the curator found the register open, the case unlocked, and Mara calmly entering the last school number. 
He spoke of disorder, then of discipline, but the pumps had resumed and his words could not fill the whole room. 
Between their iron strokes, a reclaimed stillness remained, no longer an absence of power but a place where truth could be heard. 
Mara readjusted the ledger's angle toward the returning light, and her dappled resolve settled over the names she had restored.
