None of the repaired clocks in Port Sorrow agreed, and that was how Iva Renn knew the old fire had not finished speaking.  She was the city's broken-clock tinkerer, working beneath a tilted observatory where scorched gears hung like wasp nests and every customer asked for a reliable hour.  After her precise evacuation timetable sent her brother to a gate that collapsed early, she had tried to befriend uncertainty in planning, leaving her repairs a fraction wrong so people would consider detours.  The council wanted certainty of another kind, because the wells had gone brackish and the councillors meant to seal the old greenhouse district before winter.  Iva kept thinking of the admiral's lost compass rose, a brass wind dial said to guide vanished ships by the breathing of underground rivers beneath the ashes.  By the week when coincidence becomes pattern, she had stopped calling the signs accidents.  First, three clocks salvaged from different houses hiccuped to the same southwest angle at seventeen minutes past four.  Then a soldier's widow brought Iva a water-stained chart with Admiral Orel's last bearing drawn at that crooked angle.  Finally, an ash-gray leaf blew into the shop although every window was shut, landed on the chart, and spun once toward the old garden.  Iva felt uncertainly certain, sure that the signs pointed somewhere and unsure whether her need for water had made a map of desire.  That doubts did not cancel directions was the lesson she had been avoiding since her brother's death.  She packed a coil of wire, a cracked escapement, two screws, and a notebook whose first page read Maybe, then went to the garden before the councillors' clerks could lock its doors.  The glasshouse had been neglected since the fire, but its roof still made weather, dripping condensation on palms that had outlived their labels and feeding a restless litter of leaves.  The only way to trace the draft was through the swirling leaves in a neglected greenhouse, because no surveyor's chain could follow air under broken tiles.  Iva set the loosened vents to the clocks' dying angle, then shook clock-filings across the floor to weight the leaves without stopping them.  The leaves made a slow eight-pointed rose, hesitated, and streamed under a fern bed where Iva found an iron grate pinned by soot.  She pried it with the cracked escapement, and the sound that rose was not a ratchet but water falling a long way underground.  Stairs slick with mineral ice took her beneath the garden, beneath the city, and at last into a basalt chamber lit by foxfire on wet stone.  A subterranean waterfall poured from a split aqueduct into a black basin, filling the air with cold applause and scattering rainbows that vanished before they could be named.  On a pedestal at the basin's heart sat the admiral's lost compass rose, green with damp, its brass points arranged around a needle of blue glass.  Iva understood then that the admiral had not lost it in panic; he had anchored it here so the waterfall could turn the sluices that once fed every public well.  The mechanism was jammed by ash and roots, and a hairline crack in the aqueduct was widening with each pulse of water.  If the chamber flooded, the lower streets would drink mud before dawn; if she carried the compass rose upstairs without opening the sluices, the councillors would seal a working spring as rubble.  Her notebook offered no plan equal to that, so she did what her deliberately imperfect clocks had taught her to do: improvise.  She wired the cracked escapement to the compass rose, used the two screws as a wobbling governor, and packed leaf stems into the root-clogged sleeve to give the old wheel room to hesitate.  The first turn shrieked, the blue needle swung past every marked point, and the waterfall seemed to lean toward her as if curious.  She left the setting one point off the chart's clean bearing, because a plan that admitted surprise had already saved her more often than a perfect one.  The governor chattered, the sluice gates lifted, and water that had been hoarded beneath the ashes hurried into channels under streets she had walked all her life.  Above her, through the grate and through the swirling leaves in a neglected greenhouse, the draft reversed and carried the clean smell of rain into Port Sorrow.  By morning, pumps coughed clear water, the councillors postponed their seals, and people brought their stopped clocks to Iva not for obedience but for counsel.  The compass rose stayed below; above her bench, a card read Welcome, uncertainty.
