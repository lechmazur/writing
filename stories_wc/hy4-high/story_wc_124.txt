As the transformation completes, the greenhouse for extinct plants holds its breath beneath crystal shadows. 
Glass panes split the night into sharp dark facets, and every bed contains a species the world has lost. 
Elias Vale, a wistful lantern broker, has come with a black valise and one unopened crystal perfume vial. 
He trades in lanterns that hold the last light of demolished rooms, orchards, and harbors, pricing nostalgia for clients who prefer it gently used. 
Because the impact of memories is never clean, even a clean flame can bend a stem toward a face no botanist recorded. 
That is why the greenhouse permits only one method at this hour, ultraviolet revealing. 
Visible lanterns feed recollections to the roots, but the violet beams show what memory left on glass, skin, and stopper. 
Elias is aromatically cautious by habit and by terror, wearing a charcoal veil and sealing the stopper of the vial with wax. 
He has spent eleven years trying to understand why some smells trigger tears. 
Other memories can be weighed, catalogued, and sold; a scent goes past the ledger and finds the body first. 
When orange oil or wet cedar reaches him, his eyes fill before his mind supplies a reason. 
He has never learned whether tears are weakness, warning, or a debt being called in. 
He does know that Mara made the vial the week before the fever house burned. 
Mara was the last gardener here, and she had believed extinct flowers survived as habits in the people who had loved them. 
Elias had been away, auctioning a lantern from the drowned winter palace when the smoke crossed the river and Mara died. 
By dawn he possessed her notes, her vial, and the public language of loss, but no private way to grieve. 
He sold the lantern that held the fire's last light because a client offered enough to keep the greenhouse roof repaired. 
Tonight the keeper has summoned him because the last ghostroot is completing its twenty year change. 
Under ordinary light it is a black lattice of absence; under the permitted beams, it writes back. 
When the change is sealed, the roots will either become sterile crystal seed or accept one living memory and open. 
He stands beside the bed while glass shadows gather at his feet like cut pieces of a patient animal. 
The keeper, old Sella, hands him a violet lantern and asks him not to bargain with the dead. 
With ultraviolet revealing, he passes the beam over the crystal perfume vial. 
The glass turns blue, then discloses faint prints where Mara held it, where he refused to hold it, and where someone wiped the neck clean. 
Hidden under the label, a sentence in her slanting hand fluoresces, "Smell is memory with the future removed." 
Elias reads it twice, then reads the smaller line: "Tears return what the future cannot." 
The words do not solve him; they accuse his caution of pretending that preservation equals love. 
He remembers Mara saying that a perfume is a tiny architecture, built to collapse in the nose and rebuild a room. 
He remembers himself answering with prices, because prices were safer than rooms. 
At the bed's center, the ghostroot trembles as the transformation completes, and its shadows sharpen toward a final shape. 
Sella says, "Open the vial now, or the seed will remember nothing but our caution." 
Elias takes out his broker's knife, breaks the wax, and hates the small, tidy sound. 
He lifts the veil only enough to admit one breath. 
The scent is not sweet; it is cut grass under hot dust, Mara's soap, and the mineral chill of glass before rain. 
His eyes answer first, with tears that make the violet light divide into bright crystals. 
Then his mind supplies the room he has avoided: Mara at a bench, sealing the vial, leaving him a way to fall apart after she could not stay. 
He does not convert the pain into value, does not name it rare, does not imagine a buyer. 
He grieves, awkwardly, with his shoulders bent over extinct soil while the last ghostroot drinks the salt. 
The transformation completes into a white flower no catalog has ever pictured, thin as held breath and faceted with crystal shadows. 
He understands at last that smells trigger tears when the past asks the body to finish a motion it began with someone gone. 
The flower opens anyway, not restored, but changed by what he finally allowed memory to do.
