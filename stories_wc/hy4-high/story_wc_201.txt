The surgeons tell Mara that the ultraviolet penance begins after the anesthesia wears off, because only an awake body can be trusted by the archive.  She wakes on a gurney at the edge of the ultraviolet forest with her wrists free and her mouth full of copper light.  The lamps are buried in the canopy, so every leaf gives off a color that seems less seen than remembered.  Before the surgery, she had written displaced rooted as the first coordinate in her notebook, because every equation needed a zero and this was hers.  The Ministry called her an enhanced interrogation survivor, a phrase that fit a filing cabinet better than a woman who still counted doorways before entering them.  To repair what the interrogators had damaged, they made her proprioceptively enhanced, threading silver sensors through the sheaths of her muscles until she could feel the angle of each finger in the dark.  Only the pewter mask strap was original, a dull band taken from the room where the questions had been delivered with electricity and measured pauses.  Now it fastened a filter mask over her nose and eyes, and its old notches pressed their arithmetic into the hinge of her jaw.  The clinicians called their method remembering through trauma responses, since words had been trained out of her and flinches had not.  She had volunteered for this walk not to give the Ministry another name but to discover the mathematics of mercy, if such a thing could survive measurement.  Ultraviolet penance meant crossing a forest of engineered pines whose black bark drank visible light and whose roots rose like cables from violet moss.  The tribunal said the place bleached lies; Mara knew it merely made sweat, breath, and fear visible to instruments hidden under the leaves.  At the first ring of the lamps, the recorded voice of an attendant told her to brace.  Her body obeyed before pride could object, shoulders locking, calves measuring the soft grade, left hand opening toward a door that no longer existed.  The archive recorded that angle, and a seam of blue fire ran down the nearest trunk to mark one recovered second.  Years earlier, under the mask, she had begun counting the spaces between harms, because a count was the only possession the interrogators could not confiscate.  One interval had been water held at her lips, another a strap loosened a single notch, another a guard saying nothing while the current was ordered higher.  None of those moments had saved her, yet each had changed the shape of what followed, and she wanted the exact weight of that change.  In the forest, remembering through trauma responses became a compass: a swallowed breath meant a chair, a tremor in the right foot meant a boot, and the pulse under the strap meant the seventh notch.  Whenever the forest brightened, the same recorded voice said brace, and the pewter warmed against old scars as if metal, too, could be ashamed.  She moved among roots with the displaced rooted certainty of a tree washed downstream and replanted by hands it would never forgive.  Her enhanced sensors named every tilt of moss and every shift of her hips, so the forest became less a place than a diagram of where she had been forced to stand.  At the clearing, a glass lectern waited beneath a pine hung with small lead weights, each one stamped with the number of an interrogation room.  The Ministry wanted her to touch the weight that matched her file, completing the penance by turning one recovered man into a defendant.  A red line pulsed on the lectern, asking for certainty, and the old mask strap tightened by half a degree as her jaw tried to brace again.  The trauma answer came first, a bright command to identify the guard who had held the cup and then vanished from the transcript.  Mara did not brace this time; she let her knees soften until her palms touched moss, adding the ground to the equation.  The forest read the collapse as another response and opened the archive, so she fed it no name, only intervals and ratios.  She entered the pause before water, the loosened notch, the silence of the guard, the number of breaths she had stolen between one order and the next.  Then she subtracted each act of harm from the record that called itself civilization, and the remainder was not innocence but a debt no court could round away.  The first theorem of mercy, she decided, was that the unit of measure had to belong to the person who survived the measurement.  When the ultraviolet penance ended, she unfastened the pewter mask strap, set it around a root, and left with no verdict but a proof warm in her hands.
