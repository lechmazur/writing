I arrived at nautical twilight on the opal dock in mist with a Board order to investigate an eccentric lighthouse keeper named Ansel Rook.  The horizon and first stars shared the sky, and the island felt like witnessed isolation once the launch cast off.  Rook met me with a copper funnel over one ear and asked whether I had watched the dark between the third and fourth flash.  I had watched the flash, so he sighed, led me into the lantern room, and opened a child's copybook of borrowed memories.  At that hour, he said, the Fresnel prisms borrowed the last clear sights of people who had looked toward the light while the sea took them.  They appeared only in the gaps between flashes, were softly persistent, and had to be entered in the log as names, gestures, or three small taps.  Rook had learned to investigate by observing gaps in the lamp, wreckage, testimony, and silence.  The habit began as penance: as a young keeper he had written "signal indistinct" when his brother waved from an overturned dory.  The boy survived until dawn in a pocket of air, and Rook had spent decades answering gestures that arrived too late.  A scrape on the seaward stair brought a stranger in a soaked wedding coat through the fog.  He wore a dried Edelweiss boutonniere with its yellow center torn away, and his pockets held only a ticket ending in rose.  Before he died, he touched the flower, made a cradle of both hands, and opened them toward a wedge of shore the lamp never reached.  Rook said his reason was to decode a stranger's final gesture because an addressed gesture made the lighthouse a witness.  I reached for the radiogram, but salt had killed it, and the tide was already lifting weed along the pilings.  Rook covered the body, gave me the copybook, and began to investigate by observing gaps.  He noted the flower's missing heart, the ticket's blank, the ring's pale band, and the lamp's long gap.  That gap aimed at the same unlit wedge, a notch no chart called safe.  He told me to watch absence; snow light filled the long gap.  A young woman pinned that same dried Edelweiss boutonniere to his coat on a chapel step.  The borrowed memories shifted to a wet deck: ALPENROSE, a lifebelt, the woman's cradled hands.  She pointed beneath a tarpaulin and toward the dark wedge, repeating the sign with softly persistent calm.  A final sight showed a skiff pushed from the sinking packet, its tarpaulin hiding the hollow where a child had lain.  Rook read the gesture plainly: a child had been placed in the one gap where the coast would not be seen.  The gaps agreed: ticket, flower, prism, and coast all wanted what the light had omitted.  He did not look triumphant; he looked like a man correcting a line written years too late.  We took a rope and my lantern through the trapdoor beneath the opal dock, where mist swallowed all but the blind notch.  The tide breathed around the piles, and a softly persistent tapping came from an overturned skiff under the third crossbeam.  Three taps, a pause, three taps again, exactly the count the borrowed memories had kept returning.  Rook wedged into the gap while I held the rope, and his boots vanished among wet opal stones.  He passed out a furious girl of four, clutching the missing yellow heart.  Under the tarpaulin lay the mother, beyond help, with one arm curved around the hollow where the child had been.  The stranger's final gesture had asked neither for burial nor revenge, only for attention to what the light taught us to ignore.  We wrapped the girl in Rook's coats, fed her warm water, and waited until the radiogram crackled into speech.  By morning the Coast Guard had names from the packet: Emil, Liese, and Katia Voss.  Rook entered them not as visions, but as borrowed memories returned to the living as far as his station could manage.  He crossed out "signal indistinct" and wrote his brother's name beneath it, which made his hands shake more than the rescue had.  I had come to judge an isolated man, and the fog had made me witness to the opposite verdict.  My report said fit, though necessary was truer, because someone had to watch where light failed.  When the relief boat came, I stayed to help Katia, while Rook, softly persistent, kept asking the gaps for no more than they could give.
