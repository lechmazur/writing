Some afternoons begin within the fold of a daydream, and Mara Venn, a vivid mural artist, paints with light because pigment sulks in fog.  The place is the ruined orchard district lost in centuries of thick fog, where apples hang as ideas and walls lean in.  She has come to rediscover lost joy, not to rescue property, although the fog has been stealing roof tiles for ages.  Her colors grew polite after her brother Ivo stopped answering letters.  In her coat pocket rests a fresco fragment no larger than a biscuit, showing one painted eye, a blue sleeve, and the corner of a grin.  It came from a bathhouse wall built for the Laughing Harvest.  On its back, under mineral crust, someone had scratched the words exposed film and an arrow toward dawn.  Old archivists said centuries of fog behaved like exposed film, holding vanished gleams until careful light developed them.  A blunt blaze would blacken the archive, which was why the sun was treated as an opinionated guest.  Mara's method was a knowledge network of librarians, beekeepers, projectionists, and children with soup spoons.  The network answered her sketch of the fragment with three facts.  First, the blue in the sleeve was ground lapis that woke only at dawn.  Second, the fragment had to sit where a plaster mask once bent lamplight.  Third, a restored image needed witnesses, because a display kept in one head turns into hearsay.  Mara liked the last fact best, though it made her ache, because Ivo had been her favorite witness.  So she dragged mirrors, prisms, and a brass kettle up the broken orchard road.  The kettle was for tea and for making the fog feel included.  She fits the fresco fragment into a cracked wall and waits for the daydream to soften.  A row of pear stones forms a committee, and Mara nods because committees rarely require answers.  When the first pale ray finds the blue sleeve, the painted eye opens like a sleeper caught snoring.  Mara laughs, then wipes her eyes with a thumb smelling of cobalt and dust.  She paints with light by tilting mirrors until dawn passes through prisms and the fragment into mist.  The beams persuade the fog, and the archivists were right.  Silver shapes rise from hidden emulsion: ladders, baskets, a fiddle, and a woman with elbows in a pie.  She winks at Mara, and several apples blush with grave dignity.  Mirth arrives quietly, carrying old sadness by the hand, like a casserole after a funeral.  Mara sees Ivo then, not younger or repaired, but exactly as he was when he stole red plums.  He stands at the bathhouse edge with a lantern, waiting for her to display what the fog kept.  Her first impulse is to demand an explanation, which seems rude even in a daydream.  Instead, she turns the largest mirror toward the road, where the knowledge network gathers.  Librarians hold lanterns, beekeepers hold jars, and spoon children pretend not to be worried.  The projectionist adjusts the kettle until steam softens the projection.  No one asks whether the scene is true, and this kindness lets it become true.  Now Mara understands that the fragment was never a relic but a lens.  To rediscover lost joy, she must let it pass through her hands and take on other faces.  She widens the final prism until the mural spills over ruined walls and makes a display of the whole district.  The image includes broken branches, empty chairs, and fog, because joy edited clean would be another fog.  Ivo lifts his lantern, not in farewell, but in the old signal for more light.  Mara laughs at the pie woman, and the sound is rusty, then easy.  Pear stones adjourn, apples lose composure, and the fog turns pink.  By the time the daydream folds, townspeople carry baked light home in their pockets.  She leaves the fresco fragment on the step for the next curious hand.  The knowledge network argues about soup spoons, and its fussing sounds like music.  She has not recovered Ivo, repaired the orchard, or found a joy that will sit still.  She has remembered why she became a vivid mural artist: brightness must answer to whoever enters.  Within the fold of a daydream, that is enough, and even the fog smiles at the joke.
