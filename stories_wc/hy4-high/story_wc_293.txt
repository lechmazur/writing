Only after the hitchhiker vanishes does the mountain scenic pullout hold one brightness around the paper tube he left behind.  Ione Vale, the bridge-song fiddler, sees brightness steadied by altitude, not flame, and refuses to call it sunset.  Deputy Mara Sill grips the guardrail and asks whether the man fell, but the snow below is unbroken.  Ione answers by lifting a wet-plate collodion glass flake from the split tube on the asphalt, thin as onion skin and edged with silver.  Under noon glare it shows a face broken into the squares of pixelated amnesty, an old mercy for erased names.  The hitchhiker had arrived with a paper tube, polite hunger, and an odd request to recount him before the light failed.  Mara had laughed until he stepped behind the geological map, tapped three bolts, dropped the tube, and became glare in the wall seam.  Ione knows that wall because her fiddle accompanies the scaffold crew repairing the closed bridge below, where songs carry better than shouts.  She is dynamically observant, not merely watchful, reading gust, pitch, shadow, and the hesitations of frightened people.  That habit began after the bridge fire, when the county stamped "never" on claims for workers lost in the gorge.  Her brother Tovin was among those names, so she worked not for music but to sculpt possibility from the ashes of "never".  The ashes were real, for burned petitions had been swept into the mortar of this scenic wall.  From them, she believed, a stubborn hand might shape a door where officials had drawn a period.  Now the flake in her palm gives that old refusal grammar, because each cracked square aligns with a surveyed bridge point.  Pixelated amnesty had been no miracle to its inventors, only a court of lenses, damaged plates, sworn fragments, and a vow that a partly seen person could still be pardoned.  The court was sealed when the highway made the mountain scenic pullout a place for photographs rather than testimony.  Ione asks Mara to recount the vanished minute, and the deputy obeys, embarrassed by the detail that matters.  Three bolt taps, two heartbeats, one flinch at a green panel, then a pause before he said his grandfather hid the key in wind.  Above, scaffold wrap covers the repair frame in green strips knotted by the original surveyors' children.  Her method is a secret mapped by scaffold wrap flutters, a moving chart no static diagram could preserve.  Ione sets bow to strings and plays the bridge-song in short questions, matching each answer the fabric snaps from cliff wind.  That same attention notices not the loudest flap but the delayed shiver at the panel Mara remembered.  That shiver repeats three times, then twice, then once, turning Mara's account into coordinates for a hatch under the map.  Mara pries the map loose with her tire iron, and cool air rises from a stair forgotten on purpose.  Inside, a projector waits beside a brass seal, a voice tube, and shelves of unlabeled negatives.  The instruction plate says a petitioner must bring one image, one witness, and one official who will recount the appeal aloud.  Ione slides the wet-plate collodion glass flake into the holder, and the cracked face opens across the wall like dawn.  Mara leans to the voice tube; to recount the appeal, she repeats the request, the taps, the flinch, and the unbroken snow.  Her words are dry, but Ione hears luminous disbelief, the tone of a locked drawer remembering hinges.  Outside, flutters quicken; through vents, wind turns green strips into a shutters' rhythm for the bridge-song.  Ione cranks and plays; the flake's squares scatter, settle, and admit the missing crew as patches of names.  Tovin appears, not young or accused, but restored to the column where "never" had been.  The brass seal stamps a paper strip, pixel by pixel, until county grammar has no place for refusal.  Then the hitchhiker steps from the service shadow, dusty, smaller than terror had made him, wholly ordinary.  He says his grandfather kept the court ready for descendants needing a witness, not resurrection, and Ione lets the strip glow between them.
