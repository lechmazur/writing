In the hour after the time traveler arrives, every stair in the labyrinthine bookstore occupying an entire mountain changes grammar. 
I am its arcane linguist, hired to read door hinges, mold on spines, and the contracts dust makes with memory. 
The bookstore has no silence, only an unheard symphony of pages flexing under stone, vines tightening by measure, and readers holding their breath. 
On the evening the traveler arrives with snow from next winter on her coat, that music stops on one note. 
She carries a receipt dated a century ahead, and its ink says the mountain will foreclose at dawn on all lessons people refused. 
When that happens, the lost readers trapped in the library of unlearned lessons will be reshelved as warnings instead of people. 
The Keeper immediately organizes rescue missions through the low tunnels, but the old charters allow one body or one lesson, never both. 
I have waited eleven years to find the library of unlearned lessons, because my brother Tomas vanished there during the first rescue mission I led. 
I had refused his advice to turn back, convinced that a correct map should never be questioned, and the corridor taught us otherwise. 
Since then I have been carefully resourceful, because the mountain charges for waste: a spoken vowel can start an avalanche of dictionaries. 
So I keep lamp oil in a thimble, ink in a hollow tooth, and questions folded until they are needed. 
The only object I do not ration is Tomas's botanical diary, a thin book of pressed ferns, seed counts, and notes from terraces no customer sees. 
He believed plants grew toward catalog omissions, and he recorded each blank day as carefully as a blooming. 
I begin by researching gaps in shelf numbers, failed rescue reports, and the diary's unsigned days, since an arcane linguist knows absences conjugate. 
The pattern is not a route but a sentence: every omission follows the words we never said after someone went missing. 
Old rescue missions failed because we dragged survivors out and left their refusals behind, and the mountain, being literal, reclaimed them. 
By comparing those reports with the gaps, I discover that the entry for the library was deliberately blank, not lost. 
That distinction matters, because the future receipt contains a clause allowing an injured party to renegotiate any contract with an intentional omission. 
The time traveler offers to witness, provided I bring back the lesson that made her century barren. 
I agree, not for history, but because Tomas's last diary page says that pride is a map with no exit. 
We set out on four rescue missions moving to one cadence, with felt boots, covered lamps, and no spoken orders. 
In the root floors, the diary's ferns point left. 
At each turn I spend exactly one item: a thimble of light, a tooth of ink, a pressed leaf laid on a blank shelf. 
The spent things answer with doorways, because the mountain respects economy more than courage. 
At the last gap, where every catalog stops at the word apology, I find Tomas standing between two shelves, thin as an unopened page. 
He does not embrace me, because sound would finish the foreclosure; he only writes one line in the dust: you were wrong early enough. 
I answer with the same restraint, tracing a sentence into the blank catalog so the clause can be heard without a voice. 
With the time traveler's receipt as witness and the botanical diary as evidence, I renegotiate the oldest bargain from one body or one lesson into one body through one lesson. 
The grammar accepts it, because the mountain has always loved prepositions more than prizes, and the shelves loosen like hands at the end of a prayer. 
Every trapped reader takes the lesson each had skipped: a merchant accepts waiting, a pilot accepts doubt, a child accepts that crying is not betrayal. 
Tomas takes my old confession, and I take his, which is simply that a map should be a question with ink. 
The library of unlearned lessons opens into the bookstore, no longer hidden, and its first new shelf is labeled Return. 
The time traveler folds her receipt; in her century, fields of unripe paper rustle into letters that teach people to ask who is missing. 
The rescue missions come back up through the mountain in an unheard symphony of altered contracts, soft wheels, and people carrying lessons they can finally bear. 
I walk last with the botanical diary under my arm, carefully resourceful still, but no longer careful about admitting I need another reader.
