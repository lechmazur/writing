The morning fog saxophonist returned to the radiant grotto in mist with his horn wrapped in black wool. 
No one had seen him since the winter his mother made him practice scales on river steps. 
He had been famous for arriving before sunrise, when ferry lamps blurred and every window held a pale, listening face. 
Now he came back older, carrying a reed case, a tarnished key, and the posture of a man entering childhood. 
At its center waited the prism atrium in mist, a faceted glass chamber with a low door that his tarnished key still opened. 
Dawn entered the chamber one color at a time; as dewdrops form, glass seams become listening mouths. 
He had come to hush a prophecy spoken in code before those droplets finished rounding. 
The code was older than the charter, a lattice of colored flashes, intervals, and rests that named whom to fear. 
When it spoke, people remembered the future and arranged their lives to make it inevitable. 
His mother had called that obedience the saddest kind of homesickness. 
The elders called her countermeasure a vested hush, a right to withhold one sentence, deeded, sworn, and worn like black cloth. 
As a boy, he thought the phrase pompous and ran to ballrooms where applause covered every rest. 
His mother did not follow him, though her letters arrived each spring with one line of music and no salutation. 
When she died, the horn she left him carried a brass plate engraved with a single word: enough. 
For years, enough sounded like a door closed on every bright city. 
Only that morning, reading her last letter, had he understood the grammar of omission she had taught him. 
The score showed the prophecy without decisive notes; empty bars were locks, not mistakes. 
He had learned that the only way to hush that lattice was through negative space. 
Playing missing notes would complete the sentence; leaving them empty starved its sequence, cause, and name. 
Now the light was quietly vivid on calcite shelves, old ribbons, and ghosted outlines of children awaiting dawn concerts. 
He remembered her hand on his collar, wet wool, and the shameful thrill of being chosen to keep quiet. 
Behind him, the town gathered at the cave mouth, barefoot and silent, mourning a day not yet born. 
They had come because the first dewdrop had opened a blue seam, and the code had begun clicking. 
A child's voice, borrowed from no visible child, recited numbers. 
Each number tightened faces, waking private futures they dreaded remembering. 
He lifted the horn, refusing the sunrise flourish that had made hotel orchestras love him. 
He listened first, as his mother had taught him, for the scrape of a chair after the last note. 
He set the reed to his lip and let one breath whisper through the horn, faint as mist inhaling. 
He played around the coded flashes, refused their crowning notes, and filled the atrium with disciplined absence. 
Every rest had a shape, pausing where the glass expected a verb, number, or beloved name. 
The vested hush settled, not as heaviness but as legal tenderness, a roof built from words no one dared say. 
Blue light rose in the prism atrium in mist, searched for instruction, and found an omitted chord. 
The child voice stammered into a scale, forgot grammar, and dwindled to water ticking on stone. 
In the crowd, shoulders loosened, and people wept at returning from a place never visited. 
He saw his mother then, or a lamplit fold of fog, standing where she had marked time with two fingers. 
She did not bless or forgive him; she waited inside the final rest, quietly vivid as a pressed flower. 
He held the rest until dew trembled on every rim of the radiant grotto in mist, leaving the prophecy nowhere to land. 
For the first time, silence did not feel like losing her; it felt like leaving the future unaddressed.
