Reverend Abel Marsh entered the antique music box repair shop as thunder folded over the river. 
Vibrant hush filled the narrow room, where a bell under red felt, brass birds, and painted dials held color without sound. 
The penitentiary chaplain carried a rosewood box wrapped in a prison blanket, and rain threaded his black cuffs. 
To turn an apology into action, he had refused a parish funeral and taken the first ferry. 
Two nights earlier, Elias Rook had died in the infirmary with Abel holding his spotted hand. 
Rook had denied setting the Calvert Mill fire, though the court needed only one confession and one stopped clock. 
The prison physician called his tremors contrivance, and Abel, ashamed now, had accepted that first opinion. 
A charity surgeon later named the tumor that killed him, but the word arrived too late. 
Second opinions had become Abel's creed after that, because they made room for mercy to move. 
Rook's apology was not for a crime; it was for leaving his daughter Nell a ruined name. 
He pressed the music box into Abel's hands and whispered that the clockwork heart still knew nine seventeen. 
Abel wanted to deserve such trust, not by praying over it, but by carrying it past the prison gate. 

Nell Vale stood behind the bench with a jeweler's lens pushed into her hair. 
Aptly deliberate, Nell unwrapped the rosewood box, each gesture brief, exact, and impossible to hurry. 
She recognized her father's file marks before she read the note beneath the lid. 
The paper said, "Nell, forgive the silence, and then watch the second song." 
Abel had read it so often that forgiveness had begun to feel like another way of doing nothing. 
He set beside it the physician's note, the surgeon's report, and the fire marshal's typed certainty. 
Nell looked at the little archive and said, "So you brought second opinions this time." 
Abel nodded, because the hurt was cleaner than praise. 

Beneath the lid, a brass clockwork heart lay bent among pinned cylinders. 
One gear had cracked where the mainspring coiled, and a shard darkened by varnish had lodged in the escapement. 
Nell lifted it with tweezers, and tiny valves opened like ribs under a surgeon's hand. 
"This was never made to play a lullaby," she said; "it was made to give a second opinion." 
Second opinions, in a prison workshop, could hide only inside music. 
Nell cleaned the gears, replaced the missing tooth, and refused every shortcut Abel's impatience suggested. 
The storm thickened, then loosened over the skylight, leaving a gold gray hush. 
In the calm before realization, Abel held his breath while Nell wound the clockwork heart. 
It ticked, turned the cylinder past the familiar tune, and exposed a ragged row of absent pins. 
By the way light breaks through storm clouds, Nell read the scratches that lamps flattened into nonsense. 
A broken shaft of sun split on the heart's shutters and scattered bright bars across the bench. 
Where the bars touched the cylinder, punch marks rose as shadows, spelling a valve code and a time, eight forty two. 
Rook had hidden the mill engineer's notation in the pauses of the second song. 
Nell found the matching carbon copy in her father's toolbox, signed by Calvert himself. 
It recorded a blocked safety valve thirty five minutes before the official clock stopped at nine seventeen. 
The confession claimed Rook entered after the alarm; the notation placed him at the pump house before dawn, reporting the fault. 
Abel felt no triumph, only the opening of a door he should have tried years earlier. 
The clockwork heart beat on with the steady embarrassment of a fact that had waited to be heard. 

Nell understood that her father's apology had been an instruction, not a plea. 
She asked, "What do we do before the courthouse closes?" 
He named the charity surgeon, a retired fire investigator, and a judge who once demanded second opinions from frightened clerks. 
Each name was a repair, and together they made a path from the shop to the street. 
Deserve, Abel thought, belonged to the living as a task, not to the dead as a gift. 
Nell packed the carbon copy, the reports, and the clockwork heart inside the music box. 
They stepped into rain while light broke through storm clouds over the roofs. 
Abel carried the box carefully, hearing its small heart answer, and the apology moved at last.
