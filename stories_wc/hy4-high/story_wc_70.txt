After the city-wide blackout, the lightning collection tower stood unlit at nectar dusk, its copper ribs sticky with rain and clover pollen. 
Mara Venn, the candid toll keeper, kept weighing climbers because someone still had to carry an honest record. 
A lightning backfeed had left her involuntarily truthful, so polite evasions snagged in her throat until facts came out. 
The Council trusted her ledger and feared her mouth. 
Before the blackout, she had made routine bearable with copper tokens, boot counts, and small ceremonies. 
To preserve wonder in routine, her method was a matchbox containing a cryptic riddle, returned with each receipt. 
Answers were old cabinet passwords, cloud names, or prompts to notice one beautiful thing before sunset. 
Officials called the catastrophe a drone beehive failure, a tidy phrase for the night the rooftop pollinator swarms lost their dance. 
Ten thousand brass bees left their hex-cell racks, crossed flooded gardens, and buried themselves in the tower's grounding rings. 
Stored lightning reversed through the city, and nine minutes of black windows followed. 
The Council blamed weather rot and planned to scrap the hives, rods, and Mara's window together. 
Mara remembered that Director Senn had crossed that evening with a lacquer box humming like a trapped wasp. 
He called it empty; her ledger said, Empty according to Senn, humming according to my ears. 
In the seventh week after the blackout, Senn returned at nectar dusk with the same lacquer box and a clean report. 
He asked her to certify no unlisted cargo reached the tower before the drone beehive failure. 
He also wanted the matchboxes retired, since recovery had no patience for riddles. 
Mara could not say yes; she said the box had hummed and the report was false. 
Senn set the lacquer box beside her ledger and described a morning reveal that would honor the dead and close the tower. 
The word reveal tightened her chest, because the ceremony would display an answer shaped to prevent questions. 
Her voice needed proof to reveal the lie. 
Senn left her under guard with one lamp and orders to seal the ledger by dawn. 
Mara took the last matchbox from her drawer, one Senn had returned unopened with a frown and no coin. 
Inside, beside the usual cryptic riddle, her pencil had drawn six cells and an amber bell. 
The words told the reader to count six cells past the bell and ask the unpaid queen what crossed. 
At the time it had seemed a harmless puzzle about tolls, bees, and the queen relay that gave rooftop hives their orders. 
Tonight it pointed to the old storm cabinet, sixth cell from the amber bell, where keepers once locked charged honey. 
The guard cared about ledgers, not errands, so Mara fetched washing water and carried the matchbox up the spiral stair. 
At the cabinet she pressed it to the lock, not as a key but as a reminder of the answer. 
A loose honeycomb panel yielded, and beneath it lay a black command wafer stamped with Senn's seal. 
Its record showed he had ordered the drone hives to perform a light dance over his rooftop banquet. 
The queen relay misread the dance as a swarming call and led the bees into the grounding rings. 
Mara understood that wonder without truth was only decoration, sweet enough to mask a wound. 
She fitted the wafer into a hidden socket inside the lacquer box, and blue script woke beneath its lid. 
She carried it to the notice lamp, a lens that threw records across the tower wall. 
Before dawn she wrote one new riddle on every matchbox, asking which was heavier, a sealed report or six strokes from an open bell. 
When the crowd gathered for Senn's reveal, she handed the boxes out as ordinary tolls. 
Children solved it first, because adults were still applauding. 
They rang the amber bell six times, and Mara touched the lacquer box to the lamp. 
Blue script climbed the tower wall, showing time, command, signature, and the dancing bees Senn had denied. 
Senn reached for the guard, but Mara's clear voice named the order, the banquet, and the unpaid crossing. 
Because she was involuntarily truthful, no one could mistake courage for theater. 
The Council kept the tower as a witnessed machine, and wiser hives drew gold lines through nectar dusk. 
Mara kept collecting tokens, but every riddle now asked what we owe the lights. 
Her routine had not lost its wonder; it had learned to keep wonder honest.
