At the last bell, Sera sat chained to the counter of the abandoned carnival prize redemption center, listening to the wall outside settle in its sleep. 
The Authority had raised the cinderblock wall to seal off the parched districts, and by sunrise its crews would tear it down for a patrol road; when the wall comes down, they would also uncover the hidden loading bay beneath the redemption floor. 
Only Sera knew that the bay hid a clandestine seed vault, founded by carnival women who had packed millet, beans, and melon seed into glazed prize boxes. 
The guards called her lesson bait and locked her ankle to a brass spindle, but the districts called her the chained liberator because every camp she left had open gates. 
Patiently strategic, she had counted boot steps for seven weeks, saved a cotter pin in her hem, and taught the youngest guard to believe her shoulders were broken. 
After his lantern passed the popcorn roof, she freed herself, wrapped the loose chain around her wrist, and pulled a handcart from behind the counter. 
She had not come for treasure or revenge, but to plant resilience in dry soil beyond the wall. 
The vault held hard seeds that rooted in cracked clay, set pods in furnace wind, and survived a month without rain. 
Her mother had left her an arrow with strange fletching as a receipt, its vanes cut from millet husk, blue bottle glass, and a strip of hymn paper. 
The arrow's point fit the locked hasp of the piano bench beneath a faded poster for Sing a Key, the old redemption game. 
Inside lay booth keys, cabinet keys, and brass tags, arranged on faded felt like the notes of an arrested tune. 
Only by decoding the arrangement of keys left in a piano bench could she find switches to impede the demolition crew and wake the vault doors. 
The strange fletching supplied the grammar: husk meant pause, glass meant reverse, and hymn paper meant count the teeth before choosing the next row. 
She laid the keys in order, humming her mother's winners' song, until the pattern became gate numbers, bolt weights, and buried levers. 
Outside, an engine coughed, and the wall gave a dry crack that traveled through the floor more felt than heard. 
She threaded three keys into the service panel in order, and the old carnival rails answered under the counters. 
Prize chutes lowered, coin gates slid sideways, and the bronze track once used for plush bears locked across the loading bay entrance. 
It would not stop the crews forever, but it would impede them long enough for one chained woman and a handcart. 
The final key opened the floor beneath Ring Toss, revealing a narrow stair and a cold breath that smelled of paper, oil, and saved summers. 
Sera descended with the arrow between her teeth, the handcart bumping behind her, and the chain gathered in her palm, each link knocking like a bead on a rosary made for hungry people. 
The clandestine seed vault was a white tiled room with numbered drawers, a hand crank, and a skylight painted black. 
On the wall, her mother had chalked a sentence: A harvest is a promise made to people you will never meet. 
Sera touched the chalk, loaded the driest packets into the cart, and left the drawers open as proof that hiding time was over. 
At that moment the wall came down, not in a blast but in a long surrender of dust and morning. 
The redemption center staggered, empty hooks trembled, and Sera should have fallen; instead the chain caught a rail, the cart caught her hip, and the three collisions placed her upright in staggering grace. 
She climbed into daylight pulling seed drawers wrapped in her coat, and the soldiers saw only a shackled woman, a canceled carnival, and a ridiculous arrow. 
That was why they hesitated, and hesitation was the last gate her mother had built for her. 
Sera walked through the fallen wall toward fields where dry soil lay open in furrows like pages waiting for a difficult, necessary script. 
Ahead, people from the parched districts ran with buckets, shawls, and jars, not to take seeds but to receive them. 
Sera dropped the vane of hymn paper into the first furrow, then pressed millet, beans, and melon seed into cracked earth with her chained hands. 
Each movement looked like stumbling, yet each stumble landed where roots would be, and the crowd followed with staggering grace that made the broken wall seem briefly unnecessary.
