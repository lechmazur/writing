In the withered orchard deep in a subarctic crater, Mira, the last flash-paper alchemist, kneels while the world holds its breath.  The old almanac names this interval moving stillness: no snow falls, no fox calls, no branch creaks, yet sap ticks under black bark and every buried heart sends a small tremor through the iron soil.  Mira has come to translate heartbeats into maps, because a surveying party vanished three days ago and compasses here point toward the crater's cold center instead of toward the living.  Among the missing is Anni, the schoolkeeper's daughter, who once showed Mira a storybook illustration plate etched with a child crossing an orchard by following a lamp.  Mira carries that plate now, copper worn soft at the edges, not for the picture but for the ruled scratches hidden beneath it, a grid older than the failed settlement.  In her lead casket lies safekept starlight, a grain of blue fire gathered before ash winter covered the sun; the village meant to keep it untouched, but a map that can save Anni requires light that has never warmed anything.  Her method is creating patterns with discarded items, and the orchard is full of them: split labels from seed tins, bent nails, a cracked cup, strips of medicinal foil, boot lacings, and the lids of jars whose fruit never ripened.  She sets each piece at the root of a different tree, weighting it with snow until it rests in the exact direction of the faintest tremor; by dawn, if she reads the pattern rightly, the tremors will become distances.  She demonstrates patience physically by keeping one palm flat above a horseshoe nail for ninety-nine breaths, never letting her fingers answer the itch of cold, because a twitch would teach the shadows the wrong north.  Around her, the trees stand like extinguished antlers, but their roots carry the surveying party's pulses: one slow, two rapid, one so thin that she must press her ear to a trunk dark with frost to know it is a child's.  Flash paper waits beside the casket, thin as onion skin and eager to become a white absence if any common flame reaches it, which is why Mira has spent her life learning how to hold fire still.  The crater's winter has made her famous for sudden illumination, yet every brilliant sheet she ever burned taught her the same rebuke: haste records only itself.  She opens the casket only when the child's pulse pauses between beats, and the safekept starlight slips out without heat, laying blue shadows through the arranged refuse and across the hidden grid of the plate.  The shadows bend toward a shallow drift beyond the northern trees, and at once Mira understands that Anni is not wandering but sheltering under a collapsed ice cellar where the old orchard maps end.  Before she can lift the plate to the waiting sheet, a seam of returning wind stitches through the crater, the world begins to inhale, and the blue shadows tremble as if the whole map might be erased.  Mira has one choice that is also an art: to seal the storybook illustration plate against the flash paper now, using the casket lid as a press, and spend the last safekept starlight to fix the route before the wind scatters it.  She demonstrates patience physically a second time by holding the lid motionless while the gust drags at her sleeves, by keeping her ear against the trunk through Anni's next heartbeat, and by pressing only when the pulse turns toward the ice cellar.  The flash paper does not burn; it accepts, turning one brief, cold blaze into a dark line, then another, then a braid of lines that rises from the plate like a path remembering feet.  Mira seals the finished map under a wafer of clear ash, and when the rescuers arrive with their lanterns hooded, she gives them something that will vanish after one reading, so they must trust it together and move without argument.  They find Anni inside the cellar with two adults, all three hearts beating inside the small blue hush left when Mira spent the casket's starlight.  While the world exhales and snow starts again, Mira remains kneeling among the discarded scraps, watching the empty casket, the dull plate, and her own steady hands, and she feels no loss in the moving stillness, only a map continuing in other people's steps.
