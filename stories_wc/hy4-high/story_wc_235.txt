You would think that after seeing your own grave, a person would stop making plans; I made an index. 
Three thaw nights had uncovered the stone above the mountain ranger cabin, dated next spring. 
As the district's lucid dream chronicler, I recorded what sleepers steered when altitude made private dreams share a weather. 
The job required jaded wonder: swear at the ink, file the halo, keep looking up. 
We did it because to distill chaos into narrative was rescue, not art, above ten thousand feet. 
A clean sequence told wardens which climber had dreamed the cornice falling before it fell. 
The station kept those sequences on a birch bark scroll, since ordinary paper mildewed under dream frost. 
Since the grave, I had been chronologically confused, tasting supper before breakfast and answering condolences before condolences existed. 
On the night I returned, the scroll kept flipping to entries I had not written yet. 
Four rangers slept below, caught in one dream of a kitchen none had known. 
Their breathing had the soft greed of people welcomed home by a house intending to eat them. 
Old manuals called the visitation feral nostalgia: longing gone wild, a pack of memories hunting warmer selves. 
It came with thaw scents, bread and sap and a mother's coat, and followed the brightest version of each sleeper. 
A gentle memory ached; feral nostalgia chewed through the present to reach it. 
Our defense was humiliating, filed under Method: getting jealous of past selves. 
Envy made those former fools shine, and the appetite chased them instead of the breathing body. 
I had taught the trick to tourists, but never used it with a grave already waiting. 
My past selves arrived when the lamp guttered, each carrying a page from the scroll. 
One had frost in her braid and still believed rescue meant saving every voice. 
One had married a cartographer who thought maps could forgive cowardice. 
One was eleven, certain the mountain had adopted her, and stupid enough to be kind. 
They looked at my notebook and then at me with the pity of healthy animals. 
I hated them so quickly the cabin warmed. 
Jealousy lit them like doors into summer, and scratching began under the floor. 
Feral nostalgia took the bait, but my confusion had tangled it with next spring. 
It hunted the girl, the cartographer's wife, the survivor, and the corpse carved into slate. 
The kitchen dream stretched through every season I had ever misplaced. 
I tied the scroll to my wrist, sat among the sleepers, and entered as a chronicler enters a burning archive. 
Inside, four stoves burned, eight doors sweated, and a table fed a family of changing ages. 
My past selves shone at the windows while steam with antlers nosed their reflections. 
The rangers spooned soup from bowls refilled with whatever they most regretted leaving. 
Their names were sliding off the birch bark scroll, and a lost name makes the body forget to turn. 
I wanted to distill chaos into narrative again, not to outwit my stone, but because four people were drowning. 
I began with hunger, older than time and bored with calendars. 
I wrote that each bowl came from love gone wrong, each door from a season survived, and the steam was memory refusing a sentence. 
My past selves turned from the windows, offended into motion, and the steam followed their light. 
I opened the front door and found the underside of my own grave. 
In that shared dream I was lucid enough to be rude to gravity, so I told us to soar. 
The birch bark scroll unfurled; the table rose with its sleepers, and we soared through stone into air full of unclaimed dates. 
Below, feral nostalgia circled shining selves, not a monster but an appetite no one had given a story. 
I read the scroll aloud, giving each ranger a plain beginning and an end they could wake into. 
The chronologically confused entries stopped squirming because I had stopped asking time to behave like a clerk. 
One by one the rangers coughed, apologized to their soup, and vanished toward their blankets. 
My grave hung below like punctuation, neither promise nor mistake, merely where one version had stopped. 
Dawn found me at the cabin table, frost on my sleeves, the scroll covered with a clear account. 
The stone still carried next spring's date, because narrative can leash chaos without repealing weather. 
I added one last line: she came back, chose the order, and kept her jaded wonder.
