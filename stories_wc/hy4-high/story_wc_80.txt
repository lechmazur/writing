After the drawbridge rises, Bellwater no longer pretends that the tide is merely weather. 
Its two iron leaves stand open above the canal, and the raised road leaves a black gap no late cart can cross. 
The bridge will not come down until dawn, when the council's men arrive to certify the basin for burial under a flood wall. 
Until then the old basin is held at its highest lawful water, a cold green room where the submerged pavilion rises to the spring of its arches. 
A hushed blaze of storm lamps burns along the parapet, reflected in the water without a sound. 
Mara Vell works from a tarred punt under that light, one gloved hand on a lead line, the other on the cold rail of the west pier. 
The council had hired Mara Vell, a firm shore surveyor known for condemning wharves without blinking, to measure the footings and sign the certificate. 
She was stoically sensitive, not untouched: she kept her jaw level while every loosened tile struck some private chord. 
To become the archeologist of abandoned dreams, she had accepted this commission after finding her mother's name scratched from the opening program of the pavilion. 
Her mother had carved the stone songbirds above the north arch, yet the council's clean archive listed only a mason paid for rubble. 
At Mara's belt, in a wool sleeve, rode her mother's antique traveling inkwell, its brass cap sealed with pitch and its black sediment still usable when thinned with lamp spirit. 
She had no speech prepared for the men who would come at dawn, only ink, lime, waxed paper, and the patience of someone who had watched too many demolitions begin with tidy words. 
The official plan called the ruins storm damage, but Mara had spent years learning the shape of interlaced absence, the measured gaps where a promised stair, a nursery, a public supper, or a named mason had been deliberately left out. 
Here those gaps braided through the pavilion like another set of walls, leading from the broken west pier to the sealed central vault. 
Where stone survived, it spoke of subscription dinners, children's concerts, and a winter garden meant to stay open to dockhands. 
Where stone did not survive, the omissions answered back, and Mara copied both halves into her level book. 
Placing notes in foundation repairs was how she answered the council without arguing in a room where she would lose. 
By placing notes in foundation repairs, she made each patch of hydraulic lime a dated witness. 
She wrote on oiled paper with the inkwell, naming the missing stair, the vanished mason, the canceled concert, and the tide line that proved there had been no storm. 
Each folded note went into a crack behind a lead wafer, then under lime tinted to match the original shell mortar. 
The work looked like obedience, which was why the watchman on the quay stopped looking after the first hour. 
Only Niko, her apprentice, saw that she left one small notch in every repair, a surveyor's mark pointing toward the next omission. 
He held the lamp and said nothing, because grief had taught him that some people need silence as much as help. 
The pattern of notches led to a blank beneath the fourth pier, where the plan promised a music room and the masonry offered only a narrow, salted seam. 
At the turn of the tide, the sluice began to suck, and she had to wrest a copper tube from that seam before the current carried its contents into the harbor. 
Niko braced her boots against the punt while she levered with a chisel, and the tube came free with a soft, animal gasp of silt. 
Inside lay the true minutes of the pavilion committee, folded around an opening program with every worker's name restored in her mother's square hand. 
The council had closed the sluices before the gala, not after any storm, because a free winter garden would have given dockhands a place to meet. 
Mara read the lines once, dry eyed, then pressed the page to her lips before returning it to the tube. 
At the bottom, her mother had written: If the room cannot open, let the children of masons know we meant it for them. 
Mara had thought the dead wanted only names returned, but the sentence showed her that an unrealized room could still be an inheritance. 
She copied that sentence onto a fresh slip, sealed it beside the minutes in the vault, and marked the vault repair with a notch facing east. 
At dawn, when the bridge came down, she wrote on the certificate that the foundations were sound and their sealed repairs required public inspection. 
Soon, the notches led careful hands to the notes, and the pavilion was no longer empty.
