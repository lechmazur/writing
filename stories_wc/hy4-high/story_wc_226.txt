In the towers of bone in a canyon of endless echoes, footfalls returned as other griefs under a forged hush. 
Three days before the ritual, the pale spire in mist stood at the canyon heart, blank as a tooth. 
Priests taught that the price of forgiveness was one voice surrendered at dawn, letting the city say, We are clean, without an answer. 
What they omitted was the name carried into the spire by that voice, scraped afterward from every mouth below. 
Those bones had belonged to starving clans locked beyond the gates when the old council hoarded grain. 
Instead of asking pardon, their heirs invented a ritual that turned forgetting into holiness. 
Mara had watched wardens take her brother Orrin, who still spoke the names of the dead. 
The council called her a defiant artist for painting hidden faces into banners and preserving chalk marks left by Orrin. 
They offered his name back if she glazed copper screens meant to soften the echoes before the ritual. 
She accepted, not to obey, but to stand nearest the machinery of forgetting. 
Mara had returned to refract a single truth into many, because truth held in one throat becomes a knife with one handle. 
Her truth was simple: no one is forgiven by forgetting, and silence paid by another mouth buys no innocence. 
No manifesto could survive wardens, so she worked through impromptu poetry sessions in laundries, kitchens, and under bone stairs. 
Whoever arrived gave a line, the next bent it, and echoes returned it with the accent of a forgotten household. 
No script could be seized, no table overturned, only rhymes learning to travel through ribs of bone. 
Her young apprentice Lio was innocently mischievous, stealing sugar from guards and hiding rude couplets inside solemn verses. 
He never knew that a joke about the wig of the High Keeper made wardens laugh instead of listen, opening doors that Mara had closed in anger. 
Mara first wanted to scold him, then saw that mischief let people practice disobedience before they felt brave. 
By the second evening before the ritual, those sessions had become a scattered chorus with no two rhymes alike. 
That was the shape Mara needed, for the screens were not filters but thin salted panes. 
At dawn they would refract the white light of the spire into hundreds of trembling paths through the mist. 
One clean sentence would enter, and many broken ones would return, each carrying a name meant for erasure. 
The High Keeper discovered the plan when Lio left a couplet in the fold of a ceremonial glove. 
He came to Mara in the workroom under the bone arch, where brushes looked guilty merely by lying still. 
He said Orrin had been a price, not a punishment, and the price of forgiveness had always required someone to disappear. 
He promised that if Mara stilled the sessions, the spire would release the name of Orrin and let her paint his portrait. 
For one breath, she wanted that mercy more than justice, and defiance felt like another locked gate. 
Then Lio, hidden behind drying screens, whispered a nonsense rhyme about a wig that could not keep out rain. 
The canyon borrowed it and sent it back in the voice of an old woman, then in the lisp of Orrin. 
Mara understood that forgiveness, if it meant anything, could not purchase one beloved name with the loss of all the others. 
She told the High Keeper that the true price was listening until every echo had made its claim. 
He left with padded guards and no song, but they had heard the gentle noise beginning in the walls. 
At the last hour before the ritual, the people stayed away from the square, because squares make easy targets. 
They stood at wells, ovens, scaffolds, and windows, each holding one improvised line, each face toward the pale spire in mist. 
When the first bell shivered, the spire tried to gather every sound into the single permitted answer. 
Instead, the salted screens refracted its beam, and echoes answered with names, apologies, curses, laughter, and stubborn memory. 
The forged hush did not shatter; it opened, like iron under a careful hammer, and no one called that ease absolution. 
Before the ritual began, Mara saw the price of forgiveness: not one voice spent, but many voices answerable.
