After the plague ends, the sunken city ruins of Verrin do not cheer; water taps once in the gutters, and the living climb out to count the dead. 
Three accounts are already selling in the market: the priests say obedience cleansed the air, the guilds say their vinegar fires burned the fever, and the admiral says his quarantine fleet saved the worthy. 
The council calls these competing narratives, then posts guards around the archive so no account can be corrected. 
Each story spends the city's remaining labor differently: hymns for the priests, furnaces for the guilds, grain ships for the admiral, and quicklime for the lower wards. 
Mara Vale, a wannabe philosopher living in a sewer, finds all three versions tidy, profitable, and false. 
Her room is a dry inspection shelf above the North Culvert, with a charcoal stove, two rat lines, and a ceiling mapped by old leaks. 
The sewer is not romantic, and Mara has an abrasive calm about it, as if every smell were an argument too stupid to answer. 
She is rationally rebellious, so she breaks only rules whose arithmetic has failed, and she keeps receipts for each broken lock. 
Before the plague, she copied tide tables and repair chants for the Harbor Office, because she wanted to preserve ancient methods before reverence replaced skill. 
She still wants them because the sluices need timed hands when the sea enters the courts. 
The new council has voted to seal the Harbor Office, melt its instruments, and teach children that the plague ended when the admiral raised a clean flag. 
That decree brings Mara up from the sewer with a trowel, a lamp, and no interest in being inspiring. 
Her method is through sedimentary layers, because mud has no patron, no grief, and no reason to flatter a flag. 
In the settling shaft beneath the flooded council hall, she cuts cores the width of a palm and labels every band by color, weight, grit, and smell. 
Her cores hold fever glass, vinegar ash, pit lime, a flood line, and a salt crust the admiral's clerks dated wrong. 
On the ninth day, her trowel knocks against a seafarer's chronometer cog, brass, thirty two teeth, and one tooth nicked by a clumsy file. 
It lies above lime and below victory ash, so timed instruments were repaired before the council declared an ending. 
The cog is not treasure; it is older grammar, cut by people who knew a gate five minutes late could drown a ward. 
Its scratches carry a lower ward repair mark, two strokes, a dot, and the number of the tide. 
That mark shrinks the priests, the guilds, and the admiral into minor characters beside drain crews who kept the pumps in phase. 
It also gives Mara a problem, because showing the cog will bring guards to her shaft, and hiding it will let the council melt every working clock. 
She lets herself agonize for one tide, not theatrically, but with a slate of costs: exposure, seizure, riot, hunger, flood, and the loss of apprentice hands. 
Then she washes the cog, wraps it in oilcloth, and climbs to the hearing where the council will decide who receives the winter grain. 
The hall smells of wet marble and vinegar; councillors sit above a polite depth of brown water. 
Mara does not ask permission; with abrasive calm, she lays six labeled cores, the cog, and a copied tide table on the clerk's dry desk. 
The admiral calls her a sewer sophist, which is close enough to her ambition that she does not correct him. 
She points to the layers and says that competing narratives are cheap until each one is made to predict what the mud should contain. 
Obedience predicts no brass, vinegar predicts no tide number, and the fleet predicts no repair mark inside the plague boundary. 
Maintenance predicts all three, so Mara asks for grain, shovels, and one winter of teaching; the vote passes. 
No one apologizes, which suits Mara; apology takes time from cleaning gears. 
By spring, the North Culvert has twelve stools and children who argue in competing narratives before checking strata jars.
