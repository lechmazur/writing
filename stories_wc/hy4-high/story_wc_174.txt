The decision waited in the hush left behind by a heated argument, inside the decommissioned radar dome, under a meteor hush. 
Above cracked panels, meteors scratched seams; the old dish waited on dead gears. 
Adrian Vell, an urbane calligrapher who lettered warrants for Governor Sol, folded his cuffs, uncommonly serene. 
Even after I called him a lackey, his calm made my anger feel like spilled ink. 
I had shouted for one clean sentence on the transmitter before dawn: Sol barred the granary doors and burned the trapped millers. 
He answered that a clean sentence could still be a clean knife, especially when grief sharpened it. 
We had come to decide how to tell the city what the campfire story stick carried out of the mill district. 
It lay on the dish pedestal, fire-blackened elm notched by witnesses at illegal cooking fires. 
By campfire custom, a holder could speak only of what they had seen, then had to pass the stick onward. 
Read from the smooth end, its cuts said Sol's ring flashed at the locked door, militia lanterns followed, and no one opened the bolts. 
That was the version the bars would chant by noon, and I wanted it moving before Sol's men found the transmitter. 
Adrian had not come to save Sol, though old service clung to him like expensive smoke. 
He had come to explore ethical storytelling, and I had mistaken that careful motive for fear. 
For two winters we had worked through subversive puppet shows in seedy bars, staging governors, widows, clerks, and militiamen as rag figures who interrupted one another. 
Drunk dockhands learned to suspect any puppet who spoke long, while watchmen asked which silence had been purchased. 
Adrian called those small arrests half-lit epiphanies, moments when a person saw one corner of a motive and left the rest in honest dark. 
He trusted no brighter conversion, because his calligraphy had made warrants look like wedding invitations. 
I, who had lost a brother in the granary, thought scruple was another room where powerful men washed their hands. 
The argument had ended when my voice cracked on his name, and the dome kept that hush among its abandoned equipment. 
Adrian rose, touched the black stick without lifting it, and told me to reverse the dish. 
I almost laughed, because a dish built for bombers had no business listening to wood, but his hands stopped me. 
He explained that every carved stroke remembers the blade, and light from the wrong end can turn a warning into an accusation. 
I reversed the crank by three teeth, and the old reflector groaned, dragging one blade of meteor light across the elm. 
Under that half light, the cuts changed their manners. 
A notch I read as a verb became a witness's shaking name, and the ring belonged to an unseen hand. 
The militia lanterns followed rumor, and a gatekeeper had closed the bolt to keep sparks from the flour loft. 
A soot-hidden line said Sol's carriage arrived after the roof fell, not excusing him but making my sentence false. 
I felt the hush change from embarrassment to attention, as if the dome were tracking something smaller than an army. 
Adrian did not smile; he only turned the campfire story stick so the charred end pointed toward me. 
By custom, that gave me the next turn, and my silence after shouting became my first honest sentence that night. 
I knew then that a half-lit epiphany is a lamp held low enough to show road, bearer, and shadow. 
I also knew my brother deserved more than being used as the bright handle of my certainty. 
We did not send the accusation at dawn. 
Instead, Adrian copied every notch in a modest hand, blurs included, and I wrote what each witness could not know. 
We planned no transmitter verdict, only an invitation to the bars where puppets had taught people to mistrust single voices. 
At each show, the stick would pass from puppet to puppet, then through the audience. 
The first speaker would name the dead, not as fuel for revenge, but as people who had once held turns by a fire. 
Under the meteor hush, Adrian reversed the final stroke and left the last word open enough for the living to answer.
