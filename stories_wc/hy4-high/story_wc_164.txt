Under Gloam Quay, the iridescent cave inside a giant crystal geode glittered like a party held inside a tear.  Above it, lanterns bobbed on black poles while the town sang farewells to vanished roads in bright, ridiculous major keys.  The festival was called cheerful gloom, and Sella had loved it since she was small enough to ride a bucket.  Now she entered the cave as a sea-foam recollectionist, carrying a disk of crater glass and one unsteady hope.  Her gift transliterates emotions into salt beads that can be read by the tongue, which is why her pockets always tasted of weather.  At dawn the lane to the old fever house vanished, taking with it her mother's last clear story about Tavi.  The bells gave Sella until the ninth lamp, before memory fades, to discover why some paths disappear and perhaps bring Tavi's story home.  In the cavern, prisms combed the light into pink, green, and a blue so merry it seemed impolite to be sad.  Sella set the crater glass in a wall seam, and its black center filled with bright scars.  Those scars were the missing pieces of roads, not smears or flaws, and every recollectionist learned to greet them politely.  Conjecture was the next tool, a way of laying hints beside hints until they admitted they were neighbors.  Sella had one hint: a scatter of tiny green sprouts along the floor, each no taller than a toenail.  She had learned to follow vanished roads via the scattered seeds dropped by a migrating bird, because such birds eat the fruit of forgotten thresholds.  Every spring the same brown bird crossed the geode on its way north, dropped its burden, and left a map that looked like an accident.  Tonight the accident formed a crooked line, not to the lost lane, but from the fever house to the place where Sella had first laughed after Tavi died.  The crater glass warmed under her palm, and the bright scars arranged themselves around the sprouts like guests at a small wedding.  She tasted one salt bead and found her own embarrassment, fizzy and lemon colored, followed by the heavy plum of her mother's goodbye.  The next beads held relief, and it ran through every sprout, every gap in the crater glass, until the missing pieces began to chime.  Sella made a conjecture then, not with words but by placing the disk so that chime, seed, and bead shared one prism.  In that light she saw the lanes not as lines but as feelings that had grown tired of being walked.  Some paths disappear when the last needed goodbye has been said, and their fruit is ripe enough for the bird to carry.  Other paths remain because people still worry, quarrel, or owe goats money, which explained the stubborn goat track behind the bakery.  The migrating bird did not steal roads; it transplanted the merciful ones, splitting each into scattered seeds.  Inside the geode's prism, those seeds became new directions for people who could not live beside an old hurt forever.  That was why the lane to the fever house had gone: her mother no longer needed to visit the worst hour of her life every morning.  Sella should have felt cheated, and part of her did, but the part that tasted beads also tasted a ridiculous little mercy.  Her mother would lose the clear story by morning, yet Tavi's laugh might come back as a road toward someone else's courage.  The ninth lamp trembled overhead, and happy minor notes from the festival slipped down the crystal stairs.  Sella lifted the crater glass, and one bright scar stayed on the floor, hardening into a seed warm as a joke told at a funeral.  She understood that missing pieces were not failures of memory but the handles by which memory could be carried elsewhere.  Instead of restoring the lost lane, she tucked the seed into a fold of sea foam and climbed toward the lanterns.  Above, her mother sat wrapped in a yellow shawl, already smiling at a story she could not quite name.  Sella sat beside her, laughed at nothing, and let the cheerful gloom make room for what had gone and what might grow.
