By the time Elias Mar reached the forgotten library inside a lighthouse on Gull Spit, he was a crestfallen portrait artist with salt in his cuffs and charcoal under his nails. 
Once, dukes and dockworkers had queued for his uncanny likenesses, but after his sister Nell drowned, every face he painted divided into incompatible versions. 
A mouth smiled on the left and grieved on the right, and patrons accused him of theft or madness. 
He came north because a scrap of Nell's last letter mentioned a room of books that kept the shapes people lost at sea. 
His stated errand was to descend into necessary darkness, but his private hope was one honest portrait of her before he let her go. 
The lamp above had not burned for decades, yet a patient white beam moved through the stacks and left a luminescent ache on every page. 
The hour called when the walls close in began at dusk, when the tide leaned on the round stones and the shelves crept toward the central table. 
Elias felt the first pressure in his ribs, as if the room were asking him to make himself smaller than his grief. 
On that table lay a broken tarot card box, its lid split diagonally and its velvet lining furred with silver thread. 
There were no cards inside, only rectangles of painted linen knotted together, each showing a different fragment of Nell in moonlight or storm. 
A catalogue lay open beside it, and the crabbed entry named the affliction fragmented reality, as naturally as a doctor might name fever. 
Beneath the entry, someone had written questions rhetorical questions, and Elias heard the missing word as clearly as a footstep. 
He understood the warning because his old studio habit was to ask questions, rhetorical questions that drew confessions from sitters who believed no answer was required. 
What did light owe the dead, and what did the dead owe a likeness? 
Who could be saved by resemblance, and who was merely flattered into silence? 
In the old days those questions had opened faces; here they tightened the walls and fed the white beam. 
A shelf slid nearer, dropping a book whose illustrations repeated his own arrival with tiny, merciless alterations. 
The broken tarot card box trembled, and the threads tightened until Nell's brow, hands, and laughter appeared in different centuries. 
Elias wanted to ask who had done this to her, but the question rose like a hook and he swallowed it. 
The catalogue margin offered the only instruction that did not sound like a taunt: the lens must be redesigned from below. 
A narrow stair descended behind a map case, and the dark beneath it smelled of wet rope, lamp oil, and crushed violets. 
He nearly turned back when the first step vanished, yet the upper room had compressed to the width of a coffin. 
To remain in the light was to be flattened, so he chose the darkness he had named necessary before he understood it. 
At the bottom, thousands of threads hung from the iron throat of the unlit lens, each tied to a card or a painted eyelid. 
The light above was not a beam at all but a loom, sorting every person into flattering pieces because no whole face could survive its glare. 
In that place, the only useful redesign was of the loom itself, by untangling threads rather than cutting them. 
Elias set down his charcoal, opened the broken tarot card box, and matched each painted scrap to the strand that pulled when he thought of Nell. 
He worked by untangling threads, not to separate memory from memory, but to loosen knots that made panic masquerade as truth. 
One strand held the day she taught him to mix gray from black and white; another held the sentence he had not said before she sailed. 
He did not ask whether forgiveness was possible, because the dark had no appetite for questions that curved back toward the asker. 
Instead he slipped the knots free, wound the loose silver into a new shutter, and let the lens draw shadow upward through its prisms. 
Above him, the shelves stopped moving, and the compressed room exhaled dust that glittered with a quieter, kinder ache. 
The fragments of Nell did not gather into a living woman; they settled into one portrait that could finally bear grief without splitting it. 
He painted her there in a darkness that held its own pale glow, and the resulting face was neither answer nor rescue, but a place to begin.
