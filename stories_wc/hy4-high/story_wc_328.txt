By the third winter after the missing return changed, Rime had stopped hanging lanterns for anyone.  The first return had restored cousins and fishermen; the next brought antlered shadows, glass-eyed children, and footsteps that rose instead of falling.  The Bureau named this a containment problem, and Rime learned to lock doors before dusk.  Mara Vale was a cryptid researcher working undercover as Hester's apprentice, with Bureau forms hidden beneath wormwood labels.  Her private ledger held one instruction beside a scrap of her brother's handwriting: to turn bitterness into a cosmic joke.  Jonah had come back from the white fields as a pale moth with his voice trapped in its wings.  Mara's bitterness was clean and sharp, like a scalpel kept in snow, and she called it professional curiosity.  Below the shuttered apothecary, the herbalist's underground garden breathed under blue lamps, warm compost, and leaf-edging frost.  Since the altered return, the place had become gravitationally playful: trowels drifted, tears climbed, and seed packets tugged at their strings.  Only a flint arrowhead beneath the phone charger refused to float, black and patient as a swallowed star.  Mara recognized it from an evidence photograph taken at the first absence site, and Hester noticed Mara's stare.  Instead of lying, the old herbalist called the stone a tuning fork for things people tried not to say.  The changed ones, she said, had been squeezed by rumors, accusations, and old grudges into shapes they had never chosen.  Their release had to come via the static-laden confessions on an old cordless phone whose charger hummed among nettles.  At nine each night, unsent apologies, jealous verdicts, and cowardly boasts hissed from the handset in sleet-blurred voices.  Hester repeated them to the roots, and when a confession was honest enough, the garden's gravity loosened its fist.  She called the arrangement a harmony untamed, an agreement among damaged things not to become tame for anyone.  Mara wrote that phrase as folklore, then crossed it out because the ink crawled uphill.  The Bureau planned an inventory at midwinter, when frost was hard enough to justify cages and clean enough to hide blood.  Mara saw her chance: steal the flint arrowhead, tune the phone to the emergency sirens, and make buried confessions ring above Rime.  It would be funny, she thought, in the vast way stars are funny when they make officials look small.  Mara stood at the workbench with one gloved hand on the arrowhead and the other on the handset when Hester caught her.  The old woman pointed beneath rosemary, where Jonah's wings trembled whenever Mara came near.  A joke made only from other people's shame, Hester said, was just another cage with better lighting.  For the hidden ones to metamorphose into self-chosen forms, Mara's own bitterness had to enter the static first.  That requirement felt like an insult, then a diagnosis, and finally the only door left open.  On inventory night, Bureau boots crossed the apothecary floor while Mara, Hester, and the arrowhead waited beside the singing charger.  She pressed the handset to her mouth and confessed that she had wanted Jonah human again, not because she loved him, but because his strangeness made her loss public.  The static did not soften; it listened, and the arrowhead lifted at last toward the ceiling and the unseen sky.  Every siren in Rime woke with a cough of sleet, and the stored confessions spilled through streets in ordinary, miserable voices.  The grocer admitted hiding a returned girl's shoes, the constable admitted enjoying his warrant, and the mayor admitted sleeping better after other children disappeared.  What made it a cosmic joke was not laughter but gravity: each poisonous sentence curled upward, became a cold star, and lit the person it had damaged.  The Bureau agents tried to run, but their boots forgot which way down was and paddled them in slow circles.  Under the rosemary, the moth-colony opened like wet pages, and the hidden ones began to metamorphose.  They did not choose uniform humanity; one became a willow-haired woman, one a winter-eyed child, and Jonah a small warm voice beside Mara's ear.  Mara filed one last Bureau report, classifying every returned citizen as unavailable for caging because their warrants were currently in orbit, and signed it with a steady hand.  Hester called the green shoots at the drainage rim frostbitten hope, green enough to live and rimed enough not to brag.
