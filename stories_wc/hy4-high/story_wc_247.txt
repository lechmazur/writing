At the luminous harbor by moonlight, in the hour called "when no one remembers you", the Registry read its roll and left blanks for the lost.  Lanterns floated on water, nets shone like wet silver, and the ferry waited beyond the breakwater.  The law allowed the ferry to approach only after a name drew no spoken answer, a hush the clerks called "when no one remembers you".  Sign language did not count, though the rope yards had used it for generations, and Mara's mother had signed more clearly than any clerk could shout.  Because her mother was deaf, the Registry entered no answer beside her name, and the ferry carried away the last public trace of her.  Mara still remembered her, but grief without a witness had been called fancy, so she kept her fists in her pockets.  Now she was quietly charismatic, never raising her voice and still finding forty dockworkers who made room for her hands.  They trusted her because she had grown up among Deaf and hearing crews, where a flat palm asked for rope and a circling wrist gave thanks.  Her dearest object was the vintage camera with no film that her mother had left wrapped in oilcloth.  It had a brass shutter, a ground glass screen, and an empty film chamber fitted with reeds, because her mother said gestures wanted answers, not cages.  On its peeled leather back, her mother had scratched a music stave and a grid of the harbor.  The whispering advisor, Senn, had shown Mara how to align that grid with the places where the roll went quiet.  He had once murmured counsel into the Registrar's sleeve, and after losing his post for objecting to that erasure, he spoke too softly for any clerk to record.  His advice was precise: the law depended on unmeasured silence, so she could disrupt it by mapping silence before the ferry heard the final blank.  That was why Mara wanted to translate silence into music, not to make signs respectable but to force the city to answer what it had refused to hear.  For a year she taught the dockworkers a score drawn from their signs, with handshape giving pitch, movement giving duration, and palm angle giving emphasis.  It was not a code pasted onto language; it carried the grammar of their hands across water bright enough to be seen.  On the night of the reading, the Registrar stood beneath the moon with a chained ledger, while the choir held its breath and the ferry drifted nearer.  Senn leaned close and whispered that each omitted name would leave four beats of official quiet, as clean and dangerous as an open door.  Mara raised the camera, looked through the ground glass, and saw the luminous harbor by moonlight divided into squares of water, rope, faces, and waiting hands.  Every square had been marked with a small sign for someone the Registry had omitted.  The first blank came after a drowned cooper's name, and before the fourth beat could die, a girl on the mast signed his joke about crooked barrels.  Mara framed her moving fingers, pressed the shutter, and the reeds in the empty chamber sounded a bright little note.  A fiddler answered, a gull answered, half the harbor answered, and the clerk's pen shook above the page.  The Registrar called it noise, but Senn whispered that noise was any answer a frightened law had not learned to spell.  The roll continued, and at each blank Mara clicked the empty camera while the dockworkers signed names, habits, quarrels, lullabies, and old laughter.  The notes did not copy language; they made room for answers, the way a lamp makes room for a face at a window.  When the Registrar reached the space where Mara's mother should have been, the statute's moment arrived, the one called "when no one remembers you", and even the waves seemed to hold still.  Mara lifted the camera, but for a heartbeat she could not see the grid because her eyes were full of moonlight and tears.  Then forty hands rose, signing her mother's name and the patient way she had taught Mara to frame a face.  Mara found the square, pressed the shutter, and the note was low, merry, and kind.  The choir took it up, the ferry's sails sagged, and the clerk drew a stave beside the blank.  By dawn the Registry recorded signed answers and drawn handshapes, and the music held joyful sorrow, because names returned but arms did not.
