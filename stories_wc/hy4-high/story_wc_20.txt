After the search party disbands, the rain-washed foundry keeps their last sounds: boots on wet cinders, a father saying his daughter's name, then the gate yielding to wind. 
Sela Vey remains because she is the balanced alchemist, licensed to measure heat, metal, and grief when grief changes the scale. 
The guild's oldest principle is factually mystical: a true name, written on a casting slip and cooled inside bronze, adds measurable weight that no filing can erase. 
As an apprentice, Sela had loved that fact and feared it, for names were the only cargo she could not lighten by clever alloy. 
At dawn she had given the searchers a ledger of twelve names and sent them downriver, where the flood had taken the apprentices. 
By dusk they had returned with no bodies, only a zodiac coin found snagged in the sluice grate, its twelve signs polished by river sand. 
Each sign bears a tiny initial, so the coin is not charm or currency but a roll of the missing pressed into silver. 
The captain has called the search hopeless, and one by one the volunteers have loosened their scarves, embraced, and gone home to supper. 
Sela stays to understand the weight of names, because she had written those initials too quickly in the casting ledger years ago. 
She had called the apprentices by station signs instead of given names, a tidy shorthand that saved ink and cost them a portion of themselves. 
Their master had taught kintsugi intentions, saying a cracked promise could be redeemed only if the repair showed the gold line where intention failed. 
Sela had laughed then, young enough to believe precision absolved carelessness; now the foundry's wet skylight holds a luminous regret she cannot look away from. 
She sets the zodiac coin on the balance pan and watches the needle lean toward the slag pit, not because silver is heavy, but because twelve neglected names lean with it. 
To redeem what she has shortened, she will work by exploring limits, the way old founders find the breaking point of a bell by pouring until the mold confesses. 
She begins gently, cleaning the coin with rainwater, weighing it after each stroke, and recording how much of the pull belongs to tarnish. 
When the needle steadies, she speaks one full name from memory, and the pan sinks as if a child has stepped onto it. 
She speaks the second, then the seventh, then all twelve in the order of their desks, and each addition makes the beam groan against its pin. 
At the limit of speech she does not whisper faster; she opens the old ledger and reads the crossed-out names she had denied them. 
The pan strikes the table, cracks the slate, and throws a white flare through the rain-washed windows, yet Sela keeps one hand on the beam to feel the exact shudder. 
That shudder tells her the names want not freedom from the coin, but a vessel wide enough to hold their full weight without splitting the world's arithmetic. 
She chooses the river bell she had cast from that ledger, a warning instrument tuned to signs and therefore silent when the water climbed. 
Now she cuts the coin along its twelve old divisions, leaving each sign intact, and lays the pieces in the bell's cracks like bright witnesses. 
She pours gold into those seams, then tests colder metal at the rim, hotter metal at the crown, thinner walls, and finally a heat that blurs the tongs. 
Each trial asks the edge how much forgetting bronze can bear, how much truth silver will spend, and where repair becomes concealment. 
When the gold holds without swallowing the cracks, she knows the kintsugi intentions have survived contact with the furnace. 
She writes twelve full names into the molten letters, one at a time, and with each name the bell grows heavier while the room grows clearer. 
At the last syllable the scale balances, not because sorrow lessens, but because Sela sets her own name beside the others and accepts its share. 
The bell rings once without a hammer, and twelve notes travel through the open gate toward the river, each too distinct to be mistaken for a sign. 
By dawn, the volunteers will find no bodies in the reeds, only twelve clear names washed into the sluice stone where the flood has scraped it clean. 
Sela cannot raise the dead, but in the shining seams she hears the apprentices answer with the weight they have always carried, and she bows.
