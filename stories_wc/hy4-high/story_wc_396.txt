In the lighthouse orchard perched above shifting ice floes, the beacon had been dark for nine minutes, and every apple tree held its frost like a verdict. 
Below the cliff, the Salt Choir floe was grinding toward the anchor roots with sixty families aboard, and the signal lamp asked Mara Venn for one of two legal words, hold or fire. 
Hold meant letting hooks tear out the orchard's only soil; fire meant splitting their ice with the beacon's cutting beam. 
A keeper's training stretched the instant during the weighted breath before reply, and Mara used that narrow room as other people used a workshop. 
Mara Venn, the shadow-laced peacekeeper, stood under the nearest tree with her palm closed around a broken crystal ball fragment. 
The shadows laced through her coat were not ornament; each thread came from a quarrel she had absorbed before it became a killing. 
She was calmly intuitive, noticing a mitten on a hook before she noticed the raised rifle. 
She had carried the shard since a winter tribunal praised her flawless neutrality while eleven children froze behind a locked granary. 
After that, her only ambition was to map empathy onto action, which was why the shard mattered more than any seal on her warrant. 
Training named the forbidden third mark a courageous anomaly: public blame for an unauthorized act that served both fears. 
Mara had never chosen one, and the shadow threads tightened whenever a blank appeared. 
Row after row of vintage store mirrors hung between the apple trunks, salvaged when the lower market drowned and installed to bounce the beacon's weak warmth through winter. 
The ball had been ground from the same silver sand as those mirrors, so each old glass kept one facet of its broken attention. 
The discipline said she could reconstitute the broken crystal ball fragment into a lens via glimpses in vintage store mirrors if she walked the rows and accepted each face. 
Mara opened her hand; the first mirror showed an orchard grandmother with her cheek to a trunk, fearing the roots. 
She wanted roots braced, frames sheltered, and dead trees' names remembered, not Salt Choir corpses. 
Mara noted sandbags, spare hooks, and six orchard elders who would work for a reason to trust strangers. 
The second mirror showed a Salt Choir mother hiding a stolen heat prism under a baby's blanket. 
She wanted a channel, one night of warmth, and hands that would not shove her family away. 
Mara noted the old thaw sluice and a mirror angle that would tilt light without firing the beam. 
The third mirror showed Mara, younger, writing SYMPATHY NOTED while the granary lock stayed shut. 
She let that glimpse remain longest, because any map that omitted her cowardice would send people into the cold. 
Because she was calmly intuitive, she did not rank fears; she asked where each ache could put its hands. 
The fragment warmed, and one facet clicked into place, clear as water. 
The fourth mirror held no face, only sluice, shutters, mirrors, and the floe's blue shoulder. 
It was not prophecy; it was hinges, ropes, angles, and people moving before the ice decided. 
Mara understood the route: angle mirrors, draw stored heat from the unlit beacon, open the sluice, and guide the floe past roots with a warm channel. 
The orchard would surrender its cellar, the Salt Choir would repair grafting frames, and the council would charge Mara with misuse. 
That cost made the forbidden blank a courageous anomaly rather than a clever trick. 
Still inside the stretched breath, she chose the tool, turned the brass key from fire, and opened the shutters. 
The mirrors passed mild heat along the north path, and the sluice water began to run. 
She signaled elders and rope crews, each movement placing a fear inside a task. 
The facets gathered from those glimpses until the broken crystal ball fragment became a small sphere showing the next useful motion. 
When the first rope went taut, Mara let the breath go and gave her reply. 
"Lower the ladders," she said, adding her full name so blame would know where to find her. 
Orchard and floe moved toward each other with work instead of mercy; one shadow thread loosened, and apple trees shed frost into the warmed dark.
