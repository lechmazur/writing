At the hidden lighthouse at dusk, Mara Venn climbed the spiral stair with the broken puppet head under her coat. 
Salt fog hid the windows, gulls had gone inland, and the sea lay flat as a held breath. 
As the last doomsday clock adjuster, she had been trained to correct one minute and never to hope for another. 
The clock filled the lantern room, a black brass sphere of wheels, tides, and numbered omens. 
Its duty was simple: at midnight it would stop every heart, every pendulum, every unfinished song. 
Tonight there would be no next age to wind, only the last evening cooling against the glass. 
Across the hush of time's final ripple, the mechanism would have room for one gesture and no more. 
Mara had come to make that gesture, though the ledger in her pocket forbade private mercy. 
She had accepted the post for one reason, to whisper a lullaby across a thousand lifetimes. 
In an earlier life, her daughter had died during a war of bells, too young to know a bedtime tune. 
The adjuster's charter let Mara remember each turning of the world, but not interfere with its children. 
So she had memorized their cradles, their fevers, their small hands opening whenever thunder crossed a city. 
She learned that endings were not walls but parenthesized rooms, and this knowledge hurt like bruised awe. 
Above the clock, fitted where a lens should be, waited a pane of hush. 
Every adjuster was taught that a pane of hush contained no silence, only the shape silence made before sound. 
To open it was to spend the final second, because whatever passed through would outlive the clock's authority. 
Mara set the broken puppet head on the bench, and its painted eyes caught the dusk without accusation. 
It was beautifully flawed because one cheek was split, one jaw hung loose, and a copper staple shone in the mouth. 
She had found it in the ruins of a theater after the war, still warm from a child's grip. 
Other adjusters would have used a perfect horn, but perfection had no crack in which a human note could hide. 
She followed the old method through nested patterns, a spiral inside a spiral inside a name. 
Mara saw those patterns everywhere now, in the stair, in the gear teeth, in tide rings cut on the wall. 
She saw them too in the puppet's fracture, where branching lines echoed the veins of her own wrist. 
Her mother had hummed the same lullaby in three beats, then hummed each beat into three smaller beats. 
That was the code: one mercy folded into another until a thousand lifetimes could carry it without breaking. 
Mara wound the clock backward by a hair, listening for the click that meant the final ripple had begun. 
The lantern room dimmed, and all the distant cities leaned close, unaware that their last instant had grown porous. 
She fitted the puppet head to the frame beneath the pane, using wire, wax, and the patience of grief. 
When its loose jaw trembled, she heard not a toy but a throat made from everything adults had failed to say. 
"I vouchsafe this breath to the sleepers," she whispered, and pressed her lips to the crack. 
The word worked like a key because an adjuster's oath allowed one gift, if she paid for it with her name. 
The clock took Mara Venn first, then her ledger, then the memory of being called mother in any life. 
What remained was breath, threaded through nested patterns, brightening the split cheek and the spiral gears. 
The pane of hush flexed, and the lullaby passed, too soft to be a command and too stubborn to be erased. 
It went across the hush of time's final ripple as the clock struck a midnight that had nowhere to land. 
In that held note, children in a thousand lifetimes stirred before they knew fear and turned toward an unseen face. 
Some would wake with a tune they could not place, beautifully flawed by one missing beat. 
Some would hum it to their own children when bells threatened, and mercy would keep its small pattern alive. 
The hidden lighthouse at dusk went dark, the sea forgot its flat pose, and the doomsday clock adjuster was gone. 
Only the broken puppet head stayed, smiling badly at a world that had been sung into another chance.
