At pearl twilight, Mara Vey entered the marble sanctuary under aurora with a dead queen's alphabet in her sleeve.  Green light moved over white marble, and the charter lay open like a patient whose breath had been counted too long.  The vellum could be altered only as stalactites drip from ice ribs, because warm drops kept ink pliable until cold hardened it.  When dripping ceased, the sentence about unfinished names would bind children with no completed names to another year of service.  Mara was a calligraphy forger, trained to make dead hands speak with the pressure of living ones.  Twenty winters earlier, the Regent paid her to add one clause in Queen Ilva's hand: the unfinished owed labor until the crown certified their names.  She did it beautifully, and beauty made the theft believable.  The Court called that art calibrated treason, betrayal weighed in hairlines so the state could deny breaking its promise.  Each spring clerks measured the clause with silver calipers and praised its restraint, while the children it bound grew strong enough to lift stone.  Mara learned the cost from her brother, whose transport roll stopped at Tav because a clerk died before adding the last letter.  No census would free a half name, and no priest would bury what law refused to complete.  Sister Eda, the sanctuary rubricator, had been wisely enduring, refusing panic and the clean heroics that send apprentices to gallows.  On the night of Mara's forgery, Eda left a heritage rose thorn in Mara's pen and scratched one instruction beneath a flourished Q.  Mara found the note after Eda died in the salt quarries, and read the words until the folded vellum softened.  The instruction was not a plan but a verdict on haste: to forgive the unfinished.  At first Mara hated its gentleness, because she wanted a sentence that could reverse a kingdom.  Now, with the Regent dead and his daughter ready to copy the charter at dawn, she understood that Eda had been asking her to wait for one small hour.  She had come not to erase her first crime but to unlink the obedience clause from the ancient promise of return.  For that work Eda had hidden a key with no apparent lock, a flat bronze tongue cut to fit the seam between two linked rings on the charter's spine.  Wardens searched for files, acids, and seals, never imagining the old binding itself was the lock.  Mara drew the bronze tongue from her sleeve and warmed it while aurora light turned marble walls pearl gray.  The thorn followed, dark and sharp, still carrying a brown bead of Eda's blood at its tip.  Mara touched that bead to her thumb, not for magic, for the sanctuary had none, but to know whose hand steadied hers.  A stair sounded beyond the screen; the new Regent's clerks were early, boots keeping time with the basins.  Mara could have fled through the west passage, but Eda's wisely enduring counsel held her to the lectern.  A lesser treason would tear out the page; a larger one would rewrite the queen; calibrated treason required one separation no wider than an eyelid's shadow.  She slid the key into the seam and turned it until the linked rings clicked apart without opening.  That was the only way to unlink the spine: persuade metal to forget it had been continuous.  With the rings loose, she lifted the vellum and found where her young hand crossed Queen Ilva's final r.  The heritage rose thorn entered the dried ink cleaner than any knife, parting her added descender from the older ascender carrying the promise.  One drop struck the basin, then another, and the labor clause hung alone while words of return stayed linked to every unfinished name.  She pressed the rings together, removed the key, and burnished the seam until no clerk could prove the binding had breathed.  The clerks entered in pearl twilight silence, bowed, and began the dawn copy from a charter that promised return before demanding anything.  By noon the rolls would restore half names, and Tav would finally be complete enough to be mourned.  Mara left the dead queen's alphabet under the lectern and carried only the thorn into pearl twilight, because to forgive the unfinished was not to finish them, but to let them go.
