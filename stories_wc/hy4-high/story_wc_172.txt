During the lull of distant thunder, Victor Sable stepped onto the salt flat with an extinct bird's egg cupped in his glove.  Mara followed with a canteen, a flare, and copied confessions from his office.  The confessions proved what the towns already knew: the drought had been manufactured.  His manufactured drought had begun in boardrooms, with river deeds, sealed sluices, cloud planes, and relief contracts that paid him for bottled water.  He endowed clinics afterward and named them for seasons, so gratitude would arrive before questions.  Victor Sable was an amoral philanthropist, which meant he counted deaths as delays and songs as assets.  Only Mara suspected he was secretly sentimental; he kept a vanished reedwarbler's nest locked away.  Mara had taken the job as his driver after her town's wells turned salt, and she wanted gates, signatures, and a prosecutor.  The white crust rang softly under their boots, a floor made from an inland sea.  Beyond the range, thunder moved too far for rain but near enough to make the air listen.  By morning, rain would close every crack.  He followed no compass; a map drawn by cracks in dried riverbeds was the method his crime had made.  Where tributaries once met, polygons split into arrows; where nests had been, hairlines made circles.  By reading those breaks, he had found the buried listening vault and the sluice that fed it.  The vault held a copper throat built for glass wafers recorded before the basin died; Victor had bought the last wafer hidden inside a hollow extinct bird's egg.  The egg's shell fit that throat, and its mineral grain carried the tone.  In the margin of a grant, he had written the true aim: to preserve the echo of extinct birds.  Her mother had sold pots for one tin of Victor's charity water; her brother died driving a tanker past a clinic named Autumn.  Mara carried no softness for him; she carried arithmetic.  At the vault mouth, Victor's hands stayed steady until the velvet case opened on the extinct bird's egg, blue gray and small.  He looked at it as at a sleeping child, ashamed of a softness he hid behind courtesy to objects and arithmetic about people.  Mara set the confessions beside the throat and asked for no speech, only a mechanism.  The same key could wake the vault and open the river, but water would drown the copper throat, and the echo would give Victor his reward if Mara let him stop there.  Neither trusted a promise.  Mara produced a flare above the case, lifted the counterweight pin, and ordered him: release the river after the echo, not instead of it.  He smiled at blackmail, nodded at the wafer, and obeyed both.  He placed the extinct bird's egg in the copper throat.  Mara fitted the pin so the counterweight would fall nine heartbeats after the first turn.  During the lull of distant thunder, the instrument waited with the patience of a held breath.  No engine coughed; no wind scraped the crust.  Even Victor stopped breathing.  He turned the key.  A call entered the salt, thin as a nail dropped in a cathedral, and a thousand dry seams gave it back as an absence with edges.  The dead flock had no bodies, no names, no hunger, only that bright fracture under the white.  Mara felt it in her teeth and hated that she did.  Victor did not smile; grief had found a use for him, and use was his nearest approach to goodness.  The counterweight fell.  Under the flat, a gate opened and released the river, sounding like a page turning.  Water long kept for ledgers ran into the cracks, erasing the map.  Mara tucked the confessions into her coat, safe from the first drops.  Distant thunder returned, near enough to carry the borrowed flock across wetting salt.  It was not forgiveness.  It was fractured grace: a dead song kept, a living river loosened, and a man who would never be good, standing still.
