In the opal library above clouds, the last glass-orchid caretaker kept a ledger of names no living mouth could shape.  The place was a chain of deserted marvels over a white, weatherless sea.  Visitors once stored fragile feelings here; when the feelings went extinct, the visitors went home or vanished.  A day was measured across the museum of extinct feelings, from one blue hour at Farewell to the next at Reassurance.  Serra, gently systematic, followed that measure every evening, testing stems and noting failures in the margins.  At dusk she would light a beeswax sliver, because roots released stored syllables only in warmth.  Her work had one motive, to preserve the sound of forgotten names, not spellings or dates.  A name pronounced wrongly soured into purple weather; keepers called the stain foxglove regret, lovely and poisonous.  On the night a storm rose through the cloud floor, Serra found folded orchids in the Hall of Vanished Greetings.  Beside them hung a wind chime bamboo tube cracked from lip to joint, its clapper tied with black hair.  That tube had gathered each visitor's first breath and made the pitch needed for orchid pollination.  Now the pitch wandered, and without pollination the names in those petals would loosen into the clouds below.  Serra heard them already, a dry rustle like letters sliding from a dead hand.  She did not curse the vanished visitors; regret, if hurried, broke more than neglect ever had.  Only one repair remained.  The first caretaker had sealed bamboo by the echo of a long-forgotten song, notes that could find hidden fractures after words were lost.  No score survived, but an echo slept in the Banquet of Apologies, where loveless apologies had hardened into blue glass.  Serra entered with her ledger, wax, and the cracked tube against her chest.  Across the museum of extinct feelings, she gathered one note from each silent case.  When a glass swan missed an interval, she waited; when a clockwork heart tried to beat, she steadied its hinge.  By the ninth gallery she had five notes, a pause, and the shape of a breath before weeping.  The pause was her mother's.  Serra had been a child when the last evacuation skiffs left, remembering only a woman humming behind a door while labels were packed.  That was why the tube had cracked: it had held the final note of her mother's name too long.  To call the rest of the song, she must light the wax in the greetings hall and draw the echo through the fracture.  The process would spend the tube's stored breath, the last private trace of the woman humming behind the door.  Serra sat on the cold opal floor and felt foxglove regret open, purple, bell-shaped, and poisonous.  She could keep one name whole, her mother's, or keep thousands from becoming weather.  Because she was gently systematic, she cleaned the tube, opened the ledger, warmed the wax, and only then cried.  Then she spoke the notes, the pause, and the borrowed breath, and the library answered by the echo of a long-forgotten song.  Serra lighted the sliver; reflection climbed the crack like a golden root.  The cracked tube sang once, not sweetly, but exactly, and every glass orchid opened.  Each petal released a name in its original voice, and one was the small three-beat word Serra had tried all her life to remember.  She recognized it for one heartbeat, then the sound joined the others and stopped belonging to her.  That was the price of preservation in a museum of extinct feelings: no name could be saved as a possession.  The storm passed, but the deserted marvels remained deserted, and the orchids stood bright with borrowed air.  Serra wrote no complaint, only the hour, the number of names recovered, and the word sacrifice in a neat hand.  After that, her rounds across the museum of extinct feelings held one empty place and one more rule.  At the Banquet of Apologies, she paused long enough to look at regret, never long enough to water it.  The visitors never returned, yet the glass orchids kept speaking into the light she lit each dusk.  That was how a glass-orchid caretaker preserved the sound of forgotten names, by letting them echo past the ache that wanted to hold them.
