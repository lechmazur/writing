At the center of the mist-clad atrium at twilight, during the two-minute interval the law called "when the dead call", Mira Sen felt the first pulse in her palm.  Around her the benches, glass roof, and potted palms held a reeling hush, as if grief had struck the building and left it standing.  Each year, old copper in the city briefly carried the last speech patterns of people who had died connected to it, and the bureau had learned to treat those signals as weather rather than wonder.  Mira was a contact tracer for hearts, trained to follow the routes by which one death traveled through lovers, patients, debtors, and children, then find the living most likely to answer.  Director Pelham had asked her to assure the ward that the renovated speech prostheses were immune, a small kindness before patients who could barely whisper were sent home for the night.  She had taken the post to protect vulnerable voices after her brother Niko, whose rented voice box had turned his breath into sentences, died with no recording left but a case number.  Persistently curious, she checked what other tracers initialed and forgot, because silence that arrived too neatly had once cost Niko a week of words.  She expected to finish by tracking faint pulses in a decades-old hearing aid, the bureau tuning relic whose cracked pocket-box shell lay warm against her glove.  The relic was supposed to catch only the harmless aftertone of the atrium's old switchboard, not the quick, frightened tapping of a child asking to be let out.  Mira opened the battery cap with a nail, and the last photograph, folded into eights, lay under the brittle disc of a mercury cell.  When she lifted it, the paper opened to twelve children in paper choir collars beside Sister Ave, all with one hand raised to their throats.  The last photograph had been taken on the night the river broke into Ward C, hours before the official history reduced those children to a memorial plaque.  On the back, in pencil steadied by panic, someone had written, He records us through the rail, and the dead must call until we are counted.  The pulse in the hearing aid repeated twelve beats, then paused, then began again, as if the smallest throats were still waiting for roll call.  She could have filed the photograph as a grieving artifact, assured the director, and gone home; instead she followed the beats down the service corridor.  At each ventilator grille the relic clicked louder, and at each painted wheelchair mark it softened, leading her beneath the choir rail in the abandoned chapel.  Behind a panel stamped with a forgotten manufacturer's mark, she found a black cable spliced into the new prosthesis charger and the ward's evening call system.  During the two minutes when the dead call, it would drain the memory cells of every borrowed voice in the ward and replace them with the recorded pleas of Ward C.  The dead were not thieves; the late director who had forced their recitations had wired their pleas as proof of his experiment, and the renovation had woken the loop.  Pelham's confidence, which she had come to assure the ward about, had fed twelve small voices into a machine that did not know a child from a waveform.  Above her, the ward's reeling hush broke into caught breath and the tiny chime of speech boxes preparing their evening goodbyes.  She had seconds to choose between the careful report that would protect her job and a hard yank that would protect the patients.  What followed was incidental heroism, not a rescue she had rehearsed, only the next careful motion of a hand closing around a cable because a pulse had asked a question.  She pulled until the black cable tore free, and the atrium lights shivered once, making the last photograph flash white against her sleeve.  The hearing aid went quiet, then gave twelve small taps that sounded less like a warning than a released breath.  In the ward, no alarms rang; instead, one child said, "I am still here," and another replied, "Me too," in a voice newly her own.  Mira could not assure them that nothing had happened, so she told them the names written on the back of the last photograph, one by one.  Some names were blurred by river damp, and for those she said, Child of Ward C, until the listening children made a place for each missing syllable.  By morning, reporters sought heroes; Mira kept the reeling hush in which borrowed voices slept unclaimed.
