Mara kept her loom through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry, in a forgotten orchard deep in a bamboo forest.  Once, villagers had come for wedding cloth and burial thread, but the bamboo had closed over their road, and even their names had gone soft.  She was a wistful loom spinner, not because she enjoyed sorrow, but because every shuttle held the shape of a hand she had failed to keep.  Her daughter Lina had left with the last cart, small and furious, while Mara stayed to hold the orchard's edge in place.  Only years later did Mara understand that an edge held from one side becomes a distance no road can cross.  Since then she had wanted only to carry a message across impossible distance: You were not abandoned.  Birds refused the air, wells repeated no echo, and letters fed to the loom came out as lint.  The trouble was a raveling silence, a looseness in the world's weave that drew every sound into stray filaments.  It had begun when time shed its borders, and the old loom could not recommence its first beat.  Without a border, thread had nowhere to turn back, so each word traveled outward until it thinned to nothing.  Mara knew the cost of any repair, for the orchard had taught her about latent sacrifice: perfect fruit asks the branch to give up its spring. 

Her courage lived in a tree sap amber bead, stoically sparkling in a scrap of blue wool at her throat.  Lina had pressed it there on the morning of the cart, saying that plum resin keeps what rain and fingers lose.  Inside the bead, when Mara held it to the last light, a small girl's breath moved like a trapped moth.  That breath was not the message; it was the address, the warmth that could teach a woven word where to arrive.  The method came through jigsaw puzzle edges found in a rummage sale box, which the bamboo delivered one wet morning.  The box smelled of dust and oranges, and held no center pieces.  Only straight sided strips and corners remained, painted with sky, root, roof, and a sea none of the villagers had seen.  Mara nearly burned them for kindling, then noticed that every edge, even broken from its picture, still knew how to make a boundary.  A border does not need a picture, she thought, and the silence had stolen exactly that from time.  She sorted the pieces on the loom bench by shape, not color, and fastened them with bamboo fiber into a little rectangular frame.  Crooked and stiff with sap, it gave the empty shed an outside and an inside. 

At dusk she set the amber bead in the frame and threaded her own gray hair through the warp.  Her hands remembered the first two motions, but the third, the patient return, had been lost with the outside road.  She whispered to the plum trees, more instruction than prayer.  Then she made herself recommence.  The shuttle passed, the jigsaw corners clicked, and the frame held what the great tapestry could no longer hold.  With each return, the raveling silence tightened into thread, and Mara felt a memory loosen inside her.  First went the sound of Lina's hiccupping laugh, then the exact brown of her boots, then the morning light on the cartwheel.  Mara kept weaving because a message needs someone to choose it after the choosing stops being sweet.  The bead, stoically sparkling, warmed until the trapped breath beat against its resin like a small heart.  She shaped the words with the last clear picture she had: a child turning once at the edge of the bamboo.  You were not abandoned, she told the dark, and the dark took the sentence out of her mouth before she could hear it.  To seal the crossing, the loom demanded the reason for the words, and Mara gave up the knowledge that the child had been hers. 

Far beyond the bamboo, in a room bright with a later morning, Lina, old now and sorting a rummage sale box, paused.  Her fingers found a tree sap amber bead among cracked cups, though she had never owned such a thing.  It was stoically sparkling, and when she lifted it, a sentence warmed her palm: You were not abandoned.  She did not know the price, but she wept as if a door had opened.
