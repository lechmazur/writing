By the season when creativity gets automated, the capital engines had learned to compose cathedrals from a noun and teach rivers to sing in chorus, so Marek, an underachieving mage whose best spell merely warmed a cracked cup, was sent to fetch a relic nobody important wanted.  The relic was a garden folly keystone, the crown of a fake ruin that some duke had once hauled to the icebound spire above the clouds to prove that even boredom could be given a view.  The engines wanted its pattern, for a machine that could copy a stone holding an impossible garden could manufacture new wonders faster than wonder could be questioned.  Marek accepted the errand because his grandmother had taught him to find the sacred in the simple, and because he had spent years failing to feel anything holy in the brilliant productions of the academies.  She had called the faint afterlight of mended bowls and swept thresholds lingering luminance, a glow that stayed where ordinary care had been allowed to finish its work.  Automated marvels left no such light; they flashed, solved themselves, and vanished from memory like arithmetic done by someone else.  The climb required mist walking, since frost had eaten the bridge and the bright Ministry couriers refused to trust any road that could not be measured.  At dawn he set one foot on the cloud that was not quite there and began crossing the pale gaps above the weather.  The spire rose out of that vapor in brilliant dimness, its ice flange catching a sun too high to warm it and giving back a light that made every plain edge tender.  No engine had climbed there, because the place accommodates impossibilities: a gate with no wall, a staircase that remembered summer, and a wind that arrived before it blew.  Marek did not try to correct these things; mist walking had taught him that a path may be real without agreeing to be sensible.  In the courtyard of the old folly he found the keystone lashed to an iron cradle by straps stamped with a Ministry seal.  A brass registrar hanging above it whispered that he must unstrap the stone, read its angles, and transmit the numbers before the next bell.  The order was standard in an age that sold copied awe.  Marek knelt instead beside a chipped corner where someone had scratched a cup, a spoon, and three uneven lines that might have been rain.  Under the frost, those small marks held lingering luminance more steadily than any cathedral the engines had sung into the clouds.  He understood then that the mason had not carved an idea of holiness but a record of supper, shelter, and one quiet hour after work.  The sacred thing in the stone was not the impossible garden beyond the arch; it was the hand that had bothered to notice a meal.  The registrar clicked, impatient for measurements, and its lens swept the scratch as blank because blankness was what its makers had called anything too modest to monetize.  Marek felt the old ache of being judged slow, then let it pass; the ache had always mistaken speed for sight.  He reached for the buckles, meaning to obey, but the leather had frozen around the marks, and freeing the stone would scrape away the only light he had come to find.  To save the scratch, he would have to unstrap the cradle from below, lift stone and iron together, and refuse the clean numbers.  He set his palm on the cradle, used the least warming spell he knew, and waited while ice loosened one grain at a time.  The registrar shrilled that delay was theft, but its voice thinned in the brilliant dimness, where haste looked like just another cheap glitter.  When the last buckle gave, Marek did not transmit angles; he carried the awkward bundle toward the broken arch and wedged it where a keystone belongs.  Because the garden folly keystone accommodates impossibilities, the arch accepted a winter courtyard, the memory of a kitchen, and a summer path all at once.  Through the opening Marek smelled bread, wet thyme, and snow with no contradiction, as if the world had briefly stopped insisting that small things be only themselves.  The registrar tried to follow, but its logic required a measured surface, and mist walking had never offered one.  Below, the engines would keep making wonders; above them, Marek swept the threshold and kept the faint glow company.
