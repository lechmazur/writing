Mara was a reckless idealist, exiled for saying the council could not price futures it had never lived.  Beneath that council lay the catacomb orchard where tree roots snake between bones, older than law and damp with broken promises.  Here, bartered destinies were its crop: bread for long life, silk for death, favor for courage.  Brokers weighed tomorrows on brass scales, then burned the tarot card that named each fate.  Every trade left a cold hole in the soul, and the poor learned to walk around their absences.  Mara learned this when Tovin, her brother, traded his allotted old age to break their mother's fever.  He came home at nineteen with an old man's vacant gaze and a burned tarot card in his fist.  One silver point survived on The Star; fire had missed a minute of his future.  For seven years Mara carried the card, first for revenge, then for repair.  She returned to patch holes in the soul, with fiercely gentle hands that could lift a child or bind a magistrate.  The patchers had taught her one method, inlaying meaning everywhere: names in bark, lullabies in clay, bread crumbs beside bones, giving absence a border.  A patched soul never recovered its sold fate, but it stopped leaking into the rooms of those who loved it.  Tovin had begun forgetting how to hold a cup, and small mercies finally seemed worth any risk.  She entered within the glow of half-lit corridors, when bone lanterns dimmed and roots still answered names.  Above, council lamps dimmed; below, almond dust sifted over ribs and children's fingers.  At the Ledger Tree, brokers in unpaid promises offered cards to families they had hollowed.  Mara ignored them and found the white root carrying Tovin's stolen years to the vault.  It was thick as her wrist, wound with silver threads, each one a bartered year.  She had come to trammel that root with copper wire, grave hair, and the card's silver fleck.  Cutting would scatter purchased lives into bones; exile had taught her to fear neat strokes.  Sedge, the senior broker, stepped from the gloom with tarot cards fanned like knives.  "You cannot give him back what he sold," he said, with the patience of locked doors.  "No, but I can stop the root spending it twice," Mara answered, showing him the burned tarot card.  His smile failed because the silver point was a key to the whole stolen span.  The brokers had burned receipts for decades, believing ash could not testify.  Mara had learned that ash, carefully inlaid, remembered more clearly than ink.  Sedge lifted a hand, and roots began to trammel her ankles with patient, cold loops.  Fiercely gentle, she did not slash; she loosened each loop by naming the bone it crossed.  The roots hesitated, for named bones had not forgotten her.  During that hesitation, she began to trammel the white root with copper wire, winding the silver threads silent.  Each turn caught a story: Tovin stealing pears, their mother singing through fever, Sedge as a hungry boy.  Pity slowed her hands, not her purpose, and made the binding exact.  Fruit split above her, spilling pale destinies bought by tired people.  Mara could have opened them all in a blaze of justice, and for one breath she wanted to.  Then she saw the bones: unpatched souls would vanish, and patched souls would tear around old scars.  Her reckless idealism learned the shape of a wound and chose the dressing instead of the sword.  She pressed the burned tarot card into the copper winding and let its silver point touch the hidden knot.  "A bargain can be witnessed," she said, "but it cannot own the meaning we make after it."  As the last half-lit corridor dimmed, she inlaid Tovin's stolen years beside acts he could still perform.  Breakfast, a steady cup, his sister's name: borders around a vast hole.  Far above, Tovin did not regain his stolen years, but he drank from a cup.  Sedge sat hollow among bones, and Mara left him bread crumbs cut with his name.  She climbed from the catacomb orchard before full dark carrying ash, copper snips, and a rule for every threshold.  No destiny would be traded without its hole being named, and no patched soul would be called foolish for surviving.  The council could exile her again, but dusky resolve went with her, quiet as roots and harder than bone.
