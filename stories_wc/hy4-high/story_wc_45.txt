After the substitute body is found in the rain cistern, the house stops pretending Rowan drowned. 
The drowned child on the potting tray is wax, willow, borrowed hair, and one brown bone. 
The quiet ward left in his cradle twelve years ago is therefore not merely a ward. 
Mara Vale, estate physician and a forgiving skeptic, has forgiven the lies but not the wasted wrong turns. 
Her oldest motive is to turn mistakes into constellations that can guide the lost. 
In the secret garden hidden behind a hedge maze, she has built an orrery from cracked spectacles, nursery spoons, watch springs, and map pins. 
She calls it nostalgic innovation, because each new gear remembers something the house loved before shame. 
Tansy, the ward, is shrewdly innocent; her wide eyes have always made dangerous questions sound like manners. 
She brings Mara a linen packet and says, "I kept the first tooth, since you kept every mistake except mine." 
Inside lies the changeling's baby tooth, small as rice, split by a silver cleft. 
Mara believes in measurements, clean thread, and the shame of an incorrect diagnosis. 
Yet the tooth fits the notch in the false jaw too exactly to be comfort or coincidence. 
She leaves it there and names the sensation a cleft in certainty, where two impossible truths lean together without falling. 
The old skepticism remains, but for the first time she begins to yearn for a road out of proof. 
Tansy says, "Bring the body, and set it where the roses used to be." 
At dusk they carry the tray through the hedge maze, following wrong turns Mara once chalked as failures. 
Each turn catches lamplight from the orrery, until the route resembles a patient sky rather than a correction. 
The hidden garden smells of wet boxwood, old canes, and hot oil from Mara's little engine. 
Under the arbor, Mara lays the substitute on a christening quilt and covers it with a sheet. 
She uses bone setting, her grandmother's method for living wrists: a listening thumb, a patient angle, never the heroic wrench. 
Willow ribs slide from places fear had forced them, and each click sounds like a remembered lullaby finding its note. 
She is not healing a body; she is restoring the shape of a bargain made by frightened hands. 
When the last crooked finger-bone settles, the changeling's baby tooth rolls from the false jaw with its silver cleft upward. 
The orrery lifts its cracked lenses, and the recorded mistakes shine at once: the hidden cradle record, Mara's signature, the wrong cradle, the wrong grave, Tansy's borrowed childhood. 
They are not absolved; they are arranged, and the arrangement makes a door. 
In the cleft in certainty between hedge and starlight stands a boy of twelve, muddy and holding a cap too small for him. 
He has Rowan's eyes from the portrait, though twelve lost years have altered the rest. 
Mara's first thought is inventory, because both children are cold and one is shaking. 
Her second thought is that a house can forgive deception more easily than an empty chair. 
She wraps Tansy first, since Tansy has been hers long enough to be cold without ceremony. 
Then she wraps Rowan, who watches with the politeness of a guest arriving after the music. 
Tansy pockets the tooth and says, "You may keep me if you keep the map honest." 
Mara understands: no one will be reduced to the mistake that brought them here. 
Rowan asks whether she is his mother, and Mara hears another small bone needing careful handling. 
"I am the woman who kept a wrong chart," she answers, "and I can begin by asking your name." 
He tells her, and the name fits neither portrait nor grave, which is a relief. 
By morning the household expects a trial, a proclamation, or at least a theory about fairies. 
Mara gives them breakfast, hot tea, and a revised map with every false turn labeled as useful. 
She does not claim to believe; she says certainty had a cleft, a debt came through it, and two children needed supper. 
In the weeks that follow, the children trade hedge lore and songs from beyond the cleft, while Mara adds their corrections to the orrery until its stars guide anyone sent away by a frightened bargain. 
After that, the secret garden hidden behind a hedge maze becomes a workshop for making room, opening whenever someone sets a crooked bone gently and admits the map was incomplete.
