In the days after waking up changed, Nela does not return to the clinic that has offered her another date. 
She stays at the drift-ice marina, where a calved shelf has flung boats into a gray, grinding mosaic. 
The clerk had written euthanasia tourist in the margin of her consent form, meaning only that the choice was illegal at home. 
Her illness has not softened; what has changed is the architecture of her waiting, damaged by smoke, cold water, and a brief failure of air. 
Since the oxygen loss, she grips reality loosely enough that clocks arrive late, names drift, and bells ring before anyone touches them. 
The nurses tape a paper clock to her wrist, but she trusts pitch more than minutes. 
Before the disaster, a sound editor by trade, she had wanted a scheduled silence and had believed it would be polite. 
The icequake ended that belief when the clinic wall split during the cannula placement, before any lethal drug entered her vein. 
Now she wants, before signing again, to learn the sound of approaching endings, because the storm had entered without quiet or obedience. 
She enters the only classroom that does not lie about catastrophe through disaster relief volunteering, joining crews who work where endings are still in progress. 
The teams at the marina are clearing broken berths, counting missing names, and listening under floes where divers cannot safely go. 
They keep her because she can tell when a strained rope has moved from complaint to betrayal. 
They teach her to trawl the inner basin with a soft grapnel, a marked line, and three knitted floats. 
Nela carries the heirloom lace parasol tip in her cuff, unscrewed from a brittle sunshade that crossed three wars and two migrations. 
Her grandmother had used the silver ferrule as a listening pin on winter ponds, tapping to hear whether ice would hold children. 
At the relief pier, the old ice master tells her that rescue begins with a fold in patience, not with a heroic yank. 
He means that wind, tide, and floe must lean together before a trapped hull can be moved without tearing someone under it. 
Nela understands patience differently now: not a flat road to death, but a crease where heat gathers and choices can wait. 
On the fourth morning, the line comes back with a red mitten knotted around the grapnel and a faint sequence of knocks in the rope. 
The volunteers shout for winches, but Nela presses the heirloom lace parasol tip to the wet timber and shakes her head. 
Through the metal she hears the little ticks that come before a split, and through the rope, someone answers with three taps. 
A boy is trapped in the air pocket of an overturned harbor skiff, using a loose bolt to knock when fear steals his breath. 
The ice master looks at the moving cloud, at the slackening cable, and recognizes a fold in patience opening around them. 
He orders everyone to stand still while Nela keeps one palm on the rope and the other on the parasol tip. 
Minutes loosen into a long, white hush in which gulls seem to hold their corners of the wind. 
She can, however, count three taps, then two, then the thinner scrape of a sleeve against a bilge. 
When the current finally turns the floe, the skiff rises enough for a hatch to be opened without swallowing the boy. 
Sami, the youngest diver, slides in with a blanket and brings him up blue lipped, furious, and loudly alive. 
The cheer is small; other names remain missing. 
Nela does not mistake the rescue for a cure, and no one on the pier asks her to. 
She has heard an ending approach as timber, rope, weather, breath, and a child's invented alphabet; it was neither clean nor wholly cruel. 
That evening, the clinic calls with a clean new date, all legal phrases polished smooth again. 
Nela thanks the nurse, asks for one more week, and does not apologize for the request. 
She walks back to the volunteers, who are mending a torn line and setting kettles on a stove that coughs warm smoke. 
The heirloom lace parasol tip rests in her palm, no longer a family relic but a working instrument for listening before decisions. 
Above the lamps, wind lifts the smoke, carries gull cries over the floes, and settles on her shoulders as windborne solace.
