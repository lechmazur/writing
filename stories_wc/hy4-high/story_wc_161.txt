In a vintage bookshop attic above Bellwether Books, Ada Vale, the last licensed courier pigeon trainer in the river city, kept twelve blue birds, forty ledgers, and one broken promise.  She had rented the room to preserve the echo of lost songs, songs her mother had written for the Lark Choir before the occupation burned their hall.  Only scraps survived: route numbers in dispatch receipts, faint rests in carbon copies, and key signatures hidden in grocery invoices.  Ada called those linked scraps paper trails, and she followed them more faithfully than she followed any clock.  The attic had a blooming withering smell, violets opening in the gutter while mildew browned the spines of unsold hymnals.  A single skylight leaked pale sun over the coops, and dust turned there like a slow, listening audience.  During the war, censors had confiscated sheet music and shot carrier birds whose messages mentioned public singing.  Ada's lover Tomas had sold them a flight list, then disappeared with an inspector's clean boots and a new name.  Ada reinvents personal history as deftly as she ties jesses, saying she preferred solitude and that no bird had ever waited for her hand.  The lie kept her tidy, but it also kept every drawer locked and every visitor at the stair.  Nell, the bookbinder's daughter downstairs, began by carrying tea to the landing without crossing the threshold.  She had ink on her thumbs, a patience that seemed inherited from paste, and no curiosity she was willing to flaunt.  Ada first trusted her with washing cups, then with numbering loose invoices, then with reading the rain-warped catalog of birds.  The work stretched across learning to trust again, from a winter of locked drawers to a green June when Nell held the loft latch.  Each recovered song arrived gradually, the way a bulb decides to flower after frost has blackened its leaves.  Ada copied note names onto fresh staves, but she would not let Nell hear a finished phrase.  A finished phrase could be repeated, and repeated things could be taken away.  Then Bellwether's owner died, his nephew scheduled an auction, and the attic was to be emptied before the roof repair.  The last Lark song lacked one arrival stub, without which its final measures remained a door with no latch.  Ada had traced that stub through sale notes, feather logs, and twelve mouse-chewed paper trails that ended at a mahogany tray of curiosities.  On the tray lay a brass-collared stone catalogued as a mars meteorite chip, bought from an astronomer who had married one of the choir altos.  Ada had always dismissed it as ballast for an eccentric widow's sentiment, because the catalog listed seven grams and nothing more.  Nell set the object on the postal scale and asked Ada to balance it against the same entry from 1908.  The needle rose past seven grams, then trembled as if the little stone had remembered a secret weight.  Ada's first instinct was to snatch the tray back, but Nell kept her hands open and waited.  That waiting, more than the scale, persuaded Ada to unscrew the collar with a bookbinder's awl.  Inside, folded around the meteorite grit like a bandage, was the missing arrival stub, browned but legible.  Its route numbers gave the last twelve notes, and its postmark named the bell tower where the choir once rehearsed.  Ada read them twice, then laughed once, a dry sound that hurt and loosened something under her ribs.  Nell did not crowd nearer; she merely sharpened a pencil and set out copying paper.  They worked until the skylight dimmed, balancing cramped numerals against rests, ledgers against memory, and silence against the urge to claim ownership.  Before dawn, Ada told Nell the finished copy must leave with Mercy, the strongest blue hen.  The originals would stay in a tin beneath the floor, but a copy could reach the tower before the auctioneer's men arrived.  For that release, Ada had to balance the capsule against Mercy's old training weight, because a heavy message made any bird circle back.  She judged the safest updraft by mapping the swirl of dust motes under a single skylight, a craft learned from years of sending paper into weather.  When the motes bent east, she knew the roof chimney would carry Mercy clear of the bookshop's dead air.  Ada tied the capsule herself, then put the loop of the latch into Nell's palm.  No one had opened a loft for her since Tomas, and the old fear rose with the scent of warm feathers.  Nell whispered that fear could stand beside her without giving orders, a sentence Ada wished she had learned at twenty.  Mercy rose; the tower answered with a borrowed chorus, and Ada let the paper trails become voices, still withering in her hands, suddenly blooming outside.
