Between acts, the tidal arcade under stars became Nell Orra's instrument. 
The tide had left silver lanes between stalls, and broken panes admitted constellations over the wet boards. 
Nell loved its calculated wilderness: clocks governing water, gaps governing starlight, chalk governing the crowd. 
Nell Orra was an analytical street performer, and her gaze was lightly attentive rather than hungry. 
She had taken the pitch to serenade monotony with creativity, not to collect applause. 
Her sign called it orchestrated serendipity, but she kept the words small because wonder disliked advertisements. 
For one useful surprise to happen, someone had to stack the deck and hide both hands. 
Nell did that by ordering randomness, using tide tables, weighted coins, footfalls, and the coffee cart's cough. 
She never called it fate, since fate was too lazy to notice which shoes slowed at puddles. 
She had twelve minutes between acts, from the puppet bell to the moon ballet's first drum. 
Usually she used that gap to return a glove, start a courtship, or set an apprentice laughing. 
Tonight the gap held a harder problem, prepared through seven tides. 
Oren Pell kept the floodgate, winding the tide organ as regularly as its twenty notes. 
His daughter Lina played fiddle in the ballet, returning those notes every night without a ripple. 
Once they had invented sea tunes together, but grief had made employees of them both. 
Neither crossed the arcade diagonal any more, though it had been their old chessboard route. 
In Nell's apron waited a bishop's chess piece carved from driftwood, its base plugged with blue wax. 
Oren had thrown the chess set away after their quarrel; the tide returned only this bishop. 
Lina had scratched nine notes inside its collar, a joke about bishops finding slantwise roads. 
Nell would not carry it to either of them, since gifts could be refused. 
She meant to let the piece choose them, which felt more dangerous and more honest. 
At the second bell she unfolded a board and set the bishop on a black square. 
Chalk diagonals ran toward Oren's gate, Lina's stair, and a drain breathing with the tide. 
Three cups of shells lay beside it, apparently mixed, actually sorted by reach and sleeve color. 
A stranger drawing a blue shell would place it where a bishop could escape. 
Lina always took blue rosin from that table; Oren always checked the gate when the cart coughed. 
Nell had paid the cart boy to burn damp charcoal at that minute, not to spill anything. 
The wilderness in her calculation was a gull, a child, or a gust from the lower arcade. 
Such a gust arrived as the puppet audience streamed out, flattening chalk and rattling cups. 
A little boy reached for the left cup, full of red shells meant for sailors. 
Nell's lightly attentive gaze caught his wrist before her smile could tighten. 
She asked him to choose the cup nearest the bishop, because bishops distrusted shortcuts. 
He obeyed, delighted by the rule, and drew a blue shell from the middle cup. 
Then Lina came down for rosin, saw the old piece, and stopped as if boards tilted. 
The cart coughed, Oren looked over, and both recognized the blue wax before their eyes met. 
Nell turned the board so its diagonal pointed from the gate to Lina's stair. 
The boy placed the shell where the bishop could escape, and the wax plug dropped into a puddle. 
A folded scrap slipped out, bearing Lina's nine notes in her own cramped hand. 
For a breath nobody moved; then Oren set the organ one beat late, making room for an answer. 
Lina lifted her fiddle between acts and changed the ninth note into a question. 
Oren answered with a wandering slant of sound, leaving the old twenty notes behind. 
The crowd quieted in that calculated wilderness, as if stars, water, and chalk had agreed to listen. 
Nell kept her gaze lightly attentive, counting the next bell while letting the tune seem accidental. 
When the ballet drum sounded, father and daughter did not reset their faces into routine. 
They played one more diagonal phrase before the stage swallowed Lina and the gate reclaimed Oren. 
Nell took back the bishop, wiped the chalk, and left a broken moon in the puddle. 
Monotony returned, but it had been serenaded, and the next accident already had a diagonal.
