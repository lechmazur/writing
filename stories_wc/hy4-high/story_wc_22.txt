At the blooming jetty at twilight, harbor clerks have chalked four words on every tide board: after the reef dies. 
He is the gloom-hill coachman, and tonight his coach carries no passengers, no mail, and no officer's trunk. 
It carries a chest of soil, a broken coral rake, and a collection of dried seed pods. 
From the high seat the coast has the narrow vastness of a keyhole: one splintered plank, then an ocean no map forgives. 
Blossoms have rooted between the timbers, pale and stubborn, proof that his earlier sowings survived salt, wind, and official neglect. 
Years ago, two exhausted armies signed the submerged concord beneath the living reef just beyond these flowers. 
They sank their guns, swore in three vanishing tongues, and made coral witness to a battlefield that no banner could claim. 
When heat and a white sickness emptied the polyp cities, the concord lost its keeper. 
Clerks declared the seabed open, and heirs of generals ordered a dawn flag-raising on the exposed mud. 
The coachman knows their paperwork is a trap because he speaks dead languages. 
He learned them carrying dispatches up Gloom Hill for officers who could not pronounce the villages they had conquered. 
Later he carried their wounded, then their boxes, then one box marked with his son's name. 
Guilt taught him to read epitaphs, treaties, and the small print by which powerful men rename murder as ground. 
That is why he works to let a garden reclaim a battlefield, not to erase the dead but to give their names roots. 
Each pod is labeled in a script no living clerk can challenge, because no living clerk can read it. 
He has come to fertilize the powder ruts with crushed hulls, coral lime, and guano scraped from the nesting posts. 
He chooses each furrow by understanding bird calls, a method the harbor mocks until the tide obeys him. 
During the war, shorebirds nested in the reef and copied burial responses, signal whistles, and commands in those three tongues. 
Their descendants still call from the pilings, and the coachman hears grammar where others hear weather. 
A plover's two notes mean the left crater is firm; a curlew's falling phrase means shell metal lies under the soft gleam. 
Three tern cries tell him where runoff will carry seed; one gull's laugh warns that the returning tide will climb fast. 
At the last light, he unhooks the lantern, clicks to Marrow, and drives the empty coach onto the exposed road. 
The horse hates the mud, but it trusts the hand that has never whipped it toward a trumpet. 
Wheels whisper along the narrow strip, while the vast flats shine like a shield no one deserves to polish. 
When the plovers hold their note, he stops, opens the chest, and tips the pods into troughs beneath the axle. 
The wheels crack the husks and press the mixture into ruts once cut by artillery, so that feeding begins where hunger was manufactured. 
At the center of the field he finds the lead plate that held the submerged concord below the reef. 
Barnacles have blurred the royal seals, but the three words for bread remain, and he reads them in order. 
Bread, in the oldest treaty formula, means a land may not be marched upon while it is feeding anyone. 
He lays the plate in the deepest furrow, covers it with pod meal, and asks the birds to witness the transfer. 
They answer in borrowed verbs, and the sound moves over the mud more softly than any cease-fire he ever carried. 
By the time the lantern gutters, seedlings from his earlier nights stand in green seams across the old gun lines. 
The new furrows are dark and invisible, yet the jetty's blossoms lean over them like approved signatures. 
At dawn a survey boat grounds beyond the flowers, and two officers step out with rolled flags. 
They call the flat barren, because barren ground is cheaper to own and easier to dedicate. 
The coachman translates the plate, the labels, and two clauses preserved in birdsong, forbidding flags over crops. 
The officers cannot answer him in the dead tongues, so their ceremony becomes a short lesson in botany. 
One of them kneels, finds sea-pea stipules in a boot print, and orders the flags kept furled. 
When they leave, the coachman drives Marrow back along the single plank, while the open water gathers the evening behind him.
