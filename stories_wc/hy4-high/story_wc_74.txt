The work began under a waxing moon's glow, when Mira unlocked the rooftop greenhouse in a smog-choked city. 
Below her, ventilator towers coughed soot over streets where the Registry had outlawed old alphabets. 
Mira was an insomniac painter, methodically hopeful despite twenty years of broken sleep. 
Her reason for painting was to prove that strangers remember kindness, not sentiment but evidence. 
The city had scheduled the greenhouse for demolition at sunrise, replacing saffron crocuses with another filter stack. 
Inspector Vale offered one narrow stay if, by dawn, she produced twenty unrelated witnesses to the roof's public worth. 
Mira had once been carried through an Ash Fever checkpoint by a woman whose name she never learned. 
That woman had wrapped her in a shawl, whispered three foreign syllables, and vanished into gray rain. 
Mira had spent her life wondering whether the syllables meant bread, shelter, or simply live. 
Her grandmother, a calligrapher before the bans, had left her a calligrapher's moon-tipped brush. 
Its crescent of white sable held a silver ferrule and the residue of twenty erased scripts. 
Those lost languages survived in Mira's ledgers as nouns for courtesies too exact for official speech. 
One meant giving the dry half of a bench to a coughing passenger. 
Another meant leaving a bowl outside a door for someone too proud to knock. 
The Registry had called such words useless because they could not identify workers or taxes. 
Her experiment was to rouse the lost languages via daydreams sparked by moonlight, not by dictionaries. 
Moonlight on wet glass made the brush's mineral ink cast waking dreams across the fog. 
She had learned this by accident, painting tomato labels while exhausted workers dozed below the elevated tram. 
The next morning, a conductor asked whether her glass was showing old kitchens from the eastern islands. 
He remembered a grandmother's phrase for adding water to soup when an unexpected guest appeared. 
Since then she had been methodically hopeful, numbering panes, measuring condensation, and recording every returned memory. 
The greenhouse held a saffron quiet, with sodium lamps softening soot and crocus threads burning gold in their trays. 
Even Inspector Vale lowered his voice among the basil, as if loudness might bruise the leaves. 
Mira set a chair for him, wrote the hour in her ledger, and washed the brush in rainwater. 
Then she painted the pane facing the night queue at the free clinic across the street. 
She did not paint translations, because translation would have sounded like a summons. 
Instead she drew gestures: a shawl folded, a cup placed down, a hand steadying a stranger's elbow. 
Around each gesture she lettered a single character from the lost languages, thin as a hair. 
The moon struck the wet glass, and the characters slid outward in pale ribbons through the smog. 
For a long while, nothing happened except the cough of vents and the tick of cooling metal. 
Mira did not pray; she checked the angle, dipped again, and added one more bowl-shaped stroke. 
At the clinic queue, an old porter looked up from his scarf and began to smile. 
He shuffled to the chalkboard and wrote a word Mira had copied only from a seized ferry manifest. 
Under it he chalked, 'For the boy who gave me his blanket during the first evacuation.' 
A nurse added a different character, then a sentence about a woman who had held her asthma mask in a raid. 
A tram driver parked illegally, climbed halfway up the fire stairs, and left a paper packet of lemon seeds. 
His note said that a stranger once shared seeds with him after the southern quarter burned. 
Each witness wrote about receiving help from someone outside family, guild, district, or approved memory. 
They wrote in trade slang, kitchen scripts, clinic shorthand, and characters the Registry had declared extinct. 
Mira logged twenty-seven responses with the same care she used for seed germination, refusing to hurry the proof. 
Inspector Vale watched the queue grow quieter, not louder, as people read one another's offerings. 
When a young radiator fitter wrote the three syllables from Mira's childhood, the brush trembled in her hand. 
He could not say why he knew them, only that a tired woman had sung them over him in a shelter. 
At dawn, Vale signed the stay; the syllables meant 'live'. 
Mira slept until noon.
