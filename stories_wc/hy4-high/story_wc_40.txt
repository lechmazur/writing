In the shadowed library at dawn, after the last dictionary is printed, the city keeps what the Bureau calls a suspended hush.  No bells are permitted, and footsteps seem to wait above the tiles before deciding to fall.  The reading room is sonically reflective, built with glazed arches and black marble so that a Minister could hear a page turn at the furthest desk.  Elias Rook, the reticent scholar who edited the final proofs, arrives with a shoebox of vine cuttings and a catalog card that reads, crystal from chandelier.  At sixty eight, he carries a weathered innocence, as though each cruel decree has worn him thin without convincing him that cruelty is wise.  He has come to decode the pattern of growth in the ivy that crossed the east wall after the presses stopped.  The Bureau insists that language is finished, but the tendrils make loops, forks, and returns no dictionary permitted.  Elias believes they are a grammar of living things, or perhaps the apology he owes the child who was fined for inventing the word greenly.  He signed the preface that declared unauthorized meanings void, and after the boy's fine he had not spoken in any public room.  At dawn the three Censors enter, warm from the printworks, carrying the last dictionary chained to a brass lectern.  They require Elias to testify that no word outside its pages exists, a formality before they seal the library and sell the silence as civic peace.  Because continuous speech stalls in the suspended hush, he writes on a slate, asking to answer through fractured sequences, the old acoustic method used when echoes had to be sorted after a collapse.  The Chief Censor smiles, certain that fragments cannot make an argument.  Under the cuttings lies the crystal, labeled only crystal from chandelier, cloudy at the core and cut into a small unequal tear.  When he hangs it from the surviving chandelier wire, dawn strikes the flaw and the sonically reflective arches answer with a soft tightening.  The crystal returns no pure note; it gives back the room's stolen throat clearings, page sighs, and the small cough of the boy at the hearing.  Elias does not arrange the sounds by clock time.  He lays them through fractured sequences of root, pause, fork, correction, leaf, because that is how the ivy grows toward the broken pane.  For each group he places a vine cutting beside the dictionary, and the cut ends mark the same intervals as the echoes.  The pattern is not tidy; three short spaces, one long, then a twin leaf turned toward the unprinted light.  Elias had measured those intervals all winter with a ruler, a candle, and the patience of a man who had mislaid his courage.  Now he sees that each correction is preserved and then varied, like a child repeating a forbidden word until it fits the world.  The Chief Censor opens the last dictionary to prove that no entry can hold such a thing.  He finds, under green, only old uses, official and clean.  At the same instant the crystal throws the boy's recorded question across the marble: If grass grows after rain, why can't a word grow greenly?  The sonically reflective arches carry the question to the highest shelves, where dust trembles like an audience trying not to breathe.  Then the same sequence arrives in vine, echo, and silence: root, pause, fork, correction, leaf.  The library, built for surveillance, now repeats a child's grammar to every corner.  The Censors order him to stop, but every command enters the hush and comes back broken into the same five intervals.  Elias adds one last sound, the scratch of his own pen on the preface he should never have signed.  At the end of the sequence he sets the shoebox lid, blank side up, against the final page, as a place for whatever comes next.  By making the room say what he cannot safely speak, he testifies that greenly was never an error but a beginning.  A suspended hush, he thinks, is not an ending; it is a seed enclosed in glass, waiting for a hand patient enough to turn it.  The Chief Censor reaches for the chain, then stops, because the crystal has begun to glow with a green no committee named.  Outside, the ivy pushes one new leaf through the broken pane, and the shadowed library at dawn accepts it without asking for a definition.  He smiles once, with weathered innocence, still expecting morning to be kind.  The Censors cannot sell a silence that has begun to grow.
