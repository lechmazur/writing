The night clerk at the Orpheum called it "while forgotten lyrics search for tune", the half hour after the last reel when words abandoned by old theme songs worried the ventilators and waited for a melody to carry them away. 
In the vintage movie palace projection booth, hushed thunder lay under everything: rain on the roof, carbon arcs cooling with cavernous clicks, and the low organ phrase that never quite resolved. 
Mara Venn climbed the iron stairs with her cracked crystal wrapped in a handkerchief and a film strip can under her arm, moving as if a sound might wake the dead. 
For twenty years the programme had billed her as the crystal gazer, but after the palace closed her real gift was psychometry reading, a thumb across an object until its kept feelings rose in light. 
She had come back to find where unused love is stored, because her mother, Leda, had died in the balcony office with seven goodbye letters addressed to Mara and no stamp on any envelope. 
As a child Mara had thought those letters proved a refusal; as a woman she feared they proved a room somewhere filling with love that had not been allowed to arrive. 
Leda had been the palace's bookkeeper, and whenever patrons left unsent farewells beneath the seats, she preserved the goodbye letters by photographing each page onto a single film strip and filing the reel in a tin marked LOST PROPERTY. 
The tin in Mara's arm contained the last brittle length, and to make its invisible writing rise she would have to incubate it beneath the lamp without letting the nitrate ignite. 
Mara was spatially imaginative in a way that embarrassed schoolteachers: grief appeared as a pantry, apology as a staircase, and promise as a door measured by two hands. 
When she touched the reel, psychometry reading gave her no scenes but a floor plan, with a sealed chamber behind the screen and a narrow corridor climbing to the booth. 
Every unsent goodbye in the emulsion had become another wing, so the palace inside the film was larger than the palace outside, and Mara understood that love had been given architecture instead of an address. 
The crystal, useless for prophecy, made a passable condenser; she set it before the lamphouse so a blue coin of light fell across the projector gate. 
Outside, hushed thunder pressed a palm over the city; inside, she laid her own palm on the film strip. 
The booth gave her a hundred departures: a sailor leaving a peach untouched, a widow folding a bus ticket, two brothers pretending not to embrace, and Leda's handwriting closing around Mara's name. 
She almost withdrew, because shame is a door that opens inward, but the forgotten lyrics in the ducts had begun to gather around a tune, and once they found it the reel would cool into silence. 
Mara lowered the lamp, turned the crank by hand, and began to incubate the film strip frame by frame, watching blank leader blush into sentences under the crystal's patient heat. 
This was not development but hatching: each goodbye letter loosened from celluloid, not as paper but as a voice learning the shape of the mouth that had failed it. 
The spatially imaginative map altered with every frame; the sealed chamber became a room of tables, and on each table sat a place setting kept for someone who would not come. 
Mara realized Leda had not hidden love away from her; she had stored other people's unbearable tenderness until a reader with warm hands could return it to the air. 
The seventh letter surfaced last, in her mother's hand, and its sentence was simple: I kept no love back; I kept the words because you were too young to receive a woman's fear. 
Mara laughed once, not happily, and the sound was hushed thunder in a booth built to swallow applause. 
The last lyric found its tune then, a thin waltz from some lost intermission, and the ductwork ceased its searching. 
Before the palace settled, she threaded the warmed film strip into the projector and aimed it at the empty seats. 
The goodbye letters crossed the screen one by one, too large for privacy and too quiet for scandal. 
Mara read them aloud, and unused love left its storage as hushed thunder over the velvet rows. 
The crystal showed no future, only her silvered hands and the stairs down to the lobby. 
Outside, the palace kept time without a song.
