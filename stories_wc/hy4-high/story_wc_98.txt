On the island of Nami, I served as the fantasy island reality checker, tasked with testing which wonders could safely be trusted. 
My instruments were empirically whimsical: a rain gauge for gossip, a compass that sniffed promises, and moths that bit only at invented dates. 
I trusted evidence, yet my grandmother had taught me that evidence on Nami included songs, scars, recipes, and the silence after a name. 
Before monsoon arrives, as the almanac said, the council expected me to certify official memories so the cliffs would keep their places. 
The monsoon did not merely flood roads; it washed loose whatever the people had stopped remembering together. 
Our maps, laws, and even the color of dawn depended on collective memory, a fragile commons no clerk could lock away. 
That season the blankness spread quickly, and children began calling the old drum tower the stone umbrella. 
I had taken the job to protect cultural memory, not to comfort officials who preferred a short past. 
Still, I doubted my ledgers, because my grandmother had left contradictions in every margin, as if certainty were theft. 
At dawn I climbed the ancient ridge in bloom, where scarlet vines, blue grasses, and moon colored flowers opened above sleeping villages. 
In my palm lay one knowledge fruit seed, black and warm, the last gift from the tree beside her kitchen. 
The only way to waken such a seed was through interpreted dreams, but dreams had grown as thin as steam over rice. 
The ridge flowers gave off luminous doubt, making every path look both true and temporary before the rains. 
I set my rain gauge among the petals, watched it fill with whispers, and let the compass spin toward three promises. 
The moths refused to bite until I admitted that I did not know which history deserved to survive. 
So I called the fishers, weavers, children, priests, cooks, and mourners to bring sleeping mats to the ridge. 
They came because the sky had turned copper, and because even skeptics fear a story that vanishes during an argument. 
I asked each sleeper to tell one dream at waking, and I wrote every image on palm leaves without correction. 
By noon the interpreted dreams formed no verdict, only a braid: salt in a cradle, a drum under water, three boats sewn from wedding cloth, my grandmother counting spoons. 
A council clerk demanded that I discard contradictions and certify the neatest dream, since neatness looked like proof. 
I nearly obeyed, because the first rain began tapping the leaves, and a signed lie would give people one name to hold. 
Then a small girl said that in her dream the stone umbrella had beaten like a heart, and the tower answered beneath its wrong name. 
I understood that memory was not a monument but a conversation, and that my task was to keep that conversation audible. 
To waken the seed, I read the braid aloud; through interpreted dreams, every contradiction became a named thread. 
The weavers corrected the boats, the cooks corrected the spoons, and the priests admitted that the drowned drum had belonged to invaders. 
With every amendment, the seed trembled, and the moths bit gently at the dates nobody could agree upon. 
I recorded those disagreements as evidence, because the evidence of a people is often the shape of its arguing. 
The hard rain struck the ridge, flattening flowers, smearing ink, and turning pages of neat decrees into porridge. 
I pressed the seed into wet soil at the place where all paths looked temporary, and the ridge gave a soft, skeptical light. 
A sprout rose, not from certainty but from disagreement kept harmonious, and its first leaves carried names in several hands. 
People leaned close, reading what they remembered and what they had only heard, while the rain tried to erase the sound. 
The cliffs held, though one harbor moved a polite half mile and no one could prove whether it had been elsewhere. 
I logbooked that uncertainty too, because our collective memory needed room for doubt as much as for dates. 
By evening the ancient ridge in bloom shone with luminous doubt, while the names remained preserved by many imperfect witnesses instead of one clean decree.
