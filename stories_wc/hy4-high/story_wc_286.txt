At dusk, as chimney smoke flattens over the distant kiln town, the abandoned beekeeping oasis in a scorched wasteland refuses to darken.  Tamarisks hold a green ring, the cistern keeps a cold center, and hives stand upright after fifty keeperless years.  Nera arrives with red clay, a wheel string, and a square of faded lantern glass wrapped in sackcloth.  She is a zen-minded potter, patient enough to hear wet walls complain and exact enough to cut away what would split them.  She has come to understand why some places resist change, because her village turned overnight into ash, ledgers, and a road that forgot the wells.  At dawn the council will burn the hives for lime and mark a straight freight line through the cistern.  Flat smoke means no wind until morning, leaving Nera one night to work.  Nera unwraps the faded lantern glass and finds its scratches turned toward the date palms rather than the sun.  Her grandmother had called the glass phototropic to kindness, saying it leaned toward hands that mended instead of hands that measured.  Nera distrusts such stories, but tests them as she would test clay, with one clean pressure and no argument.  When she frees a stranded beetle from wax, the scratches brighten, and the nearest hive begins to thrum.  A scolding word dims them.  She works with sharp gentleness after that, freeing wax from a cracked lid and easing the beetle under a shade stone.  The thrum returns, low and precise, as if many small wings are counting one breath beneath the hive floor.  Only then does she notice hour lines painted on every lid, their gold worn to the color of weak tea.  She learns to cross the oasis by following sun patterns from lid to lid, reading noon, stolen noon, and impossible noon.  At the fourth lid, the faded lantern glass throws not one reflection but fractured timelines across the dust.  Nera sees the same keepers divide at a gate where refugees once asked for water during the worst drought.  In one strand they bar the gate, in one they sell each cup, and in one they open the sluice.  The barbed strand dries into salt, the selling strand becomes the freight road, but the opened sluice keeps returning here.  Bee lights from all strands crowd the glass, phototropic to kindness, clustering where the smallest charitable act opens a future.  Nera understands that the oasis is not refusing time; it is pulled toward the hour when mercy changed the shape of things.  That knowledge hurts, because the council road is also a future, and her village needs water carts.  She sits at the wheel string, centers a lump of clay, and lets old answers spin without grabbing them.  A pot survives by yielding at the rim and standing firm at the foot, not by never changing.  So Nera does not freeze the oasis, and she does not surrender it to the straight line.  She throws twelve shallow basins, each sized for a wagon team and marked with a sun pattern that bends freight traffic around the cistern.  She sets them in a crescent from the gate to northern hardpan, pressing cool clay into cracks where the council flags stand.  When a flag snaps under her thumb, she smooths the cut edge so no one else will bleed.  The bee lights follow that kindness, the hives thrum, and the basins fill with reflected evening though no water has been poured.  Nera lifts the faded lantern glass once more and sees the selling strand bend beside the opened sluice instead of devouring it.  The barbed strand remains as a thin salt scar, bordering a road that can carry water and leave the wells intact.  At dawn, surveyors find their flags reset among sunlit clay basins, with no hives burned.  They call it witchwork until a driver notices that the curve saves half a mile and offers shade to every team.  Nera says nothing, because explanation can be another kind of possession, and the road will need its own reasons.  Before leaving, she fits the faded lantern glass into the smallest hive, where its scratches can turn all morning.  By then she understands: some places resist change because kindness keeps making a usable shape out of ruin.
