At the first hour after the ban lifts, no one cheers in the cobalt atrium by a frozen lake.  The witnesses keep a lantern-moth hush, hooding blue paper lanterns while moths tap softly at the glass.  Even insects seem to remember the Quiet Ban, which made exact words sacred and loose meanings criminal.  Iya, whom the lake villages call the cloud-shadow poet, enters with her brother's last letter folded under her coat.  She is aesthetically driven, but exile taught her that a crooked line can keep a family from bread.  For twelve winters, to paraphrase the Invocation of Keeping was a crime, and to cross its inlaid threshold was a sacrilege.  The council repealed both offenses when the frozen lake began reflecting too many absent faces.  Now the gate stands open, but the humming quartz colonnade still divides cobalt tiles from ice.  Its pillars were cut to answer only the Invocation's sacred wording, and their low note has held for a century.  Iya has come to paraphrase that wording plainly, as a bridge for people who never learned the court tongue.  She also wants, more than pardon, to stride through boundaries once thought sacred.  Yet she remembers her brother reciting a legal phrase he could not understand while soldiers measured the shadow of his mouth.  He was exiled for pausing, and Iya for writing that pause as a darker cloud across a white page.  Under one boot, the floor gives a tremor of certainty, so small that everyone else mistakes it for distant weather.  Iya trusts small motions; large certainties had filled the prisons.  A clerk asks whether she intends to restore the Invocation exactly, and his question is the last polite lock.  She answers that she will paraphrase the law as one might carry water from a sealed well to a thirsty house.  The clerk blushes because water changes vessel but remains water, which is the argument the ban was built to forbid.  Iya steps between two pillars, and the humming quartz colonnade accepts her breath before it accepts her meaning.  Instead of chanting the sacred syllables, she speaks in a voice still suited to the lantern-moth hush.  No wall may own the route by which neighbors reach one another, she says, and then gives the Invocation in village words.  She keeps its cadence but detaches it from courtly grammar, so the paraphrase is neither mockery nor replacement, merely a second doorway cut from the same light.  At first the colonnade resists with a thin, offended whine that makes the hooded lanterns shiver.  Iya answers through dissolving boundaries, not by smashing them, because a shattered pillar would still teach people where to stop.  She lets original and version blur until each seems a translation of the other, and floor and ice blur where reflected pillars continue downward.  She lets citizen and exile blur by speaking to the clerk as if his fear deserved an answer.  Quartz does what quartz does best: it carries vibration without choosing a side.  The colonnade's hum passes from one key into another, and blue tiles release a pale vapor.  The sacred wording and the village wording meet inside the pillars, and the old threshold loses its straightness.  A seam of frost opens across the lake, not as a wound but as ink accepting a margin.  The witnesses lean forward together, and their lantern-moth hush becomes a shared breath instead of a held one.  The clerk begins repeating her sentences in the court tongue, but stops when he hears that he is already understood.  That is when a tremor of certainty returns, stronger, traveling up through Iya's heels and into the folded letter.  The letter is made of phrases censors had sliced apart, and its white gaps now seem less like losses than paths.  Come home when the borders become grammar, her brother had written, and she had thought the sentence merely beautiful.  Aesthetically driven, she had preserved the sentence for beauty, never expecting beauty to become an instruction.  Only through dissolving boundaries can a metaphor turn into a road.  Iya walks past the last pillar, across the blurred threshold, and onto the lake's new seam.  No guard arrests her; the clerk carries a lantern, witnesses follow, and by morning each law is spoken twice so no threshold can pretend to be an ending.
