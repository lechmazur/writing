In the decades after the climate shifts, Mara Venn earned her license as a corridor echo cartographer, mapping public buildings by the way their ruined halls answered a tapped cane. 
The work had become necessary when monsoons arrived in winter, limestone sweated through summer, and old straight corridors bent around new loads of salt and heat. 
Mara was sensitively perceptive, able to hear a damp register behind plaster, a mouse behind a sealed vent, and the small pause before a tired wall began to fail. 
Her gift had made her famous, but it had also made every commission feel like legacy's burden, because the first echo atlas of the city had been signed by her grandmother, Sel. 
Sel had surveyed the Hall of Forecasts, then locked its lower rain courts before the fever evacuation, and the official report called the dead a tragic miscalculation. 
Mara had inherited Sel's brass cane, her unpaid fines, and a tin holding one film negative strip whose four frames showed the same empty corridor before it was greened over. 
Now the city wanted that corridor reopened as a reservoir chamber, and Mara took the assignment in the ivy-veiled passage for the oldest reason in her family: to discover what walls dream about. 
She did not mean sleep; she meant the slow pressure of materials remembering a shape they had once been ordered to hold. 
The only usable method was through half-burned diaries found in a fireplace in Sel's abandoned map room, where charred pages still smelled of pine soot and rain. 
Most entries had curled into black lace, but the surviving lines described a listening chamber, four frames, and a wall that confessed only when warm rain loosened the ivy roots. 
Sel had written that dreams traveled backward in stone, so Mara pinned the film negative strip beside a lantern and let its reversed shadows fall across the passage brick. 
The third frame lined up with a hairline crack that no surveyor had marked, and the fourth turned a cluster of ivy stems into the outline of a blocked drain. 
Mara tapped once, listened to the echo divide, and understood why Sel had sealed the place: beneath the floor slept a cistern that could drown the archive during a storm. 
A warmer age had made that caution cruel, because the city now needed the water, and the bricks had spent years straining toward the drain Sel had denied them. 
At dusk, rain finally thickened in the ivy-veiled passage, and Mara waited with her palm flat against the lime, counting the seconds between each soft exhalation. 
Her sensitively perceptive nerves caught a tremor too fine for the cane, a repeating flutter like someone turning in sleep but never finding comfort. 
She set the cane down, held the negative strip against the wet mortar, and allowed herself to glimpse not a ghost but a pattern of pressures, cool water wanting a path through hot stone. 
That glimpse answered the family question with terrible plainness: walls dream about the forces they must resist until resistance becomes another kind of collapse. 
The diaries had hidden one final line under a soot flap, and when Mara lifted it with a knife, Sel's handwriting said, "If the seasons change, forgive the wall before you forgive me." 
If she opened the drain, the remaining diaries, the map room, and perhaps Sel's last honest record would be soaked beyond repair. 
If she left it closed, the reservoir commission would pour concrete into the crack, and the passage would keep pretending that inheritance meant obedience. 
She thought of the evacuees in the official report, of Sel choosing a dry archive over a risky shelter, and of every descendant asked to carry a decision she had not made. 
Then Mara used the brass cane to pry the fourth frame's dark rectangle loose from the mortar, because the negative had shown her where the old drain cap had been buried. 
Water breathed first, then sighed, then ran; the passage shivered as if a held breath had finally been permitted to leave the body. 
By morning, the reservoir engineers found a corridor echo cartographer sitting in the wet passage, drawing a map that measured distance in returning sound and responsibility in released pressure. 
She labeled the repaired chamber "wall dream: water path" and wrote that an atlas should be a promise to revise, not a verdict from the dead. 
The film negative strip, warped but readable, became the first plate of that atlas, proof that a legacy could be carried without being repeated. 
Mara no longer listened for approval from the dead; she listened for what living walls were trying to become.
