After the journalist calls, Irena Vale does not summon the lawyers. 
The newsprint heiress sets down the receiver inside the echoing observatory above clouds, where words return a half second later, politely older. 
Finch has found a storm ledger, she tells the dome, and the dome answers, Finch has found a storm ledger, with solemn playfulness, as though the dead enjoyed one last riddle. 
He says the midnight edition will accuse her mother of sealing six printers in the west gallery during the gale, then burning the order that might have cleared them. 
He gives her until the presses turn because the Vale papers still carry her name in the masthead, ink stuck to inheritance like weather to glass. 
Irena has no wish to polish the family tomb; she wants to discover hidden truths, even when they wear her own face. 
That is why she keeps the observatory open, not for stars, but for the family habit of studying orbits of regret, the paths by which buried decisions return with sharper light. 
Around the brass sun, paper moons represent each public boast, private silence, and delayed correction, and every moon revisits the place where a lie first felt convenient. 
Her father built the machine as a game for a devotedly observant child, hiding errors in the gears and laughing softly when Irena found them. 
Regret circles, he used to say, but truth, being less vain, will sometimes sit still. 
On the walnut desk lies the only object he refused to catalog, a rosewood pen nib, blunt as a brown tooth and made for scoring lines into soft paper rather than for ink. 
Irena takes it up because Finch described a signature made of dents, not ink, a row of crescents pressed through the storm log. 
Rosewood leaves no metallic gleam, and in dry air it squeaks, a shy sound that the observatory repeats from every brass rib. 
A copper throat under the dome once fed each notable echo to a revolving strip of smoked newsprint, so the building remembered weather in scratches rather than sentiment. 
Irena chooses the strip from the gale and pins it beneath a lens, holding her breath as if a cough might scare the evidence into good behavior. 
She asks Finch to keep the line open and hold his page near the lamp; then she says she will intervene through proof by contradiction, the family's least sentimental mercy. 
Assume my mother wrote that order, she says. 
Then she sat at this desk, split the soft page with this nib, and left its double furrow at every downstroke, because the tip cracked in the drought and never healed. 
The copper throat, tuned to this desk, would also have drawn the dry squeak into seven marks, neat as a small animal's footprints. 
Finch, reading the page, reports single crescents, smooth as practiced lies. 
Under Irena's lens, the strip shows no seven marks at the sealing hour; instead it carries six knocks from the west door and a woman's bright command, Open, open. 
If Sera Vale was at the desk signing a closed door into existence, she could not also be at that door, begging the printers to live. 
The assumption produces two Seras in one weather, a larger miracle than the gale and considerably less tasteful. 
Therefore, Irena tells Finch, the ledger page is a later copy, pressed by a brass stylus from a forged plate, and the signature is costume, not evidence. 
The six knocks are the echo of her mother's hammer, not of her guilt, and the paper moon labeled west gallery has traveled on the wrong orbit for thirty years. 
Irena moves the moon nearer the brass sun, where regret no longer circles a slandered woman but the publisher who priced a contract above six lives. 
Her father had kept the city newsprint contract by delaying the printers until a bribery report missed the last train, then hidden that choice behind his wife's dead hand. 
He built the orrery because he feared such bargains would return, and he taught Irena to check every gear because he could neither confess nor bear an inaccurate universe. 
Now the call has brought that orbit around again, punctual as a comet and just as unconcerned with dignity. 
Irena asks for one minute of Finch's silence, not for grief, but because the strip must be sealed before the clouds damp it. 
Then she says, Stop the accusation, print the contradiction, and put my name under both the correction and the bill for whatever presses pause.
