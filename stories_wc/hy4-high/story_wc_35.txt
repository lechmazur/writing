At the close of an unspoken dream, the desert fortress after rain stands black, hammered, and temporary, its walls sturdy enough for a siege and brittle enough to split at a shout.  No one names the dream, because speaking of it wakes the sleepers before the buried tide has finished turning.  In it, the sea returns beneath the gates and the drowned ask for water.  Tonight, twelve minutes of the dream remain, and rain has opened a staircase that the maps deny.  Sena is the chef who can taste memories when salt melts on her tongue.  She keeps her gloves on in the kitchen, not from pride, but because stored remorse is emotionally contagious through touch.  Whoever touches it takes an old regret as a new wound, and then passes that wound along with the next handclasp.  Sena accepted this post to sandpaper rough memories, not to erase them.  As a child, she tasted her brother's last ration and felt its crust scrape him from within.  For seventy years, the governors of the fortress have hidden every pitiless order in a coral icebound pavilion beneath the dunes.  The governors called it stored remorse, and credited that cold cellar for peace, obedience, and walls that never sagged.  Sena knows the walls sag anyway, inwardly, where no mason will look.  Tonight's rain has done what raiders never did: washed the dunes thin, loosened the salt plugs, and set the ice speaking in trickles.  The gatekeeper comes to her oven with his helmet under one arm and says she must subsume the thaw before the sleepers wake.  If she fails, the first handshake in the yard will carry seventy years of guilt through the ranks by breakfast.  There is no map for the flooded floor, only the method of analyzing tidal pool formations in the runoff.  Buried coral still answers the moon, so crescent lips, comb ridges, and collars of salt reveal which cells have thawed.  A coast cook taught her the reading through pans left by ebb tide.  Now she crouches in the yard with smoked glass, tasting each bright rim through a silver straw.  The first pool tastes of a commander sealing a well; the second, of a mother hiding millet; the third, of her brother's unwashed cup.  She does not pull back, because rough memories must be named by the mouth before they can be smoothed.  Following the leaning crescents, she carries a copper pot, a sieve, bread, charcoal, and onions down the denied stair.  The air below is winter stored in a furnace, dry at the throat and hard under the nails.  The coral icebound pavilion beneath the dunes is not a ruin but a pantry, with columns of red branchwork and ceilings furred white.  In rows of blue lobes lie quarantined apologies, unpaid wages, executions, and all the refusals that made the fortress feel light.  Sena sets the pot where every pool leans, then lays bread at the four corners to keep the stock from tightening into ice.  She pours rain into the pot, salts it, and uses a charcoal brazier to draw dark broth from onions and bread.  It steams sturdy enough to carry weight and brittle enough to break before it lies.  When she opens the main lobe with her bare left hand, the stored remorse enters as if her palm were a mouth.  Regret for orders she never gave floods her, and she feels how a dry throat can drown an entire courtyard.  Her brother is there too, not accusing her, only holding the cup she once seasoned with silence.  She wants to seal the lobe and keep the fortress neat, but neatness has always been hunger.  Instead, Sena begins to subsume the thawed regret into the stock, stirring while heat climbs her wrist.  At the last turn of the dream, a hidden pool points to the remorse of the sea itself.  Sena tastes it, adds it, and speaks no word.  When she climbs back, the sleepers wake to bowls of thin, warm stock along the yard.  Touch now gives recognition, not fever, and soldiers remember closed wells, hidden millet, and private cups without falling.  No one mentions the dream when a neighbor's sleeve brushes a hand.  Sena removes one glove and lets the gatekeeper take her bare hand.  What passes is her brother's cup, worn smooth, and no sharper than bread.  The fortress remains sturdy, still brittle, and more honest beneath the washed walls.  Its gates hold because they hold what was done below them.
