The hidden orchard behind a kaleidoscopic waterfall kept an ancient freshness, as if the first morning had been folded into bark and then opened again each dusk. 
Light came through falling water in shattered reds and greens, so every apple looked briefly ripe and glassy. 
For nine nights before the final transformation, Mira came to the orchard with a notebook, a brass spoon, and one stubborn question. 
She was an auburn-haired alchemist of the river academy, trained to distrust whatever could not be weighed. 
Yet she had come to harmonize logic and intuition, because every exact recipe in the academy still began with a guess no one admitted making. 
The orchard would spend its last bodily hour becoming a transparent law the academy could own. 
Mira wanted a law that could still hold shade, birdcall, and the shock of being greeted by something alive. 
In older books that shock was called the touch of wonder, the unnoticed instant when measurement turned into meeting. 
Her teachers had crossed the phrase out, but the orchard gave no instruction that did not depend on it. 
Each evening, the surviving method arrived through the patterns in a flock of starlings that poured from the pear trees. 
The birds crossed the broken colors, becoming ink, smoke, a net, a widening eye, and a question mark. 
Mira copied their angles, counted beats between turns, and reduced each shape to a column of numbers. 
By the eighth night the columns were perfect and useless, because one figure could mean patience or flame. 
The last operation was named flame, a command to give exact heat that asked a thing to answer. 
At the correct interval, she had to flame the frozen dew crystal and let its unfallen dawn enter the roots. 
Wrongly timed, the heat would make only steam, and the orchard would harden into an equation with no apples in it. 
The crystal lay in a walnut box beside her mother's brass spoon, and Mira pretended the spoon was merely a weight. 
Mira was secretly sentimental, and sentiment was the one impurity she had never learned to forgive in herself. 
Her mother had caught the dew on the morning she died, freezing it before it could choose root or cloud. 
Mira had written analyses of its lattice, its cold, and its refusal to cloud, but never of the hand that gave it. 
On the ninth evening the starlings waited in a dark, listening knot above the western wall. 
The waterfall thinned to white, and Mira understood the final transformation had begun beneath the colors. 
She opened the box, set the frozen dew crystal on the brass spoon, and aligned both with the notch where three root shadows met. 
Her equations said the birds would form a triangle when the heat must begin. 
Her breath, trained to obey equations, said the birds refused a triangle because it would end the story too soon. 
She almost chose the numbers, since numbers had never asked her to grieve. 
Then one starling dropped from the knot, leaving a spoon-shaped hole the flock would not close. 
Mira saw the logic of that absence, held by quick bodies, and felt intuition rise inside reason. 
The touch of wonder was not a violation of method, but the instant the method touched back. 
She was still afraid of losing the crystal, and that fear no longer disqualified her. 
With the spoon held steady, she touched a taper's flame to the wick beneath it. 
Mira flamed the frozen dew crystal in the spoon-shaped silence, and its cold answered with sweetness. 
The crystal did not melt, but opened into thin light that ran along the spoon, over her fingers, and into roots. 
At once the starlings broke into a spiral, each turn exact enough to count and alive enough to surprise her. 
The final transformation passed through apple, bark, bird, and alchemist without flattening them into the academy's law. 
The waterfall returned to kaleidoscopic color, each shard newly washed and older than memory. 
Mira left the last page blank, and the warm empty spoon weighed more than her instruments, a fact she no longer needed to defend.
