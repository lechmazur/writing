In the verdant grotto above the clouds, Ione waited during the migration of the last monarch butterfly, while pink light gathered on the wet moss and a blushing stillness kept even the dripping water hushed. 
Ione was an esoteric realist, trained to treat omens, hinges, hunger, and inherited silence as evidence, and she had climbed there to honor overlooked histories that the cloud cities had trimmed from their annals. 
Those histories belonged to relay keepers who once read weather from roofs, mixed ink from soot, and guided monarchs over the cloud sea, but whose temporary shelters vanished before any census taker learned their names. 
Ione carried no plaque, only a moth wing saved by her grandmother, a relay keeper whose scale map of notches and veins recorded the pauses between the old signal fires. 
By building temporary structures as signals, she meant to set those pauses in air again, not so the cities would applaud, but so the final butterfly could find a route made of what had been neglected. 
Her arches were transiently rooted in moss with living pegs, bright leaf panels, and cords of braided grass, each one meant to lean for an hour, speak once, and then loosen without leaving a scar. 
At every gap marked on the moth wing she paused to knot two cords together, then left the ends loose, because the keepers had believed a message should admit that someone was no longer there to tighten it. 
The old ledgers called that pattern an interrupted cadence, three wingbeats, a listening hush, and a weaker fourth, and Ione feared that copying grief so precisely might turn remembrance into ornament. 
Doubt settled with the rosy fog, and she imagined the cloud cities below sending a sleek inspector to ask why a sane woman addressed one dying insect with scaffolding that rain would erase. 
Then the monarch came through a seam in the cloud light, small as a burnt coin, beating three times, pausing as if listening to roots, and offering a fourth stroke that nearly failed. 
Ione understood that its body remembered the same broken measure written into the moth wing, but it needed the grotto to answer from the ground before it spent the last of its strength. 
She set the leaf panels trembling in sequence, made shade follow brightness in the old paused rhythm, and held her breath while every transiently rooted arch leaned toward the path as if bowing. 
A gust from below snapped the highest cord, and she reached to knot it fast, then stopped, seeing that the empty gap was the listening hush the monarch had been waiting for. 
The butterfly altered course through that unclosed silence, found the weaker fourth beat in the shivering panels, and passed over the last arch just as the cloud glow flushed deeper around them. 
Below the grotto, lamps appeared along a thousand terraces, not in applause but in the same sequence of brightness, pause, and brightness that frightened children used when they counted thunder. 
Someone had kept the old way of reading shelters, perhaps a kitchen worker, perhaps a child, and Ione felt her private errand become a correspondence rather than a protest. 
That answer changed her, for the esoteric realist had always distrusted comfort, yet now the evidence was a softening in her chest, a fact as measurable as the weight of a cord. 
She laid the moth wing beneath the nearest arch, where dew loosened its powdered scales, and she did not try to save those flakes from becoming soil. 
The monarch settled there once, opened and closed with the interrupted cadence, and then lifted beyond the dripping lip of the grotto toward a horizon no register could own. 
By dawn the arches had bowed flat, every peg greening, every cord slack, and Ione descended with no proof except the knowledge that to honor overlooked histories sometimes meant leaving a place accurate, temporary, and awake in blushing stillness.
