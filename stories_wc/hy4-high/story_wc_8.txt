The hour named in her brother's note was "after the final petal drops from the old rosebush," and Livia reached the disused railway station overgrown with ivy just as the last red scrap let go.  She had been the Republic's fictional historian, not a joke but a cabinet post, and for twelve years she made events that had never happened read as inevitable.  The Council called those inventions noble lies, because the first had kept a starving capital from burning itself and the last had kept mothers from asking why their sons never came home.  Livia had written both, and grief had not made her reckless; it had made her emotionally rational, a woman who let love choose the question and evidence choose the answer.  That discipline had finally led her back to her brother, Ansel, whose signature as a railway captain appeared on one forged casualty list and on no memorial.  The Ministry had thrown the records of that night into the engine inspection pit below Platform Two, and clerks, with terrible accuracy, called it the pit of lies.  She had come to rescue truth from a pit of lies, and the only rope long enough was an illuminated letter written in gold leaf by the commander who had ordered the firing.  A frightened clerk had pulled it from the ashes, hidden it somewhere in the station, and sent Ansel's note with a tracing of the gold vines before the censors took him.  Marshal Rook, her old teacher in the manufacture of public memory, arrived with six soldiers and one duty, to recapture the letter before the city presses opened.  He found Livia in the ticket hall, where rain moved through the broken glass roof and ivy held the departures board in a green grip.  Rook said, "You are late," and she answered that historians were always late because they waited for one more document to contradict the first.  He asked for the letter; she asked for the names of the men shot at Platform Two, and the soldiers studied the wet floor.  Rook did not deny the deaths, because denial had never been the craft; his craft was arranging pity so that it protected power.  He told her the republic was a feverish patient, that truth might bring mobs back, and that wise historians knew when to be silent.  Livia felt the old fear rise for crowded stairs, shop windows, children caught between banners, and she kept it in its place.  She answered by drawing parallels, the historian's cleanest method, as if two rails could carry a country off its single track.  With chalk she drew one line for the official riot, one for the quartermaster's ledger, and marks where both claimed grain the city never grew.  Then she drew the present quarantine beside it: sealed districts, missing sons entered as volunteers, and the same signatures authorizing bread for nameless soldiers.  The soldiers understood before Rook did that their own families lived along the second line, and their boots stopped shuffling.  Rook said, "Fine ironies will not govern a nation," but he stepped aside when she walked toward the platform.  Outside, the old rosebush stood bare beside the rails, and ivy covered the signal boxes like a hand trying to keep testimony warm.  The clerk had hidden the letter by drawing parallels too: gold vines in its margin matched ivy climbing only the third signal box.  Livia saw bright leaves in the tracing, then real leaves on the lever frame, and reached behind a loose brick.  She recaptured the tin dispatch case with both hands, not from a person but from the government's long habit of burying what it could not answer.  Inside lay the illuminated letter, scorched at one corner, its first capital wound with roses and its last line naming Ansel among the men shot for refusing to fire.  Livia's breath caught, then returned to its work, because a sob would have been honest and useless while the presses waited across the river.  Rook lifted his pistol, but the youngest soldier lifted the case first and passed it to the bicycle courier waiting beyond the ivy.  Rook could have shot, yet each shot would create another document, and the chalk parallels had taught him the arithmetic of witnesses.  He lowered the pistol and said she had destroyed a peace; Livia answered that she had rescued a country from calling peace a pit.  By dawn the letter was set in type, and truth, imperfect but named, traveled into kitchens that had buried empty chairs.
