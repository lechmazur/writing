Before the echo of morning birdsong reached the lower vents, Nia Sarin found an impossible footprint in the server logs. 
As the intrusion detection specialist for Underlight, a bioluminescent mushroom farm buried under a retired subway station, she knew every legal way into a fruiting room. 
The footprint said someone with P-17's limp had crossed Nursery Six while the door stayed sealed and the purge clock counted toward 05:12. 
At that hour, the vents would sterilize twelve rooms unless the alarm was cleared before the echo of morning birdsong traveled down from the street. 
Nia was spatially gifted in the blunt, useful way of a person who could rebuild a corridor from drafts, cable hum, and the timing of distant footsteps. 

Underlight's night crew were familiar strangers behind silver respirators, and the archived routes had the same intimacy: P-17's limp, H-4's taps, R-2's backward glance. 
The system knew no names either, only pressure signatures, so when six of those signatures appeared inside locked beds, it called them intrusions. 
The anomaly spread as a quiet wildfire of green, brightening caps without heat and making the mist almost thoughtful. 
Nia did not run, because running fogged the sensors; her pulse climbed beneath the blue glow. 
In her pocket lay a laser pointer button pried from a broken lecture clicker, its plastic cracked, its lens still true. 
Arin, her brother, P-17 on the old board, had used that red dot to teach airflow. 
After he sealed the east membrane during a blight and died in the pressure lock, Nia kept his routes in the logs. 
She had told herself she stayed to understand the physics of letting go, the measurable difference between a sealed room and a room allowed to breathe. 
Grief had made a tool of that keepsake. 

At Nursery Six she pressed it, and a thin red beam entered the fog where no body stood. 
Spores opened around the dot like a curtain, showing a slow braid of air moving through permeable barriers toward the east wall. 
The barriers were chitin thread and charged mesh, built to pass vapor, ions, and the chemical gossip by which a mycelial crop coordinated its fans. 
Nothing broad as a hand could cross them; the old alarms read every human shaped wave as sabotage. 
Nia watched the red dot scatter, then rebuilt the pattern in her head: six routes, not entering, but trying to leave. 
One route carried P-17's limp, almost breaking her into the belief that the dead could ask for doors. 
She let the belief burn, then read the pressure again. 
The signatures were not people but memories the farm had made from shed skin, breath salts, tool vibration, and years of repeating paths. 
Mycelium held those habits as electrical gradients, and the sealed east membrane had compressed them until they glowed like footsteps. 
The crop was not being invaded; it was trying to exhale. 

That changed the problem from defense to release, and release was the one thing the board had forbidden after Arin died. 
A full opening would waste spores, while a purge would kill the beds and erase his pressure trace. 
Spatially gifted, she saw a third shape: narrow alarms, pulse gradients through the lattice, and log release as consent, not breach. 
Her method would read the farm through permeable barriers, treating changed pressure as breath, not invasion. 
It would revolutionize intrusion detection in living buildings, but now it only stopped a silent fire. 
To do it, she pressed the laser pointer button until its red beam woke the maintenance photocell behind the east membrane. 
The membrane softened by a hand's width, just enough for charged vapor to carry its stored patterns out. 
Green light moved in reverse, and P-17's limp loosened into an ordinary pressure pulse. 
Nia understood letting go as a pressure difference allowed to equalize without blame. 
She cleared the alarm before the echo of morning birdsong arrived and logged the event as a scheduled exhale. 
Still before the echo of morning birdsong, the mushrooms burned cool and steady, a quiet wildfire with nothing left to consume.
