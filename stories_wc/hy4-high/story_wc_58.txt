After the last ship sails, I am the only traveler who writes obituaries for the living left inside Vale. 
It carries the court, the bronze seals, and the queen's son toward a winter treaty no one in the kitchens praises. 
Until spring, no vessel enters, no courier knocks, and no household may leave a chair empty without a posted reason. 
Vale calls those reasons explained absences, and I earn bread by making them accurate enough to outlive gossip. 
My obituaries are vividly descriptive because rumor starves on detail and fattens on silence. 
I came across three countries to understand the geography of healing: how sorrow acquires borders, watersheds, and roads back. 
My brother survived his wife, yet neighbors buried him in talk, and he never found the path out. 
Now the chancellor pays me to explain Queen Liane, who has not appeared in public since the prince embarked. 
He wants a tidy fever, a candlelit sickbed, and a sentence declaring the throne temporarily merciful. 
What he wants is a gate closed around a grieving woman before dawn brings the regents. 
I ask for one hour in the palace, and he gives me the east stair with a guard's impatient cough. 
The Long Gallery is cold, its red carpet rolled back from doors that no longer need to open. 
Under the wax smell lie pear rot, wet stone, and leaf smoke from beyond the walls. 
At the threshold, a palace carpet weft thread catches on my boot, silver gone gray with dust. 
It is no scrap of lint; its twist still holds the resinous polish used only on the throne carpet. 
The guard says the outer doors are locked, but his eyes avoid fresh mud near the wainscot. 
I follow the wet marks into the shadowed garden in autumn, where bronze leaves cling to marble fauns and a fountain mutters under vines. 
The palace keeps a lonesome splendor there, with unlit windows, bruised fruit in nets, and gold chrysanthemums bowing over gravel. 
No lamp burns, yet swept walks curve as patiently as a tide. 
I work by projecting presence into the place where a person has ceased to appear. 
A sleeve waits, a cup steams, a foot tests the stair, and absence gains direction. 
I do not invent Queen Liane; I let the rooms remember what the chancellor ordered them to forget. 
By that method, the thread becomes a road from the carpet, past the stair and scullery, to the oldest pear tree. 
The queen sits beneath it with her shoes off, holding an unsent letter against her ribs. 
Her face is not feverish; it is sharpened by the labor of walking grief one careful lap at a time. 
I kneel and close her cold fingers around the thread, an intimate act that leaves room for refusal. 
She can pull away; instead she draws the silver strand across her palm and looks toward the harbor. 
The prince learned to walk under this tree, she tells me, before treaties made a boy into luggage. 
Each night she carries one step of sorrow farther from the throne, so the carpet will not become a shrine. 
If the council calls her broken, they will stop allowing this walk and make her silence a regency. 
My maps led me here: the geography of healing is not a continent but a footpath. 
An explained absence should be such a path, not a locked room with a ribbon tied to the key. 
At dawn I hand the chancellor a vividly descriptive sheet, and the guard posts a copy in the guildhall. 
It says Queen Liane is alive, absent from the throne by daylight, and present along the east walk after dusk. 
It names the pear tree, the unsent letter, the swept leaves, and the sore shoes drying beside the fountain. 
It asks the city to leave her lamps unlit and her path unguarded until she chooses the throne again. 
The chancellor reads it twice, then says the regents will call this tenderness treason. 
Before he can tear it, cooks, grooms, and laundry women come in with mud on their hems. 
They have walked the east path, seen the silver thread in her hand, and chosen to keep her road open. 
No regent is named; the city accepts an absence with edges, weather, and a return still possible.
