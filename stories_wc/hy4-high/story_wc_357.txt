On the orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches, Mara Venn, the intrepid orchard pilot, tightened her harness as pear trees loosened theirs.  The old trees released root clamps, waiting for the rare interval when gravity inverts for an hour.  Mara had only that hour to cross the wild canopy; afterward water fell and every route vanished.  Her skiff carried basket booms, pruning hooks, and a glass tank of silty water.  She had come to find the source of resilience, not another doomed sample for the council.  Older trees survived blight, ash winters, and inversion, but grafted saplings shot up pale, then folded at first wind.  Mara had watched her mother lose the eastern terrace, and inherited the habit of flying toward fear.  In her palm lay a broken arrow tip, green with old residue, tied to her wrist by copper wire.  Soil clerks said that residue accelerates plant growth, but knew only half of a living thing.  Her mother had pried it from the first trunk, calling it the needle that made the planet impatient.  Mara suspected the rest of that needle waited beyond the highest arches.  When the inversion bell swung without ringing, canals rose in bright ropes and the world entered its whirling silence.  Leaves, pears, dust, and tools spun upward in perfect spirals, yet no scrape or cough reached her ears.  Instruments spun their needles uselessly, so she entered the arches navigating by water flow, reading bright ropes braided around stone.  A current that fell under ordinary skies now climbed, and Mara let it choose gaps her wings might survive.  The broken arrow tip dragged at the copper thread near iron in the arches, giving her a quieter compass.  She passed upside down platforms, sleeping bees, and dark bark whose scars leaned in one direction.  The scars followed the hidden pull of water, implying an orchard older than any record.  At the seventh arch, a pear burst silently into a silver sheet that showed her face reversed.  She nearly turned back, because that face looked as tired as her mother, but the hour was thinning.  Instead she opened the tank, mixed residue with living water, and watched a green wake write one line across the current.  Where the line touched moss, shoots unfurled in seconds, but bent toward echoing stone rather than light.  That was the first thing she had come to discover, changing her from a collector into a listener.  The current drew her through a throat of roots into a hollow arch where upward water paused in a spinning bowl.  Around the bowl stood the oldest trees, clamped upside down, roots braided through stone and branches buried in air.  In the center, Mara saw the matching half of the broken arrow tip fixed in a cleft.  She set her fragment against it, and both halves completed a rusted arrow no longer meant for flight.  Water striking the joined metal released green dust, and the bowl began remembering aloud without sound.  Mara felt rather than heard a gathering of echoes: roots cracking, bark freezing, bees striking mesh, pears falling, branches knitting after storms.  Each hardship had entered water during inversion, circled arches until it lost noise, and settled as a pattern in the rootweb.  The trees survived because they shared those patterns before danger arrived, sending older caution through young sap as quietly as water chooses a slope.  The residue only made room quickly; the gathering taught growth how to endure.  Mara understood why cuttings died in clean nurseries: cut away from water and stone, they had speed but no memory.  She had wanted a mineral, a procedure, something the council could bottle; instead she knelt in whirling silence with both fragments under her hands.  She left the arrow, took living water, and flew home to plant only where echoes could gather.
