In the moonlit canyon, during a period of profound transformation, Mara Vale knelt beside a listening engine and tried not to hear the city dreaming behind her.  She was a helix-minded engineer, trained to see stress, signal, and inheritance as turns of the same spiral.  Above the rim, the settlement was feeding its sleep lattices to conversion towers because the Conversion had begun, and every citizen would wake by noon in a lattice of light.  The official histories called it mercy, but the old sleep chambers still held entangled memories, paired lives braided by years of shared dreaming.  When the technicians cleaned one lattice, another lattice forgot a childhood, a recipe, or the smell of rain on a kitchen window.  Mara had come to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams, not a curator of approved facts, and the distinction had cost her a berth on the ascending ships.  Her sister Tess had left one dream behind, deliberately, as a protest against a future with no unscheduled longing.  Mara did not know whether it contained a canceled voyage, a forgiven quarrel, or a blue room where their mother sang, but she knew the Archive had no right to dissolve it.  The listening engine had been built around a broken periscope mirror salvaged from the first canyon survey craft, a child's instrument for seeing around basalt elbows.  Its silver had cracked in seven branches, and each branch caught a different angle of moonlight while the canyon shivered with distant excavation.  Under conversion static, those angles also caught the paired residues of entangled memories, because the first sleepers had once slept in the reflected beam of that mirror to calm their terror.  To reach Tess without erasing the others, Mara would have to modify the engine through synaptic plasticity, teaching a living cortical graft to read the crack as a grammar instead of a flaw.  The graft was her own tissue, grown from a childhood biopsy and housed in a warm ceramic cup, so the work felt less like engineering than confession.  She fed it paired lullabies, old telemetry, and the recorded pulse of Tess laughing during a dust alarm.  Each time the graft guessed wrong, Mara rewarded the nearest branching rather than the loudest answer, and its faint warmth changed the mirror's reflection by a hair.  Her chaotically precise timing used each unpredictable moonquake as a calibration pulse, because the cracked mirror read jitter more faithfully than stillness.  Mara held the broken periscope mirror inside its bracket with two fingers and let every shudder choose a branch.  Then the conversion towers fired, the canyon's regular shudders sharpened, and the night became a quaking reverie in which moonlight, machinery, and recollection shook from the same root.  In the engine, seven bands of silver opened like startled eyelids, and the entangled memories answered without becoming separate.  Mara saw Tess at nineteen, walking away from the embarkation gate with a paper star folded into her sleeve.  The dream was not about a voyage, a quarrel, or their mother, but about refusing the promise that every yearning must be promoted into a plan.  Beside it trembled Sen's recipe for lentil cakes, an unnamed boy's fear of red doors, and three hundred other unfinished devotions.  If Mara pulled Tess forward, the graft would strengthen that single branch and all those devotions would dim into administrative blanks.  Her hand hovered above the reward key, shaking with the same need that had brought her down from the ships.  Mara understood that an archeologist does not drag relics home, but marks their relations in the earth so later hands can approach without theft.  She changed the input, not by choosing Tess, but by widening the graft's reward to every branch that kept another branch intact.  The listening engine ceased its whine, accepted the trembling pattern, and began printing a catalog of names, dates, absences, and possible rooms.  By dawn, the ascending ships were bright needles above the rim, and the sleep chambers stood empty except for Mara's labels.  The Conversion was complete, but the canyon kept its small aftershocks, as if the stratified rock were repeating the night in its sleep.  Mara packed the broken periscope mirror in cloth, wrapped the warm graft in both hands, and walked toward the buried chambers with her catalog against her chest.  By making an index instead of a rescue, she had finally become the archeologist of abandoned dreams.
