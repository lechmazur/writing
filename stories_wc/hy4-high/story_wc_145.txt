After the falcon returns, the rusted outpost by a frozen lake settles into a worrying tranquility, as if the iron roofs have agreed to keep a secret. 
No one cheers her from the watch platform, because the three surveyors who flew her north in autumn have not come back. 
I am the dry-witted atlas editor sent to close their folio, subtly confident by necessity, since confidence waved openly freezes faster than ink. 
The Society calls the country unsurveyed, which is a tidy word for a place that has swallowed my sister Mara and left neat white space where her notes should be. 
I have come to find where the rivers begin, and to prove that Mara did not die for a draftsman's blank. 
The keeper's manual instructs me to ring the cracked bell when the bird appears, to herald the return and the first soft hour of thaw. 
I ring it at noon, though the sound goes out over the lake like a spoon tapped against a medicine glass. 
The falcon, a ragged gray comma on her perch, blinks once, unimpressed by ceremony and by me. 
Inside, the stove ticks, the shelves hold tins with labels in four languages, and the windows are furred with frost from the breath of absent men. 
On Mara's bunk, the polyglot's phrasebook dog-ear is a blunt triangle folded at the page where eleven tongues ask, 'Where does the river rise?' 
In the margin, in her small backhand, she has written singular convergence, not confluence, and underlined 'not' until the nib tore the paper. 
That phrase had annoyed me in her last letter, because rivers are supposed to begin separately, in bogs, glaciers, and arguments among geographers. 
Now, in the quiet, it sounds less like a theory than an address. 
The survey charts are gone, burned by the expedition for heat or malice, so I work the way Mara used to when officials stole her instruments. 
I boil weak tea, breathe on the east window, and study the pattern of condensation on glass as it gathers and runs. 
Then the droplets hesitate along hairline scratches, and a buried map surfaces, etched by someone with a diamond and a serious dislike of filing cabinets. 
The lines are not coastlines but first courses, rivers drawn from their mouths backward toward the high rim of the basin. 
Each blue beginning ends, or starts, at the same absent dot beneath the ice near the northern shore. 
I check three panes, each warmed by my palms, and the pattern of condensation on glass repeats with the patience of a verdict. 
The dog-ear is creased to the same shallow angle as the fault line Mara scratched through the lake. 
Held against the pane, its corner points to a pressure ridge hidden by snow, where the ice is smooth enough to be trusted by fools. 
I understand then why she sent the falcon back with no note and why the men did not follow it home. 
All of them had gone to the smooth place to measure a spring that breathes warm vapor under the lake, and the lake had closed over their arithmetic. 
The phrasebook page translates source in languages from the coast, the steppe, and two monasteries that no longer answer letters. 
Mara had circled every word for one and drawn a small dot where all translations touched, the way a child draws a sun. 
I copy the window onto vellum, naming the point by coordinates rather than by Mara, because grief makes a poor legend and because legends crowd the margins. 
Before I seal the tube, the lake gives one soft report, not loud enough for panic and exactly loud enough for memory. 
A black seam opens along the pressure ridge, following Mara's angle with the obedience of a printed river. 
I take the bell rope again, not to welcome a bird but to herald the break to the trapper cabins beyond the spruces. 
Three strokes mean unsafe ice, four mean bring ropes, and after a winter of silence I give them seven, which is apparently editorial. 
While the bell shudders over the lake, I slide the vellum into Mara's oilcloth tube and cap it with wax. 
The falcon, practical as any courier, hops onto my glove when I offer the tube, though she inspects the knot before accepting it. 
Only then do I understand that to find where the rivers begin is to send one living thing back with the answer.
