In the years after the water wars, the tidal island of Keel kept two borders, one on maps and one redrawn by the sea. 
Mara Venn arrived on the evening ebb with a paint box and the amber brooch her mother had pinned to her coat before the last evacuation. 
An insomniac painter, wryly earnest about it, she told the ferryman that night was simply a larger studio with fewer interruptions. 
The joke was old, but she meant it; sleep had fled when the reservoirs burned and never found the new roads home. 
She had not come to acquit her mother, who had opened or closed the Keel sluice according to whichever ministry survived to publish history. 
She had come to thresh meaning from experience, separating rumor from what she had seen before either rotted into anecdote. 
At the settlement, a former exchange clerk named Rook left a note demanding the brooch at low water in return for the final page. 
Mara read it nine times, then laughed with the dry courtesy of someone who expects the worst and still polishes her boots. 
Outside, the ebb uncovered chimney pots, drowned orchard stakes, and a customs hall where families had once been counted like cargo. 
What she felt was haggard awe, too tired for wonder and too astonished to look away. 
She painted until rain began, not clean childhood rain but mineral runoff carrying tracer dye from shelled cisterns. 
Wartime engineers had colored each reservoir so stolen water could be named in court, and the colors survived in dust and storm wash. 
Councils had changed the maps after victories, but water, less patriotic, ran downhill along the old pipe grades. 
So Mara set out by following rain trails, blue over slate, rust down the chapel steps, green through the drowned orchard, all drawn toward the island's broken heart. 
She carried no lantern, because the dye answered lightning better than flame and she did not want Rook to see her hands shaking. 
The trails met at a fissure beneath the hall, where the storm had peeled away silt and left iron steps breathing in the rain. 
Rook waited below with a canvas tube and the stoop of a man long bent over other people's losses. 
He did not call her mother innocent or guilty, but said the place had once had a name and a use. 
The salvage charts called it the unearthed convergence, the junction where sluice tunnels, rain courts, prison corridors, and the sea's oldest drain had been made to agree. 
Standing there, Mara saw why every account of the last night differed: each survivor had held one spoke of the same wheel. 
Rook asked for the amber brooch, not as payment but as the seal that had authorized the final exchange and made its record lawful to release. 
Such rules were the clerks' mercy after the water wars: one object for one account, one counted loss for one uncounted life. 
Mara thought of the brooch above her heart during every escape, its amber clouded by sweat, its brass catch clicking like a patient insect. 
She had loved it as proof that her mother had touched her last, and hated Rook for turning proof into procedure. 
Then water ticked from four tunnels into one basin, each drop differently colored, and the sound was too exact for self pity. 
She made the exchange, setting the amber brooch in Rook's palm and taking the tube before tenderness could pose as courage. 
Inside lay a rain map in her mother's hand, colored trails leading here, and beside the sluice a column of names too small for any memorial. 
At the bottom, under gate openings and water heights, her mother had written, For Mara: do not make me innocent; make the water accurate. 
Mara sat on the iron step until the tide nosed back, feeling verdicts loosen without becoming harmless. 
Her mother had saved children by holding the sluice open and drowned a ward beyond warning. 
Both facts occupied one hand, now only ink, pressure, and stubborn strokes. 
When Rook offered the brooch back, Mara refused; the chamber had traded a relic for a harder inheritance. 
At dawn she painted the convergence in leftover color, leaving out the brooch and giving each stain an honest edge. 
The work did not cure her sleeplessness, but it shaped the wakeful hours, as if meaning had been threshed from rain.
