In the city of forgotten fountains, water had been taught to keep a defiant hush. 
Every basin stood dry, yet people still set cups beside them. 
Each autumn, migrating geese stopped above the roofs until the stars shifted. 
Mara's chance lasted from the pause of migrating geese until one cold star crossed the Lion Fountain. 
They had emptied the fountains first because water carried names back to the mouths that had spoken them. 
Afterward the Bureau of Clean Water placed each stolen recollection inside a stained glass box. 
The box had no lock, only panes fused by hurried heat and colors that fluttered like trapped moths. 
Mara was the luminous skeptic, a lamp repairer whose skin shone from years of phosphorus. 
She distrusted announced miracles and property deeds. 
Her light made concealment hard, so she worked with her collar raised and her mouth closed. 
Her neighbors called her realistically imaginative, because every impossible plan she made included rope, hinges, and a way home. 
At first that was no praise; funerals had taught her to measure. 
When her mother forgot the son she had lost at Sea Gate, Mara measured the empty chair and official ink. 
They told her grief had been removed by procedure, not mercy, but procedure could be reversed. 
She meant to liberate trapped memories before another census made forgetting seem like citizenship. 
Old Ivo, the dismissed astronomer, taught her sidereal patience beneath the boarded Fountain of Eels. 
Sidereal patience meant waiting for stars to regain an exact angle, not for clocks to approve endurance. 
The Bureau fused the glass by civic noon, gluing each memory to the wrong color. 
At the proper stellar return, Ivo said, the glue would soften, and a careful hand could unstick it. 
Old Ivo left rye crumbs for sparrows, though sparrows needed no directions. 
For eleven years she reached his hiding places by following breadcrumbs pressed into mortar cracks and windowsill dust. 
Bakers had left them so children could find families after the first confiscations. 
Wardens swept streets, not seams where bread kept its shape. 
On the appointed hush, Mara stepped from her shop with a satchel of tools and no announced slogan. 
Above her, geese hung motionless, painted on the smoky evening. 
Neighbors stood in doorways with cups, saying nothing so precisely that wardens hesitated. 
Mara crossed the ward by following breadcrumbs, reading each crumb as a turn, a depth, or a warning. 
The trail passed the Fountain of Eels, Baker's Court, and a basin where her brother sailed tin boats. 
At each corner she marked crumbs with chalk; realism distrusted unchecked paths. 
The last crumb waited on the Lion Fountain's lip, beside a grate disguised as a saint's sandal. 
She lifted the hinged grate with a shoemaker's hook, descended, and found the stained glass box glowing on a census table. 
In red, a boy's kite tugged a string; in blue, her mother's lost son turned away. 
Mara did not pray; the luminous skeptic trusted only what she could repair. 
She set a lamp tin mirror above the box and wedged it with hairpins. 
Then she waited while wardens' boots crossed above and geese held their impossible pause. 
Twice the glass brightened and cooled, but she withheld her hand, honoring sidereal patience rather than hope. 
Only when the cold star touched the Lion Fountain's reflection did she unstick the first pane. 
The red pane loosened, and kite string, river mud, and bread breathed into the room. 
She unstuck blue, amber, green, and a gray sliver no official catalog had bothered to name. 
Each color slid into the old pipe, where forgotten fountains kept one another's addresses. 
Above, wardens ordered everyone indoors, but the defiant hush made orders sound indecent. 
Cups filled with names spoken by people who had forgotten they knew them. 
At dawn the geese resumed, and Mara checked the hinge, not the sky. 
When neighbors called it a miracle, she named breadcrumbs, starlight, and the hinge. 
Her mother spoke her brother's name, and the city answered with fountains, not applause. 
Mara stayed luminous and skeptical, because some ledgers still needed burning.
