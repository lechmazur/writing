After the chess clock runs down, Marrow stops pretending that a minute can hold a life. 
Velveteen gloom settles over the cemetery, soft as brushed coal, and even the yews lower their voices. 
Jonas Vell, the blithe cemetery gardener, whistles anyway, because cheerfulness is the tool he has sharpened longest. 
His shed is tenderly organized, with seed packets and thread samples tagged by year, weather, and the widow who could not cry. 
Marrow sews each funeral tapestry's border with colors, knots, and gaps for what the dead and living meant to say. 
The graves are mapped by path and stone, yet uncharted echoes sometimes rise from places where nobody lies. 
They sound like a hand hovering over a latch, too unfinished for Jonas to call ghosts. 
For three nights they have come from the cobalt silo under stars, beyond the last row. 
The clock, kept in the vestry, is wound whenever a grave is disputed. 
Black plays for what was done, white for what was meant, and no one may judge until both flags fall. 
Tonight the last click has sounded, and Jonas carries an antique sovereign case into the silo. 
The air smells of old grain, rain, and blue paint from the cobalt walls. 
The case belonged to his mother, who never owned a sovereign but liked the velvet wells. 
She left a note: work to discover the shape of intentions before you name an echo. 
At dawn the council will sell the silo and the pauper strip, unless Jonas proves they were meant to remain shelter. 
Inside the case lies a strip from Marrow's first burial tapestry, folded softly and snagged with new echoes. 
If he cannot untangle it by morning, the silo will be called empty, and the coffins beneath its floor will go under the road. 
The only way to untangle it is by decoding frayed threads in a tapestry's border, a craft his mother taught him beside the graves of unbaptized children. 
He opens the case on a plank, sets the spent chess clock beside it, and lays needles in a tenderly organized row of red, ash, cream, and black. 
The velveteen gloom thickens, pleated by starlight through cracks in the wall. 
Jonas smiles at the dark as if it were a difficult guest, then stops. 
He holds the frayed border to the left velvet well, and a low echo answers: not speech, but a door held open while someone escapes. 
He matches each red loop to a knot and each black gap to a skipped beat. 
The pattern becomes not a message but a sequence of choices. 
Marrow's first plague winter had filled the silo with people the church turned away, and the cemetery had no room. 
The keeper, Ansel Reed, played chess with the rector while deciding whom to record and whom to hide. 
At the torn edge, two threads are twisted so tightly that they look like one. 
One is gold from a royal ribbon; one is gray from a gravedigger's coat. 
The rector intended to list every soul in the parish book; Ansel intended to bury them below the floor, where no map could let the crown erase them. 
Both wanted shelter, one through paper and one through earth, and their quarrel made the uncharted echoes wander between ledger and clay. 
With a barley thin needle, Jonas loosens the crossing without breaking either strand, and the silo breathes. 
The echoes settle into paper and clay, leading him to a folded page in the case's false bottom and notched stones under the chute. 
The page gives names; the stones give places, and together they prove the shelter Jonas must defend. 
The shape of intentions, he understands, is not a straight line but a folded pocket made to hold another person's fear. 
His mother's cheer had been such a pocket, laughing over graves so he would not learn how easily the living are misread. 
Before dawn, he lays threads, names, and stones before the council in the same tenderly organized order he uses for seeds. 
The road bends, the pauper strip becomes blue rye, and the silo is kept. 
On the next clear night, Jonas returns to the cobalt silo under stars with the antique sovereign case empty in his pocket. 
He winds the chess clock, lets it run down, and in the velveteen gloom hears no uncharted echoes, only rye and his own softer whistle.
