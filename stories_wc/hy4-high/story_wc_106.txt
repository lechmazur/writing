Under a fleeting golden sunset, you become the golden-hour portrait thief the broadsheets invented, slipping the small painting of your brother from the magistrate's west gallery while the guards admire larger, gaudier frames. 
You do not steal faces; you steal illuminated moments, those brief hinges when light shows what a person will choose before pride has time to hide it. 
In the painting, Jonah stands with one hand on a ticket and the other lifted toward someone beyond the canvas, his face bright with a decision no one in the house would name. 
The magistrate bought it after Jonah vanished on the Emerald Line; you spent seven years learning curiosity's weak seams. 
You are quietly savvy about hinges, footsteps, and the sort of lies a servant can tell without lowering her eyes. 
That is why the alarm stays silent, why the blue velvet curtain settles back, and why no one notices the jar of captured moonbeams knocking coldly against your ribs. 
You want no ransom, no patron's applause, no cabinet of beautiful crimes. 
Since the night Jonah disappeared, your whole life has narrowed to one stubborn wish: to press your ear against destiny's door and hear whether the future is a sentence or a room with a handle on your side. 
At the emerald station by moonlight, green glass, wet rails, and silver departure boards make the concourse seem drowned and still awake. 
Porters move beneath contradictory clocks, and ticket windows glow like jar lids held to candles. 
A luminous disbelief follows you, not because the station is impossible, but because Jonah once described it exactly. 
The florist's stall waits between Platform Nine and the lost-property office, its buckets full of white lilies, yarrow, rosemary, blue iris, foxglove, and two black tulips. 
Pella built codes in flower arrangements for people whom the censors had erased from letters, and she taught you never to read a bouquet in only one direction. 
The obvious arrangement says farewell: lilies for mourning, rosemary for memory, foxglove for a lie you should stop telling yourself, black tulips for an ending. 
You almost turn toward the night train, ashamed of the boy in your coat and of every lock you have opened for his sake. 
Then you notice timetable-ink on the lily pollen, a knot of green signal wire around the yarrow, and foxglove stems cut to two unequal lengths. 
Because you are quietly savvy, you reinterpret the bouquet as a route instead of a funeral: Platform Nine, signal closet, eleven minutes past midnight, second door, false name. 
When the magistrate's men arrive with lanterns, a paid porter sends them after a suitcase full of opera posters. 
Down the service stair, the station's emerald tiles darken to the color of deep water, and the noises above fold into a single patient hush. 
At the second door, painted SERVICE over a smaller word worn almost smooth, you uncork the jar of captured moonbeams. 
The moonbeams do not spill; they rise, thin and curious, and settle over Jonah's painted ticket until the paper shines like an unspoken platform number. 
His painted hand turns, not toward the person beyond the canvas, but toward your own hand held beneath the frame. 
The illuminated moments you have stolen were never predictions; they were invitations, each one asking whether you would spend a life taking light or carrying it back. 
You feel the old wish rise again, fierce and childish, to press your ear against destiny's door and demand one clean answer about Jonah. 
Instead, you slide the little portrait through the brass slot beneath SERVICE, and the moonbeams follow it like water finding a drain. 
The worn word under SERVICE brightens, letter by patient letter, until it reads DESTINY, and the lock opens without a click. 
Beyond is not an office but a narrow platform where every arriving train is made of moments not yet chosen. 
You could step aboard and learn grief's end, but the portrait is gone, and with it goes the Jonah who belonged only to you. 
So you do what hunger has never taught you to do: you wait, press your ear against destiny's door, and listen. 
There is no verdict, only a boy counting rail ties, a woman humming Pella's flower code, and your own older breath advising patience. 
The door does not say Jonah is alive; it says the moment remains open, and that is a more dangerous gift. 
A luminous disbelief steadies you as you return upstairs, and you are more interested in giving stolen light back than in owning any answer.
