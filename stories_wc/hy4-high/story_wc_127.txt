In the amber grotto at dusk, Orra Vell laid out her case of glass ampoules with the care of someone setting a table for difficult relatives. 
She was a counterfeit emotion dealer, licensed to sell courage to clerks, remorse to judges, and homesickness to soldiers who had never left the province. 
The city preferred feelings measured, bottled, and taxed, because untaxed feelings collected in stairwells and became petitions, riots, or worse, honest conversation. 
Her stock was never wholly false; half truths were the secret of the trade, since a pure lie slid off the skin like rain from wax. 
Professionally thorough, Orra recorded the weather, the donor of each memory, the number of times a vial had been warmed, and the exact shade of a client's envy. 
Her nicest instrument was a fragrance chemist's blotter petal, a thin paper leaf once dipped in cedar, iris, and the salt of a grieving woman's cuff. 
When she drew it across a palm, sensing through static electricity told her more than confession could, because the body's small charges stuttered around what the mouth protected. 
At dusk the grotto's walls held the last light like warm syrup, and each amber seam gave off a dry, patient crackle as buyers shuffled in. 
That evening, buyers kept to the far ledge, because the resin beneath the floor was changing into a civic organ meant to feel the whole district. 
The alders had promised that magistrates linked to it would understand evictions, hunger, and factory coughs before voting on winter relief. 
Orra had entered the trade to fuse insight with empathy, after her father explained a famine so precisely that no one dared comfort him. 
The project seemed like work without invoices, and she had volunteered a case of calm for the final calibration. 
Then the chief broker handed her a sealed order asking her to subsume every donated feeling into a soft, agreeable contentment that could not accuse anyone. 
He had explained it with cheerful brutality: As the transformation completes, the city will inherit the strongest feeling in the resin. 
She had learned affectionate detachment from the fragrance chemist who gave her the blotter petal and could mourn a shipwreck while labeling samples. 
That manner had kept her tender without carelessness, and tonight the brokers counted on it. 
Before obeying, she made one last check, because a sealed order was another customer and customers deserved clean glass. 
She passed the fragrance chemist's blotter petal over the calm case, feeling for the tiny lift that meant honest mixing. 
Instead, the petal shivered with sharp grief, factory dust, a child's unanswered knock, and officials deciding how much sorrow a budget could bear. 
The calm had been cut with half truths, not to soothe the city but to hide the causes of pain inside a pleasant fog. 
Sensing through static electricity, she followed each charge back through the amber floor and found, under a velvet cord, workers waiting to be ignored. 
Their names were in her ledger, each paired with grief she had bottled, paid for, and almost called a service. 
Orra loved ledgers as she loved warm hands: they recorded contact without pretending that contact made anyone whole. 
That fond, dry knowledge steadied her as she broke the ampoules and let the blotter petal drink their counterfeit sweetness. 
She did not merely despise the order; she revised it with the patience she used on a nervous bride or a debtor. 
Into the petal she pressed her old shame, the donors' true charges, and the eviction names, allowing hard clarity to subsume the pleasant fog. 
The paper curled, warm as a cheek, and when she set it on the vat's tongue the amber accepted it without drama. 
As the transformation completes, the linked magistrates feel not comfort but the shape of each loss, held steady enough to be understood. 
Orra imagined their faces, annoyed, then ashamed, then useful, and felt affectionate distance for a student with ink on his chin. 
The chief broker called her ruined, which was probably half true, since she had lost her license, stock, and the clean glass that made loss manageable. 
She folded her ledger, nodded to the workers with a clerk's courtesy, and left while dusk turned amber into something almost like daylight.
