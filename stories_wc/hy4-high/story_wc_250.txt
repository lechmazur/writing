Rain had stopped, but the cobalt dock after rain kept each drop, so boards, ropes, and water held one breath.  Lanterns stayed hooded under curfew, and the unlit canal carried stillwater tension, a hand closed over a knife.  Mara Venn, the silent canal guardian, watched inspectors question Ila beside her father's grain barge.  Ila's brother Niven lay under a tarpaulin with salt sacks, lacking paper to breathe inside the city.  The chief had promised leniency for a name, and fear had brought Ila to the edge of giving one.  Mara had not spoken since the flood, when her shouted warning came too late for a boatload of sleepers.  Silence had made her useful, not empty, because she read palms, shoulders, and the pulse beneath a jaw.  The dock called it silent empathy, and to Mara it was simply the only honest way to listen.  Through that listening she knew Ila hated betrayal, but the word tarpaulin had reached the tip of her tongue.  All rescue had to happen within the edge of a half-spoken secret, between Ila's first words and the noun that would open the sacks.  Mara touched the dental mirror in her coat, a small round glass on a steel stem left by her mother, the dock dentist.  Mara used it to inspect gate hinges, see around shutters, and tonight catch the first star through a tear in the cloud.  Her father had run these locks before her and had taught her the code of creating patterns with shutters, used by pilots when lamps were forbidden.  He said the work was to teach darkness the value of stars, because a black canal could carry a map if given points to hold.  After he drowned in that flood, her purpose in every blackout was to teach darkness the value of stars.  Now Ila's mouth moved, and the chief leaned close enough for his lantern hood to brush her cheek.  In the dental mirror Mara saw Ila's eyes flick toward the tarpaulin and back with the helpless rhythm of a snagged rope.  Mara set the mirror beneath a crack of sky, angled a white spark onto the first shutter, and began to weave.  Her hands were spiralingly calm, each turn smaller and surer, as if danger were a spiral she could enter without waking it.  She opened shutters one, four, two, seven, and the slats cut reflected starlight into a ladder of broken light.  On wet boards and on the canal below, the ladder repeated itself as Crane Passage, meaning clear tide for grain and no boarding.  Every inspector learned that pattern before the gate names, because the lockkeeper's shutters outranked a junior warrant during tide.  The chief looked up, annoyed, then unsure, while Ila's half-formed word died behind her teeth.  Mara did not look at Ila, because silent empathy had told her that hope could bind as tightly as fear if it arrived without accusation.  She closed three shutters, opened five, and sent a second ladder toward the dark mouth of the western lock.  Old Sem, the barge pilot, had sailed with her father and had waited for this pattern with one hand on the mooring line.  He cast off without a lamp, and the barge drifted so gently that rain beads on its tarpaulin did not roll.  Crane Passage during tide required every officer to stand clear until the gates had finished moving.  Mara pulled the gate lever by inches, and the mechanism answered with only a submerged groan, leaving the stillwater tension unbroken.  The canal rose beneath the barge, lifting Niven's hidden weight as if the water had decided to keep a secret.  Ila exhaled, and the chief, denied a completed sentence, pretended he had never needed one.  He chalked the movement as tide passage, because a ledger preferred clean columns to the mess of a girl almost betraying her brother.  Mara kept weaving until the barge reached the bend, making shutters breathe open and closed so star points climbed the wet dark.  At the bend, a corner of the tarpaulin lifted, and the dental mirror showed Mara one small hand raised without a sound.  She lowered the glass, and across the dock Ila lowered her eyes, the answer passing between them as silent empathy rather than thanks.  No one had said tarpaulin, so no one could prove there had been a secret, only a tide, a signal, and a boat obeying the stars.  Mara shut the last shutter, and darkness held the small points it had been given, as if it had finally understood their value.
