I returned to the abandoned spaceport repair bay in the murmur of half-remembered dreams, when the dead docking clocks still clicked behind my sleep.  As a sensory map maker, I charted the taste of magnetic fields, the pitch of cold metal, and the colors pain left on air.  The port had been closed for twenty-three years, but its repair looms still slept under the floor like buried strings.  My mother had serviced those looms before the accident that emptied the port.  Earlier she sent me a green glass shard from a cracked approach beacon, wrapped in tissue that smelled of engine mint.  The report said a coolant fire killed eleven mechanics, including her, while the passenger liner Ilya was already safely away.  Every sensory map I made of that night had the same blank ring, as if a thumb had pressed wet ink.  I had come back to discover the weight of a withheld truth.  In my mother's last calibration book, the final line read Tone: None.  Even an empty bay has a tone, usually dust, absence, and old bills.  At the console, I saw what grief had hidden: an instrument setting.  Grief would bend any map around the missing fact, so she had stripped the channel bare to leave its weight measurable.  I set my dial to Tone: None, and the bay went terrifyingly honest.  The green glass shard matched the central socket's notch, which told me she had meant me to use it there.  Its edge caught a light no lamp made and threw green threads across the dormant cables.  Mother's logs called the repair trick weaving between worlds, borrowing an unbroken plate from a neighboring branch of reality.  Mother's crew had used it for hulls, never for lives.  The loom's breach was cyclically spontaneous, returning with each dead docking pulse yet choosing a different second inside it.  I lay in a mechanic's sling, palms on the cables, with the shard under my tongue.  When the first impulse came, I followed the taste of engine mint through parallel universes.  In one universe, the port was full of leaving passengers and my mother was too young to know me.  In another, the Ilya had burned above the bay, and every clock had stopped at one white minute.  None held the whole story, but each had a hollow shaped like the same silence.  I stitched one landmark to the next, using cold solder, orange disinfectant, and the pressure behind my eyes as thread.  The growing braid pulled me toward the accident.  At the ninth recurrence, I saw her standing at the console in a smoke-stained coat.  The Ilya was falling toward the bay with its landing fields torn open, too heavy for any sane repair.  If she let it strike, thousands would die; if she sealed the port, the eleven around her would be crushed.  She chose to redirect the failing vector through parallel universes, splitting impact into survivable fragments of disaster.  The passengers lived in borrowed branches, but those branches had no room for the mechanics who held this universe steady.  Mother fed their names into the freight scale to ballast the seam, then erased the readout so no survivor would claim the debt.  That was the withheld truth: not a fire or desertion, but a purchase made with eleven lives and hidden out of mercy.  With my instruments still set to Tone: None, I felt its mass without the flattering fog of forgiveness.  The old freight scale woke under the floor and displayed 1109 kilograms, the weight of every unclaimed death plus every life that had forgotten it was saved.  I had wanted that number to accuse her; instead it accused the silence I had been mapping as a blank.  The loom was still programmed to trim that silence each cycle, leaving the same white minute in every branch.  I could not restore the dead or collapse the borrowed lives back into one innocent world.  I used the shard to redirect the return current away from the official story and into every branch that had been spared.  I set the shard in the socket and let the names pass through me as heat, salt, and a low green note.  Across those worlds, strangers woke with inexplicable grief and the knowledge that their lives had been paid for.  The scale did not empty, but its weight no longer sat on one hidden room.  I left before the next pulse, carrying no verdict, only a map that admitted its own blank.
