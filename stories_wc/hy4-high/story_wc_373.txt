At low tide, the pirate market hidden beneath a rotting pier opened its lanterns, and Mara Vale slipped between piles slick with green weed. 
Tea urns breathed cinnamon over coils of rope, children sold warm buns beside powder barrels, and every blade kept a polite distance from every sleeve. 
The place held Mara in the comforting unease of home, where a quilt might hide a pistol and a lullaby might be a warning. 
As a moonshine runner's daughter, she knew that silence could be warmer than talk and far more easily betrayed. 
From moon-set until sunrise the market lived under a truce of hush, with no spoken names, no sworn prices, and no pistol cocked above the tables. 
By custom, a bargain marred by spoken treachery before sunrise became forfeit, but an unheard accusation was only smoke. 
That was why Mara had come to capture the sound of trust breaking, not to steal coin and not to beg for mercy. 
At sunrise, Ives would pass an oilskin-wrapped route ledger to a customs pilot, and the names inside would send Silas Vale to the gallows. 
The ledger claimed his hand had sold the creek paths, though Mara had watched those hands shake too badly to hold a pen. 
She had until sunrise to thwart the sale, and she had no witness the captains could not buy. 
In her palm lay the antique watch fob her mother had brought from a Nassau clockmaker, a brass acorn on a worn chain. 
Unscrewed, the acorn held a smoked wax drum, a mousehair needle, and a spring that could draw one minute of sound across the wax. 
Runners kept last promises in such fobs when no throat would speak. 
Mara was curiously steadfast about hers, refusing to trade it for bread, medicine, or passage north. 
Her father called the fault a gift, since creek work rewarded anyone who could remain gentle while listening for boots. 
He had also taught her that a liar could be made to finish a sentence the truthful would never start, but only through false starts. 
Begin, falter, begin again, and leave a gap shaped so precisely that the guilty heart mistakes it for shelter. 
At Aunt Sable's tea stall, Mara wound the fob and began to ask for a cup, then forgot the last word. 
Sable smoothed her apron with the same hand that had braided Mara's hair after her mother's funeral. 
A few berths away, Captain Ives slid his palm over the oilskin packet, then pretended the gesture was only a scratch. 
Mara lifted the fob as if to show its chain, let it knock a saucer, and apologized with her eyebrows. 
The spring scratched a tiny squeal, useless as evidence and perfect as bait, because Ives relaxed before anyone else did. 
She hummed one note of the creek-song, stopped where the chorus would have named a landing, and moved nearer his table. 
Each false beginning gave her a lawful reason to cross another plank, and the truce protected a stammering child more tenderly than an accusation. 
By the fourth attempt, Mara knew Sable had seen the wax, and she knew Ives had not, which meant only one of them feared the recording. 
Mara returned, set the open fob beside brown sugar, and made her last false start about whether Silas had trusted the wrong friend. 
She left the sentence broken at trusted, the way a runner leaves a gate open for someone being followed. 
Sable filled the gap from habit, with a breath first, then a confession spoken above the hush that she had written the routes herself to spare her son from the press-gang. 
The fob caught the spoon, Ives's chair scrape, Sable's swallowed apology, and the thin freeze of fifty people deciding whether to hear her. 
That was the sound of trust breaking, not a crash but a room discovering that its quiet had been owned by someone else. 
Mara closed the acorn, walked to the bell rope, and pressed the warmed fob to the brass horn as a customs launch bumped the piles. 
The wax gave back Sable's breath, the chair, the forbidden confession, and no captain could buy the room's hearing. 
Elders tore open the oilskin, fed its pages to the tea fire, and declared the bargain forfeit until sunrise, so the sale was thwarted without one shot. 
No one cheered; Sable set a bun beside Mara's hand, and the kindness was terrible. 
Mara kept her hand on the fob instead.
