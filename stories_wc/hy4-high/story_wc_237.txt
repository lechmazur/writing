The floating rock tea ceremony platform held a saturated hush, as if blue dusk, rain, and incense had soaked every sound.  Beneath its mossy rim, two storm fronts leaned close, one spent and iron gray, the other green with unfallen hail.  The elders called that narrow pause a breath between storms, and they taught that meaning could change only there.  All rune revisions happened during a held breath; the guests lent their lungs to roof the platform against the next storm.  No one inhaled after the kettle lid lifted, and even the steam seemed to wait, thick with amber and rain.  Mira, a budding rune scholar, knelt beside the kettle with a crow quill trimmed tip tucked under her palm.  She had come to transform scars into stories, because the village had begun to greet her arm before her face.  Three winters earlier, a rune house fire had folded a roof over the children she was teaching to read.  She had carried them out one by one, and a hot door frame had written white seams across her shoulder.  Later, neighbors called those seams bad luck, then destiny, then a warning, never asking what her hands had done.  She did not want the marks prettied; she wanted them given mouths, so truth could travel farther than pity.  Master Senn poured tea into seven cups, and each guest kept both lips sealed.  The amber in the cups crystallizes when stressed, turning fear into clear little knives around any forced memory.  A whole feather would prophesy, but the trimmed tip could only record what a trembling hand admitted.  Mira had practiced noble phrases in the mirror, yet when she touched ink to skin, the first phrase hardened.  Crystal climbed from the old seams, cagelike and bright, because she was trying to sound brave instead of honest.  The saturated hush tightened; even the green storm seemed to hush its hail against the held roof of breath.  Mira had to shed the glossy casing before any rune could sit on her shoulder.  No knife was allowed in the ceremony, and force would only feed the amber more fear.  Master Senn opened one hand toward Mira's childhood, a gesture used when logic had failed.  She loosened the shell by revisiting youthful superstitions she had once hidden from older scholars.  Before runes, her grandmother had taught her to turn cups, count knots, feed the wind, and name crows backward.  Mira repeated each one, feeling absurd, until her busy mind stopped trying to command them.  These gestures were older in her body than shame, and the amber softened with her surrender.  The first crystal scale shed from the longest seam and chimed softly against the cup.  Mira shed more of the armor with each silly rite, not because the rites were true, but because they made room.  The guests held, and the green storm pressed one bruised knuckle against the edge of the rock.  In that stretch, memory arrived not as a trial but as children coughing under a smoky beam.  She remembered the youngest boy gripping her sleeve, the hot latch, the ridiculous lesson about vowels she had been teaching.  She also remembered screaming at the roof, losing her temper, and wanting to run before the last child was found.  That was the part she had buried, and the buried part had made every scar mean only failure.  The crow quill trimmed tip warmed in her hand, as crow ink does when a true account comes near.  Mira dipped it in tea, then wrote along the first seam: I was afraid and I went back.  The rune did not glow; it settled into skin like a seed finding wet soil.  On the next seam she wrote: The children counted with me, and their counting kept my hands useful.  On the last she wrote: I was not chosen, only standing where the door was hot.  The sentences were plain, and that plainness felt riskier than any polished legend she had rehearsed.  A tear slid down but could not fall, caught with everything else during a held breath.  Master Senn lifted two fingers, and the guests held while Mira wrote the final line.  Across every seam, she wrote: This is a story, so you may answer it.  Then the held breath ended, and the saturated hush broke into rain, clinking cups, and aching lungs.  The new storm found stories rooted where scars had kept silence.
