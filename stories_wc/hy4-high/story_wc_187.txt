In the hour after fog becomes solid, the stormlit observatory above clouds always entered a deepening hush.  Lightning moved under the floors, and wool muffled the bells.  Ione, the summer-quill archivist, waited at the north rail with a warmed quill.  Only such quills marked cloud-vellum without melting it.  On her desk lay the prism catalogued as crystal whispering forecourt after rain.  Cut from old plaza drains, it repeated voices left on wet stone after storms.  Archivists called those echoes collective memory, but Ione heard a crowd, not a chord.  She had been preemptively nostalgic since childhood, mourning clean sheets before they were slept in.  The habit made her careful, then ambitious, then afraid of beautiful arrangements.  After her mother vanished from the flood rolls, Ione joined the Archive to sculpt identity from experience rather than decree.  The Council wanted one flood story, polished for schoolchildren and durable for statues.  Ione wanted ferries, quarrels, soup kitchens, and her mother's refused place at the west gate.  Her method was examining shadow lengths at monuments, where bronze rods were tuned to public events.  Honest versions cast long, ragged shadows; official simplifications cast short ones.  On this night, every shadow had turned white.  Solid fog covered the lower terraces and lay around the monument court like unmarked paper.  Stormlight rose from below, throwing each measurement upward into the rails.  The Keeper ordered Ione to certify the clean account before dawn.  Afterward, summer-quill pages would be sealed, and the prism scored to one chorus.  Ione lifted the prism and heard not rain but people trying not to panic.  A child wanted a blue cup, a rope seller counted boats, and a woman denied that the west gate opened.  Ione knew the last voice, which had once called her in from summer dust.  Her mother was alive in the crystal, not as a ghost but as a correction.  The approved rescue had Councillors opening that gate and leading the poor to safety.  The prism remembered neighbors carrying neighbors through the forecourt while the gate stayed barred.  If Ione signed, that crowded truth would thin into one solemn sentence.  If she refused, the Keeper would take her quill and appoint someone less attached to absences.  She set the prism down and stepped into the hush on the white court.  Solid fog gave under her boots, making the city feel recently invented.  At the first monument, the Council's story measured seven handspans; the boats measured thirteen.  At the second, a baker's riot lengthened the rod past the Keeper's decree.  At the third, children carrying lanterns outmeasured every named official.  The numbers did not fit the page she had been told to certify, yet they fit one another.  That agreement demanded conjecture, the act the Council had forbidden in a season of certainties.  Ione let herself conjecture that solid fog returned every angle the city had refused to see.  If so, the prism was witness, not relic, and each monument was a question, not a clock.  She carried that thought back, listening for authority above the storm and hearing only cloth-wrapped bells.  The Keeper waited by her desk with the seal heated.  He said, "Certify the rescue, and your mother receives a respectable footnote."  The offer was small, precise, and shaped exactly like the wound she had polished for years.  Because she was preemptively nostalgic, she could already hear herself accepting and grieving the lost crowd.  That imagined grief was tidy, and tidiness had once seemed like mercy.  She warmed the quill until its nib smelled of cut grass.  Then she wrote a ledger of angles, voices, lengths, arguments, and blank spaces.  She entered her conjecture as conjecture, not law, so uncertainty could remain part of the record.  The Keeper reached for the seal, but the prism began to answer each blank with several names.  Their overlapping voices filled the observatory without breaking the hush, deepening it instead.  Ione understood then that collective memory was not a finished statue but weather passing through many hands.  She wrote her mother's name among the boat counters, leaving room for the next correction.  The page dried slowly in stormlight, a shared past still warm enough to be shaped.
