Three nights after the resuscitation, Mara returned to the abandoned carnival funhouse with an opera cloak clasp under her tongue and a hatred that felt almost like desire.  Its repulsive allure had outlasted rain, bankruptcy, and fire, because rot had learned to imitate perfume.  Candy mold silvered the booth, wet velvet hugged the turnstile, and painted clowns shone thicker than paint.  The doctors called the glow beneath her skin lingering luminance, as if a polite name could make the afterlight of the defibrillator less intimate.  It glimmered in her palms and hair, unable to choose whether to bless or betray.  Mara had been photographically cursed since her first school portrait; every camera showed only the thing she most feared, which had learned to pose closer each year.  Fear had come back with her from the blackwater cistern beneath the mirror maze, where paramedics had dragged her out eleven minutes late and shocked her into a second life.  Since then it visited her son at midnight, leaving his pillows damp with aquarium water.  She had come not for closure, but to trick fear into revealing its weakness, because a thing that could follow a heartbeat home had to have a doorway.  Her whispering advisor waited beneath the ticket booth, folded into a raincoat the color of old saliva.  His name was Sable, the safety man who had once sold her mother one last dare.  He had advised the owners to keep the mirrors cracked for atmosphere, and her mother had vanished among them, leaving only an opera cloak and that clasp.  Guilt had thinned his voice, but vanity kept him advising, and Mara forgave him just enough to use him.  She had learned the method through paradoxical notes in a dream dictionary he pressed into her hand while hospital monitors still counted her borrowed heart.  The book's margins contradicted every printed comfort: DROWNING meant count dry steps, MIRROR meant close your eyes, and FEAR meant offer a wrong answer.  She walked backward with closed eyes through the corridor she had already left, counting boards that stayed dry, and found the mirror maze behind a curtain of silk cobwebs.  Sable came after her, whispering that fear loved courage best, but Mara noticed his lips shaping words before his throat produced them.  The realization did not frighten her, which was the first proof that fear no longer owned the whole house of her body.  At the last mirror, she took the opera cloak clasp from her mouth and held its black stone against the place where her pulse fluttered.  The clasp had once fastened velvet across her mother's throat, and its pin could still draw blood, which made it honest in a dishonest building.  She fastened it over her breast instead, following the final note: To unbosom the heart, close the cloak and speak the lie that fear wants to hear.  The mirror showed no reflection; being photographically cursed, she had become a plate on which fear must print whatever it fed upon.  It printed Sable first, because his guilt was sweet, and fear wore his whispering mouth like a damp glove.  Then it printed her son in a little bed, the blackwater, and a billion future breaths lined up like tickets waiting to be torn.  Mara smiled with an appetite she despised, because the lingering luminance had taught her how beautifully terror could glisten.  "I was never afraid of you," she said, beginning to unbosom the lie as if placing a sugary wafer on her tongue.  "I loved the shine you left, and I would choose another death to keep it."  Fear could not resist correction, since every lie offered it a throne, and the mirror cracked open with a voice like wet paper fed through teeth.  "No," it hissed through Sable, "you fear me because I live in the next breath; spend one without begging for another, and I have no mouth."  Mara spent that breath, neither inhaling nor asking, and let the afterlight flare once.  The black stone took the correction as a photograph, the only kind her curse had ever promised to keep.  The clasp snapped shut, Sable spoke in his own recovered voice, and the mirrors showed an empty room.  At dawn her son woke dry, and the funhouse lost its perfume, leaving only mold, wet wood, and shame.  Mara carried the closed clasp home, still beautiful and faintly obscene, with no light beneath her skin.
