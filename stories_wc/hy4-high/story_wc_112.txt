The Kestrel xenobiology research station hung above an ice plain where dusk rose from below, turning the world to blue glass. 
Mara Venn was the obliging sky mechanic who kept aerostats, weather vanes, and winglike solar sails alive above it. 
She answered every alarm with "I am coming," even when it belonged to someone else's pride. 
The labs were mechanically warm, heated by pipes that hummed beside frosted windows, and that icy warmth suited her: precise, distant, quietly merciful. 
Tonight, however, the summons she obeyed came from a dead woman. 
Iona Pell had claimed the station's translucent vesper moths woke not into tomorrow but into neighboring presents. 
The committee had laughed, revoked her lab, and sent her home to die before an apology letter arrived. 
Mara kept the letter because Pell's equations filled its back, studying them between repairs. 
She wanted to prove that time moves sideways and then restore Pell's name and stop linear thawing. 
In the cradle, their wings lay folded, saving last heat for subdued awakenings no linear record could explain. 
One instrument could hold the needed proof: the magnetic nexus at twilight, a cobalt device bolted beneath the observation deck. 
At equinox, when the sun balanced on the ice and iron clouds aligned, it focused the station's field into a single listening point. 
Pell had called that point a border, not a place, where a whole creature could be read from one resting scale. 
Mara understood the claim through holographic principles she used to rebuild damaged wing diagrams from fragments of reflected light. 
If the border held, the projector would reveal a wingbeat from a moment absent from the chamber's forward record. 
Only within the hush of beating wings at rest could the test begin, in the eighty seconds after the last visible flutter and before the thermal echoes faded. 
Outside that hush, the station's clocks would insist on before and after, and the sideways evidence would collapse. 
Doctor Sark, who had inherited Pell's title and caution, stood by the cradle with a shutdown form. 
"Seal the deck valve," he said; "we cannot waste another cycle on a dead theory." 
She nodded, because nodding was easier than refusing, and went to the service alcove where the valve waited beside the old calibration lever. 
The lever was mechanically warm from the heater circuit, and its brass handle held the only heat that felt offered rather than commanded. 
Pulled down, it would seal the valve, dim the lamps, and send the full battery charge to the magnetic nexus. 
Left up, it would let Sark record the moths as failed stock and leave Pell's name under error. 
She listened to the absent thunder of stopped wings, said yes to Sark, and meant Pell. 
She began the alignment through holographic principles that turned stored wingtraces into a ghost of summer over the cradle. 
The first wing stirred without lifting, and the motion monitor logged nothing. 
On the projection, a second wing opened a quarter beat beside a present the chamber's clocks had not recorded. 
Sark whispered that it was reflection, but Mara adjusted the cobalt device with her palm until the border became a lattice of quiet, subdued awakenings. 
Each moth appeared in a slightly different now, none ahead of another, all touching at the folded edges of their wings. 
The clocks kept their innocent faces while the missing wingbeat passed sideways from one resting body to the next. 
Then the pressure alarm complained, and blue numerals counted down the last of the hush. 
Sark reached for shutdown, because habit is often colder than fear. 
Mara put her hand on the mechanically warm lever and pulled the lever down. 
The lamps died, the heaters fell silent, and stars leaned close while the cobalt point held the moths' impossible stillness. 
The magnetic nexus at twilight completed its sweep, and the hologram printed itself onto the cold observation glass. 
The image remained after the power faded: a wingbeat copied from beside the present, delicate enough to deny and exact enough to measure. 
Sark logged it as evidence, not theory, and added Iona Pell's name before the automatic clock stamp. 
By morning she would repair the valve, answer alarms, and return the moths to cloud tanks under gentler protocols. 
But frost bloomed over the printed wingbeat, and that icy warmth moved sideways into her, asking her to say yes to herself.
