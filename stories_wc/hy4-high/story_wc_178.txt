In the ancient Babylonian palace reimagined as a house of observations, dusk arrived with the patience of a careful scribe.  The last king had fled, the foreign general had not entered, and every corridor stood between regimes.  Water clocks, reed flutes, and murmuring clerks kept a dusky harmony, as if the building feared a loud claim.  Nidintu, the frugal astronomer, had stayed because wax, oil, and tablets were cheaper to guard than to replace.  She patched her sandals with census cord, reused broken exercise tablets, and measured eclipses with a lamp no larger than a lentil.  The priests called such thrift insulting, but she had learned that empires vanish first from their storerooms.  Her only inheritance from old Nabu-zeri was a magic square tile, blue glazed and no wider than two palms.  Nine numerals occupied its grid, each row summing to fifteen, each column pretending to obey.  It was empirically ironic: the more exactly she checked the tile against the sky, the more its errors accused the official star lists.  Nabu-zeri had taught her that devotion hidden in numbers could outlast devotion shouted from towers.  A note beneath his last tablet named that secrecy encrypted devotion, but it gave no addressee.  With scribes burning drafts to please whichever ruler came next, Nidintu wanted to seal a covenant with the unknown.  Not with Marduk, whose fees were fixed, nor with the general, whose taxes soon would be, but with whatever remained unnamed beyond calculation.  She believed an honest record of ignorance might protect the city better than another confident omen. 
For three evenings she set the tile on the throne room floor, now painted as a flat midnight sky.  Around it she laid ration tablets, eclipse reports, door plans, and clay tokens saved from discarded offerings.  Nothing had been bought for the rite; thrift was the first condition Nabu-zeri had left her.  On the fourth evening, a courier's horn sounded beyond the river, and clerks began hiding names.  Nidintu did not hurry, because haste wasted wick and the tile's logic rewarded measured hesitation.  She began to uncloak the concealed addressee through schematic representations, not incantations, since diagrams could be checked by anyone who survived her.  First she drew the nine cells as nine courts, replacing each numeral with the old number of lamps allotted there.  Then she aligned palace doors with Moon, Venus, and the slow wanderer whose omen the previous regime had altered.  She marked where official lists disagreed with her cheap observations, and every disagreement became a black dot.  The black dots formed a second square, tilted like a shadow at sunset.  That was the empirical joke she had expected: official falsehoods, plotted honestly, supplied the missing geometry.  At its center appeared neither god nor planet, but an empty chamber labeled on no palace plan.  Nidintu knew that chamber; Nabu-zeri had once led her there to hear the absence after a hymn.  He had called it the antechamber of the unknown, and he had refused to furnish it.  Now she understood his encrypted devotion: loyalty not to a throne, but to the unclaimed interval in which truth could still be revised.  That interval was where the palace stood between regimes, and where a covenant might bind without being owned. 
She carried the tile to the empty chamber, past porters already lifting lion weights from the doors.  The room held only dust, a cracked water jar, and blue light shared by walls and sky.  Nidintu set the magic square tile on the threshold and copied the dot diagram beside it with wet soot.  For an offering she used the jar's last water, because the unknown had no altar and she had no surplus.  She spoke the terms plainly: the palace would keep a ledger of uncertainties, and the unknown would keep the stars impartial under new hands.  The tile cooled, the numerals blurred, then settled into a square whose center was a deliberate blank.  Every row still made fifteen if the blank was read as five and left unnamed.  Flutes, clocks, and armed footsteps outside found a dusky harmony again, softer than triumph and more durable.  At dawn, foreign scribes found a frugal astronomer entering corrections in a cheap clay ledger, and they mistook the blank square for damage.  Nidintu let them think so, since encrypted devotion had always preferred a modest cloak.
