The hour when patterns break themselves came when hooks sang in drawers and wells forgot their water. 
That night, the sleepwalking constellations climbed down from their charts into the sound wave garden. 
The Bear went blind first; the Seven Children butted silver fences behind him. 
Rows of glass reeds held sighs, drum gourds held thunder, and the wind purred a feral lullaby over them. 
The garden transliterates emotions into forms: fear brings nettles, longing brings ear shaped mushrooms, anger brings toothed reeds. 
Only a cool-headed fisher crossed it without growing a heart thicket. 
Marra Vale had such a head, nailed shut around grief. 
Her daughter Ila, born during a raid without a cry, had faded toward that quiet ever since. 
Marra had come to catch the echo of a goddess, the hush after the goddess sang land out of flood. 
Priests kept the song; Ila needed the note that followed and taught the world to answer. 
Old Nessa taught the method: by voicing silence, a fisher baited the absence that prayers scared away. 
Marra carried one rail spike from the drowned railway, black with salt. 
A waxed cord tied it to her wrist by seven careful knots. 
It was a hook for a pause, not a weapon. 
To reach the central well, she had to zigzag between listening beds, because straight paths were grooves for dreaming stars. 
The Bear swung left; she went right. 
The Net opened; she slipped underneath, humming hush, hush. 
A dark mushroom of worry rose beside her boot. 
She let it be, since denial would have become briars and given the stars a ladder. 
Behind her, the sleepwalking constellations nosed through rows, murmuring maps they had forgotten. 
Their old patterns broke into raw shapes from which new lullabies are cut. 
At the well lay no water, only the pause before a note, black and round. 
Marra knelt, opened her mouth, and made no sound a priest could write. 
She shaped a voiceless O from breath, lips, and the stillness under her ribs. 
Silence, voiced, answered in its own feral grammar. 
Because the garden transliterates emotions, her guarded grief appeared as one clean dark vowel. 
She drove the rail spike into that vowel, not hard but exactly. 
The iron sang low as a wolf to cubs, and the pause bent into a hook. 
Marra paid out cord, knot by knot, into a depth no lake had owned. 
Above her, the constellations smelled quiet bait and stumbled closer, all milk and claws. 
They wanted sleep, but hunger lived in their dream and sharpened their edges. 
A shout would have saved her lungs and doomed Ila, nettling the well shut. 
So Marra held the voiceless O and rocked, soothing a child with teeth. 
The cord tightened. 
Something on the other end did not fight like a fish; it turned and listened. 
Marra felt the goddess in the tug, not a voice but the warm absence a voice leaves. 
It carried floodwater, first reeds, and a singer who had spent everything. 
Her cool head cracked. 
The echo showed Ila old and silent, and Ila laughing with mud on her knees. 
Marra understood that an echo was not a possession but a path. 
If she clenched it, Ila would inherit another locked room. 
So she loosened her wrist, kept the cord gentle, and voiced one final silence. 
The echo came willingly, small as a seed and wild as a nip at the thumb. 
It settled inside the rail spike, making the iron warm. 
When she pulled, the spike came free, leaving a dark tooth in the hush. 
The constellations woke, shy and bristling, and arranged themselves into patterns no chart yet knew. 
Dawn found the sound wave garden purring softer, its nettles folded, its little teeth clean. 
Marra walked home with the warm spike held close, following no straight line. 
In the hut, Ila lay blue and waiting, fists curled like sleeping claws. 
Marra set the iron by her ear and voiced silence, not to command but to answer. 
The goddess hush passed through the child like a feral lullaby, soft, toothed, and alive. 
Ila opened her mouth and made one rough small cry. 
Marra laughed then, quietly, while outside the new constellations slept in their broken shapes. 
Hush, hush, little wild thing, the stars are only walking.
