During the reconstruction, the volcanic hot springs of Bell Caldera smelled of wet lime, sulfur, and sawed pine.  Masons rechanneled boiling water through new ceramic veins while children carried basket loads of black pumice from the collapsed bathhouse.  Every repair was provisional, but the Provisional Council wanted one old certainty back.  At sunrise they would wake the embryo in amber beneath Pool Nine and let its implanted destiny prescribe the terraces, trades, and marriages of the next generation.  Mara Venn, an insurgent grandmother with a cane of tempered pipe, had spent the war breaking such prescriptions.  Now she stood among the steam vents as a saboteur again, though she had told her granddaughter Nell she was only inspecting the drains.  Her motive was not hatred of custom.  She wanted to evolve tradition with intent, because the old First Root rite had once named her grandson an ash diver at eight and sent him into a cooling flue that took his lungs.  Nell, trained to lay freer channels, would be assigned to the same flues if the Council revived the root tables.  The relic lay in a basalt seed casket sealed with mineral resin, a plant embryo no longer than Mara's thumb, curled inside clear gold.  Centuries ago growers had bred it as a living charter: its roots would enter the warm aqueducts, signal every grafted vine in the settlement, and rebuild the old social pattern as faithfully as roof beams.  A failed raid on the Ministry greenhouse had left her accidentally telepathic with plants; she heard root pressure as hunger, mildew as gossip, and the mineral dreams of moss as a low chorus behind her thoughts.  Sulfur moss along Pool Nine kept repeating three notes: cold, iron, wait.  Those notes matched the marks on a strip of oiled cloth folded in Mara's sleeve.  It was the tail of an abandoned kite that had hung above the bathhouse since the evacuation, too torn for play and too ordinary for confiscation.  Mara's daughter Sela, the rebel cartographer who died wiring the pumps, had knotted and scorched a cryptic script into that tail before the last children ran.  Now the moss pronounced them as valve positions, because Sela had invented the script from the branching habits of ferns that grew only in hot mineral fog.  By decoding a cryptic script on the tail of an abandoned kite, Mara could unseal the casket and wash the embryo before the Council woke it.  An insurgent learned to break seals, not revise ceremonies; a grandmother learned that breaking can leave a family holding nothing but pieces.  The Council horn sounded from the lower terraces.  Mara climbed into the service niche above Pool Nine, where hot vapor silvered her braid and made the old kite cloth soften in her hand.  Below, Nell and the other apprentices were laying a new open channel that would give every garden a vote in water distribution.  The plants around the niche urged haste, but not panic; ferns remembered Sela's hands, moss remembered cold, iron, wait.  Mara read the tail stroke by stroke, pressed the cold latch, turned the iron wheel, and waited through nine heartbeats while the pump reversed.  Then she unsealed the amber lip with her pipe cane, not as a blow but as the final lever Sela had drawn.  Warm water entered the golden cell, and the embryo's dream struck Mara like a child reciting rules in sleep: root left, bind right, feed the named, starve the unnamed.  She answered with every green voice the springs had given her, a chorus of moss, fern, algae, and young vines from Nell's new channel.  The rules loosened like silt.  The black filament of command at the embryo's heart dissolved into the mineral flood, and what remained was a small, startled urge toward light.  By the time the Council guards reached the stairs, Mara had set the open amber cup into the First Root basin.  She took Nell's hand, placed the decoded kite tail beside the basin, and began the old planting chant in her cracked voice.  At the line where elders once named each child's station, Mara paused and invited the apprentices to speak the work they had chosen.  Nell chose channels open to any garden that changed its mind.  The embryo, washed clear of its destiny, leaned toward the noise of their choosing, and Mara let the old song end with a new verse.
