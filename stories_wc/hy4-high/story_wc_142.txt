In the last wet month before privatization, the perpetual carnival afloat on a raft city kept dancing because empty decks could be surveyed as vacant stock. 
Mara Venn, its culture shock absorber, explained leases to mask families and drum etiquette to dry-shore investors. 
She had taken the post to navigate gentrification: outside money could lift the sinking pontoons, but only if old neighbors survived the lifting. 
Her dual allegiance was official and intimate: she wore the Transition Office badge, while her mother stitched every carnival banner. 
Mara was orderly intuitive: she kept lease ledgers in clean columns and trusted the hitch in her chest when a smiling clause hid eviction. 
The map she copied listed each unoccupied lane at dawn as private inventory. 
The old families could not read the map; the surveyors could not read the painted walls. 
That gap made her useful. 
She decided to reorganize the floating ward through messages encoded in graffiti art, the one newspaper nobody could confiscate without admitting it could read. 
A foundation stone chip, no bigger than a tooth, gave the plan a legal edge. 
Struck from the first raft's granite ballast, it yielded dust that old harbor law recognized as marking a common threshold. 
Privatization lawyers called the law mold; Mara called it a hinge. 
If booths, shrines, kitchens, and sleeping barges stood behind dusted thresholds by dawn, the surveyors would find residents where they hoped to price emptiness. 
If she failed, her neighbors would become performers of their own festival on the clean, expensive rim. 
For three nights, Mara turned Office data into carnival signs. 
A blue fish with seven scales meant move seven households north; a crooked sun meant brace a gangway at noon; paired drums meant leave one lantern burning as proof of residence. 
Ledgers placed families; intuition kept feuding cousins away from the same brass band. 
She ground dust from the foundation stone chip into chalk, so true instructions glittered and forgers could not fake the changing shapes. 
Children carried chalk in bait buckets, and aunties followed painted arrows while pretending to chase beads. 
Because the carnival never officially ended, the rearrangements looked like another parade, with mattresses under serpent kites and soup pots roped to unicycles. 
Hopeful desperation tightened every knot: no one expected to save everything, only enough to begin again. 
On the last night before privatization, Director Pell caught her copying the vacancy schedule. 
He asked which side she served, and the question pressed her dual allegiance until she could hardly breathe. 
Mara thought of the badge, her mother, bilge-room coughs, and cranes beyond the breakwater. 
She said the Office had hired her to absorb culture shock, and honest absorption changed the person holding it. 
She handed him a mask and told him to follow the silver eels if he wanted the new city to have streets instead of exhibits. 
Pell could have arrested her; instead, fearing dull privatized streets more than paint, he stamped her as an overnight census taker. 
The stamp let her cross guarded locks openly, saving an hour to dust thresholds on Spice Raft. 
At dawn, deed boats slid in under white umbrellas while the rafts rocked with drums, rain, and frying oil. 
Surveyors expecting vacancy found a barber beneath a fresh mural, a school on the pickle barge, and seven families breakfasting under blue fish scales. 
Where tablets said empty stock, Mara's chalk dust caught the sunrise on every threshold. 
Pell read the harbor clause aloud, awkwardly, as if pronouncing a spell he might be punished for knowing. 
The lead surveyor laughed, then stopped when the crowd repeated the clause and produced rent receipts dated before privatization. 
Privatization did not vanish; it came as cranes, deposits, polished railings, and fees no ledger could charm away. 
But the map was redrawn around occupied common thresholds, and carnival families became leaseholders rather than hired decorations. 
Mara lost the badge, and her mother sewed its empty place into a chalk pocket for the smaller foundation stone chip. 
Mara kept working as a culture shock absorber, teaching newcomers that a city becomes a product only when people stop answering its walls. 
Whenever luxury decks tried to smooth the graffiti, new fish, suns, and drums appeared overnight, orderly in pattern and intuitive in place. 
The messages were instructions for staying afloat, and the raft city danced on with hopeful desperation, still deciding who belonged one threshold at a time.
