At the blue hour, the orchard afloat on a circle of driftwood in open seas began to sound uneasy. 
Restless euphony was the ordinary noise of that hour: leaves chafing, ropes ticking, bees worrying their cages, and water knocking under the root mats. 
Nera, the Leeward tide prophet, interprets cloud movements as other sailors read a compass, and at this hour the clouds were running backward. 
From the high pear tree she saw three anvil heads lean west while their shadows slid east, a sign of a pressure wave folded under fair weather. 
Everyone on the orchard knew what that meant: loose wreckage from some ruined harbor was travelling ahead of the wave, too scattered to dodge and too heavy to lift. 
The circle of driftwood had been lashed from old hulls after the last land sank, and its apple, pear, and quince trees grew in soil no deeper than a prayer. 
Nera had been a child then, watching her brother Talo vanish with the eastern rim because the crew treated the breaking swell as an enemy rather than a part to be heard. 
To turn obstacles into instruments became her old vow after that loss, and the Council of Grafts still considered it beautiful until it demanded risk. 
Now practical voices urged them to cut away half the ring, let the fruit go dark, and save the inner cistern by running before the weather. 
Nera answered with the craft they called Sketched resonance, in which a drawn line could borrow the vibration of a thing before that thing arrived and teach nearby matter to answer as its counterweight. 
Such lines worked only at the blue hour, when sky and sea shared one color and the pressure between them seemed thin enough to score. 
Fractal generating was her method: copy the fork of one torn branch into ever smaller forks until a whole ring of timber, root, rope, and fruit shared one pulse. 
The Xylophone mallet at her belt was the only object she had kept from the drowned music school, its cork head long gone soft as bread. 
With that mallet she would dub each threat by striking its nearest piece and giving its assigned note a place in the chorus. 
The council called this ceremony childish, but they also knew no tree had ever been lost during a proper Dub, only during a panicked retreat. 
At the blue hour there was no time for argument, so Nera stepped onto the windward beam with ink, salt chalk, and the mallet in her teeth. 
Below her, families knotted baskets to their waists and children held buckets under bleeding taps, all listening for the first knock of wreckage. 
Nera read the cloud movements again and saw the hidden wave would arrive in four unequal breaths, each stronger but slightly off from the last. 
She sketched that limp across a wet plank, branching it by fractal generating into hundreds of small corrections for the ring. 
Every crooked twig became a baton, every barrel hoop a dry string, every taut root a membrane that could answer one fraction of the coming force. 
To finish the score she had to dub the wreckage in the order it would touch them, not in the order fear named it. 
A broken mast, black with tar, slid out of the violet haze and struck the outer pear roots before any gull could cry. 
Nera tapped the xylophone mallet against its split end, felt the shudder rise through the beam, and gave the mast the low note of a drum. 
She marked that note on the plank, and the nearest family repeated it on an empty water cask until the sound stopped sounding like disaster. 
A galley rail, copper boiler, and doorframe raft followed, each ugly gift arriving when the clouds had promised. 
Nera began to dub them with quick strokes, while the orchard answered in clearer layers, a restless euphony with work in its hands. 
The first swell struck the mast, and the drum note broke into smaller beats along the generated branches of her chalk. 
The second met the boiler clang, the third met root skins and bucket rims, and the fourth was met by everything at once. 
Because the pattern was deliberately off, the pressure wave found no single rhythm to grip and passed beneath the ring in a long, confused shiver. 
The wreckage that should have shattered the orchard became the instrument that steadied it, still humming as it drifted free. 
When the blue hour faded, the open seas kept the tune, and Nera let grief become an unfinished note.
