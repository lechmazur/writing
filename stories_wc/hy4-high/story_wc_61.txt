In the nebula garden, luminous petals opened whenever the black moon began its transit across the failing star, and the orbital parish grew still to listen.  The registry called Sen an otherworldly sentimentalist because she came from a moon where grief was woven into cloth and she kept every useless token.  Among her relics was a calculating machine gear, a brass cog whose chipped tooth had belonged to her grandmother's ornamental arithmometer.  It clicked in her palm like a polite insect, and she loved that stubborn sound more than the sums it could perform.  Every keeper before her had carried the cog into the central lantern, where a nest of mirrors approximates infinity.  The old law gave the keeper only across the quiet sigh of falling petals to set mirrors and choose what light reached the colonies below.  Those colonies had no suns, only tubular farms and schoolrooms where children learned the names of stars they had never seen.  Sen had come to the lantern to share light without using fire, a vow made beside her mother's cooling bed.  Her mother had tried the quicker method during the previous transit, igniting hydrogen bloom to force one harvest, and flame took the east wing and her sight.  In the ledger, the disaster became obedient corrections: dimmer mirrors, thicker shutters, later openings, children taught to praise darkness as prudence.  The records called them generational patterns, as if repetition were nature rather than a series of frightened hands.  Sen read those pages with troubling serenity, smiling at each signature even while deciding to betray the caution they praised.  Visual instruments failed in the radiant haze, so gardeners guided the mirrors by sonar pinging through stems and gas.  Each ping returned as echoes locating buds, broken vanes, sleeping birds, and the metal teeth that translated cadence into angle.  Sen set the calculating machine gear into its socket, but its chipped tooth made the first equation stutter, and the lantern answered with wounded silence.  She understood that the flaw was inheritance: every ancestor had filed the tooth thinner to keep radiance in reserve for their dead.  They had loved the dead so fiercely that each generation sent less light to the living, and called the dwindling loyalty.  The petals fell unhurried, brushing her face with cold pollen that carried the recorded voices of earlier keepers.  Those voices told her to wait, to preserve, to lock the shutters before anyone below could be dazzled into need.  Being sentimental, she could not call them cruel; being otherworldly, she imagined kinship wide enough to include the unborn.  She shimmed the chipped tooth with a sliver from her mother's thimble and tightened it against the ratio wheel.  The repair was temporary, an admission that love could mend a pattern without pretending the pattern had never existed.  Then she began a final sequence of sonar pinging, not one clean command but a cluster of questions sent into the luminous weather.  The echoes found the altered gear, the mirrors turned in their sleep, and the lantern opened along a thousand narrow seams.  Light went out as pulses rather than flame, divided by the patched cog into portions for nurseries, kitchens, infirmaries, and windows where children kept star charts.  No one burned, no shutter screamed, and the calm felt unsafe, as if the garden had consented to an irreversible mercy.  Below, the colonies lifted pale faces, and teachers paused midway through constellations because real names were glowing on the walls.  Above, ancestral pollen dimmed around Sen, but she did not gather it; she let it drift toward the newly lit rooms.  The transit ended, mirrors settled, and the nebula garden resumed its troubling serenity, softer because it had been disobeyed by someone who remembered everything.  Sen kept the calculating machine gear in her palm, warm and imperfect, and understood inheritance was not a circle but a hand she could finally open.
