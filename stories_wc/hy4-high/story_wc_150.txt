Across the days spent excavating a tomb, Mara Venn measured time by the colors of dirt lifted from the catacomb orchard where tree roots snake between bones. 
A diaspora returnee born among salt docks, she had come home with folded names from her grandmother and a commission to restore lost faith, not in saints exactly, but in the old promise that no road abandoned its walker. 
She was warmly curious with the villagers who doubted her, asking after their wells, their bread, and the dead they still avoided naming. 
Ivo, the foreman, wanted straight trenches and quick crates, because the ridge road was waiting for stone. 
Mara worked instead through purposeful wandering, stepping from niche to light well with a lamp in one hand and a notebook in the other. 
Each pause had a purpose, since the roots followed ancient seams, the bones leaned toward blocked passages, and the faint drafts remembered entrances that surveyors had missed. 
Above them, almond saplings grew in narrow shafts of noon, and their pale fruit shone with a cautious enchantment that made even Ivo lower his voice. 
No one ate the fruit, not because anyone feared poison, but because the stillness seemed to ask for consent. 
On the first morning, Mara brushed soil from a row of small clay tokens tied to wrist bones, each token stamped with a harbor, a mountain, or a distant gate. 
Ivo called them souvenirs, but Mara heard interrupted journeys in the way one sandal lay beside a little cup and another stopped at a threshold its owner had never crossed. 
Her grandmother had spoken of a brother who set out from this valley to guide scattered families back, then vanished when the pass closed in winter. 
Mara had grown up with that unfinished story souring every holiday, a little silence placed beside the extra chair. 
By the fourth day, when the crew lifted a slab carved with the words For those whose journeys stop here, Mara no longer hoped merely to prove an old ritual true. 
She wanted the dead to be accompanied, the living to stop mistaking exile for punishment, and herself to learn whether return could be an act rather than a verdict. 
The slab covered a circular hollow, and under a mat of pale roots the workers found a sundial base, cracked across its noon line, with its bronze pin missing. 
Ivo wanted to crate it as rubble, yet Mara noticed that hairline roots from every niche converged on its empty center like travelers at a station. 
She asked him for one more day, then spent it wandering deliberately between root, bone, and shaft, marking where sunlight touched dust and where the dead had turned their feet. 
At dusk, that pattern led her to a collapsed stair, where a bronze pin lay inside a leather pouch clasped by fingers still holding a harbor token. 
The token matched one folded name in her pocket, and she understood that the lost brother had died not fleeing the valley but trying to lead people into it. 
Mara wept briefly, then washed her face in the cold bucket and showed Ivo the alignment, because grief would not rebuild anything by itself. 
The next morning, with the tomb finally open from threshold to rear wall, they chose to rebuild the sundial base at the mouth of the largest light well. 
Villagers who had kept away brought lime mortar, old tools, and the names they had once been ashamed to remember, while Mara fitted the recovered pin with careful, ungloved hands. 
At noon, a narrow blade of sun slid down the shaft, touched the bronze, and laid one bright line across the roots and bones. 
No miracle announced itself; only dust turned gold, the pale fruit trembled, and the workers stood as if a shy guest had entered. 
Ivo read the first name from her folded paper, an old woman answered from the stair, and one by one the villagers spoke the harbor tokens back into kinship. 
Mara understood then that lost faith returned not as certainty but as the slow work of carrying unfinished miles together. 
She did not claim every journey was completed, since the bones remained bones and the diaspora still stretched across foreign ports. 
But the rebuilt base gave the valley a place where interruption could be named, where returnees and stayers could meet without pretending the road had been easy. 
When the crew sealed the tomb, Mara left one narrow path unblocked, so the roots could keep wandering and the noon light could finish its small daily journey.
