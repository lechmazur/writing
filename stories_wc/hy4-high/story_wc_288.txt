In the ninth year after the reincarnation memory, the tidal library above clouds filled at dawn with readers who remembered other lives. 
Its shelves rose and fell on lunar swell, lifting birdcall dictionaries at high tide and maps of drowned cities at low tide. 
Mara Venn kept the dawn desk as the only contemporary of extinct species on the staff, a fact nobody found romantic. 
The reincarnation memory had given everyone a previous life, but Mara's earliest came from a child on Mauritius who fed dodos and remembered their heavy, puzzled feet. 
That morning made her useful, since many readers woke with animal lives inside their own and spoke in vanished flocks, herds, or seas. 
Physicians called these crises brittle awakenings, because a surprise could crack the present self from the remembered one, leaving someone trapped in a noise of wings. 
Mara's task was to translate isolation into independence, first for the readers and finally for herself. 
For eight years she had been the necessary interpreter, praised for a gift that kept her in a locked room with other people's terror. 
The library steadied brittle awakenings by the hum of an ancient stone circle on a drowned hill beneath the cloud deck. 
Moon tides pulled reservoirs against cables rooted in those stones, and the stones answered with a low chord through brass pipes. 
The chord held fractured minds together, but it had no words, so Mara always supplied them. 
At the floor's center hung a brass sundial, unused for time since clouds below grew brighter than the sun above. 
Its face had been polished atomically reflective, so smooth that it returned light and the least pressure of the stone song. 
Older librarians said a scratched surface split a brittle mind and called the dial the safest mirror in the sky. 
Mara believed the dial could be tuned to make the hum speak in patterns any reader could learn. 
If she wrote that grammar, the library would no longer need her, and the awakened would no longer need a keeper to choose which life was real. 
The council refused, because one wrong pattern might summon the dead too quickly and turn recovery rooms to glass. 
Mara waited for a case that left her no obedient choice. 
It arrived in Tavi, twelve, found under the astronomy ladder repeating the creaking calls of passenger pigeons. 
His human memories came in fragments, while the flock came whole, enormous, and certain that the sky belonged to it. 
Each spoken name made him clamp his ears, as if a billion wings were breaking there. 
Standard treatment would wrap him in the stone hum for three days while Mara named each call until the flock grew quiet. 
Mara saw that quiet as another locked room, with Tavi outside his life and dependent on whoever held the key. 
She told the council that the boy and the birds had to coexist, not take turns possessing one skull. 
When they would not answer, she closed the recovery room, lowered the brass sundial, and opened its gnomon toward the humming pipe. 
Tavi crouched inside its shadow, and the child who had fed dodos in Mara's oldest memory leaned close, not as a ghost but as practice. 
Mara did not turn the creaking calls into commands; she let the dial return them as rhythms, spacing hunger, flight, fear, and rest. 
The atomically reflective face showed no image, yet every beat of the ancient stone circle arrived without the scratches of her private vocabulary. 
She wrote the first pattern on Tavi's palm, then waited until he tapped the second pattern back. 
Outside, the council pounded once, but by rule, they could enter only after Mara completed the pattern. 
Inside, Tavi's breathing changed from the panic of a trapped flock to the wider rhythm of a sky with room for both. 
He opened his eyes, said his own name first, then made a small creaking sound that asked for water, not dominion. 
Mara brought water and did not correct him. 
By noon the same pattern had steadied three other brittle awakenings, and the council stopped calling the dial dangerous. 
Scribes copied the rhythms into ordinary primers, so readers could sit with the hum and find their own names without Mara. 
She was not emptied by that freedom; the dodo memory stayed, but it became companionship instead of a wall. 
On the last evening of her watch, Mara set the brass sundial for anyone, climbed to the moonward balcony, and listened to the ancient stone circle teaching strangers how to coexist.
