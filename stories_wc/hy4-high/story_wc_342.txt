At the hush just before a condemned building is demolished, the wreckers stood outside with red lanterns hooded and their hands folded. 
A heightened stillness held the tree trunk library hollowed by a scholar's guild, as if the cedar's last ring had paused to listen. 
Ila Venn, a face blind portrait artist, was famous for likenesses others recognized while she passed the sitters ungreeted in the street. 
Her faces lived in measurements, in the tilt of an ear and the weathered crease beside a mouth, never in memory's warm assembly. 
Because she could not keep her mother's face after the infirmary took her, Ila had come not to salvage but to mirror the sky inside walls. 
Her mother, the last binder of the guild, had believed that the sky was the only countenance no one could misrecognize. 
In the central chamber, shelves followed the annual rings, and blank plaster panels waited beneath an oculus sealed with oiled cloth. 
She carried no portrait of her mother, only a centuries-old coin whose face had been worn almost blank by expeditions and thumbs. 
Through faint indentations on a centuries-old coin, she read the location of the guild's hidden panel and the count of its lock. 
The dents were fractally precise, each crescent containing a smaller crescent, each cluster repeating the curve of the whole chamber. 
Her fingertips, trained to find identity in surfaces, moved from broad groove to pinprick until the shelf before her sighed inward. 
Behind it sat a secret book safe, a squat volume of dyed calfskin whose brass clasp resembled a sentence never ended. 
Ila set the coin against the clasp, counted three long hollows and seven lesser ones, and felt the lock open like a held breath released. 
Inside lay a lens of smoked glass, a vial of powdered mica, and a strip of vellum headed discovering limits. 
The scholars had written that their art was not the conquest of infinity but discovering limits: the edge that makes an image answerable. 
A sky poured through an oculus without boundary would be weather; a sky given a wall could become a face. 
Under the heading was a final instruction to craft the morning sky from mica, lampblack, and the lens before demolition began. 
Ila understood then why her mother had left her the coin rather than a miniature, a deathbed note, or a forgiving lie. 
Faces had always escaped her, but a bounded task could be held in the hands, and silence measured the interval. 
She climbed the rolling ladder, drew the oiled cloth from the oculus, and let the waiting dawn lie cold on the plaster. 
The foreman outside raised one palm, and even the dust in the shaft of light seemed to obey him. 
She ground mica into the damp plaster, feathered lampblack along the cornice, and set the smoked lens beneath a hoop of willow. 
The lens caught the oculus, divided it, and returned it to the walls in hundreds of pale, trembling dawns. 
Each reflection held the ring pattern of the tree, the coin's crescent, and a sliver of cloud exactly where the vellum required. 
She did not try to paint stars, clouds, or horizons by invention; the room supplied them and the wall refused the rest. 
This refusal, cleaner than grief, showed her the shape of a likeness she had spent her life pursuing. 
For the first time, Ila did not mourn the faces she could not summon, because every portrait had always begun with what it must omit. 
The sky on the walls was not endless, but its borders gave it the patience of something that could be recognized. 
She returned the lens, vial, and vellum to the secret book safe, because the guild had hidden tools, not relics for rescue. 
Then she closed the false calfskin cover, set the coin on top, and walked out without looking back at her work. 
The foreman asked if she had saved anything, and Ila nodded, though her hands were empty and powdered white. 
Behind her, in the hush before the first hammer, the library held a bounded morning no wrecker could carry away. 
For Ila, discovering limits was not surrender but the moment the sky, like a forgotten face, consented to have an outline.
