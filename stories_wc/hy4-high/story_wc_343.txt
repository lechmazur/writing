Amid starling murmurations, the antique airship Caldera drifted above the Vail, its brass ribs ticking while a flock darkened every porthole. 
Below, musicians and harbor pilots chattered over the wireless, but the chart room held only Lio, a curious map apprentice. 
The crew called such duty sociable isolation, because the world spoke to her and no hand could reach hers. 
Lio was diligently curious, and her oath was blunt: to chart the next great discovery, not tidy other people's blanks. 
Before her lay the Vail, a white calm where pilots said stranded luminance pooled beneath cloud shelves like lantern oil under glass. 
Official charts marked it a hazard, since the glow lured ships sideways, yet Lio imagined a country with roads no one had measured. 
If she found a stable inlet, the Caldera's failing envelopes might drink enough warmth to carry home a new atlas. 
By dusk, however, the compasses had gone blind amid the birds, and the altimeter coughed toward the marshes. 
Only two relics still helped her: the captain's brass plotting frame and a polyglot's phrasebook dog-ear pinned inside her folio. 
That thumbed triangle, torn from a book of harbor courtesies, held greasy words for left, under, wait, morning, and hidden harbor. 
Old Ors had left it to her with a warning that any language became a map when a patient listener supplied the route. 
A controller named Tavi offered rescue; Lio refused, though tea slid from its saucer and the deck tilted under her boots. 
Rescue would end the survey, and starlings crossed the low sun only during this murmuration, when their shadows were longest. 
She went to steady ballast, carrying the dog-ear between her teeth and a pencil behind her ear. 
In the cargo bay, children of earlier crews had chalked a dusty hopscotch board between the cargo rings. 
Most sailors stepped over it, but the flock folded from a question mark into a spear, and certain chalk numbers flared. 
Because she was diligently curious, Lio knelt to note the sequence: one, four, two, seven, nine, six, three. 
No honest hopscotch returned to three, so the marks were a route rehearsed until it looked like play. 
She found the answer by noting secret patterns in a dusty hopscotch board, because hidden punctures tracked each crooked landing. 
The punctures matched the phrasebook dog-ear's creases when she laid the triangle over squares one, four, and seven. 
Through its translations, the route read: wait under the left morning, count the second shadow, answer in another tongue. 
Lio understood that survey pilots had hidden harbor codes in children's games in case rival captains seized the official charts. 
Their secret needed a second shadow, so she climbed to the gallery and asked every harbor within range to say 'good morning'. 
The answer rose as a sociable storm of accents, and she angled the plotting frame until that bearing crossed the starlings' shadow. 
Where the two invisible lines met, a thin gold seam opened in the white calm, too steady for a hallucination. 
It was stranded luminance, a trapped corridor of ancient sunrise caught under the shelf by a war of cold air. 
Lio fed the coordinates to the ballast drums, and the antique airship turned, following a road no atlas admitted existed. 
The birds opened around the prow, murmuring like a crowd making room for a late guest. 
Wireless voices fell silent one by one, as if each listener alone at a distant table had leaned closer. 
When the Caldera entered the seam, its envelopes warmed, its lamps brightened without flame, and Lio's blank chart filled with measured radiance. 
She named the country Answering Light, then pinned the polyglot's phrasebook dog-ear beside it like a worn compass. 
Until dawn, amid starling murmurations, she kept noting the light while the world chatted around her and left her usefully alone.
