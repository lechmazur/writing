Mira Venn, a contemplative glassblower, entered the abandoned control room with a canvas satchel, a small lamp, and one unwilling apprentice. 
During ice melting, the glacier above no longer creaked; it cleared its throat and meant to speak in floods. 
The old engineers had named the buried blue pump the thawing heart, because each thaw made it knock against its ribs. 
With each knock, meltwater neared the galleries where Mira had begun setting clear glass alcoves. 
She had come north to craft safe zones for inquiry, rooms where children could study forbidden gauges and ask why the world had been mismanaged. 
She was thoughtfully spirited, studying bolts like prophecies, then winking as if they had confessed. 
Tali carried the lantern as if it had insulted her. 
Mira kept them moving by dramatically narrating mundane tasks, a trick from her mother when fear needed a script. 
"Behold," she intoned, "the heroic left boot tests the treacherous puddle and votes to continue." 
Tali laughed despite herself, and the laugh made a small safe zone in the dark. 
That was the weighty lightness Mira trusted: grief in one hand, absurdity in the other, neither dropping the tools. 
Frosted mugs and dead screens sat beneath a calendar open to the last honest forecast. 
Above the master console, someone had scratched a sentence into the paint: Ask gently, because answers are load bearing. 
Mira touched the cracked leather bookmark in her coat pocket. 
It marked the station log's emergency valve page, ending with her mother's last order: abandon the lower controls. 
Her mother had died in the first thaw carrying children to daylight, and Mira had kept grief frozen there. 
Tali's questions thawed that caution by asking whether pretty alcoves would become coffins without vents. 
The valves had rusted into one stiff wheel, and its central pin was missing. 
Mira unpacked a glass rod and announced that this noble ambassador would interview the stubborn wheel. 
A pipe split; water sheeted across the floor with punch cards, ice needles, and one waving glove. 
Under the flood, the thawing heart found a faster rhythm, and the scratched sentence shivered. 
Mira slid the cracked leather bookmark from her pocket and saw that its split had exposed a brass strip. 
Her mother had not preserved a keepsake; she had hidden a tool where grief would keep looking. 
Mira laughed once, and Tali stared as if an adult had confessed to being made of weather. 
"Ladies," she announced, "we commit the brass dowager to the pinhole and trust dead engineers." 
The strip filled the pinhole; the wheel still refused, so Mira, remembering even warmth, heated the rod with the lamp and bent it into a hook for leverage. 
She pulled; Tali pulled; the wheel shrieked, coughed a brown sigh, and turned. 
Beyond the wall, a sluice opened and the flood chose the drainage shaft instead of the galleries. 
The gauges woke in sequence, green eyes blinking like owls who had overslept an apocalypse. 
Tali cheered, then hushed at Mira's mother's initials beside the lever. 
The opened sluice meant that the abandoned control room would fill to its lintel by morning. 
Water would drown the screens, mugs, chairs, and scratched answers. 
Mira understood that to craft safe zones for inquiry she would have to abandon the shrine she had mistaken for the work. 
She shut the station log around the cracked leather bookmark and shouldered the satchel. 
"Our next labor," she proclaimed, "is a majestic retreat before the water remembers us." 
"Behold," Tali answered grandly, "the right boot reverses its vote with moderate splashing." 
They climbed the stairwell while the room below accepted its new weather, a dark lake without malice. 
Behind them, the thawing heart slowed, no longer a prisoner but an engine clearing its channels. 
At the upper landing, dawn lay on the glacier in blue sheets, and the melt made ordinary music. 
Mira looked back once, letting the room become a question she could carry. 
Then she showed Tali the panes blown for the first alcove, clouded with tiny bubbles like held breaths. 
By summer, children would stand behind that glass, asking what water had taken and given. 
Mira would not hurry their answers, having learned that a safe zone is a heart thawing at a speed its walls can bear.
