In the lean years after the collapse of civilization, Mara served as beacon-tower custodian in the glass humming pavilion above a city that had forgotten its name. 
On a cushion of folded sailcloth rested the broken crystal prism that had once divided the beacon's starlight into messages. 
Mara knew its history because she was retrocognitively blessed, able to touch a thing and feel the last hands that had given it purpose. 
The blessing had also shown her the prism cracking at the instant every clock below stopped, so she never called that moment an accident. 
She did not tend the tower merely to call survivors, though each winter brought fewer fires on the horizon. 
She wanted to prove that coincidences have meaning, because three signs had met on the night her mother vanished. 
A star chart had blown open to Lyra, a wedge of crystal had struck Mara's boot, and the tower bell had rung once with no hand on it. 
Her mother, leaving for the northern shelters, had kissed her and told her to answer the small pattern when it repeated. 
The old manual, printed on skin-thin metal, finally taught her that the beacon could wake only by tracking star paths. 
Its makers had distrusted clocks after the satellites failed, so they cut the prism to read constellations as moving cams. 
Each clear night Mara inked the star paths on the pavilion glass, measured the intervals between crossings, and listened to the panes change pitch. 
Those intervals were why the missing center mattered: the prism needed light, and it needed obedient darkness between flashes. 
The manual named the missing piece a shard of hush, and Mara found it sealed inside the engineer's lead-lined case. 
It looked like no crystal at all, only a cool absence that swallowed the hum when she lifted the lid. 
When her bare finger touched it, retrocognition gave her the last broadcast, a mayor's promise cut off before the word return. 
After that canceled word lay the pure pause that receivers had used to recognize the beacon, and the pause had been preserved. 
Without it, any rekindled blaze would be a continuous glare, easily mistaken for another city burning. 
With it, three sparks and a silence could say, "We are here," and mean more than noise. 
Mara began to understand her signs as intervals, not accidents, each silence holding the shape of an answer. 
What she carried then was thistle-blown hope, prickly at the root and almost weightless, but stubborn on the wind. 
On the eleventh autumn after the satellites fell, a boy climbed the tower stair with thistle seed in his hair. 
He carried a rain-warped chart saved from a dry well, and he asked whether the silent tower still knew the sky. 
Mara touched the chart and saw her mother's thumbnail, the same Lyra figure, and a pencil note about the seventh returning star. 
The boy had followed blue glints from abandoned signal mirrors because his settlement needed medicine, and every glint had pointed here. 
His arrival was the fourth coincidence, and Mara felt the old demand in it sharpen into a date. 
She told him that the star paths would cross exactly where the prism was broken that night, and that they would rekindle the beacon. 
All afternoon they set the crystal shards into their inked sockets while the glass humming pavilion gathered a low chord from the rising wind. 
Mara did not force the broken edges; she trusted the star paths she had tracked through eleven winters of ash and cloud. 
At the calculated minute, Lyra touched the first pane, the hum climbed into hearing, and the prism accepted light without spilling it. 
Mara slid the shard of hush into the center gap, and the boy pulled the cord that opened the shutters. 
Three pulses crossed the dead districts, separated by a hush so clean that Mara heard her own heart waiting. 
For a long breath nothing answered, and she understood that proof could still refuse to come. 
Then a small northern fire blinked once, twice, three times, and paused with the same impossible courtesy. 
The boy laughed into his sleeve, and Mara wept because the pattern had become a promise only after someone answered. 
The world stayed broken, the medicine still had to travel, and the glass humming pavilion kept singing its thin, thistle-blown hope into the dark.
