In the years after dreams become shareable for anyone with a palm and a lamp, the city kept its nights in the blooming library above clouds and under trellises of breathing paper. 
A donor pressed her forehead to a glass lectern, and the dream left as silent video footage no voice could revise. 
Librarians grafted each reel to a flower whose color changed when the original sleeper forgot why the dream had mattered. 
Mara Vell was the sharp-eyed dusk noticer of that place, trained to watch the last light settle on every petal, railing, and borrowed silhouette. 
Her gestures were softly deliberate, because haste made dream shadows split and because grief, once startled, clung to the wrong names. 
In her pocket she carried a river stone from the drowned district, warm only when someone nearby still believed morning would arrive. 
She had taken the post to preserve the shape of hope against bright slogans and edited dreams that never admitted loss. 
The trouble began on an evening when all the white lilies leaned west although the wind lay still. 
At the end of the longest aisle, a shadow unbound rose from the marble without any feet to account for it. 
It had the outline of a person bending over water, and wherever it passed the flower reels dimmed to the gray of unsent letters. 
The head librarian called it contamination and ordered the shutters sealed before it reached the nursery of children's dreams. 
Mara asked for one dusk, since an unbound shade could be understood only at the hour when it remembered being attached. 
She solved bindings by comparing silent video footage from decades apart and setting one gesture above another until the same need showed through both. 
On a brass viewer she laid last evening's reel beside a famine dream recorded sixty years earlier, both without voices to plead their cases. 
In the old dream a woman held a child above floodwater with one hand and a river stone in the other. 
In the new reel the shadow cupped emptiness at precisely that angle, protecting something the archive had cropped away. 
Mara recognized her mother's wrist, the one that had rocked her above a hospital cot during the last evacuation. 
Her mother had donated a public dream of dry streets and brass bands, but kept private the flood, the child, and the stone. 
When sharing became fashionable, technicians trimmed such shadows from fearful dreams, believing that hope would travel faster with no weight attached. 
This one had waited through years of bright exports, carrying the exact outline of a promise no one had dared to watch. 
If Mara destroyed it, the library would keep a cheerful city and lose the contour by which frightened people recognized rescue. 
If she named it falsely, it would fasten to a living donor and teach that sleeper to fear every future morning. 
So she set the river stone on the reading table, softly deliberate in the hush, and let the shadow cover it. 
The old warmth rose through marble, and the dark outline began to metamorphose into the small curved shelter made by two hands, not into a person. 
Mara understood then that hope was not a lamp held high but the space kept open beneath it for someone still drowning. 
She opened the trimmer's old file and restored the cropped seconds, letting floodwater, a child's gasp without sound, and the woman's stubborn grip remain beside the brass bands. 
The shadow settled around the river stone as an outline of shelter, and the lilies lifted, washed in luminous regret rather than triumph. 
By morning, pilgrims came to view it, expecting a miracle and finding instead their own private deletions moving quietly behind their eyes. 
Some left angry, some returned with untrimmed dreams, and the head librarian changed the catalog rule without mentioning Mara's disobedience. 
From then on, every shared dream carried its fear and its shelter together, paired like pulse and breath in the silent video footage. 
Mara still noticed dusk from the western balcony, where clouds opened below the blooming shelves like a second, slower river. 
She kept the river stone beneath the glass lectern, not as a relic, but as a measure: if it warmed, hope still had a shape worth preserving. 
At last light its small sheltered shadow joined the others, and the whole library held a luminous regret that made morning feel answerable.
