In the hushed hour after the phoenix rises, the fairy-tale orchard blooming under permanent moonlight opened its silver blossoms to the dead.  Mara brought a cedar box, a chipped mortar, and the old paper that court archivists stubbornly catalogued as fortune from cookie.  Sorrel had nosed its folded prophecy from a brittle cookie at a ruined Earth market before fire took him.  It read, "Do not chase; become the door."  Mara had hated the jaunty sentence because Sorrel loved it, tossing his antlers whenever she read it aloud.  As the last beast whisperer, Mara could ask owls for directions and persuade wolves to remember mercy.  She could not ask a dead hart to return, because death had no hunger to translate.  Sorrel had been her white hart, her other pulse, the one whose fear she tasted before thunder.  During the phoenix's first rising, he carried three children through flame and faded into bright hooves.  Since then, she had come each year to reforge a bond beyond death, and fled before the cost was named.  This time the paper had grown warm, and the moon would not set until she chose.  After the phoenix rises, the dead become audible for one hour, as warmth inside old names.  Mara knelt beneath fruit that held sleeping galaxies, small as pips and near as breath.  Her hands, usually restless around grief, were paradoxically steady as she opened the cedar box.  Mara began to conjure via the scent of rare spices, crushing saffron from eclipsed suns, cardamom from comet pods, and one crumb of the ancient cookie.  Cinnamon colored by dragon blood rose, and the blossoms turned their faces toward her.  A vixen, two moths, an old owl, and shadows of unborn beasts stepped from the trees to witness.  Their attentive silence gave Mara grammar, because a beast whisperer never worked alone.  She named Sorrel gently, offering every creature present a place where his missing shape might fit.  The first summons failed; smoke pooled at her knees like an animal forgetting its name.  Mara understood that chasing Sorrel through death would only teach the dead to run.  She unfolded the old paper, read the small joke that had outlived him, and heard an instruction.  The orchard's oldest law rose clear: a door for the dead required a living body willing to change.  This was the reluctant metamorphosis she had refused, not from fear of pain, but from fear of losing the hands Sorrel recognized.  Her voice, paradoxically steady, told the witnesses she did not consent bravely, only completely.  Stars leaned close with cosmic intimacy, like neighbors hearing a private promise through a wall.  She set the fortune from cookie beneath her tongue, tasting markets, laughter, dust, and burnt sugar.  Then she breathed the spice smoke in, and the borrowed grammar of every beast entered her blood.  Her fingers rooted into the quiet interval between heartbeats, and her spine lengthened into pale bark.  Her hair became leaves shaped like listening ears, and her eyes opened as two dark doorways.  The change hurt with an honesty so precise that she nearly thanked it.  Inside the door she became, warmth gathered: hooves, velvet, rain on a deer's back, impatience, recognition.  Mara could not hold him as before, yet his alertness poured through her branches.  Above them, the risen phoenix became a red seam in the moon, and the hour began to close.  Mara felt Sorrel start to scatter with the fading heat and panicked with every leaf.  The fortune had said become, not visit, so she stopped reaching for the hands and voice she had been.  She made herself a threshold wide enough for a dead hart and narrow enough for one faithful animal at a time.  The bond was reforged, not as a chain but as a path worn bright by two kinds of feet.  Later, children found a new tree at the orchard's center, bearing fruit that dreamed of running.  Beasts came without fear, and Mara, still reluctant, still herself, whispered that death was a country with open gates.
