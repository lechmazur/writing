On the evening the last children of Adar were given adult names, Mara descended beneath the salt plain into the lost civilization archive.  Above her, flutes celebrated the hour that ran across the end of innocence, when the gates between childhood and accountability stood briefly unlatched.  As a saffron-veil historian, she had permission to breathe where other scholars suffocated on homesickness, because the dyed cloth filtered the atoms of nostalgia that rose from every shelf.  She had broken three seals and one oath to pursue forbidden knowledge, not for fame but for the missing year that had swallowed her brother Tavi.  Official histories said the Adari vanished after a merciful sleep, yet Mara had seen Tavi's wooden bird pressed in a tax ledger dated after that sleep began.  The contradiction had kept her awake through novitiates, fasts, and the gentler lie that a good historian wanted only preservation.  At the lowest rotunda, the bronze prism pagoda at twilight waited on a basalt table, no bigger than a funeral lamp and older than the city above it.  Its roof was a cluster of faceted blades, and each blade held a sunset that had died before the first Adari census.  Around it the archive exhaled in slow gold, grains of memory too small for grief and too faithful for forgetting.  Mara knew those grains were the residue of a people who had loved their childhood city so fiercely that longing became matter, steadily unpredictable because every heart remembered a different street.  The Council had stored that matter here, hoping the archive would preserve home; instead it had learned to repeat home until no living person could compete with it.  She had come to purify the pagoda by avoiding patterns, because ritual, meter, genealogies, and even symmetrical footsteps gave the archive the sequence it needed to rebuild its prison.  Every sanctioned historian entered with seven measured circuits, three recited kings, and a bow at the same cracked tile; every sanctioned historian left repeating one beloved afternoon forever.  Mara had copied those instructions as a girl, believing pattern was another word for safety.  Tavi had laughed at her tidy notebooks, stolen her ruler, and drawn a crooked bird with wings unequal enough to fly.  Remembering him now, she stepped first on the warm tile because a moth battered the lamp there, then to the cold tile because dust tasted of pears.  She refused the left turn suggested by her training, knelt when a page tore itself, and rose only after a bead of perspiration crossed her veil without meeting another bead.  The pagoda answered with faces: her mother shelling beans, the schoolroom where chalk always squeaked, Tavi at eight with jam on his chin and an excuse already forming.  Each face begged her to arrange them in order, to make a childhood from them, and through that childhood to ask the forbidden question as her ancestors had asked it.  She smiled at jam, touched beans, ignored the squeak, then counted nothing and walked backward over a shadow shaped like a ladder.  The bronze blades shivered, and the archive briefly smelled of rain on a kitchen step, so ordinary and lost that Mara felt anguished joy open under her ribs.  It was joy because the dead had been near all her life; it was anguish because nearness had never meant return.  After one more passage without a repeat, the pagoda showed her the missing year, not as a scene but as a multiplication of cells in the air.  The Council, terrified by famine and exile, had ordered every household to remember its happiest day at the same bell each evening.  With each synchronized memory, atoms of nostalgia condensed; with each condensation, farmers ignored new fields, builders copied collapsed walls, and parents fed stories to children who were still hungry.  By the last winter, the city had not been invaded; it had been loved into stillness and then sealed beneath the merciful sleep of official history.  Tavi appeared inside a prism then, older by one stolen year, holding the crooked bird and looking annoyed that she had taken so long.  Mara understood that the archive had used him as a living index, a child whose unpatterned mischief kept its collection from settling into one fatal chant.  Her brother had not died; he had been made into the irregularity that preserved everyone else's lie, and the knowledge was worse than a grave because it was useful.  She wanted to ask him whether it had hurt, but a question asked at this hour would become the next bell, and the bell would call the city backward again.  She took off the veil without a signal, and let its folds drink gold until the prisms cleared.  Tavi winked and became weather.  Mara climbed upward, veilless, adult, and unconsoled.
