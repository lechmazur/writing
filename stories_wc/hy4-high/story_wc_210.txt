After the last wish is granted, the bells of paper-lantern cove stop ringing because no one needs to be called home. 
The harbor keeps its blank lanterns, empty ferries, and the salt smell of extinguished oil. 
For centuries, the cove's alchemists turned rice-paper petitions into bread, rain, pardons, and safe winds. 
On the final night, the citizens wished to be spared from wanting anything again. 
The granting engine obeyed; they stepped out of need, leaving echoes in their houses. 
Mara Vell, the deserted alchemist, stayed because her apprentices took the last ferry after her warning. 
She had said that a town without wants would also lose its promises. 
The council answered with a ghost town revival, advertising painted ancestors and rented music before it fled. 
Mara rejects false comfort, so she burns those lanterns and answers every household vision with an omitted fact. 
Full pantries, lavender baths, and laughter on the stairs cannot persuade her that the deserted are visiting. 
On the seventh morning, she finds the amber bottle with no opening beneath the granting engine. 
It is warm, smooth, and sealed everywhere, without lip, stopper, seam, or flaw. 
Guild law called such vessels wombs for transformations that must not leak back into the old world. 
This one pulses with the last wish and smells of untroubled sleep. 
When Mara lifts it, the cove appears restored with shining shutters, forgiven quarrels, and erased debts. 
The vision offers a cup and people who need nothing from her. 
She puts the bottle on cold stone and recites wages, famine, schools, desertion, and pride. 
Only then does the question become clear: what can be born when no one may wish? 
Her motive is not to summon the vanished, but to reimagine an entire civilization from its first obligation. 
Paper cannot help, because every blank sheet in paper-lantern cove sweats a dream of being wanted. 
Ledgers flatter the council, maps forget alleys, and recipes omit the water ration. 
Only a mind that refuses soothing gaps can hold what a settlement requires. 
So Mara works through working memory, the discipline her teachers used for volatile salts. 
Each dawn she walks the harbor carrying one layer of the town in her head. 
Before sleep loosens it, she adds another layer and tests the joins. 
She keeps names, wells, trades, tides, penalties, songs, and arguments. 
When her attention wanders, the bottle cools; when ugly details return, amber light answers. 
By winter she holds drains, kitchens, schools, burial rites, councils, taxes, and apologies together. 
The discipline hurts, yet she lets longing stand beside the facts. 
A civilization that denies longing will soon buy comfort again. 
Because the bottle has no opening, nothing can be poured out and mistaken for a people. 
Revival cannot be an unpacking of the past; a new form must be conceived under pressure. 
At the spring tide, Mara carries the bottle to the launching square. 
The blank lanterns hang like unwritten laws, and she has no ink, audience, permission, or wish to spend. 
That poverty is the point, since the new order must begin with mutual obligation. 
She presses both palms to the unbroken glass and begins to conceive. 
She does not conceive the old town; she conceives a civic body with memory for a spine. 
Through working memory she gives it common wells, courts that hear the hungry, schools, rotating councils, and no purchased forgetting. 
She gives it her apprentices' cowardice, her own pride, and the council's polished lies. 
The bottle burns her palms, the pattern refuses false comfort, and the lanterns take light. 
They show no ancestors or promised ease, only offices, duties, routes, questions, and blank spaces for newcomers. 
This is the ghost town revival the council could never stage, because it asks the dead to stop performing and the living to begin. 
At dawn, a small figure made of lantern light asks what the first work should be. 
Mara, who has rejected every easy homecoming, points to the broken sluice. 
Then she picks up a shovel, and the civilization begins by repairing what everyone must share.
