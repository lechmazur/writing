Across a giant's nap, the rooftop canal system weaving between towers ran backward from moonrise to first twitch.  Old Harrow slept beneath the city, and each breath tilted channels above kitchens, bells, and laundry lines.  Mira Vale pushed her skiff into Bellmonger Gutter at the first snore, with ink, pencils, and dry socks.  As the natural history illustrator of the city, she had drawn roof fish, chimney moss, and moths that drank lamp light.  But living things at the instant of change held her, so she wanted to find the place where echoes are born.  The Academy called echoes habits of stone; canal keepers called them cargo, because clocks were sold by steadiness.  Mira had a private reason: her father, a clock mender, had vanished into a reply that never returned.  For years she heard his double tap in alleys and drew the answers as rare birds.  Around her neck lay the infinity symbol pendant he left, two dark loops warmed by her pulse.  Clock menders treated it as a ledger, not a charm, and it repaid courage with seconds advanced.  Turn it once and she spent an unlived minute; turn it twice and a future page went blank.  She had lost three sketches of old age, so she used it only by borrowing time from herself.  That night the specimen was Harrow, whose breathing had developed an interrupted cadence.  At the seventh sluice, canal bells finished one note short, and skiffs collided in the hush.  Mira drew the gap as a cove cut from repeated strokes, remembering storks that changed flight on skipped wingbeats.  A pause was never empty; it steered the flock, so she poled wherever the water hesitated.  The rooftop canal system weaving between towers became a field guide of current, brick, chimney smoke, and captive stars.  Clocks argued across channels, and every argument ended in the same small silence.  Those silences led to High Sluice, where the Ministry kept a tuning cage beneath glass.  Inside trembled a newborn echo, no bigger than a moth, raw from beating against measured bars.  The keepers had caged it to steady clocks, but a living repetition could not bear a schedule.  Each strike stole one note from the breath of the giant, and water climbed toward sleeping rooms.  If Harrow woke before the cadence healed, his turning would empty the canals through every bedroom below.  Mira had come to uncage the prisoner, but the lock opened only on the stolen beat.  She pressed the infinity symbol pendant to the cage and turned it until two futures went pale.  One held wrens for grandchildren; the other held only older hands around a cup.  The borrowed minute arrived as a bright gap between heartbeats, and that gap sculpts time itself.  Inside it, the lock remembered its missing note, clicked, and let Mira uncage the echo.  The creature did not fly away; it flew backward, following its own first sound.  Mira followed beneath ivy and bridges where families slept with buckets tied to their doors.  Every channel carried an older chime as the boat neared the ear of the giant.  At the last tower, the canal ended in a dry basin ringed by ropes of moss.  There was no water, no bell, and no mouth, only a pause worn smooth by returns.  The echo folded into the pause and emerged as two, then four, then a flock of listening things.  Mira understood that echoes were answers born when an interrupted cadence refused to close.  This was the place where echoes are born, and also the wound her father had entered.  She wanted to call his name, but a naturalist learns that wanting can injure the thing observed.  Instead she drew the birthplace: a ring of almost silence, an absence with edges, a seed made from delay.  The drawing changed the debt, because a borrowed gap had become visible without spending another minute.  Below the roofs, Harrow sighed, and the whole canal network shuddered toward morning.  Mira turned the pendant back, returning the stolen beat before the giant could wake afraid.  Blank pages stayed blank, but the clocks of the city began to breathe in time.  Keepers found the tuning cage open and blamed weather, because cages are easier to explain than mercy.  The Academy filed the drawing under speculative anatomy, though she had written process, not creature, beneath it.  Afterward, she still illustrated beetles and fossils, but never again called a pause empty.
