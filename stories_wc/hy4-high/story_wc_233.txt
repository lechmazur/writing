It was after the final warning that the disenchanted queen stopped calling her sleep a sickness.  The warning came as a paper bird folded from her dream, bearing one line: If you climb, the sky will learn your name.  For nine summers, Queen Elowen had woken with cobalt chill on her teeth and bleeding starlight dripping from a crown shaped crack behind her eyes.  Disenchanted with ministers, miracles, and the magic that failed her brother, she wanted only to unravel the mystery of recurring dreams.  Her voice was softly commanding, yet in the dream another breath always answered near her shoulder.  Below that dream ridge, the first fireflies of summer opened like green wounds.  After the paper bird, palace locks stood open, the astrologer vanished, and a red seam crept toward the northern stair.  From a cabinet Elowen took the ember grotto on the ridge, a palm sized basalt relic given to her mother by cloud miners.  Its red hollow pulsed in time with her dreams; aimed north, it grew warm enough to frost her rings.  She called no army, because the dream had always required two people and one surrendered breath.  She called Nella, the beekeeper's daughter, who said first fireflies were promises keeping appointments.  Nella brought rope, a blue hooded lantern, and the patience of someone summoned by lonely rulers.  The old survey named their goal the cobalt grotto above clouds, reached where the ridge lost its name.  Elowen asked Nella to come, in her softly commanding way, and then added the harder word, Please.  Nella heard that please as apology and key, for no queen had ever thanked her before crossing a threshold.  The survey margin named the method: a shared exhale spotting the first fireflies of summer, performed where ember stone met the path.  No command could do it; the ridge wanted two lives emptying themselves at once.  They began to climb at dusk, leaving clocks, whispering ministers, and a bruised sky beneath them.  Every step tightened the lacy menace around them: spider frost on ropes, silver threads on stone, thorns like embroidery until they drew blood.  Below, clouds folded like sheets; above, stars looked freshly cut.  At the last shelf the basalt relic split, not breaking but opening its red hollow into a blue stair.  The first fireflies of summer rose from the valley, small and patient, while Elowen and Nella leaned close behind their scarves.  They spotted the same firefly, shared one long exhale, and the rock answered.  A door no wider than a confession opened into the cobalt grotto above clouds.  Inside, blue veined walls held the same lacy menace, all frill and hidden hook.  At the center hung a star no larger than a heart, pinioned in silver netting; bleeding starlight ran into bowls cut by dead queens.  Elowen understood: each queen had been made a valve for that stolen star, and each sleeping mind received the light it could not shed.  The ministers had lit two hundred peaceful years with the wound, and the final warning came from the astrologer to keep the crown's theft asleep.  Nella whispered that promises, unlike orders, could be released, and touched the netting with her beekeeper's hands.  The netting tightened, delicate as lace, and drew blood across Nella's palm.  Elowen could have used softly commanding words to send her back, but instead she set her crown in the largest bowl.  The crown had always been a plug; relinquishing it, not wearing it, was the act the dream had rehearsed.  Bleeding starlight filled its empty circles, turned cobalt to white, and snapped every silver thread like tearing silk.  Far below, the capital went dark for one breath, then lit ordinary lamps, unsure whether to mourn or be ashamed.  Nella closed her bleeding palm around Elowen's fingers, and the queen did not mistake the gesture for homage.  By morning, the ember grotto on the ridge was only cooled stone, and no map showed any cobalt grotto above clouds.  No paper bird waited on Elowen's pillow; no warning scratched inside the quiet of her room.  She returned without the crown, disenchanted still, but ruled as if command were a breath to spend with another.  When dreams came after that, they were only dreams, and she woke before dawn to ordinary, unbleeding dark.
