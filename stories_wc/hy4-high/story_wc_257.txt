After hitting rock bottom, Elspeth Vale climbed the unnumbered stair to the timeless observatory, carrying only her last tools.  As a mourning jewelry maker, she had once set hair of the dead beneath glass and sold grief small enough for a collar.  After her daughter Nell died, Elspeth built a silver girl with watch springs and a rose of pinned hair, refusing to call her merely made.  When the workshop burned and the automaton folded inward, she slept under a bridge and learned that rock bottom was the end of pretending.  She climbed now with the subdued ardor of coal that had stopped flaring and still gave heat.  She had come to teach withering to bloom again, not to recover Nell.  In her coat lay Nell's tattoo stencil on vellum, pricked for a design that never reached skin.  It showed a rose bending into seed, each thorn pierced where ink should have passed.  Nell had meant it for Elspeth's wrist, but the holes also matched positions in the observatory ledgers, as if body and sky shared one pattern. 

No clock endured in the dome, and no season won its argument.  Autumn light lay across winter frost, and every star appeared at once as spark, furnace, and ash.  Around the great lens hung the curtains of intention, woven less from fabric than from promises people refused to finish.  They could not be forced aside, because force had no intention beyond its own fatigue.  A hand parted them only after declaring, through a careful act, what sort of seeing it meant to permit.  This was why the old astronomers had abandoned spoken maps and taken to leaving origami figures with coordinates inside.  Where every hour existed together, a paper moth beneath the proper star would unfold at the moment of need and illuminate one stitch of curtain.  The next figure waited inside its crease, and the sequence made a path from grief toward instruction.  Elspeth had learned the method while folding bills into birds as creditors knocked at her ruined shop.  The practice taught her that a small exact shape could carry what speech had squandered. 

Now she set the vellum on the cold floor, pricked thin paper through each hole, and wrote its altitude, declination, and lens bearing inside a folded belly.  Her fingers, trained to fix a curl of hair under crystal without trembling, worked until the dome seemed to lean nearer.  The first paper moth went beneath a blue newborn star, opened, and made one charcoal thread in the curtain glow.  The second, a crane, illuminated a red cinder and showed Nell at eleven, bent over the stencil with graphite on her lip.  Elspeth kept her hand from reaching through, and that restraint was the hardest setting she had ever made.  Figure by figure she crossed the dome, leaving folded moths, fish, and cranes that woke only when the previous light faded.  With each coordinate, the curtains accepted less of her longing and more of her purpose.  They showed the burned workshop, the automaton's bent spine, and the silver girl she had tried to name daughter.  The sight did not punish her, because the observatory had no time for punishment; it displayed a wish that had asked silver to do a mother's work. 

At the last panel she unfolded the stencil across her knees and saw that the rose had never been drawn as dead.  Its petals were hinged in the line work, each one keyed to a pinhole, so a patient hand could raise them again.  From a spare scrap of vellum she folded a final rose with black enamel at its center, genuinely artificial and honest about every crease and pin.  Inside it she placed no hair, no stolen breath, and no plea for Nell, only the last coordinate from the pattern.  When she set the rose beneath the lens, all the earlier figures stirred and threw one pool of light onto the curtains of intention.  The curtains opened, not on Nell, but on Elspeth's hands, older and empty, fitting a hinged petal to a pin.  That was the bloom she had asked for: withering allowed to move, with no disguise over the places where it had bent.  She descended with the creased stencil as a pattern for new mourning pieces, each made to open when touched by light.  In the city below, she would make no replacements, only small honest hinges for those who had to carry loss into another day.
