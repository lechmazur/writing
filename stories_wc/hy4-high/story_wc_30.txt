The tidal atrium woke in the pale hush after tomorrow disappears, when harbor clocks had nothing left to promise.  Its roof was glass, its floor was a channeled estuary, and its long reading tables stood on legs above the brine.  The city filed its new age as after tomorrow disappears, because no calendar could hold a page beyond breakfast.  People still rose, swept thresholds, baked bread, and opened stalls, but they spoke only of this light and the next tide.  That was how wonder survived, in small rituals repeated without the promise of reward.  Mara, the titan-gear librarian, had been built to preserve wonder in routine, and she took the charge as seriously as ballast.  Her frame rose twelve feet, with book-press hands, a brass heart, and knees that chimed softly whenever salt water reached the third step.  At each return of light she trimmed the lamps, turned the damp pages, and set a fisherman's bamboo creel beside the lowest gate.  It had belonged to Ivo, who once carried smelt in it and later carried Mara's first rescued names through a storm.  She kept the creel because its woven gaps let water pass without losing the catch, a trick she had not yet understood.  Since the vanished future had loosened every name from its owner, the archivists called the drifts migrating identities.  They arrived as ink in rainwater, as a baker's laugh caught in a sailor's throat, as memories of nets resting inside a child who had never seen the sea.  The tide was the only clock left, and its pull fluctuates with moon phases, swelling at full and thinning almost to courtesy at dark.  Mara catalogued each visitor in one ledger, trying to give every wandering phrase a single rightful heart to rest within.  When two people claimed the same childhood, she erased one line, and by the next tide both had become paler.  The atrium's glass gathered a wintersun serenity, low and clear, so even her mistakes looked gentle on the wet tiles.  Yet the faces at the tables grew thinner, and the rituals meant to protect them began to feel like labels on sealed jars.  On the night of a high winter moon, the identity tide climbed over the third step and filled the creel with name-plates, keys, and tiny brass numerals.  The old municipal furnace, unused since forecasts ended, glowed under the west balcony and waited for someone to smelt the catch.  Mara knew the law: when the creel grew too heavy, the librarian must smelt its metal contents into a ring for the tide basin.  She lifted the creel with both vast hands, but the woven basket held more than metal; it held Ivo's salt, the sailors' borrowed laughter, and her own erased entries.  The first ingot cracked when she whispered that a child could not remember nets and also never know the sea.  The second ingot cracked when she insisted Ivo was gone, although his patient knot still tightened the creel's handle.  Only then did the woven gaps make sense: a container could keep by letting contradiction breathe.  Mara opened the ledger, rang the soft brass bell for attention, and asked every claimant to speak two true sentences that disagreed.  A baker said she kneaded bread at dawn and had never touched flour; a sailor said he feared water and had crossed every strait; Mara said she was built to keep order and had been wrong.  She fed those pairs into the furnace with the name-plates, working by accepting contradictions, not solving them.  The metal did not become pure; it became patient, a dark ring veined with gold that could be both burden and doorway.  They set it around the basin, and the migrating identities no longer hunted for one rightful heart; they circled through lamps, brooms, bread, and names like water through reeds.  No one recovered a future, but each returning light had wonder because a person could sweep a step as oneself and, for a moment, as someone else.  Mara kept Ivo's creel beside the gate, empty except for moonwater, and continued to trim the lamps at the same quiet hour.  Her gears chimed over the tidal atrium with wintersun serenity, and the routine she had once guarded became the shore where contradictions were welcome to land.
