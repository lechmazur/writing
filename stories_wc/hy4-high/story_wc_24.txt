At the horizon measurement station, Ada Rook did her work after blowing the whistle, when the tide valves opened and the fjord began keeping the clocks.  Only then did the engine room take on its fjord quiet, wet stone and black water holding their breath beneath the lamps.  As hydraulic clock shepherd, she walked the brass gallery, listening for drips that belonged to the hour and drips that belonged to the sea.  The title was eminently peculiar, but exact: the clocks were glass drums fed by reservoirs, and their escapements had razor edges that cut one flow into countable beats.  The Charter called those beats seconds, yet Ada had taken the post to understand what clocks really measure, because her mother's mantle clock had ticked on through the winter after the packet boat carrying her father failed to return.  Its steady indifference had felt like a wall between a child and the water, and she had never forgiven it.  On this night, just as the whistle's echo died, the west gauge dropped a division and every lower clock hesitated together.  A ship waited in the roads for the dawn horizon figure; one wrong digit would move the northern shoal a cable length nearer the quay.  Ada went to the sighting window, where two blackened blades set an artificial horizon against the true one.  To align them she had to squint until water, fog, and the far seam of ice became a single line.  The line had shifted toward the mountain by the width of a fingernail, impossible if the clocks were honest.  Rule forbade chasing the error forward; the only repair was through counting backwards from the last known zero, valve by valve.  She coiled the whistle cord and began at sixty, giving each descending number to the valve she had opened at that beat.  At forty one the west pipe knocked; at twenty seven she heard a dry catch in the master escapement beneath the water's pulse.  The fault was grit lodged against a pallet, exactly on the razor edges that made an instant out of flow.  No station tool could reach it, because the first builder had sealed the master clock behind a face with a square socket no wider than a barley grain.  Ada took from her pocket the mantle clock key her mother had used on that same clock, a private thing she called practical.  The key was eminently peculiar, double ended, worn soft by her mother's thumb, and cast from an alloy the inspectors could not name.  A sitting room tool had no business in a horizon measurement station, yet its smaller end slid into the sealed socket without a scrape.  When Ada turned it, the clock case opened, and the reservoir exhaled iron, kelp, and cold.  The grit lay bright against the dark pallet, a speck from the cliff split in the last freeze and ground down by the tide.  She could have removed it, repaired the beat, and sent the ship a clean figure.  Instead, with the key still in the lock, she looked at the razor edges and understood why her childhood anger had been too small.  The blades did not find time waiting in the world; they made a cut, and water on either side became before and after.  Her mother's clock had not measured her father's absence, nor the patience of snow, nor the length of winter.  It had measured the pressure of a spring refusing to stay wound, one release divided into pieces small enough for a house to endure.  Here the tide reservoirs did the same with the weight of the fjord, turning continuous pressure into the arithmetic by which sailors trusted a line on a chart.  What clocks really measure, she thought, is not time but the resistance chosen at a boundary, the instant we agree to call countable.  That thought made the work gentler.  She removed the grit, seated the pallet, and wound the master clock through counting backwards again, from twenty seven to zero, returning every valve in reverse order.  At zero the clocks found one wet pulse, and she made herself squint once more at a horizon settled exactly where the mountain ended.  Before fog lifted, she wrote the dawn figure in the ledger, blew no second warning, and put the mantle clock key back beside her handkerchief.  The ship passed safely at first light, small as a punctuation mark between cliffs.  By then the station had returned to its fjord quiet, but to Ada the hush was no longer a held breath or an accusation.
