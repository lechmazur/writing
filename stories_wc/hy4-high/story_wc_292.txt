In the sodden third month after the jet stream shifts, Gannet Nine, the experimental weather station, stops receiving rain and begins receiving it through its floors. 
The spongey dread swells in felt boots, water-swollen ledgers, and the pauses between barographs. 
Mira Venn, an idealistic map librarian, arrives nervously confident that ink, properly indexed, can shame a government. 
Her hands flutter, but her voice stays level when she tells Director Senn the archive sent her to audit the station's maps. 
She has also come to question the Ardent's official story, but says so only to the relic card reading copper nail from coffin. 
The nail is green, square-headed, notched seven times, and taken from the unmarked coffin of her great-grandmother Eda Venn. 
Eda had been the Ardent's chart clerk, and textbooks called her the coward who cut a mooring line and drowned forty-one sailors. 
Mira's reason for taking the assignment is private, unfashionable, and stubborn: to honor an ancestor's overlooked bravery. 
In the archive, Eda's bravery had been reduced to a footnote about weather balloons launched too late, while the captain's portrait kept its brass frame. 
At Gannet Nine, however, every wall carries the same law in flaking paint: reversible exile is a mercy because a corrected map may bring a person home. 
Under that law, the Ministry erases dissenting surveyors and flood witnesses from household registers, then loans them to remote stations until their disputed coordinates prove useful. 
Reversal requires the original return bearing, a tiny line of latitude and pressure recorded before erasure, but the shifted jet stream has made all official bearings unreliable. 
Director Senn keeps twelve such exiles at the station, feeding them thin soup and thicker work, and he calls their lost names administrative weather. 
Mira sees them in the chart room, sleeves rolled above scarred wrists, copying isobars by a lamp that gutters in the wet air. 
One old woman, less erased than compressed, asks whether the mainland still has cherry trees, and Mira answers yes because she cannot bear the more accurate maybe. 
That answer shames her into the vaults, where the sea-chest from the Ardent lies among failed cloud-seeding engines and spare barometers. 
The journals inside are shipwrecked in the truest sense: salt-stiff, pressed flat, and smelling of a bilge that no amount of cedar shavings can forgive. 
At first they seem full of devotional verses, but Mira recognizes the crooked rhyme scheme used by clerks who carried confidential bearings past censors. 
Only through coded stanzas in shipwrecked journals could Eda have hidden a method from censors who burned plain bearings as treason. 
Mira sets the copper nail beside the first blotched stanza and notices that its seven notches match the seven implausible saints named in the margins. 
When she warms the nail against the barometer, verdigris powder falls into the scratched letters and turns each petition to a compass point. 
The stanzas become a sequence of instructions: read the omitted lines, subtract the coffin depth, and wait for the night wind to cross its old path. 
Mira realizes that Eda did not cut the Ardent loose to save herself; she sent the ship's last balloon inland with the true pressure reading and stayed to hide its twin in verse. 
The official storm model had been wrong, and the forty-one sailors died because the Ministry ignored a clerk's warning, not because a clerk panicked. 
The discovery makes Mira less confident and more certain, a combination that steadies her fingers as she copies twelve return bearings onto a fresh vellum sheet. 
Before she can transmit them, Senn appears in the vault doorway with two station guards and the soft, apologetic posture of a man long practiced at preventing mercy. 
He says the exiles are safer as blanks, the mainland is flooded, and a corrected atlas would cause riots that no weather station could forecast. 
Mira chooses to question him before the guards, not with an accusation but with one clean question that the damp room seems to absorb. 
If reversible exile is a mercy, she asks, why does every return bearing in your ledger begin at a coffin? 
Senn has no answer except a glance at the guards, and that silence is the answer Mira came to find. 
Above them, the shifted jet stream drags a cold front over the antenna; everyone looks up as the old wind crosses the new one. 
She grounds the transmitter with the nail; the bearings race inland. 
By dawn, twelve names return, and Eda's footnote grows one honest sentence. 
The station stays damp, but the blanks have doors.
