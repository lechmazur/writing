In a frozen garden in mist, the Library of Unfinished Sleep kept no walls, only the boundaries of light where one possible world yielded to another. 
Those boundaries ran between ice beds and reading tables, making every lamp a border post and every snow laden branch a shelf of unwritten books. 
Rook, the feral library guardian, slept under the catalogue desk with one claw hooked through keys she never used. 
She sniffed ink for lies, bit moths that chewed moons, and growled whenever a shelf tried to become a door too quickly. 
The old librarians had taught her that panic closed more passages than theft, so she had learned to wait before striking. 
Waiting there was not idleness but a hunter's crouch inside the ribs. 
Rook was dimensionally homesick, though not for any country a map could keep. 
She had been born in a lost dimension of changing rooms, where staircases arrived only when nobody demanded a destination. 
Certainty had exiled her and could slam every route back, so she wanted to make friends with uncertainty instead. 
On the night the cultist's pendant came through the brass return slot, the whole garden held its frosty breath. 
It was a black sun no bigger than a plum stone, stamped with the creed of the Order of the Last Dawn. 
One world, one answer, one dawn, said the letters, and the mist stiffened into bright needles. 
The pendant was bait and compass, made to drag the boundaries of light into one hard line. 
Then its owner could cross without dreaming, name every book, and wake the sleepers as believers. 
Everything had to be settled through the veil of sleep, before the child who hosted the library opened her eyes. 
Library law forbade destroying an offered object until its owner appeared, because denial gave the object more room. 
So Rook crouched in the frozen garden in mist and waited among white roses while the pendant ticked against the tray. 
With each tick, paths that sometimes led to amber cities or quiet bedrooms became gravel leading nowhere. 
The knowledge came with foggy lucidity: if she fought the pendant with certainty, she would become the cultist's twin. 
She needed to hold the outside without letting it choose her inside, and the old discipline was called interiorizing exteriors. 
Rook closed her eyes, breathed fog until it lined her lungs, and set each icy rose in the chamber behind her heart. 
She took the gravel paths as veins, the reading tables as bones, and the numbered lamps as patient stars under her skin. 
Exterior cold became an interior room, so the garden no longer needed to conquer her. 
In that clarity, uncertainty seemed a guest with cold hands rather than a fog to clear. 
She set a place for the guest and kept her claws open, which was the hardest form of wait she knew. 
Near the last hour of the child's sleep, a dry robed man stepped from between two lamps, smiling with terrible relief. 
His shaved forehead marked the Order, and he reached for the cultist's pendant as if it opened his own house. 
Rook did not bare her teeth; she waited with the garden breathing inside her, every internal lamp accepting another possible edge. 
He promised clean shelves, still rooms, and children who would never wake unsure of what a door meant. 
Rook let his promise enter the inner garden, where white roses bowed without freezing and gravel paths forked around it. 
Because nothing outside had been refused, the pendant could not find one hard line to feed on. 
It opened like a puzzled eye and showed, instead of one dawn, all the dawns the sleepers might have chosen. 
The cultist stared, and relief fell from him because a question had met his certainty without obeying it. 
He sat among the roses until morning thinned the mist, and Rook sat too, merely accompanied. 
When the child woke, the library folded through the veil of sleep, leaving an ordinary chill on the windowsill. 
Back in proper dusk, Rook pinned the cultist's pendant to a blank catalogue card and marked it as a question. 
She remained dimensionally homesick, but homesickness had a companion, and the boundaries of light shone softly where uncertainty sat beside her, waiting for the next door.
