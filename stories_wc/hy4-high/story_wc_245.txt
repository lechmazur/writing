In the ultrasonic forest, Mara, a blind mapmaker, absorbs languages through skin as other people take in weather.  Bark speaks in clicks above hearing, moss argues in damp vowels, and stones keep long grammatical silences.  She had charted every ridge by touch, but no chart could tell her what remained after her brother Tovin vanished.  His last letter said only that he had gone where grief and distance share one bed.  Because the words entered her palms as salt and heat, she knew he had not meant a place.  He had meant the country inside sorrow, and she wants to navigate uncharted emotional depths before they close over her.  Only across the pause of shifting constellations does the forest hold one shape long enough to enter.  The elders call that interval a held breath, and they forbid loud tools during it.  Mara carries no hammer, lamp, or ink, only a platinum compass ring Tovin left on her thumb.  Its tiny needle never seeks north; it trembles toward whatever silence a living heart is trying to hide.  With it she can dowse not for water, but for the pressure of feelings buried under roots.  When the pause begins, every bat hushes at once.  Mara steps beneath the black boughs, and the forest writes cold syllables along her bare arms.  Under the leaf mold waits a trail of musical notes leading to a hidden melody, each note a small chilled pebble.  When she lifts one, it gives a tone too high for ears, yet her wrist understands its sorrow.  The platinum compass ring warms and turns, so Mara sets the note back and follows where the needle leans.  Each pebble answers the next, and the path becomes a phrase she feels rather than hears.  Twice the trail crosses itself where old grief has bent the ground, and twice she must dowse again.  The second dowsing opens a memory of Tovin teaching her to count rain on the roof.  She does not flee from it, although the memory burns in a quiet blaze beneath her ribs.  Beyond a curtain of needleless pines, the notes stop, and the ring spins without pointing.  Mara knows she stands at a hidden stillpoint, because every dialect of bark and bone goes mute there.  The silence is not empty; it holds all the languages she has absorbed, translated into one steady warmth.  In that warmth she finds the hidden melody, not above hearing but behind every sound she had ever mistaken for loss.  It is Tovin's breathing, or the shape of her own breathing when she finally allows his absence to breathe too.  The discovery hurts, but it gives direction, which is what she has always asked of a map.  She presses her palm to the ground and lets the stillpoint print contours across her skin.  One contour marks denial, steep and glassy; another marks the low marsh of days when food has no taste.  A third curves toward tenderness without arriving, and a fourth simply says continue.  The platinum compass ring, useless now as a pointer, becomes a seal for the map when she presses it into soft clay.  Above her, the constellations begin moving again, and their faint grinding enters her shoulders as grammar.  The trail of musical notes sinks back under the leaves, leaving no proof but the melody in her bones.  She leaves by the stones that taste of rain, because Tovin's memory has taught her to count rain.  Behind her, the hidden stillpoint keeps its place, not fixed in soil but in the pause between reaction and consent.  That is the coordinate she had lacked: sorrow can be entered without being obeyed.  In her workshop she cuts the clay map into thin leaves, one for each person who comes with a silence.  She never calls them charts of cure; she calls them approaches to the stillpoint.  When grief presses a hand against her door, the languages of it pass through her skin and become contours.  Her fingers read them for others; the platinum compass ring rests nearby, warm but no longer necessary.  Under the ultrasonic forest's distant hum, Mara works with the quiet blaze of a lamp turned low against a whole winter.  The light does not conquer the dark; it gives the dark edges someone can follow.  And across the map, every contour points less to Tovin than to the place where she can finally love him without a direction.
