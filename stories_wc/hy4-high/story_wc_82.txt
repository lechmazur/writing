After the shadow detaches, kettles in the floating island tea garden fall silent, and pilgrims face the glass basilica on a floating isle. 
The black sail of shade drops into lower clouds; released, the isle rises from the sea while the counterweighted shrine sinks toward tea rows, a motion named 'descends while ascending'. 
Only after the shadow detaches can the glass ribs hold unmixed light for dream recording, drawing sleep from rough gems before it fades. 
For one hour, priests turn dreams to memory paper, and mourners fold sheets into birds released over the cloud sea. 
That year, Mira Vale called the brightness luminous torment; unshaded leaves and cups left no place for loss to hide. 
Mira was a crafty lantern maker whose lenses held flame through storms without revealing the hand that carried it. 
Her son Tavi inherited that patience, but fever took him while she repaired beacons, and his sleepstone returned cloudy, full of an unregistered dream. 
The basilica refused such stones, because grief without a license was said to crack the archives, and Brother Pell denied her petition without looking at her. 
So she had come to fold grief into origami cranes, one for each missed night, and her hands would find a way. 
Beneath lemon leaves in a tea tin she carried the sleepstone, with a false petition sealed in wax from Pell's ledger pinned to her cuff. 
At the gate, Pell demanded the license, and she spilled three lantern lenses across his counting table. 
Each lens was polished to catch small betrayals of a hand; while he watched his fingers hesitate, the false seal reached its place. 
The trick shamed her, but she entered with the tin against her ribs. 
Inside, the basilica breathed light too bright for tears through ribs of glass. 
Dream recording was done through gem polishing, not ink or prayer; rough crystal held a dream's ridges until patient friction released its images. 
By gem polishing she could transform the hidden dream into a dream recording, and at the altar that recording became foldable paper during the double passage. 
Mira knew from lantern lenses that too much pressure scattered light, too little left it dumb, and love was the most dangerous pressure. 
At the central altar, the stair descended while the island climbed, showing her tea rows below and empty white sky above. 
Pilgrims moaned as glass pressed their dead against their faces, but Mira kept her thumb on the stone and breathed in her polishing rhythm. 
She rubbed it with jeweler's rouge in a circle no wider than a tear until the first image loosened. 
Tavi appeared in the grain, a boy of twelve on the hospital sill, trying to mend a paper lantern with a strip of her apron. 
The glass gave back not an answer but her absence, a chair beside his bed, a cup gone cold, the space where a mother should have been. 
The pain was so bright she understood why priests called it cleansing, and when Pell came with her petition, a dropped lens showed the same emptiness in his face. 
He said, very low, 'My sister left no registered dream either,' and set a legal blank stone beside her tin without forgiving her. 
Mira did not thank him; thanks would have made the moment cleaner, and she went on drawing the dream from Tavi's stone. 
When the final image rose, Tavi looked up from the ruined lantern and said, 'Hang another light, Ma; the path is dark behind you.' 
The words did not excuse her, yet she transformed the recording into a hundred trembling sheets and folded grief into origami cranes until white wings crowded the altar. 
Pell folded the last three without asking, and by then the detached shadow had caught on a lower peak; the unshaded hour was closing. 
Mira released them over the tea garden; they did not carry Tavi away or return him, but carried the shape her sorrow had learned from his hands. 
Tea leaves dimmed, the basilica settled into ordinary shadow, and Mira walked home knowing some light must be shaped before it can be borne.
