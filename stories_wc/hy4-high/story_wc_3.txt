After the spirit leaves, the magnetic arbor above the clouds holds its iron leaves very still, and Sima begins her watch. 
As the last cuneiform translator in the floating archive, Sima knows each leaf is a needle that once pointed toward the unfinished desire of a scholar. 
The priests call those lingering wants haunted ambitions, because they tug at the branches after bodies have gone. 
Today the want that trembles closest belongs to old Bani, her teacher, who stepped out of his failing body at dawn and left one instruction: reestablish the field before the second cloud bell. 
The first hour after a departure is the only hour when iron still remembers the shape of a vanished field. 
In a blue clay saucer beside his cold stylus rests the knowledge fruit seed, no bigger than the tooth of a child, its shell veined with tiny wedges. 
In this arbor, such fruit grows only from a corrected tablet, and its seed keeps the part of a lesson that a mind cannot take past death. 
If it sprouts, the final proof from Bani will enter the archive; if it dries, half a century of readings will pass into fog. 
To understand the physics of transition, Sima has spent sixteen years comparing marks pressed into clay with changes in weather, sleep, metal, and grief. 
She is kindly methodical, so before touching instruments she wraps a shivering apprentice in her own scarf and labels every loosened peg. 
Only then does she open the cracked tablet of Bani, which maps the poles of the arbor as they stood before his last breath. 
The diagram is wrong by a fingerwidth, and the wrongness hurts her more than his absence, because he never tolerated a careless line. 
Above her, clouds rub the glass floor in slow white shoulders; below her, iron fruit hangs at touching distance from the empty chair where he used to sit. 
The phrase is an old workshop measure, close enough for warmth but not possession, and she feels it now in the faint pull of the seed against her palm. 
She cannot call him back; the archive forbids it, and his spirit has already crossed the bright margin between pulse and field. 
What she can do is reestablish the correspondence between what he wrote, what the metal remembers, and what the seed is trying to become. 
She works through analogical thinking, not by forcing the broken tablet to yield a literal command. 
She murmurs that a wedge sign is a riverbank seen from above, a door seen edgewise, and a heart passing from one chamber to another. 
Therefore a failing pole is a door hinged too stiffly, a cloud that forgets how to rain, and a pupil who repeats a master without changing the lesson. 
She tests each comparison gently, aligning copper coils sealed with beeswax to the three oldest signs of the tablet while the apprentice records the tugs in a steady hand. 
The first attempt spins every leaf toward the east and fills the walkway with a whisper like disappointed voices. 
Sima pauses, not annoyed but ashamed, because the voices sound like her own secret wish to be the one who solved transition alone. 
Bani had warned her that a haunted ambition grows loudest when it wears the mask of duty. 
She writes his warning in the margin, then removes one copper weight and gives the apprentice the honor of setting the final leaf. 
This time the magnetic arbor above the clouds answers with a low, patient chord, and all the iron leaves lean inward as if listening. 
The knowledge fruit seed splits; a green filament rises, curls around the stylus, and writes in new cuneiform the angle at which a spirit enters and leaves matter. 
Sima reads it aloud, and her voice breaks only once, because the equation is simpler than she expected and kinder than any victory. 
It describes transition as a shared rebalancing: one side loses weight, the other learns to bear warmth at touching distance. 
When the second cloud bell rings, the apprentice asks whether the new vine means Teacher has returned. 
Sima says no, then yes in the small way that archives permit, and she sets the saucer beside the empty chair. 
The spirit is gone; the field is steady; the seed has become a promise with roots. 
Sima returns to translating, kindly methodical as ever, but she no longer translates to capture anyone, only to let each departure leave an intelligible path for those still near enough to care.
