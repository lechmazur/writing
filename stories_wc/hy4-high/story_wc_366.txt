After the lamp flickers out, the island becomes transparent, with the sea showing cold bones and every window holding a bruise of green light.  Mara Vell, the reticent lighthouse supply skipper, ties her boat to the wet steps, warmly observant of oil stains and the silence overhead.  Twenty years of deliveries have taught her who hides a cough and who accepts help only when it looks accidental.  In her coat rests a piece of sea glass, worn from a bottle that washed ashore after the wreck that took her mother.  Its green was the last color she trusted when shore lights vanished and the hand of her mother went slack around it.  Mara has spent her life trying to bottle the feeling of safety in flour tins, spare matches, and needs no one had spoken aloud.  That wanting made the anonymous letter chain after a storm drowned the mail boat and left three villages hungry and proud.  Into a tea tin Mara placed one folded sheet; each household added a line, copied the whole, and left no name.  No names meant no debt, though Mara knew the hands by flour smudges and crooked letters.  The chain carried warnings and taught a way of stacking stones as messages when ice trapped the boat.  A flat slate meant bread; three upright stones meant lamp failing; a tilted tower meant someone must protect the house.  The last letter said the third pane had cracked and no names should be spoken above the stairs after the lamp failed.  The lamp had died one minute before Mara stepped from her sloop; beyond the reef, a fishing boat felt blindly for the beam.  In the kitchen a kettle boiled dry, a bottle lay unstoppered, and the keeper was absent.  Because she was warmly observant, those three details told Mara more than any hurried note.  She climbed toward the haunted lantern room with the piece of sea glass and an unlit supply lantern.  The lamp lay cold and the prism starred; in every pane, drowned faces leaned close, kept drowsy by the beam.  Mara shut the logbook before its names could feed their whispering, and understood why the anonymous letter chain kept no signatures.  Under the curtain she found the child who tended oil, lips parted to answer the whispering.  Blood on the stair told her the keeper had fallen, but the child was breathing, so Mara worked to protect that breath.  Mara stopped the child from saying her own name, then pressed the piece of sea glass into the small wet palm.  The green token had traveled in the chain without an owner; Mara tapped three times, the sign for all hands coming.  With no clean oil and a cracked prism, Mara led the child to the gallery where the cairn stood above the tide line.  Without the beam, surf gave the cairn a faint phosphor shine, enough for a watcher who had learned to look.  Kneeling in spray, Mara reset it, using a way of stacking stones as messages: three black stones for a dead lamp, a slate toward the reef, and a tower crowned by sea glass.  The green crown meant a child hidden, a house to protect, and help needed before the tide turned; each stone felt unsigned.  Across the water someone saw that green wink, and the chain answered without a shout.  A shore boat brought a fresh wick and line, and a headland fire drew the fishing boat home.  The first hand ashore returned the sea glass from the cairn without asking who the child was.  In the haunted lantern room the faces thinned, because no name had been given and the village had answered without the lamp.  The keeper was found with a broken ankle, the crew came in cold with relief, and the child slept in a coat too large.  Before dawn Mara began another letter: all were ashore, the pane must be replaced, and the green stone should keep moving.  She wrote no signature, because safety was not hers to bottle and not hers to claim.  A bottle would hold only her own breath, while the chain held dry socks, hot tea, cairn stones, hooded flames, and shared noticing.  When the new wick caught, the panes filled with a luminescent ache, the tender shine of a place that had almost lost someone and learned to answer.
