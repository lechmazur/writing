At the Ministry of Inquiries, waiting rooms were maps with lost legends, and the glass atrium beside a quiet brook held questions too hot to answer indoors.  Mara Venn, a dueling philosopher with a mechanical arm, had been sent there while waiting for the border commission to decide whether two provinces could share the River Orm.  She was diplomatically fierce, bowing to every junior clerk, thanking every hostile witness, and conceding no ground that a clean argument had won.  Her brass arm had been built after a war over who owned water, and its wrist held a protractor instead of a pulse.  Since then she had taught a stubborn doctrine: to turn questions into destinations, because an answer that could not be visited usually became a marching order.  If provinces asked who possessed a river, she walked them to the ford where children had voted with their feet.  The commission had recessed for one hour, and the atrium clock was stopped so reflection could not be called contempt.  Beneath the stopped clock, workmen had left a half-painted sundial, blue on the west, old bronze scratches on the east.  A Danish legation seamstress named Fru Hall had mended a torn treaty that morning, then was escorted out when her credentials were challenged, leaving her kit.  From that kit Mara lifted a Danish tin sewing needle card, a stamped case whose six needles lay in numbered slots like meridians.  The card held no cipher, only a Copenhagen maker's mark and hinge pinpricks, but Mara recognized an instrument for measuring patience.  Captain Ochs, who commanded the commission's guards, said the kit must be sealed until the provinces agreed on an official alphabet.  Mara thanked him for protecting grammar, set the card on the sundial, and said that sealed objects made poor roads.  Ochs drew his foil, because ministry law allowed an officer to challenge any philosopher who touched evidence during a recess.  Mara bowed, clicked a narrow blade from the channel in her mechanical arm, and invited him, very politely, to fence with the point kept above the ferns.  What followed was dignified chaos, as clerks moved inkstands, pages called hits, and the brook kept its soft minute.  Ochs fenced like a man defending a dictionary, each thrust correct and late; Mara fenced like a surveyor, giving him lines he could not help following.  While their blades made parentheses above the card, Mara's brass fingers held three needles at exact intervals against the moving light.  She understood then that the peace offer had not been hidden in words but in time, for Fru Hall had sent a hidden overture via the coded angles in a half-painted sundial.  The blue arcs supplied recess hours, bronze scratches supplied old claims, and needle shadows supplied the places where those claims crossed.  Every angle asked who owned the Orm; the intersections answered with a bend, a millstone, and a shallow bank that belonged to neither province in any useful season.  Ochs saw the pattern with Mara, and his next attack was not at her heart but at the third needle, which would erase the crossing.  She parried with the protractor wrist, bowed again, and told him that defending an alphabet was honorable, but defending a needless war was not.  That sentence struck harder than the foil, because Ochs had lost a brother at the ford that all maps left blank.  His guard dropped by one deliberate inch, and the pages, who understood ceremony better than mercy, recorded it as a touch for the philosopher.  By ministry law the evidence remained with the winner, so Mara laid the six needles along the last sunlit angles while waiting for the commission's bell.  The angle sequence became a tune of degrees the oath-trained clerks hummed before they understood it was also a route.  It led from the glass wall, past the brook's coldest stone, to the shallow bank where hostile survey lines met under blackberry canes.  Under the canes Mara found the treaty's lost lining, stitched with proposals for a shared bridge, a market, and an orchestra paid by both provinces.  When the bell rang, Mara returned with wet cuffs and laid the lining, the card, and Ochs's foil before the chairman.  She refused to say who owned the Orm; she asked the delegates to put on their boots and follow the route.  The minutes recorded no verdict, only a bridge site, and the atrium emptied with the grave disorder of people going somewhere.
