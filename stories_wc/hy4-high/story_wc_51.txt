After the first snowmelt, the bronze grotto under starlight opened its old roof crack above our valley, and I, the last fractal weaver, climbed down with a kerosene lamp burner wrapped in wool. 
To collect the weights of different burdens before the thaw let them calcify into the walls, I had brought a copper scale and a ledger of soft lead leaves. 
Whalebone reverie hung over the bronze ribs, salt-scented though no sea was near, with starlight sifting through cracks like old songs. 
Shingled hush was the elders' name for the layered quiet pressed from winter troubles, scale overlapping scale until no cry could rise straight out. 
The silence repeated itself smaller at every turn, so grief learned to copy a first defeat until no one could find the original. 
Boundary-lessly trapped, nine villagers walked calm spirals in the grotto, including my brother Ivo, who had carried our father's debt like a second spine. 
I did not intend to rescue only him, because a fractal weaver who saves one private loop leaves the larger pattern free to close again. 
The village had left food at my door and kept my name out of its prayers, for such weaving was welcomed only when roofs of silence began to shed. 
The kerosene lamp burner was a blackened wheel and wick collar, fitted by my mother to a shallow tin cup after its glass chimney broke. 
I fed the cup three spoonfuls of kerosene from my flask and lit the wick with a match that smelled of wet pine. 
Bronze light gathered on the walls, revealing shingled overlaps only where the flame leaned exactly along them. 
By orienting properly, I could enter, weigh, and leave without adding another layer to the roof of quiet. 
I turned the burner wheel one tooth at a time until the smallest tongues of flame aligned with the biggest seam, and a stair of dark water appeared. 
I descended with the scale in one hand and the cupped burner in the other, listening for the separate hush of each burden. 
The lost villagers were not distant but repeated, their footsteps copying a smaller version of the last turn around and around. 
Beside the nearest woman, a gray film had begun to calcify around her boots, making a fresh shingle for the hush. 
I knelt and asked the silence for the weight, not the story, because stories clung and weights could be lifted. 
The scale dipped as if unpaid bread bills had been laid across it, and I recorded seven ounces of worry on a soft lead leaf. 
The gray film cracked, she drew one uncopied breath, and I turned her toward the seam where the stair had opened. 
Ivo was deeper in, walking the tightest spiral, and when he saw me he smiled with the apologetic patience he had used since childhood. 
His burden should have been ours, a father's debt divided between two children, but I had gone north to study patterns and left him holding the whole sum. 
For a moment I wanted to take his hand and run, which would have pleased the shingled hush by adding one more small repeat of my old abandonment. 
Instead I set the copper scale between us and asked for the weights, mine as well as his, because the largest pattern had always contained us both. 
The scale shuddered, then settled three times: eleven ounces of his borrowed guilt, three ounces of my clean room and unopened letters, and one thin shared ounce that neither of us had ever named. 
That last weight was the worst, light enough to ignore and dense enough to keep a person boundary-lessly trapped. 
I wrote all three measures in the ledger, and the third line made the kerosene flame stand straight though the grotto held no draft. 
That straightness showed the proper orientation at last, not toward escape or blame, but toward the seam where separate weights could be carried side by side. 
Ivo and I turned together, and the lesser spirals opened behind us like scales lifted from a roof after rain. 
The other villagers followed, each bearing an exact weight I had collected, and the gray films around their feet broke into harmless dust. 
At dawn, nothing in the bronze grotto tried to calcify again, and the shingled hush lay open to weather. 
Outside, the snowmelt carried nothing heavier than its own name.
