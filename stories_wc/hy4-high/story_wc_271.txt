The lantern learned to blink without a human hand after the last lighthouse keeper retired, and the arboretum perched on a seaside cliff inherited his weather ledger, his brass key, and his habit of telling the truth.  Mira Sayeed arrived two months later to nurse windbroken shrubs, repot seedlings, and prepare a memorial grove for donors who liked words such as renewal.  On the resettlement form, under reason for placement, a clerk had typed drone strike wedding survivor, and Mira had let the ugly precision stand.  Her wedding had been held under white canvas in a village inland from this same coast, with cousins drumming on tin trays and Nabil laughing because rice had caught in his beard.  The strike came between the vows and the meal, turning music into a flat white noise that Mira still heard whenever gulls battered the glasshouse roof.  In her pocket that day was a crumpled photograph of Nabil holding a paper cup, one corner burned, his smile creased but visible.  She kept it now inside a tin of labels, because names, dates, and damaged edges seemed more faithful than any polished story about survival.  Mira was quietly talented with injured wood, able to fit a scion to a torn branch so cleanly that even the old volunteers stopped talking to watch.  She did not boast, since boasting felt like another way of sanding grief smooth, but her grafts held through salt wind and winter rot.  The board called the planned ceremony Second Chances and asked her to say that the cliff had healed.  Mira wanted to anchor optimism in realism, so she wrote a different speech with soil depths, erosion figures, the names of the dead, and the number of saplings likely to fail.  Director Vale said donors would not fly in to hear failure, and he suggested leaving the crumpled photograph in the tin.  Mira answered that a photograph was not an ornament but a witness, and that optimism without witnesses was weather reporting from a locked room.  After work she often climbed to the empty keeper's room, where the automated light revolved above shelves of tide tables, storm notes, and a scratched shell containing no pearl.  On the ledger's last page, the keeper had written that fog made liars of eager men, so he waited until he could feel a pearl of stillness behind his ribs before trimming the wick.  Mira had first met that phrase in a clinic brochure, but her therapist gave it use through somatic markers: the cold before memory, the scar tightening before fear, the sole of a boot before escape.  Those signals did not make her brave; they made her accurate, which she trusted more.  On the morning of the ceremony, a survey drone rose beyond the hedge to film the ribbon, the donors, and the rows of young trees trembling in the sea wind.  Its thin busy whine crossed the years before thought could stop it, and Mira did freeze beside the cypress she had grafted at dawn.  Her hand locked on the pruning knife, the tin of labels dug into her pocket, and the old white noise filled the arboretum until every polite face blurred.  She returned through somatic markers one by one, naming the knife handle, the tin edge, the scar in her palm, and the salt taste that belonged to this cliff rather than to that village.  When the cold behind her ribs gathered into a pearl of stillness, she set the knife down and opened the tin.  The cypress beside her had split where her locked hand drove the loose stake sideways, and the fresh graft hung by a strip of bark.  No one moved to help, perhaps because they had seen the photograph in her other hand and did not know whether they were watching grief or work.  Mira knelt, matched the pale living edges, bound them with a strip of wet raffia, and pressed the stake back until the root ball stopped rocking.  She worked without display, yet the small rescue changed the crowd more than any ribbon could have, because it allowed hope to look like a task with dirt under it.  When Director Vale reached for the microphone, Mira took it first and placed the crumpled photograph on the stone ledge beside the keeper's brass key.  "This is Nabil," she said, and then she gave the date, the village, the meal that never came, and the names of eleven guests.  "These saplings may fail," she continued, reading the erosion figures while the automated light turned above them with patient, indifferent accuracy.  "We plant them anyway, not because loss is smaller than it was, but because careful hands can still keep something alive."  No one applauded; the crowd leaned closer, holding the names.
