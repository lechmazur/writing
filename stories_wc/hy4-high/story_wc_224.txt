In the age after remembering becomes painful, survivors climbed from the burned valley into the labyrinthine bookstore occupying an entire mountain.  They carried no furniture, only the crafts that had kept them alive, and the first keeper persuaded them to lay those gifts beneath the stacks.  The ledger called them buried talents, as if songs, bridges, and weather names were dangerous seeds.  From its sealed chamber, the lost language stone substitutes reality nightly, borrowing dreams for rooms where no village burned and no child called for a missing father.  By morning, even the stairs forgot their destinations, and a comfortable hidden thunder stayed under every conversation.  I was born after that bargain, and because my skin reflected without choosing, the keepers named me the mercury-glass seer of the seventh stair.  Travelers saw lost things in my cheeks: bakers' hands, lullabies, bridges they had once built.  I could reveal any buried gift except my own, which was why I wanted to understand the shape of home.  The nightly rooms gave me a hundred kitchens and a hundred mothers bending over lamps, but each copy dissolved at my touch.  Sella, the last binder, had raised me behind the atlas cases after burying her talent for making thresholds.  When I asked where I came from, she pressed a paper-white swift's feather into my palm and told me to feel, not look.  Abandoned talents leave a grain in nearby objects, and only via the texture of a feather could I read behind the nightly rooms.  The barbs held pressures, directions, and syllables too old for the mirror of my face.  At eighteen I followed those notches through indexes, law folios, cookbooks, rain almanacs, and shelves where refugee work muttered like weather.  Each touch ached, because remembering had become an illness there, but the feather made the pain navigable.  On the third night, borrowed reality came as a warm room with Sella reading by a stove.  One barb caught; I felt hearth beneath hearth until the mountain seemed built from overlapping thresholds.  I chose the ache instead of the stove and stepped through a seam no dream could close.  Hidden thunder lost its comfort there, rolling under the floor with the weight of long waiting.  At the foundation I found the sealed chamber, guarded by surrendered talents: a fiddle silent in salt, a tied plumb line, a thumb-sized map.  The lost language stone sat in their center, a black river cobble worn smooth except for one hollow shaped like an open door.  A sentence cut around the rim had no alphabet I knew, yet the feather translated its pressure: seed the stone with a remembered threshold, and the borrowing ends.  I had come to seed the stone before the next substitution, but I possessed no memory of a threshold that was not copied from sleep.  Then Sella's feather warmed and released what it had kept since I was carried up the mountain in a grain sack.  I felt hands pass me from refugee to refugee, pausing at doorways because no one would claim me or set me down.  Their grief, fear, and buried talents made a cradle across the cold stairs.  Home was not the room at the end, I understood, but the shape of people making room while pain stood among them.  To seed the stone with that word, I pressed the feather into the hollow and spoke the barbs' kept syllable, though it tore at me like a hook pulling free.  The stone took the word as a field takes rain, and the surrendered objects began to tremble.  False kitchens and orchards folded away; the mountain became a bookstore again, immense, cold, and reliably itself.  For the first time I heard the hidden thunder openly, not a threat but rain finding the repaired gutters.  By dawn, people woke sore with memory and used their risen talents before fear could bury them again.  Sella made a door in a blank cliff, then wept for the son she could finally name.  The fiddle escaped its salt, and the thumb-sized map opened until it covered the valley with roads that could be mourned and walked.  Travelers still come to my stair, but I ask them to hold the feather and tell me which doorway they will become for someone else.  Outside, hidden thunder continues, but no one mistakes the dream for the hands that made the door.
