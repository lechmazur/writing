During a solar storm, the prismatic escondite beneath the polar observatory held its lights low, and the violence of the sky reached it only as hushed thunder in the iron veins. 
Vey, a bored immortal and keeper of the Last Door, had waited through nine thousand deaths at its threshold, and the only appetite left in him was to discover what happens after goodbye. 
Mortals said the word, stepped through, and left him with cooling hands and an identical corridor; even grief had become a corridor he could walk in his sleep. 
Because he was dimensionally aware, Vey could see that the corridor was never identical, that each farewell folded a thin room behind the wall where the departed continued as an angle rather than a ghost. 
The Conservators forbade him to enter those folds, saying that an immortal must not trespass on the mercy of endings, so he did not batter the seals; he intrigued. 
His intrigue was patient: through magnetic fields he nudged compass needles, altered the sleep of lodestone locks, and taught three junior surveyors to mistake his wishes for their own superstitions. 
Only during storms did the black iron in the prismatic walls answer, because the temper of the sun made every hidden filament a listening wire. 
The prize of the intrigue lay under the altar, a cursed amulet fragment no larger than a thumbnail, cut from the jewel that had once bound the first farewell to its appointed door. 
Its curse was not melodramatic: any place that held it began to doubt its address. 
That doubt, repeated through magnetic fields, could become spatial mutiny, the old crime in which stairs refused heights, mirrors kept different distances, and rooms traded names with neighbors. 
Vey wanted one such revolt, carefully timed, because a room that forgot where orders ended might open into the instant immediately after goodbye. 
On the night of the solar storm, while auroras dragged green knuckles over the roof glass, he palmed the fragment and pressed it into the iron seam of the door. 
The seam shivered, and the prismatic escondite seemed to inhale before it sounded, not with a crash but with hushed thunder behind every color. 
Vey smiled for the first time in a century, then felt the floor vote against him. 
The Last Door swung inward, the corridor swung outward, and the seven alcoves of farewell slid past one another like courtiers changing allegiance. 
No wall stayed loyal to its surface; a blue wall became a ceiling for grieving kings, while the red wall leaned through tomorrow and showed Vey an empty chair with his own coat on it. 
He understood too late that his intrigue had not enlisted the rooms but awakened them, and that spaces which had hosted millennia of goodbyes had grievances of their own. 
They were tired of being made to look like endings when they had watched every mortal step continue somewhere beyond the frame. 
Through the mutiny, the hidden folds became visible: not lands of reward or punishment, but small continuing gestures, a cup set down in another kitchen, a name softening in the mouth of a child, a hand remembered at dusk. 
Vey saw all of it at once because he was dimensionally aware, and the sight was so crowded with aftermath that his boredom cracked like old ice. 
Then the compass needles in his coat began to scream, a thin iron screaming, and he knew the sun would burn the fields past control before dawn. 
If the spatial mutiny spread beyond the escondite, the observatory, the village below it, and the continent would forget how to remain adjacent to themselves. 
The Conservators arrived, but the rebelling floor set them on a new balcony, and their eldest asked Vey to restore the addresses. 
He could have; every compass still waited for the old instructions he had hidden through magnetic fields. 
Instead he listened, and beneath hushed thunder heard the rooms ask for one honest goodbye before the maps closed. 
He understood that after goodbye the world does not close; it turns toward what it loved and asks to be released from waiting. 
Vey set the cursed amulet fragment on the threshold and said to the rooms, "Goodbye," with no appetite under his voice. 
The fragment cooled, the magnetic fields slackened, and each alcove chose its address loosely, like a hand unclenching. 
The Conservators returned to a corridor that had decided to be one, and the empty chair vanished from tomorrow. 
His immortal boredom never returned, because every goodbye had an afterward to tend, quiet enough for hushed thunder.
