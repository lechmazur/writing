Each winter, after humans hibernate, the coastal cave network belongs to the handful exempt from sleep, a wakeful guild that keeps the tide gates and dreams in balance.  While nine thousand sleepers breathe salt air in limestone niches, Maren, a sensory map maker, walks the dark with copper mesh under her boots.  Her method is heat-mapping emotions: the mesh reads warmth leaving dreamers and stains stone red with fear, blue with longing, or white with calm.  A good chart warns which chambers need lamplight, which valves must open, and which sleepers are losing the wish to wake.  Maren wanted more than maintenance; she wanted to become the voice of the unknown, the speaker who named uncharted passages at spring waking.  Her mother had vanished in the western tunnels, and the official map stopped at a blank shape called the Throat.  Maren believed that if she learned that darkness, the council would let her speak for it.  The sleepers carried transitional objects into hibernation: cups, braids, keys, knitted mice, and stones rubbed smooth by other winters.  Manuals said such things eased the crossing from noisy selves to folded bodies, but Maren knew they did stranger work.  The Bureau called them factually mystical, because their heat could be measured and their mercy could not be explained.  On the nineteenth night, her chart flared cold and black above the lower dormitories, where no terror should remain.  At that hour, a storm tide pressed the coast, and the eastern valve answered with a clogged groan.  If it stayed shut, air would foul, water would climb, and three hundred sleepers would not wake.  Maren followed the cold stain, passing rows of quiet faces and small objects tucked beneath their hands.  The trail ended at a sealed niche beyond the nursery, where a chalk shelf held one yellow leaf pressed into it.  It was an elm leaf fossil, impossible in marine limestone and older than any forest the coast had kept.  Beside it, a message in the hand of a child tightened her throat: For the summer I must leave.  No record named that child, yet Maren understood the object, because every map maker studied the first hibernation.  When humanity first slept through a killed summer, the fossil was placed at the cave mouth as a promise that green time would return.  It was a transitional object for an entire species, walled away when the promise grew too painful to keep.  Maren pried the stone loose, expecting proof for the council and a path into the Throat where her mother had vanished.  Instead, the elm leaf fossil warmed with her wanting, lifted from the shelf, and began to float above her mesh.  It did not drift with cave wind; it turned toward the lower dark like a compass given a conscience.  Maren could have cupped it, carried it to the council, and claimed the unknown had chosen her.  The valve groaned deeper, and a cup in the dormitory chimed once against stone.  Maren set her palm flat, let the fossil float ahead, and read the emotions it borrowed from her by heat.  Her ambition burned orange, fear of losing it burned green, and beneath both lay a colder blue belonging to the sleepers.  She understood that the object was mistaking her private hunger for the public crossing it was made to guide.  So she stopped naming it proof, opened her chart, and offered the fossil every measured warmth from nine thousand dreamers.  The leaf shone with small wishes, spun once, and shot through a hairline crack beneath the nursery wall.  Maren squeezed after it until she reached the buried throat of the eastern valve.  There the fossil wedged itself against a mineral latch, and the captured heat of cups, braids, keys, and mice pushed the mechanism free.  Air moved first, then water fell back from the steps, and the storm became a long, ordinary sigh.  By morning the fossil was gone, leaving an elm shaped stain on her chart and a dry passage west.  It opened into the blank shape where the official map ended, but far chambers kept their own weather.  At spring waking, the council asked Maren to speak, because she had saved the lower dormitories and entered the Throat.  She accepted the title, then described the cave as a chorus of sleepers, objects, tides, and doorways refusing a single name.
