Mara reached the mangrove labyrinth inhabited by singing spirits in the space where an apology waits unspoken, clutching her brother's last letter.  The tide stood still between two heartbeats, and every root held a soaked bundle of unsent letters tied with hair, thread, or ribbon.  Above her, drowned chapel panes formed a corridor of windows breathing salt.  Behind them, singing spirits rehearsed sentences their owners had never mailed, tender as gulls and merciless as knives.  Sable, the cryptic medium, waited at a misted window, her coat stitched with pockets that bulged and fluttered.  Sable survived by trading in messages in condensation on windows, because spoken words were snatched before they reached any living ear.  In her right hand she carried a gargoyle's rainspout shard, gray and older than any church in the swamp.  One finger shaded its broken edge as Sable wrote on the window: the gargoyle's rainspout shard heals in moonlight, so every cut closes when the moon clears the leaves.  The question on the nearest pane was simple: What do you want me to evoke?  Mara answered with her fingertip: I came to find the moment before deciding.  She meant the morning after she and Jonas had quarreled over their dying mother's care, when his boat still knocked softly against the pier.  She had written the words I am sorry six times, decided pride was safer than his silence, and by evening the storm took him.  The seventh letter, still unsent, had warmed under her dress until it felt less like paper than a second, accusing heart.  Sable read the answer and looked at Mara with fierce tenderness, as if she meant to shield her and refuse her in the same breath.  Sable wrote that the dead are not lost but badly addressed, then scratched a hairline door into the fog with the shard.  A spirit behind the glass sang in Jonas's voice, only the roughness of breath held beyond a door.  Do not chase him, Sable's next message warned; chase the hand you almost lifted.  They moved through the labyrinth as pale light gathered beyond the highest leaves, and each pane offered a different version of that morning.  On one pane, their mother's cup sat unwashed; on another, Jonas buttoned his coat; on a third, Mara's mouth opened without sound.  The singing spirits pressed close, and their chorus made every unsent letter in the roots tremble like a fish in a net.  Sable flinched at one note, and Mara saw a pane hidden in her coat, addressed in the same careful hand.  For years I have guided others to the words I owed my son, Sable wrote, the letters shaking until her thumb steadied them.  Mara understood then that the cryptic medium's riddles were not armor but bandages, wrapped too tightly because the wound beneath them never closed.  She touched Sable's sleeve, and the older woman did not pull away; instead, she pressed the shard into Mara's palm with a grip that hurt.  Only an unhealed edge can evoke the pause, Sable wrote; moonlight is nearly here.  Mara scored the last window before the moon broke free, and the glass exhaled fog that smelled of tar and her mother's kitchen.  There was the moment, not years behind her but alive in the pause between her hand and the latch.  Jonas stood beyond it, angry and young, holding an apology of his own that he had not yet decided to spend.  Mara saw that she had mistaken one choice for a locked door, when she had been gripping the latch every day since.  The singing spirits softened; they did not forgive her, because forgiveness was not theirs, but they made room for one unaccompanied human voice.  With the shard shaking and already knitting smooth beneath the rising moon, Mara wrote on the fog: I am sorry, Jonas, and I was afraid.  The window showed no answer; it became clear, letting mangrove roots, trembling panes, and Sable's wet face appear beyond it.  In that clearness, the apology that had waited unspoken was no longer a hiding place, and the still tide began to move.  Sable opened the hidden pane and breathed until a message formed, while Mara kept the healed shard warm in her palm; neither mended quickly, but both began.
