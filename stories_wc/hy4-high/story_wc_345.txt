The orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches was chosen by mistake, a refuge where broken ships grew gravity fruit until the war passed. 
In the era they called after strangers become family, nobody asked which ship had planted which row. 
Children slept in mixed houses, recipes carried three accents, and harvest rolls began with dead names. 
The nebula of respite came once each long orbit, violet weather that let trees and people sleep while twin suns burned. 
Old settlers called it mercy, but children knew it as the week adults sat down. 
Mara, an altruistic ghost, had missed the first mercy by nine days. 
She drowned in a culvert carrying water to infants from ships that had not learned to share kitchens. 
Since then she could lift chalk dust, chill cups, and apologize in dreams for wet boots. 
She worked through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn, when frost made pavement soft enough for thin fingers. 
Because one warning kept infants from a mudslide and another named a cracked seal before gauges stirred, Mara was strangely trustworthy. 
Even council skeptics carried chalk in their pockets. 
Mara's purpose was to orchestrate collective effort, because the labor ahead needed more hands than any ghost could borrow. 
Each seventy year passage of the nebula of respite required arches washed, weighted, and tuned, so hollow stone could hold mist instead of spilling into thorns. 
For generations that work had been split into songs, one for every street. 
Lately the songs were lullabies hummed without listening. 
People believed the nebula would forgive them, as family does after a hard year. 
At first light a new message appeared outside the bakehouse: REEXAMINE. 
Under it, in smaller letters, Mara had written the butterfly garden watering bell. 
Nadi, who kept the garden and resented riddles from a dead woman, almost scrubbed the words away. 
Her grandmother had called the bell a relic, a bronze cup sprinkling dew over blue butterflies for vanished tourists. 
It hung above brambles, vines, and silk wings feeding there. 
The next dawn added one line to every deserted walk: Bring tools, not opinions. 
By the third dawn, each street had a different instruction, and none made sense alone. 
Bakers saved sour mash, masons chipped lime, children gathered wing scales, and gravediggers opened the first survey case. 
People grumbled but went, because Mara never wasted their effort and Nadi carried a ladder. 
She did not want to reexamine anything; she wanted to prove the ghost wrong and sleep. 
Inside the bell, under mineral crust, her fingers found seven notches, a map of arches, and a sentence from the first ships. 
The survey case supplied the translation: The bell is not for butterflies; it gives the stone its note. 
Only then did Nadi reexamine the old songs and hear seven notes for seven districts that had to sound at once. 
The watering was resonance, the mist was chorus, and the nebula of respite would settle only over arches sharing one chord. 
Nadi ran home with the bell under her coat, laughing and cursing, while chalk bloomed behind her in assignments. 
That afternoon the council split the planet into crews by note, not ship. 
Children pasted wing scales on the clapper, bakers glued lime, and gravediggers read first names so no founder became a stranger. 
At dusk the violet cloud reached the outer moon, and heat cracked empty canals. 
Arches drifted lower, heavy with unborn mist, and thorn rows snapped sparks where their shadows crossed. 
Nadi lifted the butterfly garden watering bell over the plaza, and seven districts struck tuned stones on her counted beats. 
One by one, old songs rose from separate streets, then steadied like hands pulling one rope. 
The arches answered with a low silver note, and mist poured from their pores. 
Under that roof, the suns spent their fire on nothing that could be hurt. 
For seven days the planet slept, and brambles softened enough for blue butterflies to feed. 
When the people woke, no one claimed rescue, because every roof, hand, and song had belonged to everyone. 
At the next dawn, the sidewalk by the bakehouse held one dry line: Rest is a thing we build. 
Nadi left the line unswept, and the garden kept its thorns, its wings, and its brambled hope.
