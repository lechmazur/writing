In the wet spring after the cryptocurrency crashes, the Reconstruction Office stopped asking Mara Venn to forecast anything and hired her to map cultural patterns instead.  The rain-beaded skylight loft above the failed Helix Exchange smelled of damp paper, cold solder, and the candle wax her mother had melted into every floorboard.  Mara was an onyx-ink cartomancer, which meant that her cards showed queues, evictions, kitchens, ferries, and empty wallets instead of cups or towers.  She had inherited the deck, the lease, and an unfinished instruction: Not prophecy, triangulation.  Because official ledgers had vanished into frozen wallets and embarrassed silence, rumor had become the city's most reliable census.  Clerks wanted to know why candles appeared at dead ATMs, why miners traded rigs for soup pots, and why children drew ladders on bankrupt galleries.  Mara was steadily curious rather than dramatic, so she spent weeks matching those small ceremonies to the incomplete readings her mother had left in a cedar trunk.  Each reading was an answer without a question: black cards pinned to butcher paper with no scale or date.  She called the work reverse engineering, because she was not asking the cards for fate but recovering the arithmetic by which her mother had turned panic into a diagram.  The only instrument that made sense was a cloth measuring tape coiled beside the deck, its centimeters faded and its edge pricked with tiny black marks.  At first Mara measured spaces between cards, then between skylight rain tracks, then between shrines visible from the tram.  The black marks repeated those distances at smaller scale, as if her mother had folded neighborhoods into a pocket.  That turned heirlooms into instruments, and made the loft feel less like a relic room than a patient argument.  Standard cartomancy chose one Seeker and laid one future, but Mara worked by multiplying paths, giving every card as many exits as a frightened crowd could imagine.  A closed exchange might breed a protest, a bread line, a shrine, a rumor, a debt circle, or a song, and every branch needed measuring.  By midnight the floor was a net of threads, and the measuring tape lay through it like a river that had learned to count.  The trouble was a blank seam near Ninth Street, where every multiplied route bent toward Ivo Kade's bakery and then disappeared.  Ivo had written wallet software; after the crash he baked rye and pinned rent notices beside useless coins.  Mara had been taught never to confide in clients, but the blank seam required a witness who trusted stories more than balances.  Before dawn she carried the deck, the measuring tape, and three overlays to his warm shop, where loaves were scored with a razor.  She chose to confide in Ivo about her vanished mother, the uncanny tape, and her fear that she had reconstructed not a method but an obsession.  Ivo listened, unlocked a floury drawer, and confided that his mother had kept the Exchange's complaint book after the servers were removed.  People had queued there to list lost sums, but his mother asked who had walked with them, shared a phone, promised flour, or wept.  Each answer became a bread ticket, filed by the route people took afterward, not by the money lost.  When Mara stretched the tape across those tickets, its marks matched the bakery routes, including the courtyard turn she had read as absence.  Mara understood that their mothers had built halves of one instrument, separating numbers from names so no official could seize both.  The blank seam was not missing data; it was where people stopped mourning alone and began practicing mutual aid, which the old deck could not name.  That was why other maps failed: they counted balances instead of customs, and customs survive by multiplying paths when doors close.  Back in the rain-beaded skylight loft, Mara rebuilt the overlay with Ivo, adding one new card: The Shared Table.  She delivered no single route, only transparent sheets, so clerks, nurses, tenants, and cooks could lay path over path and see their choices reflected.  By autumn the overlays had reached clinics and union halls; the inherited mystery had simply acquired more hands.
