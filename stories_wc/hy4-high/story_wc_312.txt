On the sapphire viaduct at dawn, after the last fish is caught, the city of Lume weighed what was left of the sea.  Blue arches dripped into numbered bowls, and clerks called each drop a fraction of the world's debt.  Oren, the lukewarm mage, held the broken ship's bell beneath the seventh arch, able only to keep rainwater from trembling.  Earnestly naive, he believed large disasters were small mistakes with long shadows.  Stars rose early, fish cast two shadows, and the moon negotiated with a well.  Lume treated each plausible impossibility as an accounting error, not a miracle.  The Bellwardens called it a cosmic imbalance and ordered a perfect restoration.  Their brittle devotion had cracked before the bell did.  For three centuries the First Vessel's bell had taken nine exact strokes, with no interval altered by weather or arithmetic.  When the catch shrank and the sea's weight changed, they struck harder instead of listening, and the bronze split.  The lost note entered the dripping rates.  By sunrise, Oren had to rebuild the bell and to right a cosmic imbalance.  Warden Pell gave him an approved mold and a warning not to improve anything.  Oren thanked him, too earnestly naive to hear a warning as a wall.  He laid the mold aside and held a tin cup under the nearest seam of blue glass.  Drops fell in three tempos: quick pairs, a slow central beat, and a stutter where the lost note had vanished.  He marked fillings and used lukewarm magic to stop the chill from hurrying them.  By understanding dripping rates, he saw the viaduct as a rain instrument tuned to the First Vessel.  Each interval named a missing gram of bronze or a necessary gap in the first bell.  The approved mold hid all of that under devotional paint.  Pell found the wet labels and said the sacred mold allowed no improvisation.  Oren offered a label; Pell's refusal carried brittle devotion, clean and proud and already broken.  Pell left to prepare the ninth stroke.  Mara, who had caught the last fish, climbed up with a basket smelling of cold salt.  She asked why he listened to cups; Oren told the truth because he had never learned a useful lie.  She gave him bronze net-menders: the fish had been light by one scale, so perhaps heaven was short by one note.  Oren chose to rebuild by listening, not by forcing the pieces into the approved outline.  He wedged shims under the crack, tuned each with a lukewarm drop, and waited for the interval to change.  Fast drips demanded more lip; slow ones needed a hair of metal filed away.  The bell grew ugly, ribbed, and honest in the way only mended things can be.  Above it, sapphire arches sweated one rhythm, and clerks stopped writing fractions.  Pell returned at sunrise's edge and saw the uneven bell, cups, and Mara's empty basket.  The first beam crossed the viaduct; drips struck bronze, and the mended bell sounded without being hit.  It was not a grand toll, only the missing note, passing from blue stone into morning.  The ninth hammer stroke landed in silence; the count was already complete.  Under the harbor, the emptied sea shifted by the weight of one fish scale.  The fish's two shadows flowed together, the moon left the well, and the icehouse stairs melted in order.  Clerks entered zero beside the world's debt and cheered with professional embarrassment.  Oren checked the final dripping rate against the new bell instead of cheering.  His devotion was no longer brittle, because it had bent toward evidence and survived.  Pell set down the hammer; later records claimed the mold had been followed.  Oren let it stand, still earnestly naive enough to prefer repair to punishment.  He left the scar on the broken ship's bell visible, and each dawn listened for shoals in the drips.  After the last fish is caught, when dawn turns the sapphire viaduct into blue arithmetic, he still rebuilds one small interval at a time.
