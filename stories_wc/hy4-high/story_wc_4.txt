All spring, as vines climb the smokestack and ladder the broken windows, Mara Vell unlocks the gate to the lavender field tucked behind rusted factory ruins, carrying a dove gray map case.  She is the district's subtle puzzle archivist, keeper of mislabeled deeds, orphaned riddles, and maps that showed routes people had been too frightened to remember.  Years earlier, the mill explosion had taken sixty-two workers and left the survivors speaking in careful fragments, each guarding a private shard of noise, smoke, and blame.  Mara's mother had swept the office that morning, and Mara has returned to the ruins not for justice, which the courts misplaced, but to transmute pain into a masterpiece.  She is perceptively calm, noticing which neighbors need silence before they need tea, and she lets her hand linger on the latch until the bees settle.  Inside the dove gray map case lie not maps but forty black-and-white photographs, salvaged from lockers, bake sales, union picnics, and the hospital tent.  The town council calls them evidence; Mara calls them a puzzle with a gentler solution.  At first glance the pictures show only ordinary gestures: a girl tossing a ball, a welder shading his eyes, a choir bending toward a note.  Yet through each image runs an arc, drawn by smoke, rope, umbrella ribs, backs, arms, or the curved shadow of the ruined water tower.  Mara photographs these arcs again, pins their tracings to linen, and discovers that, arranged by the week of the explosion, they form one enormous unfinished circle.  The missing segment corresponds to the lavender rows, where no one has stood since the funerals because the scent had sweetened the smoke.  She writes no announcement, only delivers small envelopes: Bring the photograph in which you are present, and stand where your arc points.  On Saturday, as vines climb another brick, the baker comes first with flour on his sleeves, then the nurse, twins, widowers, children, and the retired foreman.  They expect a ceremony, but Mara offers clothespegs, string, and the perceptively calm instruction to linger beside any picture that makes breathing difficult.  People linger.  They notice that the ball girl's arc points to the welder's raised arm, whose curve points to the choir, and so on through every lost and living face.  By noon the black-and-white photographs hang in a great uneven ring around the lavender, not as a wall of grief but as a path.  Each neighbor adds a sentence to a shared label, and the labels become the story no single survivor had been able to carry alone.  Nell Orr, who blamed herself for ringing the shift bell, reads that the bell also called the rescue parties; her shoulders loosen as if a knot had been untied.  The foreman admits he hid the maintenance ledger, then places it beneath his photograph, where its curved spine completes a stubborn gap.  Mara does not scold him; she fits the ledger's arc into the circle and steps back, because the puzzle has asked for truth, not punishment.  The collective healing passes from hand to hand via arcs in black-and-white photographs, until even the quietest child points out where lavender stems echo a nurse's bent wrist.  This is not forgetting, the labels insist; it is making room for the factory, the dead, and the living to belong to one another.  A breeze moves through the field, the photographs bow, and their arcs join the purple rows, the climbing vines, and the softened rust.  Someone laughs at a picture of the mayor losing a pie contest, and the laughter, startled but earnest, does not apologize.  Mara opens the dove gray map case one last time and takes out a blank linen panel, the final piece she had feared to claim.  On it she pins a photograph of herself at sixteen, sitting outside the gate with an unopened lunch pail, her own shoulders forming the smallest arc.  The children help her hang it between her mother's union picnic and the rescued ledger, so the enormous circle closes without pretending the center is whole.  As vines climb higher by evening, the visitors linger along the completed ring, reading aloud and swapping names, recipes, apologies, and the absurd nicknames of stray cats.  The masterpiece is not the installation, though lavender and paper shine together; it is the town learning, arc by arc and with earnest whimsy, to hold what happened and still keep supple hands.
