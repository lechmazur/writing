Across the pause in a difficult conversation, Mara listened to water collect one note at a time in the ancient grotto under stars.  Lio waited for an answer to the question that had hurt them both: would she leave the coast at dawn and come inland with him?  As the tidal-pool chronicler of that shore, she had learned to answer no one while a pool was changing its mind.  Her ledgers chronicled transition periods, thin intervals when sea became basin, basin became mirror, and mirror became rumor of returning waves.  She did not record them for harbor officials, who wanted only tide heights, but to discover the texture of time before it smoothed into memory.  Lio called it a way of refusing life, and his last words before silence had been gentle enough to be unfair.  Mara, warmly observant, saw hurt under his stillness: a thumb rubbed raw, a breath held too politely, shoulders tilted toward the exit.  The grotto held black water in a bowl of stone while stars pricked a seam in the roof.  Salt had whitened carved fish along the walls, each shown with an open mouth, as if paused midanswer.  On the rim lay a jade cicada, no longer than Mara's thumb, left when their mother died of a winter fever.  Their mother had said the insect knew buried years and the shock of wings, so it belonged to thresholds, not grief.  Mara disagreed, because grief was also a threshold, with a before and an after that kept dripping.  Now the jade cicada caught starlight on one wing, and the pool held a second, trembling thread.  Between those glimmers, Mara used the method she trusted: through tangential approach, never staring at the thing she hoped to know.  To foresee the next surge, she watched limestone dust, a hermit crab's hesitation, and Lio's tilted shadow on wet rock.  Each clue stood aside from the event, yet was pulled by it, as a sleeve before the arm decides.  This was why she chronicled pools rather than oceans; oceans announced themselves, but margins confessed.  The pause between Lio's question and her answer was another margin, with a grain she could feel.  It was crowded with shy outcomes nudging one another like bubbles under stone.  She wanted to learn whether edge-time was gritty, silken, brittle, or warm as an offered palm.  Instead of shaping a reply, she gathered signs that would let her foresee where the silence broke.  A drop swelled beneath calcite; the crab withdrew; the jade cicada's bright wing dimmed by one degree.  From those sidling clues she knew seawater would slip over the lip before Lio found courage to speak again.  She knew what he would say: he had not come to take her from the pools, only to ask whether she still had room for him.  It arrived not as prophecy but as the sum of his question, his raw thumb, and his leaving the cicada untouched.  The sea entered without drama, a cold lace crossing the lowest groove, and the pool changed every reflection.  For a moment the stars in water and the stars above no longer matched, and Mara felt the texture she had chased.  It was not smooth; it had a nap like worn cloth, resistance in leaving, and give in leaning close.  Her mother's fever, Lio's departure, her own watches, and the tide's return were all cut from such cloth.  She understood that leaving and staying were not clean doors; they were pools joined by a nick in rock.  The question was how to pass between them without pretending the water had stopped moving.  Mara set two fingers beside the jade cicada, close enough to warm it but not to claim it.  She let the gesture answer: yes to a season inland, no to an ending, yes to carrying the ledgers.  Lio saw her hand by the cicada and loosened the breath he had been holding.  When he spoke, his voice no longer asked for victory, only for a place beside her next low tide.  The pause ended, but not as a door closing; it became the first shallow channel where two lives began to mingle.
