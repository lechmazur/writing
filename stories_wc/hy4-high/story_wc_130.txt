After the orchard grows silent beneath the moon, the cavern orchard nourished by dripping stalactites keeps one small sound for itself.  Water ticks into limestone basins, and roots tighten around their dark sweetness as if listening.  Above, a roof crack admits a white disk, so even underground the trees have weather.  Elias Vey, a secretive conjurer, comes then, because the villagers leave the upper paths at dusk.  He is gently inventive: wax cups soften leaks, wire mirrors share moonlight, and his ladder folds into a staff.  He hides them because tidy answers have hurt the village, and his work concerns his sister.  Orra had walked into the orchard during a harvest feast, laughing at a riddle, and had not walked out.  No grave was dug, no farewell spoken, yet her song still gathered in pear skins after rain.  The village called it ambiguous loss, letting no door close and no table be cleared.  Elias had come to understand the mathematics of emotion, hoping to give such waiting a shape he could carry.  Under his caution lay buried optimism: measured grief might be shared.  In his coat rides the taxidermist's glass eye pair, amber and gray, made for foxes.  He bought them under a false name, since a conjurer studying grief with animal eyes invites cruel speculation.  One eye sees what is absent; the other sees what refuses to leave.  At the orchard's center, half buried among roots, lies a mosaic stepping stone from the first planting.  Generations of feet and falling fruit have chipped its tiles into a chart of gaps, arcs, and staggered squares.  The old growers used it to reckon water shares, but Elias has learned to read it as a grammar of almosts.  To dovetail the views, he sets each eye in cedar and reads corrections via the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone.  The left eye takes the drip that fell when Orra vanished; the right takes the drip falling now.  He writes intervals in chalk, subtracts years, and adds the taste of winter pears.  The answer changes whenever a drop strikes a basin, so his equation grows a pulse instead of an end.  He almost smiles, because an unending equation is not yet a defeat.  At the third alignment, the glass pair catches moonlight through the roof crack and casts two small circles on soil.  Between them, dust rises as a seated woman thinner than window breath.  Elias does not say her name, fearing it might seal the apparition into a conclusion.  He asks whether she is dead, and the dripping waits with him.  The dust figure points not to herself but to roots under the stone.  A pear seed, pale as a tooth, has wedged itself in a chipped tile and split there.  Elias understands that Orra's absence has been feeding something, though he cannot tell whether the something is Orra.  Ambiguous loss stops sounding careful and starts sounding honest, like a door left open.  He wanted clean proof, but the stone offers a joint where uneven truths bear weight.  That is what it means to dovetail grief, he thinks, not to make the missing piece disappear.  Beside the water marks, he scratches a remainder with an upward curve.  It is not an answer, but it gives tomorrow a place to enter.  The dust woman leans close, and her face is his own, older and less hidden.  He realizes secrecy has been a second absence, a vanishing beside Orra while still taking room.  The thought hurts gently, like a seed pressing against soil it has already chosen to trust.  When the circles fade, Elias leaves the taxidermist's glass eye pair out of his coat.  Instead, he dovetails cedar strips, turns the mirrors outward, and fixes the eyes above the stone.  Anyone coming after moonfall can look twice and count what the orchard remembers.  Nearby he leaves chalk, the folded ladder, and a paper scrap bearing an unfinished equation.  The village may laugh, or add its losses to the margins, beginning a common mathematics.  Before leaving, Elias presses the split seed deeper into the tile and covers it with cave soil.
