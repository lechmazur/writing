The guild timed all bartered awakenings for after the phoenix rises, when the city furnace still purred and glass could remember a borrowed hour.  In the glassblower's courtyard, brokers laid pillows for sleepers and silver cups for buyers, calling the exchange mercy.  Mara, a colorblind painter, came to buy one hour for Oren, the old glassblower who had taught her to look at cracks before colors.  Her motive, repeated whenever patrons demanded scarlet skies, was to find beauty in imperfection.  Because she could not tell scarlet from soot, she noticed wobbling lips, trapped seeds, bubbles, and the thumbprint that made a vessel honest.  Such noticing had made her innocently cunning, for people mistook her mild questions for ignorance and answered them exactly.  Since the rising burned bells dumb and left ledgers curling into ash, the guild did business by communicating through plant arrangements.  A pot of upright ferns meant a fair price, bent ferns meant refusal, and a ring of nettles around rosemary meant debt.  Mara read the courtyard as she would read a face, and found no fern set straight.  Every bargain nodded toward the silver cups while the sleepers' pillows sank deeper into cinders.  Oren lay beneath a cracked glass canopy, breathing thin, his right hand blue from the last phoenix feather he had blown.  Mara had no coin, only a small panel painted with his shattered eyes and the lopsided lilies that grew beside his furnace.  Broker Vale accepted the panel with two fingers, smiled at its uneven petals, and offered a sleeping moth in a paper box.  He explained that the moth held one discarded fragment of Oren's waking, too short for auction and thus cheap.  Mara asked why a fragment would be discarded when Oren's breath was still warm.  Vale, amused by the colorblind woman who missed his ruby seal, explained that whole awakenings sold best, while flaws became keepsakes.  He did not notice her arranging the weeds at the edge of his table, because he thought plants were only a clerk's habit.  Mara placed nettle around rue, then rosemary inside a bent fern, asking the only question her innocence could safely ask: who kept the largest piece?  From the cooling oven's shadow, a child's hand answered with three thyme stems and one crooked marigold, a sign for the furnace master's privy purse.  The answer made sense; Vale had arrived in a coat heavy with hidden brass.  Mara did not accuse him; she painted his crooked hem, and while he preened, saw the outline of a concertina reed tongue.  Such tongues tuned the glass bells that drew borrowed hours from sleepers, and a perfect tongue could command a whole clean waking.  The one in Vale's coat was nicked at the tip, so the guild had thrown it aside with the flawed fragments.  To Mara, that nick looked like the crooked lilies by Oren's furnace, and she wanted it more than any unbroken instrument.  She returned the moth and bartered her silence about the privy purse for broken metal and permission to arrange the auction plants.  Vale agreed because silence seemed cheaper than suspicion, and a colorblind painter with weeds in her lap could not read guild music.  He was almost right: Mara could not name tones, but she felt the reed tremble when the furnace breathed.  She spent the next hour among pots, communicating through plant arrangements with apprentices who had lost sleep to the same ledgers.  Ferns passed hand to hand, nettles circled every silver cup, and marigolds tilted toward the cracked canopy where Oren lay.  At the auction bell, buyers lifted cups for one clean hour apiece; instead each cup held a bent stem and green sap.  Mara set the concertina reed tongue inside Oren's last lopsided vase and lowered it toward the furnace mouth.  Vale understood too late that broken stems pointed toward broken vessels, and that a nick could be a gate.  Mara meant the reed's first note to awaken the sleepers, not to fill another silver cup.  It shook loose every stolen hour at once.  Green sap climbed each stem, bent ferns straightened, and sleepers beneath the cracked canopy breathed together.  Oren opened his eyes, saw Mara's crooked lilies, and laughed at the blue stain on his imperfect hand.  Vale reached for the silver cups, but apprentices with nettles refused another price.  By sunset, the guild's ledgers were mulch, and no bartered awakenings remained in the courtyard.  Mara painted the rescue without scarlet: a nicked reed, bent plants, waking faces, and glass full of stubborn bubbles.
