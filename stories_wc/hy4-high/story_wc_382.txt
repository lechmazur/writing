When I took the room beneath the Harrow Building's roof, I wanted to learn the language of old buildings before the place finished telling me something I was afraid to understand. 
The stairs had begun tapping my grandmother's private nickname for me, three soft knocks, a pause, and two more, while the banister smelled of the lemon oil she used in a kitchen I had never entered. 
I had been hired to catalogue each room before the owners remade it, and every measured wall seemed offended by my tape. 
On my first evening the tea tin was empty, so the woman downstairs sent me to Ivo Vale, whom the house directory still listed as the dead language tutor. 
His door opened before I knocked, and the warm room beyond it held the same lemon oil, a kettle, and a chair drawn to my exact height. 
I asked for sugar because that was the ordinary sentence my mouth knew how to make. 
He gave me a blue cup heaped with it, said, "Bring the cup back when it is empty," and smiled as if we had rehearsed the joke. 
What began as borrowing sugar that turns into weekly tea sessions was never an accident, though I called it one for several visits. 
By then I knew that Ivo taught inscriptions no one spoke aloud, and that the other tenants left his cup unwashed with the care people reserve for relics. 
At first we drank beside his window; later he carried the tray up to the rooftop garden, where bean vines leaned over the parapet like listeners. 
He was patiently nimble, never spilling a drop as he moved cups, books, wax, and silence into the exact order a lesson required. 
"Old buildings do not have words," he told me, "only stresses, habits, and debts that keep their shape after everyone who named them is gone." 
"To interrogate them, you must borrow something small, return at the same interval, and accept the answer they can manage." 
He made that sound like grammar, not superstition, because he wrote each knock, draft, and damp stain in a narrow notebook. 
Only once did I ask whether he believed the Harrow Building was listening, and he said, "We have agreed upon lies; let us keep them clean." 
The first lie was that he was alive, though his hand never warmed the blue cup and no footprint followed him across the frost on the roof. 
The second was that I came for lessons in Sumerian, not because the stairs knew my grandmother's nickname. 
The third was that the Harrow Building was silent, which everyone in it repeated with such tenderness that the walls seemed to blush. 
Ivo said those lies were not curtains over truth but handles for lifting it without breaking it. 
He called them the mortar of any language, and I believed him because the building's taps grew clearer whenever I stopped demanding facts. 
The object at the center of every lesson was an antique desk seal, brass darkened to the color of tea, its face cut with a tower and a crooked vowel. 
Ivo warmed red wax over the kettle, pressed the seal into cracks, and showed me how a question could be asked without using the throat. 
A sill answered with a hairline print; a chimney answered with soot shaped like an opened hand; a loose tile answered by refusing to move. 
The last lesson unfolded in the rooftop garden during a meteor shower, with meteors combing the dark above the bean vines. 
Ivo set out three cups, though only one of them ever emptied. 
Each falling streak made the roof tiles flutter with brief silver reflections, and the building answered in a low, familiar syntax of settlement. 
I told him I had found his name in the office ledger, under a column no clerk would translate for me. 
He only refilled the blue cup and said, "A good student does not interrogate the teacher before the house has finished its sentence." 
I pressed the antique desk seal into warm wax to interrogate the parapet. 
My patiently nimble fingers lifted wax printed with a plan of a sealed room. 
I knew the loose tile before Ivo pointed. 
Inside lay my grandmother's letters and his last lesson. 
Buildings survive when wants are translated as rooms. 
I catalogued the hidden space as a load bearing absence. 
The owners accepted that lie because repairs sounded costly. 
Weekly tea continues; sugar is borrowed; his cup stays full. 
The house taps, and I answer in agreed upon lies.
