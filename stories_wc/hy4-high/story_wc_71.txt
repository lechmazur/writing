In the quiet bay in spring, even the gulls seemed to apologize before calling, and the water held a hushed blaze of pink light long after the sun went down.  It was while the streetlights daydream, milky and premature above the cobbles, that Mara Vell walked toward House Seventeen with her notebook and a small blue packet.  As the town's creeping normality noticer, she recorded changes too gradual to alarm anyone, such as a step sinking one fingerwidth each winter or a green door fading toward gray until the neighbors swore it had always been gray.  She had taken the post, which paid in flour and lamp oil, to understand why some houses feel alive, because after her mother died their own perfumery had become a correct arrangement of bottles and not a home.  In the blue packet lay the perfumer's orris root shard, a pale, knuckled piece her mother had used to fix scents that wanted to vanish.  Her mother used to say that orris held more than fragrance, that a room treated with it could keep a shard of hush from the moment after a beloved voice stopped.  Mara had considered this beautiful nonsense until grief made her notice how silence clung to some objects and slid off others.  Now the council wanted her to sanctify House Seventeen before the summer tenants came, since the empty place had begun to make respectable people remove their hats.  They complained of shutters opening before rain, of a stair that creaked at six each evening, and of a warm square on the wallpaper where no picture hung.  To Mara, such reports usually meant hinges, damp wood, and sun, but she had learned that ordinary causes did not explain why the heart obeyed them.  Nils, the carpenter's boy, carried her ladder and complained that dead houses should at least keep their furniture where a body could find it.  The old rite in the council book was practical and severe: To sanctify a house, one worked through displaced objects, never with candles, bells, or loud consoling words.  A spoon went into the bedroom, a boot into the pantry, and a chair was turned to face the wall until dawn, so every room had to admit it had been altered.  Mara had never believed the rite changed a house, only that it made the living look carefully enough to stop calling a place empty.  House Seventeen received them with a dry sweetness of floorboards, lilac from the yard, and the faint violet dust of orris when she opened the packet.  Nothing leaped out, which disappointed Nils and relieved Mara, because her work depended on the world being slower than fear.  She began in the kitchen, listing each oddity: a blue cup in the umbrella stand, a clock key inside a left shoe, and a flour tin holding three polished pebbles.  Most people would have called the muddle of a dead woman forgetfulness, but Mara's notebooks from twelve years showed a sequence as gradual as tide.  Ada Bell had moved that blue cup three inches a month, then from table to shelf to stand, always after the supper hour when her drowned husband used to reach for tea.  The stair creaked because Ada had shifted a loose tread with thin wedges each April, keeping the little complaint he made when he came home.  She had not built a ghost, Mara realized; she had trained the house to expect a man so slowly that expectation became its own tenant.  Nils, squatting by the boot in the pantry, said that maybe a house feels alive when somebody keeps missing someone in the same place for years.  His grumble was inadvertently profound, and it annoyed Mara because it fit the evidence better than the council book did.  She set the perfumer's orris root shard on the mantel beside a square of unfaded wallpaper, where the room's hush gathered most strongly.  As orris fixed a perfume, the root seemed to fix that hush, not abolishing it but giving it edges, so Mara could finally hold what Ada had made.  The hushed blaze at the windows deepened, and the streetlights outside kept their soft, useless vigil as if still daydreaming.  To complete the rite, Mara chose one last displacement, not from the book but from Ada's ledger, whose final note said only: Move the cup again, or the room will stop waiting.  Mara placed the blue cup in the pantry beside the boot.  By morning she understood that sanctifying was keeping a careful place for absence, not driving it out, and that was why houses felt alive.
