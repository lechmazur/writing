The prism sanctuary under starlight made no softened shadows; every facet divided the night into hard colors, and Mara Vale worked in that lucid unrest with a cracked funeral mask across her knees. 
People called her the broken mask restorer, because she could fit split cedar, pearl, and lacquer back into faces that still knew how to weep. 
Such masks were not ornaments in that coastal order; they were anchors, and a finished face gave the dead a shape they could inhabit without wandering. 
Mara had taken the office to ground restless spirits, but she also took it because her brother Iven had gone into the reef wearing a mask she had left unfinished. 
In the chapel below the glass, the prism sanctuary under starlight accumulates silence, each panel trapping one breath from every burial until the air lies heavy as wet rope. 
The old keepers called that weight an interlaced hush, a woven quiet in which one hush held another, so grief could be borne without breaking into noise. 
Only an interlaced hush could ballast a ghost; plain silence thickened around it. 
That winter, seven repaired masks began sweating brine, and their painted eyes turned toward the floor where no one had opened a grave for a hundred years. 
Mara understood the summons: the sanctuary had stored too much hush, and beneath its central prism lay the names of fishermen snarled into a net from the first wreck. 
The abbot's instruction was exact: she must exhume those names by unraveling knots in a centuries-old fisherman's net, loosening each mesh without cutting a single cord. 
Cutting would scatter the spirits, because their names had been knotted in the order of their drownings, and only patient fingers could persuade the sequence to release them. 
For such work Mara carried a tapestry needle, blunt at the tip and rubbed bright by her mother's hands, the same tool she used to draw silk through fractures in masks. 
She had never used it on the dead, and the thought made each star above the sanctuary seem like the eye of someone waiting to be recognized. 
The rite allowed one interval, across sharing a secret, from the first spoken word to the listener's answer; after that answer, the glass would seal. 
Mara brought Nella, her apprentice, because the girl had steady hands and because a secret was not shared until another person agreed to carry it. 
Together they lifted the central prism, revealing a floor of black sand and the net, stiff with salt, folded around a hollow where no bones remained. 
The first knot was no larger than a tooth, yet the whole sanctuary leaned toward it, eager to spend its accumulated silence in one suffocating breath. 
Mara set the tapestry needle beneath the strand, began her confession, and heard how small the truth sounded against the clear, unrestful light. 
She said, "I cut the channel knot before Iven sailed, not to hurt him, but to free the porpoises my father had trapped, and I let the town call it a storm." 
Nella's lips parted at once, but Mara looked at the net, and the girl understood that mercy meant withholding the answer until every knot had been given time. 
So the secret remained suspended, neither hidden nor finished, making a bridge of duration over black sand. 
Under that bridge, Mara worked: knot after knot yielded to the needle, and each released name rose like a minnow, silver, bewildered, and unwilling to return to pressure. 
As they rose, she threaded them through the cracks of the waiting masks, not to hide them but to give each face a memory it could hold. 
The hush changed as she worked, not lessening but interlacing more finely, until silence had strands and the strands bore weight without closing around anyone's throat. 
Mara felt Iven near the last knot; his unfinished mask warmed against her thigh, and the old guilt became specific, almost usable. 
She told Nella the rest without decorating it: she had restored a thousand faces because she could not bear to look at his. 
Nella waited until the final mesh loosened, then answered, not with pardon, which would have sealed the glass too soon, but with the plain weight of receipt. 
She said, "I carry it." 
The glass closed like a held breath accepted, and the masks stopped sweating brine. 
Their painted eyes no longer searched for sea; they looked at the floor as if it had become ground. 
Mara did not feel forgiven; she felt placed, and she lifted Iven's mask without flinching.
