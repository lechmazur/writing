In the ledgers of the nocturnal observatory on a floating isle, Mara Vell wrote the fortieth year after spoons bend reality, the stubborn phrase survivors used for their broken calendar. 
She was a budding rune scholar, quietly deliberate with lenses, star scuffs, and the grief that made other apprentices rush. 
She had taken the night watch to find where shadows go at noon and learn why her mother had followed one into brightness. 
Forty years earlier, polished silver spoons feeding a solar lens had bent cause, distance, and every honest shadow. 
At noon, shadows folded into narrow loops and vanished; panicked scholars sealed every waking instant in copper caskets below the observatory. 
They called those stored moments the sealed awakenings, hoping a damaged day would never become conscious of itself. 
Since then, people woke without the instant of opening their eyes, and noon arrived with no darkness to measure it. 
Mara's mother, once Keeper of the vaults, had left a note saying only that the shadows had been put somewhere for safekeeping. 
On the night the isle crossed its own dawn line, Mara found a clockwork automaton key in her mother's tool drawer. 
With brass feet and a sparrow's body, it carried a tag: For one question only, close every circle. 
The old grammars said such a key could question one sealed awakening, but its bearer must relearn the forbidden art of return by closing loops. 
Opening any loop bred another noon, and another noon could pull the isle into the wound left by the spoons. 
Mara knew only safe runes of straight lines, so she began to relearn by closing loops around kettle rings, lens rims, and orrery tracks. 
Her quiet deliberation mattered; haste made chalk stutter, and a stuttering line remembered the old catastrophe. 
When she set the clockwork automaton key on a track, it walked one circle, waited, and refused the next groove until the first was closed. 
She closed loop after loop, each smaller, while beyond the dome the floating isle's ropes creaked under a city of inverted stars. 
Then the sealed awakenings ticked beneath the floor, pressing softly against her ribs like a thousand sealed instants turning in sleep. 
A careless scholar would have pried a lid for her mother; Mara rubbed out a faulty arc and began again. 
That refusal cost her the rescue she thought she wanted, but the key's bow opened on a sliver of noon. 
In the sliver she saw no face, only the answer her mother had guarded. 
At noon, with the bent sun leaving no honest dark, every shadow slipped into the last unbroken darkness: the instant before eyes open. 
The sealed awakenings held them folded behind human lids, giving waking people enough night to see the damaged day. 
Unseal the vaults, and shadows would flood the sky, blinding the world or letting noon notice itself again. 
Her mother had followed one shadow, understood the rescue, and remained as a Keeper inside the final loop. 
The key offered one question because the answer was a door, and doors are loops that must know when to stay shut. 
Mara closed her hand around the clockwork automaton key, not to open the caskets but to wind the last gear backward. 
She retraced every circuit in reverse, by closing loops she had already closed, until first circle and final circle touched without a seam. 
The sliver folded away, the ticking stopped, and a dark mark like her mother's profile remained on the brass rail. 
Mara did not call it proof, because a budding rune scholar learns that proof needs witnesses, patience, and honest light. 
She recorded the answer, sealed the drawer, and left the key for another careful hand. 
Years later, when apprentices asked about the closed noon shutters, she showed them star charts threaded with tiny black seams. 
By then she had found where shadows go at noon, and kept it with quietly deliberate care, as closed as the last merciful loop.
