The Bureau stamped every report with the era, after numbers change meaning, as if the phrase itself could keep a clerk from reaching for a clock. 
Afterward, seven could mean a chord, a debt, or a direction, and dates sat on paper with the stubborn shine of old paint. 
Because I had been revived with a loan of unclaimed duration, the Bureau named me a borrowed-time timekeeper and gave me the dawn watch in the emerald silo. 
The silo had once held grain, but its green glass ribs now held clocks whose faces showed chalk, tar, rainwater, and skin instead of digits. 
I accepted the watch to understand the texture of time passing, because a debt told me only how much life I owed, not how it felt. 
The old timekeepers had measured intervals; we measured the weave, and the weave had begun to snag. 
My mother, who catalogued ordinary losses before the Change, had warned me that the first snag would feel like a message rather than a failure. 
When censors burned the archive, she and her circle used a quieter method, embedding messages in garage sale item descriptions. 
After the Change, a price of four meant beneath, nine meant almost forgotten, and the adjective pear-green meant the silo, so a shopping notice could become a map. 
I had copied my mother's last listings into a sleeve of oilcloth, and I carried it even though every sensible clerk had abandoned paper. 
On the morning the weave snagged, the wall under my hand went slick as river stone, and the nearest clockface flushed the exact green of bruised pears. 
In the oilcloth sleeve, the listing for a crooked lamp read, pear-green shade, four dollars, asks to be lifted. 
Another notice offered, urn chip, nine dollars, warm to the thumb, poor for flowers, which meant nearly forgotten, touch the warmth, look below. 
I pried up the floor plate beneath the wall clock, expecting a wire, a drowned rat, or one of the Bureau's expired listening devices. 
At the center of the pit lay a damaged time capsule, split along its seam, with wax seals curled like dead leaves. 
Its label had been soaked away, its clockwork spacer had burst, and a fine gray dust clung to the rim as if a whole afternoon had dried there. 
Inside, wrapped in the same oilcloth my mother used for seed labels, was a cremation urn fragment no longer than my thumb. 
I knew the curve of green glaze because I had carried that urn through the rain to the municipal pyre. 
Someone in her circle had broken it after sealing. 
A hairline scratch crossed the clay, and when I tilted it toward the green light, the scratch arranged itself into my mother's handwriting. 
It said, do not repair the count; the count was the cage. 
Before I could decide whether to laugh or call the Bureau, dust rose from the cracked capsule and settled on my eyelids without falling. 
The emerald silo was suddenly timeless. 
My borrowed pulse hung between beats, and the clocks held one green hush. 
I should have been terrified, but the stillness had the clean expectancy of a sealed envelope, and the old listings had trained me for anticipated mysteries, not solutions. 
In that stopped hush I began to rediscover the buried sequence, not by counting listings but by listening to what each adjective made my fingers remember. 
Pear-green meant the silo, beneath meant the floor, nine dollars meant my mother's deliberate near-erasure, and urn chip meant the only piece of her the Bureau had not certified. 
The damaged time capsule had not failed; she had cracked its ordered gears so the preserved moment would spill as texture instead of record. 
Repairing the count would have rebuilt the cage that let officials decide which lives were consecutive and which lives were waste. 
I understood then why a borrowed-time timekeeper had been given the watch: someone made from misplaced duration could feel the cheat in any neat sequence. 
I could have lived forever inside the loan, but texture without passing would have been wallpaper, not time. 
So I set the cremation urn fragment against the broken spacer, where it clicked with the intimate authority of a cup returned to its saucer. 
Time resumed as a rough green weave, and a new garage sale item description offered ash, pear-green, poor for flowers, free.
