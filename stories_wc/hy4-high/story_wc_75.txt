After the beekeeper's last visit, the grotto city behind a monumental waterfall smelled of roses at midnight, though the rose gardens drowned two valleys away.  People called it rosewater dread: the sweetness of welcome bread baked beside a danger no one would name.  Mira Sen, a cultured museum guide in the old opera house, knew glazes, arias, and every founder's date.  A historically oriented curator, she had taken the post to rescue dying arts before the council melted craft tools into hinges.  The newest displaced persons came with river mud on their hems and trades folded into sleeves: net lace, bell casting, fish-skin varnish, clay nightingales that whistled when warmed.  The clerk gave each family a number, a cot, and barley, because numbers moved more easily than names.  Mira answered with measurements, labels, provenance cards, and cases, admiring survival until she nearly missed the survivors.  Only the beekeeper had warned her about names; on his last round he brought wax, honey, and a chipped emerald-tinted opera glass lens from a flooded balcony box.  He said green glass made old salt writing legible, and such writing usually told who had built a place.  Then he crossed through the spray, the bees deserted the chimneys, and the waterfall hardened into a milky wall.  The caverns cooled, flues clogged with rime, and the council ordered useless mouths over the north pass.  Useless meant the displaced persons whose crafts could not be eaten, and Mira understood that her archive had become a tidy obituary.  That evening she held the emerald-tinted opera glass lens to her eye and found hundreds of initials beside sealed vents, small as bee tracks.  Greened by the lens, the walls confessed that the first masons, singers, glaziers, and bakers had been refugees allowed to stay because they could thaw ducts without cracking limestone.  Mira's historically oriented mind raced, but shame followed: she had catalogued the newcomers' arts as survivals instead of claims.  Her method was a family custom of baking bread shaped like initials.  In her grandmother's house, such loaves honored the dead, dusted with rosewater beside an empty chair.  Mira baked them for the living, one letter for each maker, using the last honey in the public ovens.  She shaped an A for Ana the net-lace maker, a B for Besian the bell caster, and thirty-one other letters.  She sent children with crumbs and a message: come to the opera house, bring one tool, and answer to your name.  The displaced persons arrived pale, carrying awls, molds, reeds, cracked whistling birds, and the pride of people asked to be grateful for floors.  Mira set the loaves along the footlights while the museum smelled like a kitchen before a frightening feast.  Through the lens she matched flue marks, loaves, and crafts needing heat: lace near steam, bell wax near warmth, nightingales near the hottest brick.  Together they opened the ovens, cleared rime, and began to thaw the city from its oldest chambers outward.  Water ticked, then ran, and the monumental waterfall answered like a curtain drawn by a tired hand.  The noise hid the council's arrival until ministers found displaced persons teaching apprentices to knot, cast, glaze, and tune birds while warm initials steamed on the rails.  Mira, powdered with flour, did not apologize.  She set the lens before the lamp, showed green-lit salt inscriptions, and dated them to the founding winter, when the charter sheltered every bearer of a necessary art.  The charter had no phrase for useless mouths; it had only the blunt words, displaced persons, beside the right to work.  The ministers, who loved precedents more than mercy, were trapped by history and bread, and they rescinded the north-pass order before the first loaf cooled.  A decree, Mira knew, was only another card if the hands stopped moving.  She made the museum a school with benches, clay, wax, reeds, and one rule: no object entered a case while its maker still wanted to teach.  Each winter, citizens remembered the beekeeper's last visit, baked bread shaped like initials, and set the first loaf beneath the green lens.  Bees sometimes returned to warmed chimneys; no beekeeper did.  The scent remained rosewater dread, sweetness teaching them to watch over what could disappear.  When visitors asked what saved the dying arts, she handed them dough: a culture survives when a displaced person is given a name and fire.
