Upon the hush of budding hope, while mist braided the cliffs, Sela climbed to the verdant parapet beside a waterfall. 
There the orchard grew in two directions, roots in her village's stone and crowns in a neighboring world whose bells had grown faint. 
A radiant hush held leaf, spray, and moss, as if morning feared to lose a color. 
Sela was the wise orchard pruner, known less for what she cut away than for what she taught trees to remember. 
For sixty springs she had practiced weaving between worlds, tying shoots across the seam where dusk leaned into another dawn. 
Her pruning was speech: a notch turned greed toward fruit, a wound made room for light, and a graft returned fragrance to air. 
She was also insatiably creative, though age had taught her to hide invention inside usefulness. 
From reeds, bark, and spider silk she had made a loom fine enough to gather the shimmer of ordinary things. 
She asked each river, loaf, and laughing child for a freely offered gleam, never stealing. 
The village called this clever until the far world's apples came in white, its songs came in whispers, and its painted doors faded to rain gray. 
Then they understood why Sela had spent her life trying to preserve the color of wonder. 
They brought their fears to the parapet, but she had seen the answer in a dream of a seed. 
At her gate, Tovin from the faded orchards held out an heirloom seed wrapped in linen. 
His grandmother had carried it through the closing border, trusting that the pruner beyond would know how to succor a world without plundering it. 
The seed was no larger than a tear, but amber veins warmed Sela's palm like a small yes. 
The grafting books said such a seed could not wake in soil; it had to be woken by capturing essence, and only upon the hush of budding hope. 
Tovin asked her to capture the waterfall's roar, the brightest windows, and all remaining red from his home. 
Sela smiled, remembering the grand mistake of her youth. 
She told him that taking too much would leave a saved world unable to recognize itself. 
She set the loom on the parapet, laced it with shoots from both sides, and chose gentler gifts. 
From her world she took the essence of bread cooling on a sill, a mother humming over a scraped knee, and the first blue shard a child named sky. 
From Tovin's world she took rain on a chalk step, an old dog's patient blink, and sweetness buried in a colorless apple. 
Each gift entered the silk as dew, and Sela's shears clicked only to open channels, never to wound for profit. 
The waterfall softened; even foam seemed to listen, and the radiant hush deepened until Tovin heard his heart making room for hope. 
Sela pressed the captured light into the heirloom seed, but the shell stayed shut. 
One essence was missing, and wisdom, she knew, was a pruning of the self. 
She laid down the bright net and offered what no tool could take: her memory of why she had first loved color. 
She remembered a red kite above winter pears, her sister's green ribbon, and impossible gold in puddles after storms, and gave the memory freely. 
The seed split without sound. 
Green rose through stone, found the seam between worlds, and wove itself with old grafts until the parapet shone like a living panel. 
Tovin gasped because the faded bells rang blue, then bronze, then a green so tender it made him cover his mouth. 
Sela steadied the shoot with a cut no thicker than a mercy, to succor both worlds by leaving their differences intact. 
Color returned first to small things: a bruise on a pear, a thread in a sleeve, the wet black eye of a snail. 
It did not flood or dazzle; it settled as if wonder had been allowed to keep its shyness. 
Sela told Tovin the passage would need cutting, listening, and new inventions each spring, because wonder frays when people stop imagining one another. 
He promised to learn which branches to spare, and she placed the empty linen in his hands, asking for stories, not trophies. 
By evening, the waterfall spoke over two kinds of blossom, and no one could tell which world had given the brighter white. 
In the radiant hush after the work, young leaves trembled, and between worlds, something whole began again.
