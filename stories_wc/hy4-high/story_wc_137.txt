During whale migration, Tavi found Mira in the stargazer's open tower, kneeling over a loose magic square tile.  Below the cliff, whales surfaced in long intervals, their breath hanging briefly above water black as polished slate.  She had come home to sculpt space for reflection after the ferry disaster crowded every room with names.  Grief had made the island loud; the council wanted bright rails, plaques, and a roof for visitors.  Mira wanted inward benches, a bare floor, and an opening where stars could fall without interruption.  The island called her a sympathetic skeptic, because she mended hinges while doubting every household ghost.  Tavi set a lamp beside her, then held out a scrap copied from his drowned sister's notebook.  His sister, Nia, had collected sounds people missed: rope creaks, kettle sighs, whispers before apologies.  The scrap showed nine dusty digits, arranged so every row, column, and diagonal made fifteen.  Mira recognized the pattern on the tile, a square of blue clay cracked at its center.  Old keepers claimed that, during whale migration, the floor held entangled memories rather than private ghosts.  They said one recollection tugged another, as if grief had been knotted underwater by patient hands.  Mira had heard that claim since childhood and had filed it beside charms for favorable winds.  Still, she did not laugh at Tavi, whose fingers kept smoothing the paper as if it shivered.  Tavi asked her to transcribe the last line before the herd turned north, without looking up.  The council would vote at dawn to cover the tower unless Mira presented a usable restoration plan.  Her plan was almost finished, but the old songs in the margins still made no honest sense.  The tower's founder had called the combined motions of stars, whales, and breath an unheard symphony.  Mira considered that pretty nonsense, yet Nia had copied the phrase in careful, delighted letters.  On the tile, the central cell was missing its glaze, leaving a pale square like held breath.  Mira's mother had left a ledger with one instruction: never copy numbers, only copy omissions.  At first Mira obeyed because that instruction sounded practical, not because it sounded sacred.  She measured worn stair treads, gaps in lamp soot, and places where visitors had never stood.  Only through forgotten patterns could she transcribe what the tower had expected listeners to notice.  She began with the whales, marking blow intervals as rests in a score no one would hear.  Then she added star gaps, stair gaps, and the pauses her mother used before saying her name.  Each omission fitted one cell of the square, and the square began to feel less numerical.  It became a quiet machine for attention, arranging absence so absence could be shared.  Near midnight, while she transcribed, a memory arrived that she knew could not be hers.  She tasted Nia's peppermint candy and saw gulls counted from a ferry rail in sunlight.  Tavi, across the floor, whispered Mira's mother's lullaby, a tune she had never told him existed.  The remembered voices did not prove spirits; they proved that lives had crossed and left traces.  Mira kept her skepticism, but she held it more gently, like a lamp cupped against wind.  Rain arrived from the east, scattering her charcoal across the unfinished center of the tile.  The final omission was outside, carved on the northern arch where no lamp could reach.  To read it, someone had to climb the broken ladder above the seaward drop.  Tavi started forward, but Mira tied the rope around her waist and smiled once.  She was not fearless; she was courageously kind, and that distinction steadied her hands.  The ladder groaned, gulls cried below, and whales answered in tones the stone understood first.  She pressed paper to the arch, felt three shallow cuts, and copied their spacing by touch.  Back inside, she set the cuts in the blank cell and closed the magic square.  The tile hummed without sound, transferring a faint tremor through benches, knees, and teeth.  An unheard symphony gathered: starlight, whale pulse, rain, chalk, and all the unsaid names.  Tavi did not ask for Nia again; he sat inside the silence as if invited.  Mira finally understood the founder's plan: reflection required an emptiness no plaque could occupy.  At dawn, the council found villagers seated around the pale center, and postponed the roof.  Mira's plan, with inward benches and uncovered sky, was approved without argument.  Below, whales carried north the unheard symphony, leaving room for memory to answer.
