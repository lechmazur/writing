After being given six months to live, the island caretaker thanked Doctor Senn, paid in pears, and went home to sharpen his pruning knife.  He wrote the date, then one order in his ledger: remodel.  The doctor called it a brazen calm; he knew panic would bruise the fruit.  His charge was the temple orchard set within living stone pillars, where oaks grew between slow columns sweating lime and ticking at dusk.  Pilgrims called the columns saints, bees called them weather, and the shellback tortoises used their shadows as law.  For three centuries the orchard had been arranged so humans took the center path while every other species waited at the edges.  He had spent his youth repairing it, gently systematic with labels, wash water, and lint for scraped shells.  Now death made keeping seem too small a virtue.  He decided to foster interspecies respect before his hands failed, not by sermons but by thresholds, perches, shade, and fruit within reach of differing mouths.  He trusted most a botanical diary left by his grandmother, with margins of rain totals, grafting sketches, and blunt notes.  On the last page were architectural secrets, a phrase he had once mistaken for piety.  The secrets were practical: pillars were rooted mineral animals, arches answered pressure from particular oak limbs, and the temple could be persuaded to make room.  His grandmother wrote that architecture was a durable argument about who deserved space.  Each morning he added one line: Therefore amend the argument.  In the first month he dismantled the raised human table, laid stone ramps for tortoises, and moved the bell rope from petrel nests.  His method was copper wire wrapped around oak branches, tightened after rain, when bark was soft and pillars listened.  The wire did not cut; it suggested, carrying a mineral signal the pillars understood, and the oaks leaned a finger width each week.  Under that red pressure the living pillars opened seams no mason had seen, making low doors for shellbacks and high pockets for petrel nests.  He set pollen shelves behind the arches, water stones below them, and trained a pear limb to fruit at three heights by turns.  The temple council arrived in the second month with clean shoes and the complaint that holiness should not be altered by a dying servant.  He served cider, showed the botanical diary, and said six months made courtesy simpler: he could disagree and be gone before anyone needed to forgive him.  A dean asked whether tortoises could respect an altar, and one climbed the ramp, paused before the old step, and waited for a child.  The child moved, instructed by another creature's patience; the dean said nothing.  By the third month, bees no longer boiled around harvest tables, because their water stones stood untrampled in eastern shade.  Petrels stopped striking the bell, because the oaks had grown a green baffle between rope and nest, so sound arrived as weather.  People lowered their voices near the low doors, not from council rules but because quiet let them hear the pillars tick.  In the fifth month a hard storm came from the east, carrying salt and a noise like planks being counted.  The first gust found every old joint, but the copper wire sang instead of snapping, passing strain from oak to pillar to root.  Seams narrowed for shellbacks, widened for children, held dry pockets for bees, and roofed petrels in high seams.  No species was saved first; each was given a shape it could use, and every shape depended on another's restraint.  When the storm passed, the council found him asleep on warm stone, his ledger under one cheek and a bee on his cup.  They did not wake him with praise; by then they knew sudden human noise was trespass.  On the last morning of the sixth month, he added to the botanical diary: The orchard is not finished, only legible.  He left the book open, and the island caretaker went to look at the sea with all six months used up.
