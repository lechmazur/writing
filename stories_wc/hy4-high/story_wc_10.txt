The city calendars labeled the era "after noise becomes impossible", but inside the winterglass conservatory people called it simply the Hush.  Mineral frost had entered the air that year, swallowing vibration before it could leave a lip, so bells warmed and split while singers felt only their own throats.  Children learned to read eyebrows, floor tremors, and the colored skins of public walls, where temperature wrote what sound no longer could.  The winterglass conservatory had been built as a refuge for such reading, its panes holding summer heat against the snow and turning every change of warmth into visible grain.  Under those panes, Mara Vey kept the last garden of the old harbor and a ledger of things that had survived disaster.  The ledger was titled "unshaken marvels", though Mara knew the phrase was a wish rather than a fact.  She was a capsize survivor from the ferry Aster, pulled from black water at fourteen with her father's tuning fork case strapped under her arms.  Her father had tuned the ferry's engines by ear before the Hush, and her mother had drowned because the warning horn gave no cry.  Mara's voice closed after the rescue, but grief made her silently expressive: a tilt of wrist, a flushed cheek, a cold palm could argue with any sentence.  When the conservatory hired readers of feeling, she became the best of them because she never mistook stillness for emptiness.  Their method was heat-mapping emotions: visitors pressed their palms to winterglass while remembering a loss, and thermal blooms spread through the panes like ink.  Mara copied those blooms as marks of duration, color, and pressure, then sent timed pulses through the brass ribs under the benches.  The mineral fog could not eat contact, so feet and spines received what ears had lost.  She wanted, more than pardon or sleep, to translate silence into music.  Not old music, with trumpets and applause, but an exact art in which warmth became tempo and absence became a note that everyone could feel.  On the twentieth anniversary of the Hush, the city council threatened to melt the conservatory for housing, unless Mara's composition proved that the garden still served the living.  She called the piece after her ledger, and all afternoon survivors, widowers, cooks, children, and thieves filed between the beds of lavender and frost fern.  Each person touched one relic from the cases: a cracked cup, a fern pressed in ice, a baker's scorched glove, a baby shoe, a chart of currents.  The panes recorded their feelings without a whisper, and Mara wove the readings into a gentle, shining score that made the councilors nod before the final page.  Then old Sella, who had pulled Mara from the harbor, laid two fingers on the chart of currents and stared until her hand burned red.  The glass showed a dark ring where Mara had trimmed away every surge of terror, guilt, and rage, leaving the drowned as decorations in a pretty pattern.  To rectify the lie, she would have to stop treating survival as cleanliness and let her own capsize enter the score.  She fetched the tuning fork case from the locked cabinet, its leather furred with salt and its catch bent from the night she had used it as a float.  Inside lay seven forks, six bright and one bowed by water, with her father's pitch marks scratched beside each prong.  Mara struck the bowed fork against the rail, not for air, which would take nothing, but for the metal spine that ran beneath every chair and winterglass wall.  The flawed pulse climbed through the benches, uneven as a heart that has decided to continue, and the panes caught the heat of her answering face.  She did not hide the cold hollow at her chest or the flare along her jaw; silently expressive at last, she let the readers see the shape of being spared.  Sella added her hot handprint to the chart, other visitors added theirs, and Mara rewove the score so the lovely lines broke around those honest burns.  The councilors felt the new music through their soles: no melody restored, no trumpet revived, only intervals of warmth, refusal, and breath held together by glass.  They kept the conservatory open, not because its marvels were untouched, but because the score proved that broken things could keep time.  Afterward Mara changed the ledger's first line to read that unshaken marvels are not objects that never tremble, but patterns that hold after trembling.  She left the tuning fork case open beside the frost ferns, and each winter taught newcomers to press grief to glass, to read the colors, and to translate silence into music.
