Three weeks after planting the wrong seeds, Tallow Fold woke to find extra footsteps beside every bed, some going toward breakfast and some going toward a day that had never been allowed.  Because our valley lay inside a gravitational anomaly, the prints stood upright on the walls as often as the floor, and no one screamed; we had long ago learned that alarm fell upward and came back as laughter.  There was strange comfort in those mild impossibilities: warm bread drifted to the ceiling, but always buttered side down, and the dead kept their customary places at the table without asking for more tea.  I was the celestial signal interpreter for the district, licensed to read the one ledger that the anomaly could not bend, the cold pulses of stars beyond our tilted sky.  The work had made me confidently uncertain, sure of the grammar of flare and eclipse, unsure whether any message from light could be called true.  By the second week, editing history had become another household chore, like skimming cream or sweeping the feathers that gravity forgot.  We were not changing the day my brother Niko drowned, or the winter the bridge burned, or the mayor's ridiculous tax on odd spoons.  We were trimming the false roots that had sprouted from archive seeds mistakenly sown as rye, roots that made Monday remember itself as Thursday and widows remember husbands who had never proposed.  Those seeds had been meant for the museum vault, each one a compressed hour that history had considered and refused.  The wrong planting gave refused hours soil, and the anomaly gave them weather.  My only tool was a dried-up fountain pen with exotic nib, a split crescent of meteoric metal that my mother had used to annotate the Book of Hinges.  Its reservoir had been empty for thirty years, but the nib did not need ink; it scratched causal joints into vellum, and the air filled them with brown light.  The Book of Hinges taught no heroic versions of the past.  It said every repair must be made through contingent relationships, the humble ifs that held larger thens upright.  If Widow Pell had not lent her blue shawl to the bell ringer, the storm bell would have sounded four minutes early, and Niko would never have gone to the millrace.  I worked through those links to thresh meaning from experience, separating the nourishing kernel of what happened from the dry husk of what I had blamed myself for.  Since Niko drowned, I had believed he went to the mill because I had mocked his fear of deep water and he wanted to prove me wrong.  The Book, however, kept returning me to the blue shawl, the late bell, the borrowed cart, and the loose hinge that Niko had promised to mend.  Each relationship was small enough to hold in the palm and strong enough to carry a life.  That was why the false spring tempted me.  In it, Niko came home smiling with river weed on his boots, married Iva from the bakery, and taught our nephews to whistle through apple stems.  He also never carried the hinge, so the millrace gate stayed shut, so the lower fields flooded, so half the children in Tallow Fold simply faded from the school photographs.  A living brother is a large mercy, but a vanished village is a poor price, and I had learned to distrust any comfort that ate its neighbors.  A triple pulse from the Sieve told me to cut the false root at its first contingency.  Pell brought the shawl, Iva brought rye cakes, and I brought the pen, because touched relationships needed their owners.  In the observatory, tethered chairs tugged, and the kettle kept trying to pour.  The curled nib could write only possible histories, so I pressed it between two warm spoons to unbend it.  I wrote: The shawl delayed the bell, so Niko went to the mill for the promised hinge, not for my cruelty.  Brown light sank into the vellum; by morning the school photographs were crowded again.  Niko remained drowned, but my name was no longer the hook in that sentence.  At breakfast, Iva set the rye cakes beside my plate, asking nothing about the ledger.  Pell draped her shawl over me while crumbs circled the ceiling.  The pen was dry again, and the stars kept sending corrections that settled into our strange comfort.
