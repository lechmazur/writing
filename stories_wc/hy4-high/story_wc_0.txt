After the flood, Mira Anand, a neutron star researcher, returned to the storm-damaged greenhouse behind the hill observatory with a broom, a notebook, and a tin of dust from butterfly wing. 
The panes had been starred by hail, the benches tilted in silt, and every label on the seed trays of her mother had loosened into pale curls. 
Mira had grown up among those trays, learning that a wing opens by increments and that a star can spend centuries losing a fraction of a second. 
Her department hoped for a public lecture called "Death of a Star," but she had come to strip needless drama from both the headline and the damaged room. 
For twenty years she had measured a pulsar whose beam swept Earth every second and a half, and the famous flare in the press was only the slow decay of a timing glitch. 
The phrase she kept writing in her notebook was gradual change, because that was all the data ever offered and all the flood had actually done: water, then more water, then a line of silt. 
The portable reduction bench by the door preprocesses reality, as the technicians joked, because no raw photon reaches a graph until the software has divided out the habits of the instrument. 
It did this through accumulated dust patterns, flat maps made from specks on mirrors and glass, so a mote would not be promoted into an event. 
The tin held the first flat she had ever made: dust from butterfly wing, scraped from a blue morpho her mother had found dead under the jasmine and sprinkled across a square of lantern glass. 
The scales scattered lamplight evenly enough for a girl at fourteen to prove a dirty lens could imitate a nova. 
That modest standard had followed her from a school science fair to radio telescopes, through fellowships, launch delays, and the winter her mother stopped answering letters. 
Now the flood had made the whole greenhouse an accidental flat. 
Silt, pollen, wing scales, and powdered putty had dried on the remaining panes in overlapping fans, each one a record of afternoon winds, leaked humidity, and the last push of the storm. 
Mira had come with the broom because she believed dust was contamination, but the slower she looked, the more the fans resembled her pulse profiles. 
If she cleaned the glass first, she would erase the only local record of how decay had entered by degrees. 
Instead she set the old camera on a tripod, aimed it at the east pane, and began to record decay as the damp room dried. 
A label fell, the fan turned once, then stopped; water kept time. 
Nothing in the sequence was grand enough for music, yet each frame taught the bench which shadows belonged to the room and which should be divided away. 
Through accumulated dust patterns, she could subtract the greenhouse from the image and see the small motions the flood had left behind. 
By late afternoon, the new flats were clean enough to compare with the observatory flats that had worried her for months. 
A faint drift in those telescope maps had looked as if the decay of the star changed speed, or as if a crack in the detector had opened, depending on who wanted a better headline. 
When Mira ran the butterfly standard against the greenhouse map, the drift resolved into ordinary contamination, a brief breath of humidity on cold glass. 
The timing glitch remained, but after correction its decay was shallower, a gradual change in a crust no headline could inflate into catastrophe. 
She almost laughed at the pettiness of the correction: dust from butterfly wing had kept a neutron star honest. 
Instead she wrote the revised slope in her notebook, then crossed out the sentence promising that the flood had changed everything. 
The flood had only made visible what years had prepared, seam by seam, in the broken greenhouse and in her own appetite for crisis. 
That appetite had been with her when the press called, when the department used the word violent, and when she mistook the quiet greenhouse her mother had kept for a relic that needed rescue. 
At dusk she lit a small alcohol lamp under the broken roof and warmed a cup of water while the bucket kept its patient tick. 
The flame did not conquer the damp; it made a circle where a person could work without pretending the room was saved. 
What rose in her was a kindled humility, warm and practical, the knowledge that patient light had been entrusted to her for a while.
