In the winter after discovering aliens, the benthic city of Nacre stopped teaching north. Old charts named currents and shelves; the trench margin had become a black border. Beyond it, instruments answered pings in starlike pulses, and three crews vanished following them. Nacre buried empty suits beneath memorial glass; windows burned all night with cautious lamps. Mara Venn, navigation instructor at the Hall of Currents, had taught those crews to distrust unmeasured horizons. After the funerals, she made cadets practice silence before calculation, since grief was as real as depth. The council trusted her because she was balancedly curious, opening sealed files, keeping one hand on the deadbolt. She wanted, more than rank or absolution, to learn to navigate by starlight before another pilot crossed the border. In Nacre, starlight arrived not as brightness but as angles folded through water, basalt, and alien lenses. The alien transmission called the result unfolded horizons, a way of making sky and sea floor share one chart. Inside unfolded horizons, a pilot steered by a coast, a crater lip, and cold star positions at once. Mara reduced the impossible claim to edge geometry, because edges were the only honest things a benthic city possessed. She measured dome rims, vent fractures, hull scars, and anchor shackles in the harbor museum. From those boundaries she inferred hidden curves, then the places where starlight had been pressed into the sea. Her most private instrument was a boat anchor chain link salvaged from her grandmother's last surface boat. It was heavy, salt pitted, slightly bent, with an inner edge no machine had made true. Mara kept it beneath her lesson board as a witness to the surface boundary, not as a relic. The aliens had asked for such witnesses, which frightened the council more than any territorial demand. At dawn, by clocks kept for an unseen sun, a sealed order arrived from the Tide Gate. The beacon beneath the gate had opened a hairline aperture, and the council wanted Mara to expand it before the memorial tide. One courier boat would pass through, they said, and return with a route no sonar could invent. Mara read the order twice, remembering empty suits and the way the border widened after each disappearance. Her apprentice Ilo found her rubbing the chain link, as if polishing grief could make it useful. He had lost his sister on the second survey, and asked whether the aperture would show where she had gone. Mara promised no rescue, only a measurement, and then let him carry the link down the gantry stairs. The Tide Gate lay below the oldest dome, where basalt ribs met glass worn by anxious feet. Engineers waited in memorial coats; no one spoke above the pumps holding back the ocean. Mara placed the boat anchor chain link in the calibration cradle and aligned its worn edge with alien light. Using edge geometry, she compared that curve with the dome rim, the gate seam, and trench shadows in her lesson board. The first alignment failed, and every lamp dimmed with a patient, almost courteous sorrow. Ilo whispered his sister's name, then steadied the link with both hands instead of pulling it away. Mara saw that grief had made the truest edge in the room, and corrected the angle by one degree. The aperture expanded to the width of a held breath, and the glass floor filled with stars precise as old wounds. It was a somber marvel, an avenue of light where lost survey pings returned like bells heard under water. For a moment, Mara believed the dead waited at the far end, faithfully recorded. The council signal flashed for the courier to launch, but Mara held the aperture at that breath. She had not come to conquer distance; she had come to test a course and keep faith with the vanished. From the chain link's shadow and three stars on the glass, she took the first star fix any human had read from unfolded horizons. Ilo copied the angles, his sister's name held beside the numbers rather than inside them. Then Mara closed the gate to a thread, denying the council its road but giving Nacre a lesson. In the benthic city, cautious wonder became a bearing, and starlight stopped belonging only to the sky.
