People in the office call her a death certificate error survivor, because a clerk signed her death before the fever left her lungs.  The registry kept the certificate but lost the name, leaving a nameless identity that opened no door.  Because paper declared her dead, she filters reality differently: manifest omissions press like thumbs and silences have weight.  Her motive is not revenge but to discover the texture of memories.  Each morning she enters the towering labyrinth of shipping containers in a port city where cranes stitch rust colored light over the water.  Containers rise nine high, their numbers bleached by salt, and their narrow aisles change with every shift of wind and tide.  Across the rhythm of tides, open gates become walls, flooded gutters become mirrors, and the workers' footsteps move from the upper rows to the lower quay.  She learns the timetable of voices: crane men at flood tide, welders at slack water, widows at the ebb.  She works by collecting fragments of overheard stories, never asking questions, because a question makes the poor remember the office and go still.  The object that starts the search is a square of green paper, a peeled warning catalogued in old dock slang as label from poison bottle.  Its skull is mostly rubbed away, but beneath the stain she reads a lot number, a ship mark, and the word antidote.  On the certificate that buried her, the cause had been fever, while this scrap gives fever a number and a berth.  She keeps the label inside her coat, not as proof but as grit, because proof belongs to courts and she belongs to doorways.  No one in the port asks what she is called; clerks call her the dead one, watchmen call her the gray coat, and children call her the listener.  At low water she must layer each new scrap under thin paper, matching smears of grease to the painted numbers on a stolen container map.  A cleaner says his uncle cursed the sweet almond smell in Bay 3 before his hands turned blue.  A cook remembers carrying broth to men who could not swallow, and how steam hid the taste of metal in the galley.  Two widows argue over whether the drums were marked with a black diamond or a green skull, then fall silent at the same gull cry.  A night watchman hums a loading chant whose third line names the company inspectors who tore pages from the manifest.  She does not write conclusions; she writes salt, wool, orange peel, lamp oil, the scrape of a ring on a tin cup.  By the second tide, the lot number from the label appears in a muttered apology outside a pay window.  By the third tide, an old rigger recalls a girl carried from a fever shed still breathing, though a clerk had already inked her among the dead.  She understands then that her error was not a clerical joke but a seam left open when erased histories were stitched shut.  A shipment of poison had been unsealed during a strike, the sick had been counted as fever, and the dead had been made cheaper by losing their names.  The company needed every certificate to agree, so one breathing child became the only discrepancy in a ledger otherwise smooth as glass.  Because she had no recognized name, no inspector came back to correct the line, and the mistake survived with her.  On the fourth tide she finds the container whose number matches the ship mark, sealed behind newer rows and warm from the sun.  Its hinges are packed with white crystals, and when rain reaches them the whole aisle smells faintly of almonds.  She does not force the door; the tide has taught her that everything sealed eventually meets water.  Instead she carries her layered papers to the old steps at ebb and pins them under the iron rail where widows wait for the ferry.  Beside each fragment she places a scrap of plain tin, and on the central sheet she fixes the green label from the poison bottle.  The sheets contain no names, because the names were taken too cleanly, but they hold the pressure of hands, the taste of broth, the color of gloves, and the spaces where a chant breaks.  When the next flood lifts the ferries, crane men read the notes aloud in pieces, and each voice supplies the pause another voice had lost.  She keeps her nameless identity, but now it feels grained with salt and breath, the texture of memories no certificate can flatten.
