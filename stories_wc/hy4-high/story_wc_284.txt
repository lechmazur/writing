At the tilt of the earth toward spring, Mara climbed zephyr bluff in mist, where a silver hush made every footfall sound like a sealed envelope being opened.  She had been a burned spy in the capital, and the word burned counted twice: a fire had erased her fingerprints, and a traitor had erased her cover.  Now she mended kettles and clock springs in a border village, charging too little because fixing what's broken had become her only honest trade.  Ivo found her at dusk, bringing the engraved pillbox that had come home among his father's effects after the border network collapsed.  The lid was scratched with lilies, the clasp was bent, and under the velvet lining lay a strip of oiled paper no thicker than a thread.  Mara read the message twice, then held the strip above her lamp until heat raised ink that said, vouchsafe the dawn or lose thirty names.  It meant that watchers across the fir valley would open the safe houses at dawn only if a trusted signal reached them before the patrols did.  Only Mara could vouchsafe that signal, because the old watchers had been taught that her scarred left hand, missing its print, was a seal no forger could copy.  She could have sent Ivo, but the boy had his father's habit of hoping, and hope was a poor cloak on a watched road.  So Mara closed the shop, repaired the pillbox clasp with a pin, and told herself she was going to save the names, not to revisit the place where her nerve had died.  Yet as she climbed through wet grass and lingering drifts, she admitted the cleaner truth: she had come to find where unused courage waits.  That winter, radios had betrayed three couriers, and the first station had relied on codes in accumulated snow, pressed into a north ledge that held frost long after the valley greened.  Each notch meant a door, an hour, a direction, and at sunrise the thaw would blur the marks before any patrol could read them backward.  Tonight, below the ledge, a patrol lamp moved in a slow arc, and beside it stood the man who had sold her name.  She knew him by the way he kept his gloved hands apart, as if even he objected to their touching.  Prudently daring, she counted the lamp's turns, waited for the west wind to thicken the mist, and chose the exposed minute when the guards would be looking inward.  Then she stepped onto the ledge with the engraved pillbox open, using its repaired lid to cut clean angles into the packed drift.  Her scarred left hand shook, but she gave it work: three notches for the mill door, two for the ferry, a long slash for the chapel cellar.  Between the groups she pressed the lid flat with her scarred palm, leaving lilies and a printless seal, the old proof that the hand and the warning belonged together.  She was halfway through the final name when the traitor called from below, using her real name in a voice polished by old dinners.  Mara did not answer, because an answer would trade thirty lives for the small luxury of being recognized.  Instead, she set the engraved pillbox in a cleft where the watchers would find it, a token to vouchsafe the signal when morning melted the code.  The traitor started upward, and the patrol lamp swung toward her; still, she stayed until the last notch was deep enough to cast a blue shadow.  Only then did she retreat across the crumbling cornice, where prudently daring meant trusting a rope she had tested twice.  At dawn, from her attic window, Mara saw thirty thin plumes rise from safe houses where families were boiling water for people who should have been arrested.  The signal snow was already collapsing into bright water, taking the code with it and leaving only a braid of lilies above the valley.  Ivo came with news that the traitor had been arrested by his own patrol after a false alarm; they found no paper, no radio, and no woman worth charging.  He asked whether she felt brave, and Mara thought of the ledge, the rope, and how fear had not left her; it had simply been given a task.  She told him that courage had been waiting in the work, not in the wound, and that fixing what's broken had finally included the broken messenger.  Outside, the mist thinned over zephyr bluff, and the silver hush gave way to the first ordinary birdsong of the turning year.
