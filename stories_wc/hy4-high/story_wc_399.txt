After the prophet arrives, the murmuring pavilion stops repeating travelers and begins repeating one sentence.  No one agrees on the words, because the woven walls soften consonants until every faction hears its own claim.  A whisperfield calm settles over the camp, not silence but a field in which every hush has weight.  Mara, the half-masked line walker, waits at the northern rail with her uncovered eye lowered.  Her black lens hides the eye that once looked directly at the kinetic parapet beneath the dunes and came back blind to edges.  Since then she has been strangely honest, admitting what she cannot face before it becomes a flattering legend.  The law leaves a pause after the prophet arrives, yet the interpreters who exiled her for honesty are already carrying water stones toward the awning.  Mara wants to preserve the echo of wisdom, not the noise that people make when they possess it.  In her order, an echo survives only inside conjured clearings, open sands held apart from the wind by a watched absence.  To make such a place, a line walker must feel the moving wall underfoot through peripheral awareness and never stare at the center.  The old teachers say the center is greedy; whatever enters it is named, priced, and finally emptied.  Only the rim, glimpsed sideways, keeps a thing itself.  The prophet sits beneath a blue awning, thin from the crossing, while scribes crowd close with ink combs and eager thumbs.  He has said only that a river should not be stored in a cup, yet by dusk two camps have sworn the river means them.  Mara hears their certainty in the pavilion's murmur, where each reed drops the doubt.  She tells the elders plainly that a carved sentence would please the living and kill the echo.  Because she is strangely honest, no one can call the warning factional, though several try.  An old keeper finally asks what she intends, and she answers that before moonrise she will consecrate a clearing.  The word passes through the pavilion as softly as spilled flour, but it changes every face.  To consecrate ground here is to refuse ownership of it, and the interpreters understand refusal as theft.  They order the rails locked, forgetting that rails are only eyelashes of the deeper machine.  Under every dune, the kinetic parapet shifts, joint by joint, carrying boundaries from forgotten sieges.  Mara steps onto the northern line, places her staff in its notch, and lets the camp recede to the corners of her sight.  She does not look at the prophet; she notices him as warmth at the edge, a small sun beside the scribes' chill.  She notices locked rails, the interpreter's ink, and a child who has not chosen a side.  Through peripheral awareness, these fragments become a single circumference, and the buried wall answers by easing left.  Sand opens ahead of her, not swept away but persuaded, forming a round clearing where no wind dares cross.  Inside it, the prophet's sentence loses its factions and remains a breath shaped like running water.  The elders fall quiet, because the whisperfield calm now holds them.  Mara could stop with a private miracle, but honesty forbids a refuge meant for one order.  She walks the circumference, striking her staff until the clearing widens past the pavilion and includes the rival fires.  The interpreters object that she is stealing the revelation, and for once their accusation is nearly true.  She is taking it from no one and placing it where no one can own the echo.  At the final notch, Mara kneels, presses her masked cheek to the rail, and asks the people to speak only what they heard after they stopped arguing.  One by one, they do: a cup, a river, a warning, a cup, until differences lie inside without becoming weapons.  That consent is the consecration; the kinetic parapet settles, the dunes close over its labor, and the open sand remains unclaimed.  By morning the prophet has gone, leaving no cup, no tablet, and no committee authorized to explain him.  Travelers still enter the murmuring pavilion expecting an answer, and the reeds give back softened voices.  Mara keeps the northern line, strangely honest about the limits of her seeing.  When children ask what wisdom sounds like, she tells them to look elsewhere, and in that sideways hush they hear the river continue.
