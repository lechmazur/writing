After the shutdown, the time dilation observation deck was locked behind a blue hatch, and its long windows kept a pale, echoed silence that made every footstep return like a remembered order.  No one entered except Ila Marr, the blessed pariah of Meridian Station, who came each year with a charcoal pencil, a folded skybook, and permission no councilor wanted to sign.  They had named her blessed because, at thirteen, she had carried the evacuation ledger out of the failing lens; they called her pariah because clocks stuttered whenever she passed.  The last pulse had left her temporally stretched, so a minute often arrived in her as a wide landscape, with hours at the edges and a thin path of now underfoot.  That damage made her useful in the dead deck, where old seconds still slid across the glass, but it also made her a reminder of the day the station stopped agreeing with itself.  Afterward, calendars split by corridor, children celebrated birthdays twice, and compasses spun toward whatever grief had been strongest in the room.  Ila did not come to mourn that confusion, although grief had taught her the corridors; she came to turn confusion into exploration.  Her method was a family custom of sketching the night sky on anniversaries, begun by surveyors who believed that patience could reveal what instruments were too proud to admit.  Every wedding, death, and landing day, they had drawn stars on translucent leaves, then pinned the leaves together with a gilded compass rose applique whose longest ray marked true north.  In the old stories, true north was not merely a heading but the promise that separate observations could share one honest center.  Ila's mother had made her repeat the lesson while they traced winter constellations above the family table, and the gilded compass rose applique had flashed between them like a small, stubborn sun.  When the lens was shut down, her mother stayed inside to collapse the field from within, and the council sealed the deck before Ila could understand why the skybook felt heavier than a body.  On the twentieth anniversary, Ila unsealed the hatch and stepped into air that smelled of cold metal, dust, and the faint ozone of interrupted lightning.  The deck's circular floor held thirty empty chairs, each turned toward the black bowl of space where the gravity well had once been made visible as a silver rim.  The echoed silence was not empty; it was layered, returning the small click of her boot, her mother's imagined breath, and a softer click that had not happened yet.  Because she was stretched, Ila could wait inside those returns long enough to glimpse the deck as it had been during the last public observation.  Families appeared as pale smears, pointing at delayed starlight; her mother stood near the window, younger than memory and holding the same applique.  Ila did not reach for her, because the custom had always required first the sky, then the center, then the interpretation.  She set fresh paper on the glass, pinned the gilded compass rose applique at the corner she had used since childhood, and began to sketch what the deck showed rather than what the almanacs promised.  There were seven skies in the pane, overlapping like transparent hands, and each carried a slightly different version of the station's last second.  Her first impulse was to call them errors, but her stretched mind had learned that fear often named unknown territory too quickly.  Instead, she treated each delay as an island, measured the space between stars, and compared the newest leaf with anniversary drawings made before the shutdown.  The old pages, softened by many hands, had been drawn for births and reconciliations, but together they formed an instrument more delicate than any clock the engineers had trusted.  When she laid the applique over the newest drawing, its longest gilded ray slid across three versions of the same dark star and stopped where every sky agreed.  That point was the true north of the rupture, a fixed relation hidden inside apparently broken time.  Ila understood, with a calm that felt almost borrowed, that the shutdown had not erased direction; it had folded direction into layers no ordinary instrument could hold.  Following that center, she drew a narrow path through the bowed star fields to the maintenance spine, where a sealed service wheel could still realign the station's spin.  The route was not a repair in the old sense; it was a map of when to turn each bolt, so that hand, metal, and borrowed seconds met without tearing.  She opened the hatch to the waiting crew and gave them the map.  For the first time, they followed her without flinching from the clocks.  Together they sketched a new anniversary sky, and confusion became a horizon.
