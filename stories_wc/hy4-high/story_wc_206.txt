Ivo Venn was a music box repairman who listened to hinges before oiling them.  In Larkspur, kettles kept time, so six sunless mornings had shamed every kitchen into silence.  The old calendars said that thirteen minutes before dawn hung in the balance, and night and morning weighed the same.  At the first bell, stolen glow would harden into midnight, beyond mending.  Ivo descended into the resonance chamber beneath the clocktower to reclaim the light stolen by midnight.  He told the waiting councillors he had come for the city, which was true but incomplete.  His daughter Nell slept above his shop by a covered lamp, asking whether the sun had forgotten their street.  The chamber answered his steps with tinkersong wonder, all glass reeds and brass rain.  Pinned cylinders and combs circled a floor veined with honest brown rust.  At the center, the Grand Aurora Box usually coaxed dawn over the roofs.  Its gilded case now shone so brightly that Ivo saw his worry in every panel.  Chancellor Orrel claimed that a mainspring had torn and must be replaced before sunrise.  Ivo unwrapped a chip from philosopher's stone, no larger than a barley grain.  A pale moonflower soldered to it was his master's truth gauge; it wilts near dishonesty.  At Orrel's claim, the moonflower bowed toward the floor.  He said that nothing was torn, and the flower lifted.  The oldest law of his trade was a covenant of rust, trusting metal to remember time.  One red grain told dawn where to return; polished away, light forgot its address.  Orrel's face tightened, and Ivo heard the rest.  The council had gilded the cylinder to sell endless daylight, giving midnight a mirror and a door.  The chamber amplified their vanity until the light slipped its moorings.  Yet the moonflower trembled at Ivo's own unsaid fear.  He had polished his grief, pretending Nell's questions did not frighten him.  He confessed that he wanted one small room lit again.  The flower opened fully, and every reed gave a forgiving ping.  Only then did Ivo use the method called mending what isn't torn.  It fitted a note between two whole parts that had forgotten they belonged together.  Such a bridge could carry a promise across an unbroken surface.  In the balance, it reached backward into the hour before the theft.  Ivo demanded the cylinder scrapings, and Orrel produced a velvet pouch.  The scrapings were rust hidden like evidence.  He touched the chip from philosopher's stone to the grains, persuading gold back toward iron.  Red light crawled across the panels, and the box looked old enough to trust.  From the rust Ivo drew one fine wire and listened for dawn's pitch.  Every music box in Larkspur answered with the small sound of households waking.  He needed one true note to tether the stolen light before the scales tipped.  Nell's lamp had a tin shade that rang a clear C when her mother sang.  He had kept it silent because silence seemed easier than hope.  Now he sent that note through the reeds and into the waiting wire.  The chamber filled with tinkersong wonder, sparrows and first breaths in bright machinery.  Midnight answered from behind the panels, imitating Nell's sleeping breath.  The moonflower stiffened, not wilted, and Ivo knew the envy was honest.  He tuned around the chord, mending what isn't torn by giving darkness harmony without melody.  Then he began to tether the wire to the cylinder with a simple knot.  The chip grew warm, the rust flared, and a thread of sunrise ran into the knot.  Orrel reached for it, but a covenant of rust held him to the truth he had buried.  At the first bell, Ivo tightened the invisible seam, and every clock exhaled.  Gold faded, brown returned, and dawn unfolded over Larkspur.  Above the shop, the tin shade rang, and Nell's footsteps crossed the ceiling.  Ivo held the tether until the last vibration, then climbed from the resonance chamber.  Orrel offered clean gold, but Ivo asked only for the pouch of scrapings.  He sprinkled one red grain into the box, honoring a covenant of rust like a scar.  Later, while Nell ate breakfast in sunlight, she asked whether he had repaired the spring.  He said no; he had only mended what isn't torn.
