Mira was a creeping normality noticer for the Ministry of Continuity, trained to mark bells gone quiet and queues grown shorter without cause.  Everyone else accepted a smaller loaf, a quieter dawn, a blank wall, because each change arrived politely.  Her mother had taught her to find the sacred in the ordinary, so Mira saved a heel of bread from the vanished bakery in a tin.  She bought that heel on the last morning of Rovel's shop, then filed the disappearance as unconfirmed because the ledger allowed tenuous absolution to noticers without witnesses.  The stamp forgave her delay, but only while the absence stayed unremembered, and Mira felt it thinning beneath the tin lid.  That winter, a card beneath the tin lid said to come after the birds fly south, when no beaks steal seed balls and no courier birds cross the silent courtyard.  Mira followed the scent of warm yeast across the weed choked moat.  Beyond it stood a secret tea house in an overgrown palace courtyard, hidden under ivy that guards called architecture.  Mira expected a password, perhaps a confession, but living roots cloaked the door and would yield only to someone willing to uncloak it through guerrilla gardening.  A woman kneeling among cracked tiles looked up, wiped soil from her thumb, and told her she was late by one bakery.  Mira knew the voice from delivery mornings and understood this was Rovel's daughter Sella, older by slow arithmetic the Ministry never taxed.  Sella was heuristically gentle, making rules from what her hands learned, never forcing a seedling to obey a diagram.  She did not reproach Mira; she asked for the tin, warmed water in a dented kettle, and crumbled stale bread into it.  Yeast woke slowly, not miraculously, and the smell of Rovel's ovens rose through tea steam as if an ordinary morning refused to die.  Sella poured two cups and called the tea tenuous absolution, not your ledger's kind, because it lasts only while we feed what we failed to keep.  Mira wanted to say she had only observed, but that word had sheltered her longer than any lie.  Instead she admitted signing the unconfirmed report to keep her post, and every promotion afterward tasted like unsalted bread.  Sella nodded, because the tea house held confessions too small for courts and too heavy for kitchens.  Around them, gardeners in patched coats packed seed balls from clay, crumbs, mint, rye, and damp yeast paste, counting them like contraband pearls.  The palace had been erased one balcony at a time, and these night plantings were its only surviving records.  Each seed ball, Sella explained, would be pressed into cracks where shops, stairs, or songs had been normalized out of memory.  By spring the green marks would make questions visible; by summer roots would uncloak the courtyard door for anyone carrying grief.  Mira understood then that absolution was a practice, not a pardon, a thread tied each morning by hands that noticed.  She removed her Ministry badge, not dramatically, and used its pin to loosen soil along the tea house threshold.  Sella watched it vanish under mulch and said that the first rule of guerrilla gardening was to leave authority where it could compost.  They set out after moonset, heuristically gentle, testing walls with fingertips and changing course when brick dust argued.  At the blank wall that replaced Rovel's bakery, Mira pressed the last seed ball into mortar and whispered the price of bread.  A watchman passed, saw kneeling, and went on, because kneeling in a city of errands looked like prayer or fatigue.  By dawn, green offenses dotted the Ministry steps, the blank bakery wall, the silent aviary, and the dry fountain where children once christened marbles.  No proclamation followed, no bakery returned, and no stolen dawn was restored; the city merely stepped around proof.  Clerks complained about weeds, mothers paused over mint, and an old man asked why rye grew where he once bought loaves.  In that small disturbance she helped uncloak the lost bakery, not as a building returned, but as a question rooted in public ground.  Mira stood nearby with dirt under her nails, feeling the stamped forgiveness in her file turn frail again.  Yet Sella brought tea in a chipped cup at noon, and ordinary warmth seemed briefly sacred enough to hold her.
