Only after the anesthetic kicks in does the mist-clad atrium at twilight stop pretending to be a courthouse. 
The bombed hotel's glass roof holds sea fog, sterilizer steam, and two armed delegations who have come to decide whether a cease-fire survives one boy's wave. 
On a table beneath the broken fountain, Tavi sleeps while surgeons prepare to split the plaster on his forearm. 
The arm broke at the checkpoint where a water convoy was fired on, and both sides now claim the child signaled for guns. 
If the claim stands, the harbor flotilla will shell the ridge before dawn. 
Mara Ione, a pacific conflict-zone translator from the Pacific Islands, keeps her voice shallower than mortar fire. 
Years of truce talks have taught her that enemies usually hide shared anxieties inside different uniforms and different grammars. 
Shrewdly innocent, she asks questions that sound like nursery lessons and make lieutenants correct her with the truth. 
She has not come to certify innocence; she has come to graft new meaning onto old wounds before children inherit the names of borders. 
That is why she asks the nurse for Tavi's cast before the plaster saw touches it. 
It is crowded with jokes, dosage marks, prayers, and haze from hands trying to comfort him without waking fear. 
Tucked in the sling is a brain coral fragment, pale reef blasted from the harbor wall and rubbed smooth by his good hand. 
During shelling drills, Tavi pressed its wandering ridges into the damp plaster, leaving labyrinth lines through the notes. 
Mara has until the first strip falls to unknot the accusation by decrypting smudged notes on a child's cast. 
The nurses call the hush a lotus-hearted ache, because it rises from mud without pretending the mud is clean. 
Holding the coral, Mara matches its grooves to the loops under iodine, charcoal, and sweat. 
Atoll children played a reef game in which one line followed every furrow and returned to the place fear began. 
Tavi made that line a private alphabet, and adults had written over it like a map they were ashamed to read. 
Mara asks the ridge captain, as if puzzled by a game, what a child calls a white flag when white paint is gone. 
The captain says saltcloth, the strip used to cover water tins, and his correction reveals two letters beneath a brown smear. 
She asks the harbor lieutenant whether sailors fear poisoned wells; he says every mother on the coast fears that. 
Those answers give her the missing sense: the note over Tavi's name is a plea to let water through before dark. 
A frightened clerk had copied the gel-blurred word through as shot, and both commands had built revenge on that small error. 
Following the coral line, she reads the cast in order: a sister's mine warning, a gunner's thirst, a medic's saline, and Tavi's plea not to be used as a reason. 
The sentences are awkward, but their shame is identical, and no one reaches for a radio while she speaks. 
Shared anxieties sit at last in one language, not forgiven, only recognized, and recognition makes the room gentler than agreement could. 
The captain admits his spotter fired at saltcloth; the lieutenant admits the convoy hid rifle rounds beneath pump sacks. 
Both truths hurt, yet neither requires another mother to wait beside a covered well. 
Mara calls them to the operating table and lays Tavi's sleeping hand across their ledgers. 
She translates the boy's final line twice, once in each official tongue, so the wound cannot be owned by either side. 
Then the surgeons cut the cast away, clean the fracture, and set the arm in fresh plaster while twilight thickens around the ferns. 
Mara keeps the brain coral fragment, not as evidence, but as a small pale graft for the treaty table. 
The delegates add a water corridor to the cease-fire and agree that white cloth means thirst, not surrender. 
Before Tavi wakes, the atrium is darker, the lotus-hearted ache still open, and his new plaster waits for cleaner sentences.
