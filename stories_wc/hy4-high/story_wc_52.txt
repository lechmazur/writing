In the rain-beaded skylight loft, Mara set the ash wood carving blank beside the copper kettle before its first shiver of steam. 
She was a lion tamer's widow, and she had only the time while the kettle boils, from first murmur to whistle, to finish one carving. 
The rain thinned the October light to rust-hued peace over brick dust, old chains, and the closed cage door below. 
She had come to harvest lessons from loss, not to ask the dead man for one more evening. 
Emil's ledger lay open beside her elbow, hauntingly practical in its columns of hinge oil, paw span, appetite, weather, and the names of apprentices who had forgotten to look. 
At the bottom of the last page, in his square hand, he had written five words: Never trust a quiet latch. 
He had died proving it, stepping between a restless lion and a gate whose new catch had smiled shut but had not locked. 
The crowd remembered the roar; Mara remembered the small click missing behind it. 
He used to say the roar, the applause, the lifted chair, and even a life were here and gone, so attention was the only mercy a trainer could leave behind. 
A bent chair, a cracked whip handle, Emil's latch key, and the ash blank had each kept the last afternoon in their seams. 
According to the widows before her, warmth and cold could draw an hour out of such things as crystallized time. 
The copper kettle breathed toward the skylight, rain chilled the glass, and the minutes began to show as pale grit along the iron sash. 
Only through crystallized time could a carver contain an event without preserving the suffering that grief kept calling love. 
Mara meant to contain the last afternoon in the blank, then carry the carving down to the apprentices before dawn. 
If she chose badly, the wood would hold a handsome dead face and teach them nothing about the gate. 
The kettle ticked, gathering itself; the whistle would scatter the grit and leave only steam. 
She held Emil's latch key over the blank and waited for the grain to answer. 
One crystal formed where the key had worn thin, no larger than a grain of salt, and in it she saw not blood but the gate's false shine. 
Another crystal, softer and gold, offered Emil laughing in his shirt sleeves as he oiled the hinge the morning before. 
Her knife hovered, because a widow can mistake tenderness for instruction when both arrive wearing the same face. 
The ledger's columns steadied her: the lion's weight, the faulty catch, the apprentice sent for oil, the minute nobody checked the second pin. 
With the kettle's mutter rising, she cut a shallow rectangle into the ash wood carving blank, then pared a tiny hook that would never quite close. 
She shaved a notch beside the hook, a second pin's absence made visible enough to catch a thumb. 
The gold crystal trembled on the skylight, here and gone in the running beads, and she let it go. 
She set only the salt-bright grain into the cut, pressing it with the flat of her knife until the wood drank the cold flash of the unlatched gate. 
The blank warmed, and the carving contained no roaring, no last words, no portrait, only the lesson of a latch that had seemed quiet. 
Mara sanded the edges with a scrap of his old glove, because her hands shook and the work did not. 
The kettle's lid began to knock; there was just enough boil left for one test. 
She passed her thumb over the carved notch, and the pause in the hook stopped her as surely as a hand. 
That was the verdict Emil would have wanted: not memory applauded, but attention rehearsed in the body. 
The whistle came, thin and ordinary, and the remaining crystals dissolved into clear drops on the rain-beaded glass. 
Mara lifted the kettle from the flame, made no tea, and wrapped the carved latch in her apron for the apprentices. 
Below, the empty cages held the color of wet iron, and the loft's rust-hued peace settled around her without asking her to stay. 
Emil was here and gone; the caution he had paid for would go down the stairs in her pocket, useful and warm.
