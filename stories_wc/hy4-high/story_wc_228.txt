After the mirror cracks, Elias Venn leaves his quay studio without sweeping up the silver needles.  For forty years, the nostalgic quay painter has painted vanished berths and refused the harbor as it is.  He is intimately aloof, knowing which widow sugared her tea yet never accepting her chair.  From a paint stained drawer he takes a seashell, painted decades ago with herring, two candles, and his mother's unlined hands.  Beneath those supper colors lie other harbors, other storms, and one first picture he covered when grief made him ashamed of wonder.  On the back of an old canvas he once wrote, after the mirror cracks, stop painting what is gone and reveal what was buried.  It was less a prophecy than an instruction to keep a hidden promise to oneself, made by a boy who feared courage needed an appointment.  He has postponed it by forgetting it so thoroughly that only a broken reflection can accuse him.  Now the split in the glass divides his white beard from the startled boy he still sees behind it.  At low tide he walks beyond the last ferry shed, where the cliff opens into a coastal cave network chalked with old smugglers' marks.  The tide breathes out of the passages, and his lantern throws a small, obedient moon over wet limestone.  At the third fork, where the safe gallery bends left, he hears water falling behind a curtain of weed and follows a wrong turn.  That mistake, repeated from childhood, leads through a throat of black stone to the hidden oasis under a pinhole of sky.  Fresh water gathers there in a bowl the sea cannot claim, ringed by pale ferns and little shelves of salt.  Once the light strikes the pool, the ceiling vanishes and the whole cave holds an upside down afternoon.  At twelve, stranded and thirsty, Elias had called this sight a temporary eternity and meant it as praise.  He drank, filled his pockets with shells, and chose one whose ridges fit his palm like a held breath.  With colors stolen from his mother's workbox, he painted the pool, the ferns, and his own face leaning over them.  Before leaving, he hid the damp shell inside his shirt and swore that someday he would bring it back clean of all later disguises.  Courage had arrived without trumpets, only with a cracked mirror and sore knees.  Now he kneels at the pool and sets the seashell, painted and repainted into a small history of avoidance, on a flat stone.  He does not pray, because prayer would make the place too large and the promise too public.  Instead he wets his thumb and rubs away the herring supper, then the candle flames, then his mother's hands.  Layer by layer, the quay he invented to replace the cave gives up its blue weather.  When the last storm thins, he reveals the first picture: a child, a pool, and an impossible sun caught in fresh water.  The child's painted eyes are not brave; they are only certain that someone will return.  Elias laughs once, and the cave returns the sound more gently than any neighbor ever could.  He understands then that his nostalgia was never love of the past, but love with the door held nearly shut.  The hidden oasis has kept its hour for him, yet its water has changed with every tide and will change again.  That is the only eternity the place ever offered: exact, perishable, and renewed by being entered.  He dips the shell once, letting the loosened colors drift like smoke, and does not try to rescue them.  The first picture remains long enough to be seen, then softens into the pool that taught it brightness.  He lifts the shell, empty as a spoken word, because an object should not carry what a life can hold.  The return through the coastal cave network is easier, though he takes no pride in knowing the correct turns now.  At the quay, dusk is repairing the ferry shed with ordinary orange light.  Marta, who sells bait and has watched him pass for twenty years, asks whether he found what he lost.  He considers saying nothing, the old kindness of his distance, and instead reveals the bare shell in his palm.  "I found the hour," he says, and because she waits, he adds that he has come back willing to be seen.  They watch harbor light flatten, briefly endless and already leaving.
