Sir Everan had been called a withdrawn knight so often that the title fit him more closely than mail.  He kept silence as other men kept watch, and in it he had buried the loud oath that sent him into the Chancellor's war of settled endings.  In the alchemical orchard greenhouse shrouded in endless night, he pruned trees whose fruit held liquids, memories, and unborn weather.  The place was factually mystical, not merely lovely: copper sap obeyed equations, prayers, and pruning knives with equal patience.  Its glass roof held no sunrise, only pale condensations from experiments that had forgotten which century they served.  Everan had learned to work as reality shifts, in the brief hour when each fruit rehearsed the life it would not keep.  He had come to capture the shape of possibility before the Chancellor's final grafting turned every branching future into one obedient crop.  The Chancellor believed a kingdom should contain no maybe, and Everan had seen villages burn because a king called certainty peace.  So, refusing banners, he tended roots instead of armies and spoke only to count grafts.  He found the abandoned marionette theater program folded beneath a graft bench, its cover soft with mineral dew.  At first he nearly left it, because plays required audiences, and audiences required answers he had sworn never to give.  But a corner showed a puppet knight with a hinged jaw, and beneath it someone had written, No spoken oath, only a murmured allegiance.  The words suited him so precisely that his fingers trembled, not with fear but with recognition.  The program listed wooden kings, glass children, and The Unfinished Heir, but the creases mattered more.  He solved it through forgotten patterns, reading pinholes, fold lines, and old prune scars as if they were steps of a dance.  Those marks matched the trellises, silver strings above the central pear tree, and grooves in his dented breastplate.  The program had not been discarded by accident; it was a score for teaching the orchard to perform one future forever.  It was abandoned when the last rehearsal produced a marionette with no fixed face, which the Chancellor called an error.  To Everan, the error was the only harvest worth saving.  He laid the program on a fogged pane and saw pinholes gather lamp light into the outline of a small, uncarved body.  It had no crown or future, only joints waiting for motion.  He understood that his task was not to choose a child for the world, but to keep the world capable of children.  As the hour narrowed, fruit swapped flavors, and the path under his boots forgot whether it led inward or outward.  Everan followed the dance from the program, pausing at each trellis to turn a leaf by the width of one breath.  The glass roof sang, a thin note that made his scars ache with shivering delight.  At the central pear tree, a silver clasp had grown around the trunk like a collar, awaiting the matching clasp beneath his gorget.  On the last page, a blotted direction read: when strings tighten, the knight must unsnap the clasp and hang it on the tree.  The Chancellor's voice came from the speaking tube, promising a world where no child would surprise him.  Everan did not answer, because loud voices had always asked him to make the future smaller.  Instead, he gave a murmured allegiance to the unfixed, to the uncarved body, to every path that might yet refuse a map.  The orchard heard him.  Strings shivered above the pears, the fogged outline bowed without a puppeteer, and all the possible fruits leaned close.  At the last chime, Everan made himself unsnap the clasp at his throat and hang it on the collar.  The sound was tiny, yet every pane answered with a bright crack that did not break.  The strings fell slack, the collar opened, and pinholes drew the outline on his palm in points of light.  What rose through him was shivering bliss, cold as dew and tender as a hand released from armor.  Reality shifted again, but nothing closed; each fruit held a different morning, and the endless night seemed full of gentle doors.
