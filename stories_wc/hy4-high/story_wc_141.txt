The last professional myth buster on that coast climbed to the abandoned observatory on a cliff with a lantern, a screwdriver, and a seashell, painted with blue constellations from her brother's ceiling. 
Her brother Tomas had died insisting the place listened, through its cracked dome, for the question from which stars were made. 
Mara had made skepticism into a gloomy cheerfulness, laughing at leaks, ghosts, and the stubborn grief that kept her company. 
She had come to decode a universal riddle, prove Tomas wrong, and go home before sentiment could ambush her. 
His final letter claimed the observatory woke for one lifetime inside the whisper of closing pages, when the catalog of every sky shut between dusk and dark. 
Hidden there was an ephemeral compass, a needle that pointed not north but toward whatever was about to vanish. 
The letter said the ephemeral compass reimagines past conversations, since every vanishing thing drags an unfinished sentence behind it. 
Only if a living witness would retell such an exchange through resonant hums in a half-finished cello could the needle hold still. 
Mara had underlined the instruction three times, annoyed that Tomas could still give homework from the grave. 

At the summit, gulls cried like wet hinges above a floor silvered with salt, guano, and old lens dust. 
The half-finished cello waited beneath the telescope, ribs clamped, belly unvarnished, two strings sagging from untuned pegs. 
She checked for wires and mirrors, smiled at finding none, and wished a cheerful debunking could warm the room. 
Then the ledgers lifted once, as if a polite assembly were rising, and the pages began to close. 
Mara set the painted seashell against the soundboard, and its hollow lip caught the draft with a small, embarrassed note. 
Blue flakes on the shell matched the star map around the dome, a neatness that offended her professional instincts. 
On the dome, in seven extinct hands, the universal riddle stayed chalk-bright: WHAT ANSWERS THE SKY WHEN THE SKY FORGETS ITS NAME. 
She copied it, added PROBABLE HOAX, and felt the whisper widen into a whole evening of rain, regret, and work. 

She drew a bow across the slack strings, and through resonant hums in a half-finished cello she began to retell their last kitchen argument. 
Kettle screaming, she had accused Tomas of inventing beautiful lies because real measurements frightened him. 
He had said fear was also a measurement; she had said grief was not evidence; pride had done the rest. 
The first hum returned her exact words, sharp as tacks, and no compass appeared. 
The second hum warmed them, and she heard what Tomas might have meant if she had not been busy winning. 
Fear, the cello suggested, was a needle trembling before it chose a vanishing thing; grief was the hand that kept time. 
Mara almost laughed, because the explanation was outrageous and useful, the two qualities she had loved most in him. 
She stopped repeating the quarrel and let the hums retell it as a conversation between two frightened people who had hated leaving. 
The painted stars shivered, dust arranged itself into a circle, and a needle of gray light rose from its center. 
It pointed to the lantern, then the rain at the broken window, then the dimming sentence in her own mouth. 
Mara understood that the ephemeral compass was not finding a place but a duty. 
It pointed toward the next thing that would vanish if no one answered: her chance to say she was sorry. 
She bowed again, hummed an apology, and smiled with gloomy cheerfulness as a leak chuckled overhead. 
The answer arrived not as a sentence but as a tilt of the needle toward every closing page in the room. 
A sky forgets its name whenever no living voice gives it back; a universe keeps its bearings by being retold toward what is disappearing. 
That was the riddle's answer, and it ruined her report in the most agreeable way. 
She could still bust the frauds with hidden wires, but she could no longer call every beautiful impossibility false. 
When the final pages hovered, the cello's hums thinned into ordinary surf, and the compass spent itself on an apology. 
Mara packed the seashell, painted and missing a few stars, and left the screwdriver as a courteous tip for the dead. 
Before the whisper thinned, she was already planning another climb, because a universal riddle decoded once grows fainter, and someone had to keep answering it.
