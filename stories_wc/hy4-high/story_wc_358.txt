Only after the town goes silent does accident investigator Mara Venn enter the towering labyrinth of shipping containers in a port city, carrying a lamp, a tide table, and an unsigned report.  Cranes hold still above her like folded insects, and salt haze softens every edge while her numbered flags and pencil marks give the wreck a hazy precision.  Nine hours earlier, a stack in Bay Nineteen had walked off its locks, crushing crane operator Jun Reyes and silencing the harbor.  The official account called it fatigue, because fatigue was tidy and could be entered in a box.  Mara had come to cultivate foresight in action instead of decorating a blame ledger, since her last investigation explained a ferry fire after the widows stopped asking.  She is rapidly adapting because she abandons a theory before pride cuffs it, the dock pilots say.  That habit makes her listen to hinges, diesel vapors, and the small suck of air around cold metal.  She begins her survey at the smashed corner, where a damp flight engineer's patch hangs from a twisted latch by three silver threads.  Jun had worn it on his work jacket after years servicing cargo airships, and the other stevedores teased him for trusting cloth wings in a place without sky.  Mara does not pocket it yet; she photographs the threads, because they lean northeast although the storm report claimed southwest wind at the moment of collapse.  A patched sleeve could be torn by falling steel, but fibers combed the same way are witnesses, and witnesses deserve a careful chair.  She sets her first ribbon on a lamp pole, then opens the case that she uses for documenting wind patterns between buildings and container rows.  Here the buildings are customs sheds, refrigerator blocks, and stacked containers tall enough to make streets with roofs of rust.  Between them, wind behaves like water that has learned to hurry, and the ribbons twitch before her ears hear anything.  She walks each lane with a notebook, a vane, and a tide watch, calling compass points to an apprentice named Ilias.  He had helped Jun and has not spoken since the cranes stopped, but he holds the measuring pole level as if level ground could keep the dead steady.  Together they mark where a refrigerator exhaust pushes warm air upward, where an open door makes a throat, and where a row of empty containers draws a hard sideways breath.  Mara sketches the readings in layers, using blue pencil for gusts, black for obstructions, and red for human choices.  At the third red mark she pauses, because the red line means Jun left his cab with thirty seconds left before the stack shifted.  The company had omitted that detail, since a dead operator moving toward danger complicates the cleaner story of metal giving way.  Mara returns to the patch, and Ilias finally says that Jun went to close the gap.  The gap was a narrow slot between two container walls that stevedores used as a shortcut, though a rising easterly could turn it into a funnel.  Jun had spent his airship years learning how small openings could steer enormous forces, and he had tried to swing a door shut before the next squall arrived.  The latch took his sleeve instead of his hand, which is why the patch waited there, damp and precise, to accuse the official weather chart.  Mara fills the last page with a singular convergence in which ordinary causes meet and become one force with a new appetite.  A warm exhaust plume, a cold tide, two cranes pausing as if in courtesy, an open door, and a man hurrying to help had converged at Bay Nineteen.  No one cause was large enough to condemn, but together they lifted the corner casting, twisted the locks, and walked the stack into the path where Jun stood.  Instead of signing fatigue, she calls the night foreman and asks for three crews, two lanterns, and permission to move one empty container before the tide turns.  He argues that the harbor is closed, and she answers with the ribbon readings, the leaning threads, and the hour when the same easterly will return.  They shift the container six feet, seal the shortcut with a bolted panel, and angle the refrigerator vents away from the throat it made.  At dawn, the lanes still knuckle together in the fog, but the ribbons hang slack where Mara expected the fatal funnel to form again.
