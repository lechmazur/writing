At Saint Oriel's, a radiant haven above the clouds, the ward kept a pulsing stillness that made each monitor blink sound courteous.  Mara Vale was a serene hospice music therapist, but serenity in her work meant attention, not drifting.  She was decisively kind, the kind of person who carried extra forms because grief should never have to search for a pen.  Doctor Iqbal had said, "I can authorize the sky lift only after the blood work comes back."  At noon the results arrived, and the numbers gave Jonah Pell not a discharge but one emergency visitor and perhaps six clear hours.  His pain was controlled, his eyes were open, and his hand rested on a piece of a broken vinyl record.  The black shard held three seconds of his wife Annette humming in the kitchen, captured before cancer, debt, and one stupid argument divided the house.  When the record snapped, his daughter Iris took the larger half and left, while Jonah kept this piece and a pride that had outlived its use.  Mara's motive was to execute a flawless goodbye, not a painless one, because she knew the difference and Jonah had asked for the first.  She called Iris before fear could rehearse its excuses, and she said only that her father was dying and had asked for the other piece.  Mara had until dusk to reunify a daughter, a father, and a song that would never play cleanly again.  Next she set orange peel, a clean towel, and a heel of bread on the warmer, because flowers were forbidden and the scent of hope had never been floral.  It was soap, citrus, warm crumbs, and the ordinary promise that a room could still receive someone.  Her method was hymning the mundane, giving plain objects a melody until frightened people could breathe in the same measure.  She sang to the water pitcher, the bed rail, the cloud beyond the glass, and the blue bracelet waiting at the lift.  Jonah smiled at the bracelet verse and whispered that Annette used to sing the kettle into boiling, which was the first memory he offered without defending himself.  Iris arrived at three, pale from the ascent, with rain on her coat and the other half of the record sealed inside a grocery envelope.  No one said forgive me at once, because Mara had given them smaller words: cup, latch, stair, sleeve, spoon.  She hummed each item in a low, steady tone until Jonah's breathing stopped fighting the mask and Iris stepped out of the doorway.  Then Iris came to the bed and placed her half beside his piece, and the jagged edges met with a dry click that made Jonah close his eyes.  The record could not be played, but Mara touched the join and began Annette's three surviving notes, carrying the tune past the break by ear.  Iris knew the next phrase because she had heard it as a child while bread cooled and orange peels dried beside the stove.  Her voice entered thinly, then Jonah's followed, and the room filled with the scent of hope rather than the perfume of reconciliation.  Mara did not conduct; she kept time with one finger on the bed rail, decisively kind enough to know when not to intrude.  In the pause after the last hum, Jonah said, "I sold the piano because grief made every room too loud."  Iris answered, "I took the record because I thought you were selling her too," and her father nodded as if that sentence had needed a chair.  Before dusk, Mara checked the pain scale, signaled Doctor Iqbal, and made sure comfort was not confused with silence.  When Jonah asked whether the goodbye was good, Mara said, "It is complete," because flawless had never meant unbroken.  He looked from Iris to the joined vinyl, smiled at the warm bread smell, and let one long breath finish the hymn.  The monitor kept its green pulse for a moment, then settled, while the pulsing stillness of the ward deepened instead of breaking.  Afterward, Iris carried both halves down through the clouds and hummed about a kettle to keep the goodbye from becoming a relic.
