The cobalt observatory in autumn held an echoed silence, as if its blue dome had swallowed every voice. 
During the weeks before the coup, no one named it, but dust waited on the rails like held breath. 
Mira, the galaxy-chart embroiderer, repaired royal star-cloths with silver thread, black silk, and a lamp hooded from the wind. 
Her official task was to make the equinox sky flawless for the Chancellor's procession. 
Her private reason was to listen for unheard stories, especially the one missing her brother's name. 
Tolan had navigated the northern expedition, yet the returns listed only cargo, weather, and a zero. 
Mira believed cloth kept what paper discarded, because stitches tightened around hands that had once hesitated. 
Whispers returned thinly, so she learned the complaints of needles, hinges, and leaves scraping the grates. 
Only Bavil, a licensed fool who disguises wisdom as foolishness, crossed the guards unquestioned. 
He wore a paper crown, tied knots in his sleeves, and praised the Chancellor too merrily for suspicion. 
"Mouths hide in bad stitches, my dear," he sang, dropping crumbs into the seams. 
Beneath a black-glass lens burned the archive's oldest object, catalogued as flame from eternal candle, a bead without wick or fuel. 
Keepers said it came from a shrine where every possible future was lit at once, then narrowed to one blue tongue. 
Mira was told never to lift it, but Bavil called forbidden lights lamps for the poor. 
When the equinox charts arrived, three constellations had shifted, and one border province was gone. 
Such changes could steer transports by star, seal a pass, and make seizure look like ceremony. 
Mira pulled one loose silver thread, and a second chart glimmered beneath it with the province alive and bright. 
That was the old stitchers' method, through adjacent possibilities: set the recorded life beside the life that almost happened, and let differences accuse the forger. 
To her, unraveling conspiracies meant finding the obedient thread taught to forget a name. 
She set the flame from eternal candle behind the silk, and the dome filled with roads not taken, ships not burned, and couriers not intercepted. 
In one nearby chance, Tolan stood at the observatory door, holding a report that named the Ministers of Tide and Grain. 
In another, Mira had signed the altered chart, and her brother's zero came from her own careful hand. 
The sight clarified the trap, because every bribed future required the same minute of darkness. 
At the equinox, the orrery would pause, the bell go mute, and palace lamps blink on the Chancellor's signal. 
To expose the order, she would need to synchronize the loom, the orrery, the candle's pulse, and Bavil's little bell during that darkness. 
Bavil stopped pretending to trip, and his face became as still as winter constellations. 
"They killed him for a weather report," he said, "because weather tells armies when roads are honest." 
He tied crumb-colored knots at the province, the North Gate, and the bell rope, turning nonsense into a score. 
Mira heard the story beneath her brother's silence: passable roads, paid ministers, and soldiers promised a bloodless triumph. 
All night she stitched a counter-chart of errands: a baker delayed, a sentry changed, a widow with petitions, a boy under a cart. 
Each small life held one thread of the conspiracy, showing where the false sky depended on people being unseen. 
Before dawn, autumn leaves muffled boots while Bavil hid the hooded flame inside his paper crown. 
At the procession, the Chancellor raised his hand, and the great room kept its customary hush. 
When the orrery paused, Mira drew the counter-thread taut and nodded. 
They began to synchronize: her needle clicked with the muted bell, his bell with the candle's pulse, and the pulse with the loom. 
The blue flame opened into a wheel of adjacent possibilities, and the counter-chart threw every hidden errand across the cobalt walls. 
The crowd saw the erased province burn beside the palace route, and Tolan's report written in the gaps. 
No speech was needed; the observatory's echoed silence carried unheard stories until soldiers lowered their eyes. 
The Chancellor's signal died in his glove, and the guards arrested the ministers whose names were shining on the wall. 
Bavil bowed to the hush, crown askew, and Mira restored Tolan's report to the public cloth stitch by stitch. 
Afterward, people spoke of the day the coup failed to happen, but Mira kept the deeper tale in echoed silence, listening for the next unheard story.
