In the years after violence becomes impossible, Last Quay learned to quarrel with knots instead of knives. 
Its legs stood on the sublittoral fringe, below the lowest tide, among barnacles bright as teeth. 
Fists opened before landing, bullets folded into pollen, and every blow returned as a need to apologize. 
People stayed vicious, but gentle while they did it. 
On the wet planks Mara Venn was called the graceful dock worker, because she crossed swinging cranes with salt and never spilled. 
Grace was not softness. 
It was winning a tide and an argument without giving anyone a bruise. 
Beyond the last piling lay the disappearing island of Vey, surfacing for one night each ninth moon, then going away. 
The Council called it property, chained by a bronze tendon and a register of safe natures. 
On Vey, people shed assigned selves for one night before the island vanished again. 
Mara kept her name off that register by wrapping her ribs and pretending the sea did not answer when she hummed. 
Under linen, two slits opened with the tide, and her lungs liked pressure. 
Her mother, Red Nell, had named this an inheritance, not an illness. 
Before she disappeared, Nell stole the pirate's whalebone scrimshaw from a customs chest and pressed it into Mara's hand. 
"It unlocks guarded hearts," Nell said, because a heart is only a door with a coward on each side. 
The bone showed ships, a whale, and a woman refusing to kneel. 
Held close, it made people hear what they would rather bite off. 
Using it on herself had been the worst quarrel of her life. 
It made her admit she did not want a drier job or Ossen's love. 
She wanted to find freedom in embracing one's true nature, even if that nature had gills and an appetite for black water. 
Ossen kept the register and key and had loved Mara since she was a furious eel of a girl. 
Since pain was outlawed, they practiced combative tenderness: his snarls mended her gloves; her insults salted his soup. 
When Vey rose, he found her at the tendon, sleeves rolled short, scrimshaw at her throat. 
"Do not start with me, child," he said. 
"You start with everyone, old shark, and then call it care," Mara said. 
The tide tugged the tendon until bronze sang under the planks. 
He said, "The chain keeps folk from vanishing." 
"You mean it keeps me from vanishing," she said. 
His jaw worked, but no blow could come, only truth. 
Mara put the pirate's whalebone scrimshaw in his palm, not gently enough to spare him and not roughly enough to hurt. 
The carved woman seemed to stand straighter. 
Ossen shut his eyes and said, "I want to keep you because I love you, and I know that is a cage." 
"There, that is the first honest thing you have thrown at me all year," Mara said. 
He cursed her laugh, then laughed, a rope giving way. 
The old pilot books said the island could detach only by the call of a whale from the deep. 
Council engineers preferred three signatures and a padlock; freedom looks untidy in minutes. 
But under the quay something older than minutes began to call. 
The note rose through piling, bone, and teeth, so low that lamps shivered and guarded chests ached. 
Mara did not imitate the whale; she answered as herself, slits open, salt moving through her blood. 
The scrimshaw warmed until its carved whale swam through black ink. 
Ossen put the bronze key in her hand and said, "Fine, then, go before I call you darling." 
She kissed his knuckles, unlocked the padlock, and pulled the pin. 
The tendon detached by that call, not by strength, and Vey slid from the quay into darkness. 
On the shore no one fought; they held one another and argued about how to let go. 
Mara stepped after the disappearing island with her gills bare, no longer graceful for wages, only free in the shape that was hers.
