At the lunar observatory above the clouds, the clouds were not weather but dust, silver gusts lifted by miners below the south rim. 
The piers stood above that dim surf, and through their floors came each month's deep moonquake, a reminder that even stone has tides. 
Mara Venn, the sage camera repairer, had kept its instruments alive for thirty-one years, not by making them rigid, but by teaching them how to yield. 
Her answer to Lian's death was to harness the ephemeral, because brief light seemed the only honest likeness of a life. 
Lian, a cloud cartographer, had died describing a bright Pacific edge fading from her screen. 
After that, every repair carried a luminous ache, as if each lens held borrowed daylight. 
On her bench lay the clock hand that won't stop spinning, a blue steel minute hand from the cracked expedition clock. 
Freed from its dial and hung in a magnetic cradle with a keeper coil, it had no hour to tell, only a soft whine above the work lamp. 
Its rate remained changelessly constant, and Mara trusted that stubborn little orbit more than clock software in a quake. 
Near it stood a pressure tube holding the last oak leaf Lian had mailed from Oregon. 
When Mara inverted the glass, that leaf slid down through cabin air, scraping the wall with a dry, apologetic whisper. 
The old manuals said that when foundations shake, shutters must be reset by ear, so she had learned to restructure the camera by the sound of a leaf falling. 
Each change in the scrape told her which brace had gone proud, which mirror wanted mercy, and which bolt could be surrendered. 
The younger engineers called this superstition until they watched her save a spectrograph during the Long Quake without touching a screen. 
Afterwards, they called her sage with less irony, because she listened to what the building was about to become. 
That evening, Earth hung in the floor window, cloud-wrapped and blue, while the forecast named 03:12 for the perigee shock. 
At the same minute, sunrise would send a seven-second fan of polarized light across the Pacific, an omen Lian had waited eighteen years to chart. 
The great camera could record it, but the laser mast was split, and no dish could whisper the data home. 
Only the old rail beneath the dome remained, a launcher for sample capsules that had to reach escape velocity before Earth's nets caught them. 
A blank wafer waited in the capsule's nose, ready for Lian's impossible glint to become numbers. 
Normally the firing coils took their rhythm from software, but a quake turned every circuit into a liar. 
Mara planned to borrow the clock hand's patient contacts, one pulse per turn, and let the leaf decide when each brace should yield. 
It was a dangerous marriage of steadiness and frailty, which was why no manual had dared describe it. 
At 03:12, when foundations shake, the whole observatory rang like a glass held too close to music. 
Dust clouds rose below the piers, the floor window blurred, and the first image of Earth opened in the camera's unblinking eye. 
Mara inverted the tube. 
The oak leaf began to fall, and its whisper thinned whenever a pier tightened against the coming shock. 
With her left hand she loosened shutter latches, and with her right she began to restructure the camera and rail into a path for the tremor. 
Where the leaf hissed sharply she gave a coil slack; where it went almost silent she drew the pulse tighter, matching the spinning hand's changelessly constant beat. 
The record filled the wafer while the Pacific fan unfolded, a green veil no wider than a promise and no longer than grief allowed. 
On the second shock, the rails woke in sequence, each contact clicked by the clock hand that won't stop spinning. 
Mara heard the capsule leave its clamp, felt the floor stop fighting her, and saw the last shutter close on an Earth already altered. 
The launcher gave one bright kick toward the dark, and the capsule crossed escape velocity with the wafer's seven seconds folded inside it. 
Nobody cheered, because the image was gone from them at the instant it became safe, and that was the cost of sending anything beloved out of reach. 
Mara understood then that to harness the ephemeral was never to cage it, but to give it enough structure, and enough speed, to answer someone else.
