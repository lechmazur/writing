The abandoned radio tower city suspended over a gorge always woke during lunar perigee, though waking meant sparks, creaks, and wind bills.  Its aerials hung from cold cables above black mist, each tower tied to its neighbor by walkways and old legal spells.  Mira Vale crossed the swaying post road with a toolbox, copper coils, and the title celestial signal interpreter sewn inside her coat.  The title once meant clean rooms and pensioned tea; now it meant sorting weather from messages.  Everyone else had left after the Last Broadcast, when anchors answered the moon like unpaid bills.  Mira stayed because her mother had vanished into that broadcast with a screwdriver and one ridiculous promise.  The promise was to find the beginning in every ending, and Mira made it as plain as checking fuses.  Tonight the moon sat close enough to press blue coins into puddles on the planks.  At the shortest shadow, the Last Broadcast would repeat and pull one more bolt from the city.  Mira meant to tune the anchors first, not for rescue, but because loose cities were bad engineering.  For the work she carried the hollywood star fragment, a chipped pink pentagon catalogued by conductivity rather than awe.  Inside its brass rim slept a shard of hush, not emptiness but the shape left when a famous voice stopped.  The relic was serenely electric, warm as a banked stove, and it made Mira's wrist hairs lie down.  Ordinary crystal screamed under moonlight; this fragment kept a courteous silence that could hold a spark without waste.  At the central tower, Mira set her lantern on a crate and wrote failing cables in a grocery ledger.  The switching desk held eight lead plaques in slots that old operators had named assumptions.  Each plaque narrowed hearing and carried a sensible lie: SKY, SPEAKER, WIRE, SILENCE, LOSS, ABOVE, HUMAN, DONE.  Mira had trusted those plaques for years, because training teaches which fences to mistake for walls.  Her mother had tuned by discarding assumptions, one stamped certainty at a time, preferably out a window.  Mira unscrewed SKY, and the aerials stopped craning upward and bent toward the gorge, where first survey pins still shone.  She dropped SPEAKER, WIRE, and HUMAN into a bucket, then emptied it and charged the loss to maintenance.  With every discarded assumption the city grew less abandoned, as if the bakery and school had only been waiting for someone to stop naming them gone.  When SILENCE, LOSS, ABOVE, and DONE remained, the moon touched the underside of the gorge and the Last Broadcast began.  It came not from speakers but from bolts, cracked cups, dish racks, and the fillings in Mira's teeth.  Her mother's voice filled the room, brisk with static: stop chasing the last word.  Mira almost kept the recording, and that almost was the most dangerous clamp in the room.  Instead she unscrewed DONE, because an ending was often a door labelled by tired people.  The static opened, and under the farewell lay the first clang of the survey hammer that founded the city.  The beginning had been buried inside the ending, not as miracle but as a maintenance tone for anchor spells.  Mira fitted the hollywood star fragment into the cracked dial, and the shard of hush cupped the tone before the moon could steal it.  The desk became serenely electric, calm enough for arithmetic, bright enough to show grease on her fingers.  She discarded SILENCE, and hush proved to be a handle, a grip on the place where sound could be turned.  LOSS followed it into the gorge, and Mira stopped listening for her mother and listened for what her mother had repaired.  ABOVE was hardest, since celestial work taught her to lift her chin even when the fault lay below.  She dropped it, and the receiver swung down toward the first survey pins, singing since before anyone called them anchors.  Then Mira did plain magic: she tuned the pins with a wrench, ledger, and one unblinking note.  The first clang was a command: set west anchors three turns before the blue shadow leaves.  She pulled west wheels until towers steadied; the city gave a relieved, unromantic clank.  Sparks settled into lamps above empty bakeries and schoolroom desks, serenely electric and useful.  Her mother's voice thinned to the hush after work, and Mira let it go.  At dawn she opened a fresh ledger page, labeled it BEGINNING, and listed bolts, leaks, and breakfast.
