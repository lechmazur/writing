During the last hour before the pen meets the blank page, the obsidian spire by a frozen lake held every sound like a cupped hand.  Mira Vale, the ignorance cartographer, stood at the northern window and watched blue cracks vein the ice below.  Her title was not an insult, though courtiers sometimes used it that way; she mapped the exact shape of what the realm did not know.  She was groundedly imaginative, trusting grain tallies, frost depths, mended axles, and children's hard questions as much as dreams.  It began after her father followed a perfect map into an unmarked marsh, and Mira spent twenty years making lines honest enough to save strangers.  Below her, the Council of Ledgers argued over the Winter Sentence, the single line that would govern every province until spring.  In that chamber, writing the future was not mysticism; it meant choosing the orders, taxes, road crews, and school lessons that would make one forecast come true.  The previous scribes had written smooth certainties, and famines had followed them as neatly as copyists followed a ruler.  Now only one sheet of vellum remained, because the northern convoys had not arrived and the ink allowance had been cut to a thimble.  On the table beside it lay the kingfisher feather quill nib, its blue calamus split so finely that it could draw a confidence interval as a hairline.  The nib held one dip of ink, and the law allowed one sentence, no erasures, no marginal notes, and no appeal until thaw.  Mira had come to seed innovation within a constraint, not to beg the councillors for a gentler certainty.  She wanted the sentence to leave room for a thousand small experiments, each bounded by the same rules of mercy and measurement.  High Steward Orren wanted a decree naming three granaries, two roads, and one market price, all copied from the models of thirty calm winters.  He called her blank spaces cowardice, and his supporters laughed with the tired relief of people who prefer a closed door to weather.  Mira did not answer at once; she set her slate on the table and drew the lake as it truly behaved, with pressure ridges, hidden springs, and fisher paths that shifted each dawn.  Then she added ledgers, sickbeds, missing letters, salt prices, bridge repairs, and questions from village teachers.  Mira worked through predictive modeling, folding those ordinary signs into hundreds of possible springs, then testing which sentence would keep people fed when the ice chose an unfamiliar hour to break.  Orren's decree prevailed in the calm runs, because calm runs repeated the past with excellent manners.  It failed whenever a bridge failed, a rumor outran a tax notice, or a district had no spare sled and therefore no way to report hunger.  Mira would prevail through predictive modeling, not through ancestry, volume, or the old comfort of a confident chin.  She showed them the runs in which a fixed price made merchants hide grain, then the runs in which a public ceiling plus local barter ledgers kept bread moving.  She showed them that uncertainty was not fog to be denied but a field whose edges could be marked, watched, and shared.  On her slate, every blank space carried a date, a witness, and a permitted test, so ignorance became a schedule for learning rather than a pit.  The kingfisher feather quill nib had first drawn that map beside a breathing hole, where Mira copied the bird's patience in exact strokes.  Its fine point mattered now because a single hairline on the official page could stand for a continent of revisions made lawfully by others.  Orren leaned over the slate, saw the early thaw branch, and asked what any district was supposed to do if the model itself was wrong.  Mira touched the largest blank, the one labeled local knowledge, and told them to correct it only where the cost could be counted.  That answer changed the room more than any thunder could have, because it gave responsibility a shape that frightened no one beyond endurance.  A baker asked about trial ovens, a roadmaster about bridge reports, and a clerk about owning a useful mistake.  Mira had prepared for those questions, and each condition she offered tightened the constraint while widening the number of minds allowed inside it.  By the seventh bell, the councillors had stopped defending Orren's three granaries and were arguing over fair limits for trial grain.  Mira had prevailed; the page stayed blank, and a sustaining transformation waited in the held ink.
