Mara Venn, an ex-band roadie turned antiques appraiser, arrived at the quarry office trailer the morning after failing publicly on a televised appraisal in Leeds. 
The pit below was closed, but wind returned from the limestone with echo valley longing, as if each blast had left an audience calling. 
She had spent her twenties after the applause, coiling cables while singers signed posters and the crew loaded the night into trucks. 
When Cal, her brother and the band's monitor engineer, died on the motorway, she had needed to discover purpose after loss, and appraisals promised dates, makers, and merciful endings. 
The previous night, with cameras warm, she had called a machine-made sideboard Jacobean, then watched its owner reveal the factory stamp while the host smiled into silence. 
By sunrise, every clip had found her, so she answered with the confidently uncertain line she had invented for clients: value is a probability with a pulse. 
Mara survived by insulating emotionally with facts, reciting that the oak was 1890, the veneer was quarter sawn, her error was one object in thirty years. 
Nell Pike had hired her anyway, perhaps because the family could not price its own dead father's furniture or the tin room where he had ruled. 
She handed Mara a ring of keys and said the trailer held whatever the auctioneer had refused. 
Inside, dust lay on weigh tickets, a cracked adding machine, and a steel desk whose bottom drawer had swollen with damp. 
Mara had to wrest it open with a crowbar, and the jolt loosened a sheaf of wage books. 
Under them lay a speaker grille cloth scrap, brown diamond weave, no larger than a playing card and smelling faintly of solder. 
Her hands knew it before her mind did, because Cal had patched that pattern onto the band's spare amplifier before the last benefit concert. 
That concert had been held in this quarry, in a white ravine, to raise money for miners who had lost their lungs. 
After the applause, Cal had sent the crowd home and sat beside the amp, writing a list of names no song would keep. 
A brittle envelope under the ledgers was labeled Pike, and the grille scrap had been folded into it as a bookmark. 
The letter said Cal had agreed to record the quarrymen's stories after the concert because applause was too brief to carry their names. 
He died before returning, and Nell's father had kept the scrap, the letter, and a cassette in the desk, ashamed that the work had never begun. 
The cassette held ten minutes of room tone, a few guitar notes, and Cal asking someone to begin with the first name. 
Mara felt the church hall's heat rise again, not because a fact had escaped her, but because she had confused being right with being useful. 
Nell asked what the scrap was worth, and Mara nearly gave the number she would have given on air. 
It was common amplifier cloth, perhaps three pounds, unless provenance lifted it into a museum case, and even that was a smaller truth. 
Instead she said, with a voice confidently uncertain, that the cloth was worth almost nothing and the promise inside it might be worth a season of work. 
She offered to catalogue wage books, identify photographs, and record families before the quarry became a lake, pricing what could be priced and leaving honest blanks. 
Nell looked toward the pit, where wind struck cut faces and returned as a patient, wordless chord. 
The workers had called that sound echo valley longing, and now Mara heard not sadness asking to be soothed but attention asking to be kept. 
Nell said yes, and the word was small enough for a trailer and large enough to start an archive. 
Mara stayed until dusk, pricing chairs and label makers, then opened a notebook headed after the applause. 
Under the heading she wrote one uncertain fact, Cal Pike intended to return, and she let that be enough. 
When the quarry PA crackled once before dying, she flinched, then smiled at the old reflex of listening for the next cue. 
Outside, no crowd waited, no reviewer corrected her, and the silence did not forgive her, but it made room. 
She had come to price a dead man's things, and she left with a task that could outlast applause, embarrassment, and her need to be certain.
