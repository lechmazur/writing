The siskin alder grove was intensely quiet, as if snow had been laid over every thought.  Mara entered at dusk, carrying wool-wrapped tools and a white porcelain insulator cupped against her ribs.  No bird moved among the alders, because the valley had forgotten how to carry sound.  People touched throats to speak, tapped cups for warning, and slept facing one another to feel breath.  As the last tree-song sculptor, Mara carved living wood into shapes that held remembered intervals.  The old craft had once coaxed wind through branch flutes, but now it made only tremors for the hands.  Mara wanted to create music in a silent world, not comfort and not proof.  Mara, symphonically minded, dreamed of separate strains answering without merging.  Her teacher had named that discipline empathy in motion, the art of another pressure changing your posture without disappearing inside pity.  She found the lost score by analyzing stress cracks in a long-forgotten sculpture.  Moss hid it beneath the oldest alder, where root and carved knee had grown together.  It showed a musician bent over a hollow chest, one hand lifted, the other pressed to the ground.  When the valley lost its hearing, the final song had entered the wood faster than the wood could open.  The trapped force left a fine map of splits, each crack a rest or interval made visible by failure.  Mara read them with lamp oil, charcoal powder, and the patience of someone listening through skin.  The widest split gave the bass, the hairline forks gave higher voices, and unbroken bands gave their rests.  She copied the map onto narrow copper wires, because metal remembered tension more faithfully than memory did.  The porcelain insulator came from the abandoned telegraph pole above the burying ground.  Before the hush, that pole had carried messages of births, storms, debts, and sleeping children.  Its glazed cup had kept each current separate from rain, earth, and the claims of other poles.  Mara needed that courtesy for vibration, since damp roots swallowed a wire's motion too quickly.  Without it, every note would collapse into one dull shiver, and pity would do what pity always did.  With it raised on a forked stake, each wire could remain itself long enough to answer another.  The old inscription on the sculpture had supplied the only hour that mattered.  Its last line told the reader to strum after the hedgehog uncurls.  A hedgehog slept beneath the carved knee, curled so tightly that frost silvered only the outer spines.  Mara checked it each dusk, never touching, because the hour belonged to the small body, not to her wish.  If she strummed before sap rose and winter loosened its grip, the sculpture would split past all repair.  On the appointed evening, the air held no promise.  Mara set the stake, looped the wires across the glazed cup, and tied their ends to seven living alders.  She brushed charcoal from the sculpture's lifted hand and waited with her palms above the master wire.  At the grove edge, three villagers stood where she had asked them to stand, saying nothing.  They had come because each had lost someone to the hush, and because Mara had offered no cure.  Then the hedgehog uncurled.  Its nose emerged first, then one foot, then the soft belly that had guarded the root all winter.  Nothing sounded.  Even its claws on bark made only a darkness that Mara felt through her boots.  Only after the animal had taken three steps did Mara lower her thumb.  She did not strike.  She let one finger strum the master wire as lightly as one might smooth a sleeping brow.  The porcelain insulator held the first tremor apart from the earth.  Then the alders took up their separate cracks, and the wires returned those cracks to the sculpture.  Mara felt the bass in her heels, the high voices in her teeth, and the rests along her tired arms.  The villagers did not move closer.  Each stood inside a different ache, yet the ground altered under every foot by the same small grammar.  That was the music she had wanted, and it made no noise at all.  It was empathy in motion, not one heart swallowing another, but many hearts lending their weight to a shared line.  When the wires settled, the grove stayed intensely quiet, but it was no longer solitary.
