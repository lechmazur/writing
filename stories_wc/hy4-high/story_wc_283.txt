The amber warehouse beneath cliffs held everything the town had misplaced, including winter coats. 
Its air held an unearthed silence, the hush of resin and patiently recovered dust. 
Mira was the hourglass turner for nine thousand glasses measuring the obedience of other people. 
Each dusk she tipped them, restarting the one future the town permitted. 
She was quietly inspired by scraps in mended coats, not by the proctors above. 
Those survivals suggested ordinary choices outlived records that denied them. 
The ledgers called the last working hour as the truth unravels, when official accounts contradicted themselves. 
Only then did glasses stutter, showing sand climbing upward against the decree. 
At the center stood the canyon echo in a jar, sealed with black wax. 
The proctors said it repeated a prophecy: the cliff would fall unless everyone obeyed. 
They had clipped that sentence from the canyon and used it to close every question. 
Mira wanted to spin a prophecy into raw possibility, because her brother died obeying a timetable. 
His glass emptied while he waited for permission to leave a collapsing shed. 
No record named who shouted, pulled, or was ignored. 
The button drawers kept that answer, though authorities thought buttons harmless. 
Each lost button arrived with thread, smell, and the pressure of a particular hand. 
Mira had learned shared wisdom from sorting them, since no single button explained a life. 
Together they showed routes, quarrels, favors, refusals, illness, and escape. 
The old rule said the bottled sentence could be changed only by cataloging lost buttons. 
So Mira began to unscramble it with pearl disks, brass anchors, and wooden toggles. 
She placed buttons where the hum of the jar thinned, then wrote the memory each released. 
A bone button from a baker recalled carts turning back before the bridge cracked. 
A blue button from a child held a warning whispered under a desk when the noon bell failed. 
A plain button from a widow remembered a door barred against inspectors, not neighbors. 
Hidden cautions separated from the stern arrangement of one forecast. 
Mira understood that prophecy had been made by stealing many cautions and flattening them into one command. 
The jar had never repeated the future; it repeated the loudest editing of the past. 
That frightened her, because a fixed fate was simple to hate. 
Raw possibility required choosing without any guaranteed ending. 
She looked at the hourglasses waiting for her hand to confirm the same noon. 
Instead of turning them, she carried the drawers into amber light beneath the jar. 
The floor covered an older canyon bed, and its cold seams breathed dust. 
Each catalog card became a witness filed beside another until the silence felt crowded. 
She trusted that unearthed silence, full of people holding their breath together. 
When the shift arrived, the proctors came down with lanterns and clean gloves. 
They told Mira to turn the first glass and send the decree upward. 
She set a brass button on the ledger, and the seal of the jar split dryly. 
The released sound did not fit one sentence, no matter how sternly they listened. 
It spread through the warehouse as hundreds of advisories attached to remembered buttons. 
One scrap said leave early; shore the wall; carry the children; open the granary; wait for the sick. 
The proctors called it disorder, because it offered no single hour for surrender. 
Mira turned the hourglasses sideways, so every sand stream crossed another and refused to finish. 
The motion was her old task, yet it changed from repetition into invitation. 
Above them, the cliffs did not fall at noon, because the town was already moving in several directions. 
Some reinforced the wall, some fetched the sick, and some sat together until fear loosened. 
No one cheered; the new future arrived like a tool finally handed onward. 
Mira kept the jar open, no longer an oracle but a vessel for shared wisdom. 
Whenever anyone brought another lost button, she cataloged it beside the rest and listened for what might yet be possible.
