After the magic trick fails, the army does not cry out.  Screens of silver have collapsed into the reeds, and the duke's conjurer stands knee-deep in black water with his cards floating away.  The trick had promised to reflect the face of whoever stole thirty children from the valley, but every mirror showed only eager soldiers.  Now the enemy camp across the lake lifts its spears, the duke's ranks answer without haste, and no one breaks the hush.  The veterans call it warlike serenity, a shield wall of quiet breath and sharpened attention.  Mara has borrowed that calm like bark armor, because Mara is the guarded druid who finds lost things.  She does not find coins first, or keys, or the thin rings soldiers leave in taverns.  Her method works through expanding circles: one absence names the next, and each ring pulls the world closer to what is missing.  A dropped bead leads to a trampled ribbon, the ribbon to a sled track, and the track to a child who has forgotten how to speak.  Such successes made strangers clutch at her sleeves, so she learned to keep her face closed and her pockets full of salt.  Salt keeps grave dirt from taking root in the living, and it keeps a druid from trusting easy gratitude.  She has come to the lake to discover what mirrors don't show, not to choose a side in the duke's patient war.  Her brother Iven marched with the stolen children as their guard, and the mirrors returned his face wearing an enemy helmet.  Mara knows Iven's left hand shook when he lied, and the reflection's hand had been steady as a lance.  Whatever the glass displayed, it had not shown her brother.  Behind the failed trick, winter opens on the water although the low hills are warm with summer.  At the center of the widening frost stands a swirling orchard vortex at the heart of a cursed lake, turning apple boughs, pear trunks, and black water into one slow spiral.  The trees wear fruit like clenched helmets, and their roots revolve beneath the ice with the measured tread of archers.  Each revolution carries a reflection farther from the shore, as if the lake were drilling its army for invasion.  Mara steps onto the ice while both armies watch, and the serenity on the banks tightens but does not crack.  She sets a copper pin in a crack, circles it with salt, and waits for the first loss to answer.  The pin had belonged to a drummer boy, and its small tug points toward a mitten frozen beneath the apple trees.  She draws a wider ring; the mitten pulls toward a broken slate, the slate toward Iven's missing knife, and the knife toward a silence under the vortex.  The silence is not empty, because lost things often gather around a truth that no one dared to carry home.  Mara takes from her belt the only key she trusts, a flesh-eating plant seed no larger than a tooth.  It grew in the mass grave after the last border battle, where every generous flower learned the taste of soldiers.  Mara will not plant it in living soil, but the lake below her is full of drowned hands that can feed one narrow root.  Before she can set it, she must unbend the seed's hooked shell, which curls toward any warm pulse.  Her gloves are lined with lead, her breath is slow, and she presses the hook straight while the armies hold their own breath like shields.  The shell yields one inch at a time, and beneath it the ice begins to mutter in a dead queen's voice.  The oldest story in the valley says a ruler confessed beside this water, but no soldier heard the price she named.  The lake stole the words before they reached the ranks, locked them under winter, and gave back only polished pictures of blame.  That is the frozen confession, and every generation has marched toward its reflection instead of its sound.  Mara sets the unbent seed in the crack, and a root as fine as a bowstring descends through ice toward the dead queen's buried mouth.  It eats only what is already dead, and the flesh around that old honesty softens enough for one syllable to rise.  "I sold the first thirty children," the dead voice says, "because the lake promised my dynasty victory."  The orchard fruit splits, releasing the stolen children and Iven, who carries his recovered knife like a white flag.  Mara's circles reverse, returning every lost thing except the queen's guilt, which remains plain in the warming air.  Both commanders lower their spears with parade calm, and Mara pockets the empty husk without smiling.
