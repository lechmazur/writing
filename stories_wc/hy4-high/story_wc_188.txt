The festival at the blooming fortress at dusk is held during the hush of a night with no stars, and no one looks up.  Ash from the long siege has sealed the sky, and the stones seem to pull every face downward.  Nessa Vale, a wandering fireworks designer, arrives with mortars named after ridiculous clerks.  Her serious playfulness is bait for people who have forgotten curiosity.  She has come to defy the gravity of hopelessness, not to flatter governors.  She chants while walking around the inner wall, because a marching rhyme keeps grief from seizing her hands.  The chant measures tiles, steadies fuses, and gives children something to follow.  Every third step brings her above a drain where the old mosaic sweats saltpeter and violet pollen.  The council calls the floor ornamental, but Nessa calls it a note in mosaic.  Her mother laid those tiles before the war, when flowers leached powder from ruined magazines.  Read correctly, the fortress becomes an insulted cannon.  Read badly, damp grains clog and burst into the governor's balcony.  Thus she must filter the sweat before measuring a single charge, though no lamp may be lit.  The hush forbids metal tools, and even a struck flint might wake the mines.  Her method is through ambient awareness, learned in fields before careless celebrations.  She listens to bees choosing warmer stones, smells linseed in grout, and feels cool air below.  They show which tiles hide the message and which drains breathe beneath siege dust.  She presses four blue squares, three chalk squares, and a black wink.  Under her palms, grout becomes letters only sunset and patience reveal.  The sentence reads, filter what the flowers drink, then hang the stars beneath your feet.  Nessa laughs once because her mother enjoyed giving orders from underfoot.  At her belt hangs the fire spinner's kerosene rag knot, a black braid carried since Tallo vanished.  Tallo made flame bow like a polite dog, then gave her the knot after one illegal performance.  She has never used it because lighting it meant admitting that some people do not return.  Now the note requires tight linen and one oiled end willing to hold a patient spark.  She untwists the braid, cleans its dry heart, and keeps the kerosene tail from children.  With the inner cloth stretched over a cracked cup, she begins to filter saltpeter, pollen, ash, and rain.  She works through ambient awareness, judging each pinch by smell, tack of air, and hush.  Grit stays in the cloth, blue crystal gathers in the cup, and the crowd's breathing follows her chant.  Children creep closer, solemn as judges, while she names charges after tiny sins.  Their mothers do not smile yet, but their chins lift a finger's width from their collars.  Nessa mixes filtered crystals with flower dust, making paste scented with rain and struck matches.  She packs paste in clay thimbles under drains, where roots cradle heat without cracking the mosaic.  Each thimble sits in a message square, so old words become a fuse map.  The governor watches like a man guarding a purse from joy.  Nessa does not hurry, because hopelessness has weight but no sense of timing.  She waits through ambient awareness until blossoms still, bees settle, and held breaths go shallow.  Then she touches the oiled tail of Tallo's knot to a coal hidden in a warmed tile.  Kerosene catches without a crack, and the braid becomes a slow orange sentence across the floor.  One by one, thimbles answer outward, opening blue stars under astonished shoes.  The children laugh before remembering fear, and laughter pulls faces down toward the ground.  Then heated roots exhale, lifting blue fires through blossom cups into a climbing constellation.  Every head follows, and that small, ridiculous motion is stronger than the pull in the stones.  When sparks fade, the sky is empty, but the courtyard is full of raised, practical faces.  Nessa leaves the cooled knot with children already chanting while walking.  She will leave at dawn, lighter because even wandering grief can be taught to rise.
