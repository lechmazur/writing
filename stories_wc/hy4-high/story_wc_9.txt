Only after the birth of my daughter did the clock in my breast admit what I had done.  I was an onyx-ink cartomancer, famous for laying black cards on silk and promising grieving clients that their futures could be cleaned.  Each reading took a minute no one would miss, a skipped breath at a window, a pause between a question and its answer.  I told myself such minutes were husks, but they gathered in me like coins in a drowned purse.  My daughter arrived with no cry, her eyelids trembling around a darkness deeper than sleep, and the midwife whispered that some children are born between seconds.  I knew the debt had come to collect the first hour of her life.  I climbed the white stair beyond the convent roof to find where lost time accumulates.  The old women called the passage cloudbound repentance, because no one returned lighter until every borrowed minute had been given a name.  At the top waited a midnight glade above clouds, where grass grew from moonlit vapor and stars hung wet on the branches.  Its choking beauty was so bright and thick that breathing felt like swallowing lilies and ash.  I had brought no sword, only my deck, a roll of linen, and the dolls' tea set saucer crack, a black line on porcelain from the birthday when I wished a dull afternoon away and heard the saucer split.  A stolen minute had shone in that hairline whenever I lied about a reading's cost.  Mother had kept a family custom of tracing hands on birthday tablecloths, pressing each child's palm in berry dye beside the year and the cake crumbs.  I spread them over the grass, and the glade shone through every colored hand like a congregation of small, patient windows.  To begin, I had to unfasten the blue ribbon from the roll and the silver clasp from my card box.  With the deck open, the last card turned faceup without my touch, showing a cradle roofed by a black sun.  I shut my eyes before its meaning settled, and for once I remained willingly perplexed.  A cartomancer who knows the ending will always spend someone else to improve it, and I had spent enough.  The grass bent toward the dolls' tea set saucer crack, and the crack filled with cold light until it looked like a narrow road.  I set the porcelain crescent at the center of the oldest hand, my mother's broad palm from a birthday before I was born.  Clouds below us opened, and I saw where lost time accumulates: not in a vault, but in the pauses people abandon when they are afraid to feel.  The minutes lay heaped there, each one holding a face, a room, a spoon halfway to a mouth, a kiss almost given.  Cloudbound repentance demanded that I sort them, not keep them, and the birthday hands gave me a method.  I traced each palm with onyx ink, speaking the absent person's name, and the stolen minutes trembled toward their proper outlines.  Some rushed home to kitchens where soup had cooled; some entered the lungs of sleepers who suddenly turned toward someone they loved.  The first hour of my daughter's life remained, curled in the crack like a bee in a blue flower.  The card under my palm warmed, offering one clean trick to send that hour home untouched by sacrifice, but I kept my eyes closed.  Perplexity was the only mercy I had never learned to sell.  When I unfastened my own collar and pressed the saucer shard to my throat, the debt understood the shape of the thief.  The hour poured out of me, or into me, taking with it every glib future I had sold and leaving a silence spacious enough for one child.  Above the glade, the clouds loosened into rain, and below them a baby began to cry in a room full of exhausted women.  By dawn I was home, with no deck, no gift for prophecy, and a tablecloth stained by berry juice, rain, and onyx.  My daughter slept against my ribs while I traced her hand beside mine, not to claim a year, but to let this one arrive.  The light on her fingers was almost unbearable, a choking beauty with room in it for breath.
