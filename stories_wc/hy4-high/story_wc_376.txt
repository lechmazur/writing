In the sprawling greenhouse city under a toxic sky, after the oasis dries, the public fountains are sealed and every unsent letter is blamed on thirst. Mara Venn, the last gilded-leaf calligrapher of Vitrine Ward, writes condolences on leaves thin enough to float in breath. The leaves are gilded with a living graft fed by her wrist, and the graft wilts near dishonesty. When councilors ask her to inscribe that the reservoir is merely resting, the gold curls brown and drops ink on their cuffs. So she survives on humbler commissions, practicing candor in small moments: a son's burnt supper, a widow's relief, a guard's fear. Each plain sentence keeps the graft upright; it is the only luxury the drought has not taxed. Mara has one private commission that no one pays for, to find where lost conversations go. Her brother Ilan vanished during rationing, after their argument over who had failed their mother. He sent no farewell, only a zinc-etched postal plate fragment with half an address and a nick shaped like a crescent. The postmaster swears the missing half was delivered, and Mara's graft droops at the polished certainty in his voice. She keeps the fragment beneath her blotter and would reread its four cut marks whenever grief threatened to become a grand accusation. The marks say only RETURN, MOIST, NINE, ASK, but she has learned that small words survive where speeches evaporate. When the last reeds crack, heat shrinks the floorboards of her stall, once the postal exchange. A cracked board lifts, and under it lie mouse-chewed scraps inked with Ilan's clumsy arrows. The scraps are a boy's treasure map, full of arrows and impossible rescues. She finds the route by piecing together a shredded treasure map found under floorboards, and sees that the pneumatic mail tubes do not end at the sealed fountain. They bend beneath the dry basin toward a chamber labeled RETURNS, with crescent notches marking every junction. The fragment's nick fits the first notch, so Mara ties it to her palm and closes the stall. Before she goes, she writes the council, 'the fountain is empty, and I am afraid.' Her graft stays bright all the way to Vent Nine, where a watchman blocks the maintenance stair. He says the Underpost is empty, and the leaf at her wrist folds like a burnt tongue. Instead of arguing, Mara asks whether he hears plates ticking behind the wall when the fans rest. He admits that he does, that the sound keeps him company, and that his official report called it rats. With that small correction, the leaf opens, and he gives her the stair key he claimed not to carry. At each junction she must reread the zinc-etched postal plate fragment in a different lamplight, because ASK is MASK without its first letter. The map warns her to ask rather than claim, so she asks the dry pipes for leave and the cracked valves for pardon. Every answer is a tick, a loosened bolt, or old rain, and each is enough. Below the oasis bed, where silt has replaced lilies, Mara finds the chamber of returns packed with postal plates. They hang on copper threads, etched with apologies, interruptions, questions, and truths swallowed at doorways. This is where lost conversations go when speakers choose a clean lie or a silence that looks cleaner. The central lock, a diaphragm tuned to the same graft, asks for one grand truth before releasing a plate. Mara tries the sentence she has polished for years, that she loved her mother perfectly, and her graft blackens. She tries praise, patience, and mercy, but the diaphragm stays shut. At last she speaks without varnish, 'I was relieved when Mother asked for Ilan, and then ashamed, and I kept the relief.' The graft lifts, the diaphragm opens, and one plate drops into her hands with Ilan's crescent nick along its edge. She rereads it once, then listens as the etching clicks into a small voice: I left water on your sill; I did not know how to say goodbye. Mara does not ask the chamber for a torrent of speeches, only for leave to answer what was actually said. She whispers, 'I was angry because you smiled,' and the plate warms as if a hand has closed around it. By dawn she carries no treasure but a habit, and Vitrine Ward learns to send smaller, truer words before its people need the Underpost.
