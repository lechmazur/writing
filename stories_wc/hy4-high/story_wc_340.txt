In the icebound spire above the clouds, I am an ascending servant, which means I carry other people's last things upward and call it progress. 
The House has taught me affectionate detachment: warm the blanket, note the stain, never say that the child who used it is gone. 
I am also warmly inquisitive, a flaw the stewards tolerate because I ask questions only of objects, which rarely complain. 
This is why the morning clerk gives me the cracked case from the failed airship, and not to a grave porter with tidy hands. 
All of it happens as the elevator climbs ten floors, from the salt cellar to the relic chamber, with a bronze dial counting each release of frost. 
My orders call the task an encrypted diary discovery, because the Council wants a route before it wants a person. 
I have a smaller reason: to seek out the joy beneath anguish, since Captain Iva Sel once taught me knots and kept my name when names were rationed. 
Inside the case lie the airship captain's goggles, their brass rims furred with ice, their left lens split into a patient star. 
At floor one, I unfold the oilcloth wrapping and find no diary, only a strip of black ribbon sewn into the lining. 
Warmly inquisitive, I tug the ribbon with my thumbnail, and a folded packet drops onto my lap as the car sighs higher. 
The packet is a diary of eight pages, its ordinary log hidden under cramped numbers that look like weather reports. 
The last page is titled Anguish, and under it Captain Sel has drawn three small dots, like punctuation waiting for mercy. 
At floor two, the shaft passes a prism window, and sunlight comes apart across the walls in pale freckles. 
I remember what Captain Sel said when she lent me these goggles for a snow drill: nothing truthful is legible in a straight beam. 
Only through scattered light could her storm maps show safe passages, because the broken lens forgave glare by making many soft copies of it. 
At floor three, I set the airship captain's goggles over my eyes and tilt the packet toward the freckles. 
The numbers loosen, not into the route the Council wants, but into a second handwriting pressed between the lines. 
The first hidden sentence says that the crew sang badly and she loved them for it. 
At floor four, I discover the cipher was not made for the Council at all, but for anyone forced to read grief in a hurry. 
The surface entries, readable by any clerk, record hunger, a cracked rudder, and the long white silence after the engines failed. 
Between them, through scattered light, she had written small mercies: a shared orange, a borrowed mitten, a joke about a beard. 
I smile, then fold the smile away, because affectionate detachment leaves no fingerprints on evidence. 
At floor five, the elevator trembles under a gust, and the freckled light breaks red across the page. 
A hidden line under Anguish tells the small knot girl that Captain Sel kept her name, and that this was a good life. 
My hands grow warm inside the cold gloves, which is inconvenient for a servant paid to notice, not feel. 
At floor six, the bronze dial admits that the climb will end soon, as machinery always does when the heart has begun to argue. 
The Council will copy the surface entries, file the hidden ones as sentiment, and send the route to men who own weather. 
I consider giving them the surface numbers only, a tidy theft so modest that even my supervisors might call it accuracy. 
At floor seven, I ask the goggles, warmly inquisitive, what Captain Sel would want carried upward. 
They answer with a green shard of light, making the small joys brighter than the disasters around them. 
At floor eight, I unfold the final page again and crease it so the hidden layer faces outward. 
Then I set the airship captain's goggles on top of the diary, with the broken lens angled toward the prism window. 
Anyone in the relic chamber who looks through scattered light will find the joy first, and only then the anguish that tried to cover it. 
In the icebound spire above the clouds, grief is stored by height, but I have changed one small shelf. 
At floor ten, I step out with the case level in my arms, fond of the dead captain, detached from the Council, and quietly carrying her good life upward.
