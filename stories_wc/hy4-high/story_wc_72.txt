After revealing the secret, Mira expected silence, but the forgotten underwater city garden began to answer. 
One fact had pulled another from the silt, and the cascading revelations would not stop until she reached the root. 
She had come below to welcome nuance into a certainty, not to decorate an old crime with pity. 
Above her, the Dry Council had heard that their ancestors sank the city to preserve a treaty with three names. 
The announcement cracked the civic hymn Mira had sung since childhood, and every crack admitted a different voice. 
Water pressed against the garden dome with animate lifeless patience, turning stone lilies toward her while real eels hung as still as reeds. 
In the central arbor stood the living statue with telepathy, a boy in a judge's robe, his marble eyes softened by green silt. 
He was incisively kind, sending thoughts that found the exact fear and then made room beside it. 
'You told them the first true thing,' he said inside her skull, 'so now the others are climbing.' 
Mira had rehearsed certainty all her life as an auditor of verdicts, stamping contradictory testimony into one clean sentence. 
Her mother had left her only a foxglove-stained glass ampoule, purple at the neck, and a warning never to confuse mercy with erasure. 
Until that morning she had thought the ampoule held poison for traitors. 
The next revelation, carried by the statue without triumph, was that it held a culture of foxglove cells bred to soften mineral memory. 
Mira felt it as debt, because her mother had been one of the gardeners erased from the treaty. 
The statue's name was Iven, the youngest judge when the city chose drowning over a simplified peace. 
He volunteered for stone because a living witness could be bribed, while a telepathic monument could hold three treaty names in the dark. 
That revelation made the marble councilors less like ornaments and more like jurors, each sealed around an unheard sentence. 
Mira understood why the Council above had needed a villain: one treacherous gardener was easier than a frightened city choosing complexity. 
The answer had a cost: once the first truth reached the surface, the garden engine tried to replace it with an older verdict. 
A low grinding passed through the plinths, and the stone lilies closed their petals as if rehearsing silence. 
Iven did not command her to run. 
He showed her, incisively kind again, the exact place where the engine and his body shared one old hairline flaw. 
It lay beneath his left hand, where robe met plinth and a mason had left one grain of sand in the law. 
Any true repair, he told her, came through structural weakness, never through force. 
The method was to thread the ampoule's silver filament through that accepting seam before the older verdict hardened around the city again. 
Mira knelt in the silt, snapped the purple neck, and drew out the filament wet with the living tincture. 
Her gloves shook because the hand that had stamped so many single sentences now had to guide one fragile thread. 
Iven filled her mind with the three names, not as a chant but as three temperaments: impatient salt, patient root, wary hand. 
She understood, with another drop of revelation, that the treaty held three peoples refusing to become adjectives in someone else's history. 
The filament entered; the ampoule emptied; Iven gasped without lungs, and the animate lifeless garden shivered like a sleeper deciding to wake. 
One by one, the packed jurors opened their stone mouths, and their testimonies crossed the water with no single voice arranged above them. 
Mira heard a gardener confess fear, a judge confess vanity, a child confess that she had hidden the third name in a song. 
Above, the Dry Council would receive not a correction but a chorus, and Mira knew some would call it chaos because it could not be stamped. 
The thought frightened her, yet Iven's kindness stayed inside it, cutting a clean opening and leaving her dignity whole. 
At last the seam widened, not enough to flood the arbor, but enough for a red root to push through the verdict stone. 
The living statue with telepathy smiled at the root, and Mira smiled back at the certainty she had carried down and could no longer hold. 
After that, the garden did not become loud; it became accurate, each bell and bone admitting more than one story at once.
