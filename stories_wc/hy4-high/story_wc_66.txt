After the story famine, the city kept music the way a prisoner keeps a thread: not for beauty, but because a thread can measure a wall.  Nera Vale was a diligent orchestra copyist, and diligence had kept her hands cleaner than her conscience.  Each morning she inked sanctioned scales for the State House orchestra, copying the same obedient notes onto paper that had once held epics.  Each evening she carried the shaved margins home through fog, because margins were too small for censors and large enough for the dead.  Her refuge was an ember alcove in mist beneath the ruined public baths, where coals from burned shelves glowed under wet stone.  Thistled calm lived there, prickly with caution, and Nera had learned to breathe as if an inspector might hear the breathing.  The shortwave radio dial glow made a small amber island on her worktable, the only warm thing that never lied about the hour.  It came from a hidden transmitter in the old observatory, which sent one pirate broadcast each week before the curfew bells finished.  Speech could be triangulated, but a humming chorus wandered, so the rebels sent memory via elusive song lyrics that changed at every refrain.  Nera copied those fragments into second violin parts, where an accidental could be a road and a rest could be a missing mother.  Her proudest craft was ink made from lampblack, mica, and dust swept from the banned library's floor, secretly luminous when the radio tubes warmed it.  By day the marks looked like mildew, but by night they rose as pale narrative threads, which was how she meant to teach dust to tell stories.  Nera's mother had shelved romances in that library, and when the furnaces took it, Nera kept one apron pocket of ash.  She did not believe the ash was silent; she believed it had forgotten the shape of a listener.  On the night the broadcast failed, fog pressed its palms against the alcove and the dial shone three short pulses, then one long.  The signal did not carry a tune, only a cough of static and a boy's whisper asking for the ending.  Without an ending, the fragments scattered in kitchens and attics would remain beautiful scars, not stories with breath enough to travel.  The next morning, an Index clerk came to the orchestra room with a ledger and a new order.  Tomorrow's concert would be relayed on every public speaker, and all copied parts must be inspected under blue lamps before dawn.  Blue lamps were the reason Nera had chosen an ink that woke only to heat, not to light, and she blessed her old fear with thistled calm.  She understood the trap: the public relay could carry the pirate broadcast farther than the observatory ever had, if she dared cloak the ending inside sanctioned music.  Daring, for Nera, had always meant adding one extra dot to a note, not placing her mother's ash inside the mouth of the state.  She sat until the coals paled, writing a last verse about a girl who gathered dust from a burned shelf and taught it the word once.  Then she broke the verse into initials, breaths, and bow lengths, because plain words would bring Index vans and a song could vanish before the vans arrived.  To cloak each phrase, she gave the flutes a question, the cellos a road, and the chorus a line about rain remembering where a house had stood.  The lyrics were elusive enough to please the censors, since every listener heard a different loss and no clerk could name the loss they shared.  Before dawn she carried the parts to the orchestra room, past posters promising silence, and set them on the stands with her usual square precision.  The blue lamps showed only obedient scales, though Nera's sleeves smelled of coal smoke and her left thumb shone faintly from the secretly luminous ink.  The clerk leaned near that thumb, and the room held the thistled calm of a field pretending not to hide a bird.  Then the conductor entered, irritated by a broken shoe, and slapped the score so hard that dust jumped from every page.  The clerk sneezed, waved the pages through a shaft of winter sun, and signed the ledger without looking at the margins.  At the concert, footlights warmed the margins, the chorus read what the censors had missed, and the public relay carried the pirate broadcast through every kitchen.  By morning, children were asking what happened after once, and Nera knew the dust had found its listener.
