On the lavender bluff under aurora, Mara Venn carried her grandmother's tin compass ring in a coat sewn with obsolete train schedules.  She was a luminous skeptic, bright from orbital lamps, famous for exposing weather cults, yet she took the night assignment to learn why some people carry weather with them.  The Ministry called her grandmother's damp shawl faulty equipment, but Mara remembered rain gathering in those sleeves after forest polls.  In that season, while forests hold elections, skyports close because ballot ions overwhelm instruments during solar storms.  Registrars gather on open ground and count by sensing through taste changes, with old tooth implants turning civic ions into salt, violets, iron, and bread.  Below her, the lavender rows recalled wild hills; above her, auroral green moved over antenna pines and the ribs of retired weather towers.  The scene was terrestrially cosmic, with mud perfume and solar fire braided together, and the futuristic nostalgia made Mara distrust her ache.  At the count stone, six registrars waited, each carrying a private weather.  Old Sena wore cinnamon fog; an orchard boy beaded frost on his cuffs; Aunt Vale brought a thin rain that fell only over her shoulders.  Official reports called such envelopes leakage from personal climate mesh, but leakage did not explain rain that followed Vale for forty years.  At the first bell, they opened their mouths and named their tastes while Mara logged salt, violet, copper, lavender, then a sudden tin bite.  The aurora strengthened, satellites went blind, and the official tablets showed blank squares where returns should have been.  Chief Registrar Hallow asked her to certify failure, trusting a luminous skeptic to kill a romantic story.  Instead she set the tin compass ring on the stone, keeping the promise that disputed ballots should always meet metal and tongue.  The fair prize had a dead needle and a blurred north, but condensation gathered along its groove in seven bright beads.  She tasted each bead and found one registrar in every flavor, sharpened by the forests voting below.  They disagreed at first: cinnamon drought, mint frost, warm bread under rain, and Mara's own lamplight mixed with old metal.  She asked the registrars to move one step with each flavor change, and their private weathers began to converge above the ring.  Fog folded into rain, frost softened into mist, and the dead needle trembled toward an unlit footpath to the seed vault.  There, under aurora light, Mara understood that they were not leaking hardware but carrying unfinished ballots.  After the Climate Peace, forests had needed witnesses who could leave their roots, so civic nurseries trained children by sensing through taste changes until implants, breath, and sweat could hold small climate permissions.  Only some kept that knack, and the forests renewed it by voting faint weather around them every election.  The ring, passing those flavors through tin, became an emergent compass, a direction assembled from many partial hungers rather than a needle obeying north.  Hallow wanted a number for the reservoir courts; Mara gave him the footpath, and the seven followed their tastes toward agreement.  Inside the seed vault they found glass seeds from orchards erased in the dim years, still releasing mineral ballots into the cold.  The instruments had declared those orchards extinct, so their votes were missing, and every summer since had been counted with a concealed absence.  That absence bent pressure toward people who remembered the lost trees, which was why the old seemed to carry weather more faithfully than satellites did.  Mara opened the cache, and the forests below changed their vote from reservoir concrete to replanting corridors near abandoned rail towns.  The returns arrived on every tongue as lavender, rain, bread, and clean tin sweetness, and the compass ring warmed in her luminous palm.  By dawn, the private showers had thinned, and even Sena's fog smelled less like an argument than like breakfast.  She filed ion counts, implant logs, and forest law, with one unofficial sentence: the dead had voted through the mouths of the living.  Years later, when children on the restored bluff asked why some people carry weather with them, the luminous skeptic let them taste the ring after rain.  Under a rebuilt aurora, with rails humming below the lavender, she answered that weather is sometimes memory looking for a place to converge.
