After the last resource war, Latch became a scrapyard settlement spanning a cracked wasteland, its streets made of tank treads, doors, bedframes, and the ribs of weather balloons. 
At dusk the place held a wild hush, as if every torn turbine, feral dog, and child under tin listened for a country that had stopped answering. 
Mara kept a stall there, writing labels for objects whose owners had vanished, and her gift was intuitively descriptive: she could call a dent the thumbprint of a man who would not let go. 
Such sentences made widows laugh, then weep, then stand straighter, because a lost spoon or boot came near enough to be held in the mind. 
Her one stubborn motive was to translate distance into closeness, not by promising return, but by making far things answer to exact names. 
The war had scattered people among dry wells, lithium pits, and fenced orchards, and the voice mast above Latch had once carried their descriptions home. 
Now the mast was silent, and the council meant to strip it for copper before the next dust season. 
Mara's mother had serviced its northern relay, and had sent back only an autumn leaf preserved in wax before the armistice closed every road. 
The leaf hung from Mara's throat on a wire, amber and vein mapped, still carrying the faint orchard smell of rain over sawdust. 
Her mother had said, Keep the leaf until you need a color that knows you. 
Bram, a bloodhound retired from finding bodies, slept beneath Mara's bench with one ear twitching toward any spade that struck stone. 
He had found too many missing people after the last resource war, and he now refused the command that had once made him famous. 
Instead he tracked softer losses: a child's mitten, a stolen kettle, the place where a family had eaten before leaving. 
The old signal engineers had built the mast around tinted revelations, because they believed no truth arrived without the color of the need that sought it. 
Blue glass showed routes and repairs, red glass showed graves and betrayals, and amber showed what the living still hoped to recover. 
The council called those lenses superstition, but Mara had seen blue letters rise from a cracked gauge when the moon hit it just so. 
On the morning the stripping crew arrived, Bram woke, pressed his nose to the wax leaf, and pulled Mara toward a collapsed ambulance. 
Under its rusted cot lay a phonograph cylinder sealed in lard tins, wrapped in a scarf that smelled of the same orchard. 
Mara began by reading secret texts in the grooves of a phonograph cylinder, using a blunt needle and a colored lamp to raise the buried letters. 
The texts were not sound; they were hair thin engravings that jumped into focus only when a tint crossed them, each color surrendering one uneasy sentence. 
Through blue windshield glass she found the mast's broken circuit and the names of camps still listening beyond the salt flats. 
Through a red tail lens she found her mother's warning that copper taken in haste would silence those camps forever. 
The stripping crew paused, ashamed of their ropes, because even hard men in Latch knew what forever had cost. 
Bram did not move toward the red glass; he lay with his muzzle on Mara's foot, as if refusing to search graves again. 
Then Mara held the wax leaf over the lamp, and the amber text opened with her mother's last instruction. 
Riddle me this, daughter: what can cross a cracked country without feet and make one room of two horizons? 
Latch had a custom from the war: to open a sealed relay, the operator must riddle, naming a far thing so exactly that its echo could be found. 
The crew shouted easy passwords, home, water, mother, peace, but the cylinder's grooves stayed dark under each word. 
Mara sat inside the wild hush and understood that an answer had to be a description, not a claim. 
She thought of scattered camps, of her mother's orchard under rain, of Bram choosing a mitten over a grave, and of a voice arriving before a body ever could. 
So she riddled the mast with the truest nearness she knew: a leaf's amber veins, sawdust rain, a tired dog breathing, and listeners leaning into one another's names. 
The cylinder turned, the mast drew breath, and instead of thunder it released a wild hush threaded with distant coughs, kettle clicks, and people saying, I am here. 
Bram slept beneath the mast, and the wax leaf kept its amber light.
