After the last print newspaper was folded away, the coast stopped believing in mornings.  Its final page had promised a sunrise, then joined the presses in the museum.  Afterward, weather arrived as a flawless strip on every wall, bright, brief, and impossible to question.  People woke without smelling ink or finding whose sorrow had been set beside the tide tables.  At the emerald lighthouse under stars, the Laughing storm herald kept an otherworldly routine.  Each night he polished the glass lantern wick, laid out empty type forms, and listened beneath the horizon.  The wick had never burned; it was clear glass drawn from sand struck by the first lightning.  When starlight entered one end, trapped rays woke shadows lodged in the emerald walls.  He called the shadows ancestors; the town called them dirt in expensive green stone.  His grandfather had worn the same oilskin and laughed from the gallery before every gale.  The first time the boy predicted a flood, he was mocking a councilor's dry shoes.  By supper, water climbed the council steps and carried the shoes away like boats.  After that, his notices were accidentally prophetic, and his laughter became a courtesy before disaster.  He softened each warning with a joke, because fear made listeners cruel to the messenger.  He learned that a joke can be a door, and that he had been living outside it.  What he wanted was larger than warning boats or keeping an obsolete title that amused children.  He meant to shape a new dawn using ancient shadows, because clean light had made everyone weightless.  The old papers, for all their lies, had given grief an edge and joy a readable face.  Without shadows, the town accepted each official morning as if it had never lost anything.  On the fifteenth winter after the press closed, a storm approached without any name in the feeds.  The wall strips still showed blue sky, but gulls flew inland with salt foam on their backs.  The herald climbed the stairs with the glass lantern wick tucked under his coat.  He was laughing, but the sound had rain in it, and he no longer hid either.  Outside, the horizon rose like a shoulder, shutting out one star after another.  Inside, ancient shadows gathered: hands setting type, eclipses over reed boats, a child's profile bent above a candle.  They were not ghosts, only impressions of light blocked long ago, yet they leaned toward him.  The otherworldly routine had always been a kind of asking; tonight it demanded an answer.  He set the wick in its cradle, aligned its tip with the brightest star, and opened the shutters.  Through total internal reflection, the glass held every entering ray, refusing the waste of an ordinary beacon.  The trapped starlight folded through emerald, gathering shadows without flattening them into print.  He understood that his accidental prophecies had not been mistakes; the routine had been listening for the angle.  To steer the unnamed storm, he had to choose where stored darkness entered morning.  His hands shook, because choosing meant confessing that every joke had also been a rehearsal.  Below, the town slept under blue wall light, trusting the smooth dawn purchased for them.  He laughed once, not at them, but with the bleak delight of someone who found the door.  Then he tilted the glass lantern wick by the width of a fingernail.  The beam did not flare outward; it slid inward, turning until the storm's black belly filled with patterned night.  Through total internal reflection, he steered that burden upward along the curve ancestors kept in shadow.  The storm broke over the lighthouse, and its rain fell in shapes: doorways, margins, handwritten names, fish ribs.  Where drops struck wall strips, purchased blue faltered and admitted a bruise of violet.  People woke to mornings with depth again, and some wept because loss had returned with texture.  Others stood in doorways, smiling uneasily at shadows they could not delete.  The herald watched from the gallery, soaked, exhausted, and full of shadowed elation.  He had not restored the papers, and he had not made anyone remember correctly.  He had given the new dawn an older grammar, so gladness could cast a shape and grief could answer it.
