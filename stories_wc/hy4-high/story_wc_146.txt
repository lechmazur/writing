They had nine nights before the collapse, and under the ancient archway a wild hush lay over Marrow Vale.  The notices in the lowland stations called the return a ghost town revival, all fresh paint and bargain rents.  Mara arrived with a deed to her grandmother's bakery, meaning to sell before the streets became valuable.  The window was boarded, the oven cold, and the square beyond it empty as a held breath.  Only the cryptic bell tuner waited there, infinitely patient with dust, delays, and every skeptical face.  He carried no ladder, no ropes, no ledger of wages, only an ebony tuning fork blacker than soot.  When Mara asked what he wanted, he touched the fork to a cracked cobble and listened.  He said he had come to restore shared balance, not to save property.  The surveyors laughed because their instruments had already measured the keystone's sag and named the night of failure.  The old people did not laugh, since they remembered that every house in Marrow Vale had been raised to a walking cadence.  The cobbles were tuned bars, the walls their resonators, and the bell above the archway their common tongue.  He asked them to rebuild via the rhythm of footsteps on cobblestones.  No hammer would be swung until the street found its chord.  The agent for the revival company sent a wagon of lime and tile straight across the square.  Its iron wheels struck four hard beats where the old millers had always left a soft gap.  A lintel split, plaster sifted down, and the wild hush tightened around the ancient archway.  The tuner did not scold the driver.  He set the ebony tuning fork against the sill, waited until the false ringing died, and marked the gap with chalk.  Mara told him she had come to sell a ruin, not dance on it.  He nodded as if she had confessed hunger, then gave her the fork and pointed toward the bakery.  The wood was warm, and through it she heard a small exact pulse beneath her feet.  Her grandmother had walked three soft steps and one sharp one before every dawn bake, carrying yeast to the widow on Bell Lane.  Mara remembered the sound from childhood, not as music but as the signal that bread was coming.  She tried the pattern, felt foolish, and heard a shutter answer high above her.  After that, the tuner assigned no one a pace belonging to someone else.  He made each returning family search its own memory, because shared balance required difference held in time.  The smith found a dragging step, the lamplighter two quick taps, the schoolchildren a skipping figure too intricate for adults.  At night, footfalls without owners crossed the empty lanes and paused wherever a living walker missed a beat.  By the seventh night, dust had begun to rise from mortar joints and settle back as clean gray lines.  By the eighth, the bell above the archway hummed when no hand touched it.  Mara still meant to leave after the sale, but she kept the deed folded inside her sleeve instead of showing it.  On the last evening before the collapse, rain came hard from the northern pass.  The surveyors fled with their instruments, the agent with his contracts, and the keystone opened a hairline crack.  The tuner stood beneath the ancient archway, holding the ebony tuning fork and saying only one word: now.  Mara stepped first, and the others entered where their remembered routes required them, not where pride would have placed them.  Footsteps on cobblestones gathered into a rhythm broad enough for shopkeepers, children, masons, widows, and the unseen dead.  The ebony tuning fork shivered, the bell found its tongue though no clapper hung there, and the arch drew level.  Cracks closed, roof beams straightened, and the bakery door unstuck itself with a modest click.  Mara understood then why he had been cryptic.  No one could be given another person's pace as an order; each had to choose to carry it.  She also understood that his patience was not softness but a long refusal to replace anyone.  When the measured hour passed, the town did not cheer, because noise would have broken what it had earned.  The rain thinned, the silent bell kept humming, and the wild hush changed from warning into shelter.  Mara unfolded the deed, laid it on the warm threshold, and walked back to take her place in the next circuit.
