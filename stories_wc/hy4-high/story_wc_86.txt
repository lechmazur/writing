A furrowed hush lay over the storm-worn windblown parapet in mist, as if grief had plowed the stones and then ordered them to listen. 
Only the central seven slabs were neutral territory, scrubbed by rain from both flags and claimed by neither army. 
Mara Vell, prisoner exchange negotiator, stood on the seam with her gloves folded and her breathing held small. 
The guards called her uncommonly thoughtful, never guessing how many funerals she had rehearsed before sunrise. 
That thoughtfulness had made her walk the parapet twice, testing slick mortar, loose rope, and each captain's favorite lie. 
The armistice lived between apology and forgiveness, because the northern chancellor had confessed to the prison fire at dawn. 
The parents of the burned had not answered yet, so every regiment waited with loaded muskets and wet fingerprints. 
If forgiveness failed by moonrise, the valley would resume killing before anyone could countersign the lists. 
Mara had come to recycle mistakes into wisdom, not to polish her reputation or punish the side she once served. 
Nine years earlier, she had trusted two tower clocks during an exchange, and the clocks had disagreed by four minutes. 
Four minutes had been enough for frightened guards to fire, for six names to become graves, and for Mara to keep the desk handle. 
The antique drawer pull in her pocket was a brass crescent, notched with the eight phases of an old clerk's moon. 
It had opened the registry drawer where the dead prisoners' names were filed after she sent the wrong signal. 
She carried it as evidence, a little weight that reminded her hands to be slower than her fear. 
Both armies had refused bells this time, since each accused the other's tower of stealing honest time. 
Her method, synchronizing through moon phases rather than clocks, gave them a referee no colonel could bribe. 
For a month she had sent almanac leaves to both banks, each marked with the waning moon's agreed meridian. 
Tonight the mist erased the sky, but the almanac, the notched brass, and the tide beneath the culvert agreed. 
Two black lanterns crouched on the neutral slabs, one facing each gate, their cords tied to a single iron ring. 
When Mara chose the instant, she would untether the ring's center knot and let both lanterns fall outward together. 
A fall meant release, because each side had sworn that its prisoners would step forward only at its own lantern's drop. 
A premature drop meant ambush, and ambush meant that the narrow truce would die before the parents finished deciding. 
Captain Iven arrived first, with eleven southern captives roped loosely and a sergeant who stared at Mara's gloves. 
Across the mist came Assessor Rook, leading eleven northern captives and an old woman wrapped in a scorched shawl. 
The old woman was Elian Marr's mother, and her answer to the apology would decide whether forgiveness had any country. 
Mara bowed to neither flag, because on neutral territory even courtesy could be counted as an advance. 
She placed the antique drawer pull beside the ring, its brass crescent aligning with a chiseled lunar mark in the slab. 
Rook said, "The sky is blind, Negotiator, and so perhaps are your calculations." 
Mara did not defend herself; she turned the pull one notch until the crescent covered the mark's third groove. 
The motion was small, but Iven's sergeant loosened his sword when he saw the answering leaf tremble in Rook's hand. 
Mara noticed, because thoughtfulness had taught her that panic often entered by the least important hand. 
She asked Iven's sergeant to hold a cup of rainwater level, a task too humble for pride and too public for treachery. 
She poured one drop from her flask, then held the antique drawer pull over the ripples and read its crooked notch. 
When the third ripple died, at the instant printed on both almanac leaves, Mara reached for the center knot. 
The old woman made a sound like a door closing, and Iven's captives lifted their chins from the rain. 
Mara did not untether yet, because the old woman's shawl had snagged on a spur of the iron ring. 
A younger negotiator would have trusted the timetable; Mara stepped across no border and freed the cloth with two fingers. 
After three heartbeats, Mara chose to untether the knot, and both lanterns fell outward as both groups stepped onto the seam before forgiveness could be asked to do more than wait.
