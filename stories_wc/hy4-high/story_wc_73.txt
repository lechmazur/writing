Vera, a grim optimist, climbed the infinite staircase with chalk and a certainty that each tread would betray her.  She carried spare chalk because betrayal, properly measured, could be useful.  The staircase had no walls, no roof, and no horizon but another step; the natives called that boundless confinement.  At each landing, a brass stamp read Timeframe: None.  The register offered no date or hour for any lost chance, because the stairs digested duration into motion.  It listed Vera's purpose plainly: to capture the texture of a missed opportunity.  It did not name the refused door, for refusal erased doors from the register.  Under Timeframe: None, her only coordinates were roughness, grain, and the small cold beside an unturned handle.  The pair of dice, one chipped, lay in her left pocket, carrying the pressure of a lower landing.  A companion had rolled them to decide whether to take the narrow door that smelled of rain.  Vera had kept her hand closed; the door became a smooth tread under the general traffic.  The fair die remembered equal chances; the chipped die leaned toward a nicked corner and made every official equation limp.  Officials allowed no hammers, levers, or prayers; every alteration came through mathematical proofs, the only tools for recursion.  Their theorem named the staircase a perpetual motion engine in which each avoided choice supplied the energy for the next riser.  The next riser manufactured the same hesitation, so no loss could accumulate and no exit could be funded.  Vera had checked that theorem against cracks and the inward drag of her own chest.  She was balancedly daring: one foot tested the tread, one hand held the rail, and the remaining courage wagered the whole invariant.  A louder climber would have shouted for freedom; Vera wanted a limited rupture, wide enough for fingertips and paper.  She did not intend to recover the companion, reopen the door, or improve the staircase; those belonged to optimists with cleaner faces.  Her hope was narrower: a lost chance had a surface that could be copied before the engine polished it away.  The first lemma began with the nick in the chipped die, because a damaged cube does not divide possibility into perfect sixths.  She wrote its bias as a remainder, a stubborn fraction that could not be exchanged into upward motion.  The second lemma set that remainder inside official conservation like a stone in a shoe.  The third proved that any step holding such a fraction must halt the exchange or spend the surplus on a seam.  Since the theorem promised perpetual motion, halting was illegal, and the staircase had to choose a place to open.  Auditors in gray chalk masks warned that proof could not rupture a world.  Vera agreed, in the grave way of a grim optimist, and added that the world had been built from its proofs.  If the structure could not obey the conclusion, the structure would have to enact it.  That was the balancedly daring edge of her work: she risked collapse, but only along lines she had checked.  She set the pair of dice, one chipped, on the tread that had been the door.  The chipped die landed with its wound upward, and the fraction in her chalk found a body.  A seam appeared along the riser, not as light or air, but as a dark parting in the arithmetic of the step.  The rupture ran from the chipped corner to Vera's paper, a thin wire colder than any rail.  Through it she felt more stairs, endless and intimate, a boundless confinement pressing countless faces together.  The opening offered no escape; it offered texture, the only honest thing she had asked from it.  She laid the sheet across the seam and rubbed with the flat of the chipped die until graphite, dust, and cold rose into the fibers.  The print held the nap of an unturned handle, the grit of a held breath, and shallow warmth where another palm had been.  The seam closed, and the staircase resumed its perpetual motion with one tread no longer perfectly smooth.  The auditors filed a chalk complaint, then erased it because it relied on the disproved conservation rule.  Vera folded the rubbing between two slates, expecting theft by the staircase and preparing for it with spare chalk.  She did not smile; she took the next step, carrying proof that a missed opportunity leaves a roughness no engine can entirely eat.
