I kept the last watch among the towers of bone in a canyon of endless echoes, where every footstep returned with an older voice. I was a harmonic stone skimmer by trade, though my hands had gone stiff from listening longer than casting. Our work was to lay thin slates on old air and read the hum they made when the canyon answered. Caravans trusted that hum to choose a safe shelf before the echoes folded distance into traps. I had taught many apprentices, and I had buried more, through centuries of layered dust.
I was historically reverent, because the dead had left better instruments than pride. Still, reverence had weight, and some days it felt like another stone on my knees. My reason for staying was simple: to learn to navigate without moving. A skimmer who walked added new noise to old noise, and the first trail vanished under their courage. So I sat at the mouth of the high rib tower, letting thirst, age, and duty nail me to the sill.
The trouble beneath every lesson was a pulse left wandering, an unanswered beat from the first migration. It had no grave, no name, and no listener when it was sent, so it borrowed each later sound and lost its road. When it passed, ledges seemed near and chasms seemed level, and even careful people stepped toward their own echoes. I had heard it steal a water party, a wedding song, and the last call of my brother, who had gone to find it. After that, I refused to chase anything.
Under the sill, wrapped in a strip of grave linen, lay the stone arrowhead my brother had carried. It was small, gray, and chipped along one edge, with three nicks that apprentices mistook for damage. The old books said such a piece was not a weapon but a tally of directions, and that a skimmer must appraise it before any rescue. I had delayed that duty for years, because to appraise the arrowhead was to admit my brother had died carrying an unfinished map.
On the morning the ash birds returned, the canyon gave me no choice. They came as a flock in flight above the bone towers, black commas turning in the wind that the pulse disturbed. The old method required me to appraise the arrowhead through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight, never by striking stone or asking the echo to repeat itself. Echoes flattered the impatient by imitating them, but birds answered with their whole bodies or fell. I set the arrowhead on my palm without rising, and watched the flock unravel into a hooked line. The line bent left at the first nick, doubled back at the second, and broke into three trembling arrows at the third. That shape was the final path of my brother, but older, for the birds flew the same correction the first migrants had failed to hear. I understood then that the pulse had been wandering because every generation had tried to send it onward instead of giving it a place to stop. The old books had not said to chase the beat; they had said to house it.
With my thumb, I turned the stone arrowhead until its three nicks pointed where the flock had turned. Then I wedged it in the crack beneath the sill, where my brother once left a dark polish. I did not cast a slate. I did not stand. I only slowed my breath until the canyon began to borrow my stillness, as it had borrowed every other sound. The wandering beat entered the bone tower, found the old angles, and settled into them with a small, tired click. Above me, the ash birds stopped writing and became ordinary birds again, scattering toward seeds in the dust. The echoes thinned.
For the first time in living memory, a shout from the lower shelf arrived as one shout, not as a crowd of dead imitations. A lost salt caravan had been feeling its way along that shelf, and the boy leading it heard me call the safe turn before he stepped into the drop. What I felt was not triumph, only weathered resolve, the same patience that had kept the records accurate and the living possible. At dusk the boy asked how far I had traveled to find his path. I showed him the arrowhead and answered, nowhere. Then I listened for the next traveler, content that the road would come to my stillness.
