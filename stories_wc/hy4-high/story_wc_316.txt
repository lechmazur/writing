The floating rock dojo drifts above the capital, its granite floor wet with cloud and old incense. 
Below, while history rewrites itself, avenues change flags, ancestors change names, and treaties become rumors before lunch. 
The court has brought its hardest clone custody battle to this neutral stone because old records cannot stay loyal. 
A slate above the altar reads Tone: None and every official face obeys it, because feeling can be quoted by the next version of the law. 
Sera Vale has come as an ego death survivor, licensed to witness identities that the morning may erase. 
Once, in a white bell temple, she let the self she had defended for forty years dissolve into a clear flame. 
She survived, but grief, appetite, and affection went away like servants dismissed from an empty house. 
She takes this assignment to defeat emotional numbness before it becomes the only citizen inside her. 
Her attention slices through pretense; it cannot be flattered, threatened, or persuaded to look away. 
On a lacquer table rests a brain in a jar labeled duplicate, fed by silver threads and a patient clock. 
It is a cortical witness grown from Iri, the seven year old clone whom two adults claim as son, heir, and evidence. 
In the antechamber, Iri sits with a wooden practice sword across his knees, too polite to tremble. 
General Vey says the boy must continue her dead brother's line, and her medals flash each time the past changes. 
Toma, the baker who warmed Iri's first bottles, says only that the child knows where the cinnamon is kept. 
The boy himself has been ruled too young, too copied, and too rewritten to testify. 
The brain, however, carries the minutes of his life before each new history edits the room around him. 
To vivify that witness, the dojo requires paired claimants and a form older than any court. 
Everything here works through complementary opposites: the rock rises because black stone pulls down and white stone yearns up. 
A bell sounds once, and Vey and Toma step onto opposite halves of the circular mat. 
Vey bows while Toma rises; Vey advances while Toma yields; Vey clenches her fist while Toma opens both hands. 
Their breath enters the silver threads, one hot with ambition, one warm with bread flour and fear. 
Sera watches the jar fog, then clear, then fog again as the duplicate brain tries to dream. 
Because her mind has no throne to protect, she hears what the claimants do not say. 
Vey does not want a child; she wants a uniform that history can never confiscate. 
Toma wants Iri, but he also fears loving a copy who may be declared imaginary by supper. 
Neither person is clean, and the cleanest thing in the room is the label on the glass. 
Duplicate, it says, as if one life were the canceled draft of another. 
Sera feels nothing at the insult, and that nothing frightens her more than the rewriting capital. 
She steps between the claimants and breaks the pattern by taking both roles inside her emptied self. 
With one palm she accepts the hot current of possession; with the other she gives back the cool current of release. 
The opposite currents meet in the jar, and the brain opens an eye that is not an eye. 
A child's voice fills the dojo, thin as steam, asking why everyone keeps calling him a second version. 
The judges lean forward, but their faces remain arranged by the order of Tone: None. 
Vey answers with lineage, Toma with recipes, and both answers make the jar cloud with loneliness. 
Sera understands that the true custody question is not who owns Iri, but who can bear being changed by him. 
She puts her palm on the glass and admits, without drama, that she wants to feel that change. 
The admission is small, but in a woman who outlived her ego it has the weight of a first step. 
The brain's eye turns toward her, and warmth rises through her chest, inconvenient and therefore real. 
She tells the court that a duplicate is only a life seen through a coward's ledger. 
The judges rule that Iri may choose his own guardian, since no rewritten history can make a person property. 
Someone scratches None away from the slate, leaving Tone: unfinished and the room free to breathe. 
Iri walks in from the antechamber, carrying his wooden sword, and chooses Toma to walk home beside him. 
He also asks that Vey visit on Sundays, not as an owner, but as a woman who must learn a child's name. 
Vey removes her medals, because even ambition can be embarrassed by a seven year old's mercy. 
Inside the dojo, the jar's small voice fades, but Sera keeps the warmth, imperfect and awake.
