Ione had been the corn maze architect's lost child for twenty one years, long enough for search parties to become a proverb.  After the butterfly effect, even proverbs needed permits, because a careless sentence could tilt rain toward another county.  On the drifting prism terrace, where her father had built one last maze in colored glass, the world settled into a fractured hush.  The terrace moved a finger's width above the old cornfield, following warm air, and each prism split the silence into thin, incompatible colors.  Ione came to discover why some thresholds feel sacred, though she said only that she had come for her father's chair.  She was featherly determined, narrow as a seed stem, and no official could make her wait beyond the permitted distance.  The official pointed to a row of white stones that marked the necessary distances between visitors, doors, memories, and weather.  Those distances had kept the settlements from unraveling since one woman's sneeze once delayed a train and drowned a coastal town in moths.  Her father, Oren, had designed corn mazes as arguments against hurry, bending paths so strangers almost met but never brushed sleeves.  When the first causal storm crossed his fields, he opened a green gate to look for Ione, and the maze folded her out of every record.  She returned each autumn with a different name, but this year Oren was dying, and the terrace had stopped pretending to be still.  Under a bench of pale oak lay the pilot logbook weather code, a stiff folio of cloud signs, altitude marks, and departure stamps.  Oren had trained weather pilots to read it before they flew over the maze, because even a small wind could multiply a choice.  The last page listed only a circle, a black square, and three figures that looked like a child's counting error.  Beside them Oren had written that the wood, not the sky, should be asked.  So Ione found the missing intervals by analyzing the grain in wooden furniture, reading each chair as a patient instrument.  The oak bench held a tight winter at its center, then a widening that matched the logbook circle, because drought had once paused the terrace there.  The black square appeared in the dark knot of Oren's chair, where he had sat through the years she was missing.  Three narrow lines in a cedar table gave the figures their order, not numbers but widths of hesitation before a door.  Ione understood then that her father had hidden the distances themselves in the furniture, so no gust could carry them away.  A caretaker brought tea and would not set the cup within her reach, honoring the white stones with a sad, practiced hand.  Ione did not mind, because the space between them let her remember Oren without forgiving him all at once.  When the prisms chimed once, the colored paths shifted, and the glass maze opened a threshold no wider than a held breath.  Beyond it, in a room made of refracted afternoon, Oren lay under a blanket the gray of cancelled flights.  He looked smaller than the stories, but his eyes sharpened at the pilot logbook weather code in her hands.  He said that she had come the long way, and the words arrived broken by glass, each syllable landing at a different distance.  Ione wanted to cross to him, but the white stones ended at the sill, and after that no rule could measure the necessary distances.  She had to intuit the last distance, to choose a gap that would protect him from the storm her nearness might summon.  The fractured hush deepened until she could hear dust deciding which color to enter.  She set the folio on the floor, placed her palm over the cedar lines, and waited for the wind to finish asking.  Only when the prism light stopped trembling did she move one step short of the sill, leaving room for her childhood to remain lost.  Oren smiled at that exact refusal, and the lines around his mouth repeated the bench's widening.  He told her that a sacred threshold was never a border against love, but the first place love learned the weight of consequence.  He had opened the green gate too quickly once, and the world had answered with twenty one years of necessary distances.  Distance held two lives apart without making either a ghost.  She slid Oren's chair to the measured gap and stayed beyond his reach.  The terrace drifted, and Ione felt the threshold bless the space she did not cross.
