Beyond the murmur of lost languages, when even lullabies had thinned into weather, the crystalline orchard perched at the edge of a shimmering crater kept its glass fruit like unsent letters.  Old tongues still moved beneath the stones, so settlers distrusted spoken claims and read knots instead.  Between glass trunks they strung webs of kinship, cords recording births, borrowed meals, dangerous favors, reconciliations, and the dead who kept advising the living.  At the center stood the glimmering obsidian arbor, a black bower cut from the crater's first cooling and dusted with mineral light.  No one owned it; each year someone chose a new grief and was allowed to dedicate a strand to the communal weave.  Nalen, a marsh-reed sculptor from the drowned delta, arrived with hollow stalks, three scars across his palm, and no one to vouch for him.  He was boldly sympathetic, unable to pass a stranger's sorrow without stopping, even when stopping cost him a meal or a safe bed.  The orchard's old phrase for belonging was to graft oneself onto community, and Nalen had repeated it all winter beyond the north wall.  Here no council stamped a name; the method was a shared search for an elusive street artist's tag, and the search bound seeker to sought.  The artist, called Latch because no one had seen her face, had once marked the drowned streets below with a loop and two short slashes.  After the flood, refugees carried her marks upward by memory, and fresh ones appeared on crystal bark, cistern lips, and faint salt paths.  Each tag showed where two lives had secretly held, so finding one meant admitting that no orchard life had ever been solitary.  Latch revealed connections and hid each revelation from pride.  On the morning Nalen petitioned, the glimmering obsidian arbor was hung with blank white cord, and Yara the bell-keeper handed him a chalk satchel.  Find the next mark before the crater light reaches full glare, she said, and bring back every story that points toward it.  Because a tag was believed to answer all previous tags, no one could hunt one alone; a solo clue was only a rumor until several mouths made it a path.  If Nalen wanted a knot, he would have to listen and be corrected by listeners, harder than any commission he had refused.  He accepted, though his hands itched to solve the puzzle with reeds and silence.  He failed when a silver flicker proved to be a moth under crystal peel.  The children laughed, not cruelly, and their laughter drew him to Mora, the salt-baker, who hummed a lament whose nouns had vanished.  He meant to ask about the tag, but asked instead about the emptiness beside her oven, and Mora told him about a son adopted by beekeepers after the flood.  While she spoke, Nalen bent a wet reed to the cadence of her breathing, then gave it to her without explaining why.  Yara called it useless tenderness; Mora said he had heard the shape before he could see the mark.  That rebuke changed the search, and by noon the party had grown to include children, grinders, a widow translating bird calls, and two brothers sharing one coat.  Every person added a fragment: a borrowed cup, a shared funeral, a joke no child could answer, a rescue no one had confessed.  Nalen made small reed bends, each a listening rib, and set them in the blank cord circling the arbor until the strands held many gestures at once.  By degrees his sculpture stopped resembling private skill and began resembling a net of hushed breaths.  At midday the orchard's light turned to a luminescent ache, bright enough to hurt and too tender to resist.  In that shining sorrow Nalen understood that Latch's tags were not bright at all; they were absences cut into brightness.  A sign lived where light was interrupted, so only a lattice built from shared memories could frame one.  He asked everyone to stand where their story had touched another, and no one mocked the request because it sounded like the oldest rule.  They stood among the glass trunks, and the listening ribs threw a lace of dark lines across the glimmering obsidian arbor.  At the keystone the tag emerged, framed by all their shadows, and Nalen knew the mark had found a web.  At dusk, the elders asked him to dedicate the arbor as Shelter for Borrowed Voices.  He tied reeds to the oldest knot, not as a signature but as an answer.  He felt that aching light as welcome.
