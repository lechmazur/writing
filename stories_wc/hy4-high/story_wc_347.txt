Nella was the reluctant caretaker of a cursed orchard above a cliffside labyrinth patrolled by mechanical birds, and she kept the job because the last three people who tried to resign still walked below, turning at every wrong corner. 
The curse had taken the orchard's fruit, then names from anyone touching bark with bare skin, so her mother forgot Nella by degrees and called her the quiet tool at the end. 
Nella wore gloves, refused to prune, and told herself she stayed to guide lost travelers to safety, never because the orchard needed her. 
Her mother's rule was blunt: finish before the night frost forms, because afterward stone glazed and birds stopped reading soft marks. 
Nella was offhandedly exact about distances, angles, and minutes, the way other people were careless about breathing. 
She could say, without looking up, that the west gate lay ninety seven steps past a cracked bell and that a gull cry lasted two heartbeats less than a hinge squeal. 
Exactness gave grief edges, which was the only comfort she allowed herself. 
The other inheritance was stranger: near dust, pollen, or wet pitch, the orchard's shadows leaned until they pressed shapes before any branch or hand arrived. 
Her mother had used the same method for leaving impressions without touching, because a true handprint on maze brass woke the birds and marked the maker as prey. 
The birds had been built to read dents in dust, arrows of weight leading pilgrims from the cliff, but their orders had thinned into loops and hunting habits. 
At dusk on the first cold evening, a hawk sized bird struck the outermost pear trunk, shed rust, and dropped a small nut beside Nella's boot. 
It was six sided, thumbnail wide, and threaded with the patient precision of something made to hold a turning against weather. 
Before she could kick it into the sea, a horn sounded from the lower maze, three weak notes, a pause, then one held too long. 
Nella knew from the spacing that five travelers had missed the ferry turn and were being herded toward the Drop Stair by two birds. 
She also knew, with a sour jerk of the heart, that she had perhaps forty minutes before frost would glaze the stones. 
That was when she remembered the cedar chest under her bed, packed for leaving and never opened because it smelled of her mother's gloves. 
Inside, beneath empty seed packets, lay a forgotten blueprint on six oiled skins, a plan the orchard had erased from every head that tried to describe it. 
It was no treasure map but a mercy drawing: pressure arrows, bird circuits, cliff walls, and a central signal wheel whose spindle required one small nut. 
The nut was therefore not rubbish, and because the blueprint was folded around a scrap marked for Nella, she understood that her mother had left the repair beyond the curse's reach. 
She carried skins and nut to the turning court, moving with clumsy haste past apple trees that creaked like wet chairs. 
At the court, six copper vanes lay where old caretakers had stacked them, edges green, slots mismatched, obedience long expired. 
She began to assemble the signal by leaving impressions without touching, nudging each vane with a branch shadow until it settled into damp grit. 
She set the small nut on the spindle by breathing across a groove, and it turned once, twice, then held the angle, offhandedly exact despite her shaking hands. 
Wind poured through the vanes, and the first bird jerked, listened with brass feet, and changed direction so sharply that two rusty feathers fell. 
Nella fed the court a chain of dents, not grand symbols but blunt crooked arrows, and the birds carried those orders along the maze like gossip. 
From below came panicked boots, a child's cough, and the ragged relief of people who suddenly saw a clear line bending away from the cliff. 
There was graceless beauty in it: crooked trees bowed, rusted wings clattered, and five travelers reached the ferry gate a minute before frost found the stones. 
Nella did not forgive the orchard, but she unfastened her packed bag that night, and the small nut stayed on the spindle through winter.
