On the third morning after the procedure, Mara Venn found that her conscience had been replaced by an appointment card. 
The clinic specialized in clean slate seekers, and her file, before it was scrubbed, had been so dirty that the clerk wore gloves. 
Mara had once been a clean slate seeker with a dirty past, but after the procedure she could say that phrase as calmly as a weather report. 
Needles had removed grief, recognition, and the small civic organ called empathy; wanting remained, because the state still needed shoppers and voters. 
Wanting was enough to make her open the card, which instructed her to kindle empathy where none remains. 
In her coat pocket lay a baseball signed by a time traveler, soft as a bruise and impossible by several decades. 
Its signature read Tavi Arlen, 2189, returning passenger, and the ink smelled faintly of rain that had not fallen yet. 
The card told her to bring the ball below the old pressroom and ask for nothing that could be bought. 
She obeyed because Tavi had trusted a stranger with an errand, and because an errand was the only thing the procedure had left intact. 
The journey began at a laundromat drain, and she made it by reading between newspaper lines. 
The clinic newspaper looked blank between its printed paragraphs, but Mara's fingers remembered the old codes of leading, ink weight, and intentionally crooked letters. 
Before her memory was washed, she had edited such codes for the Ministry, hiding addresses for arrests in the charitable spaces where mercy should have been. 
She could not remember doing it, yet her hands read the hidden route: four drains west, the broken elevator, then the stairs used by dead printers. 
The descent was cold, wet, and lined with obsolete headlines pasted over limestone like warnings from a civilization that had enjoyed administering itself. 
By the time the tunnel opened, her lamp had died, which seemed considerate of it. 
The underground bioluminescent lake lay beneath the city in a vault of black stone, glowing with organisms that had fed for years on ink, tears, and whatever else prisoners could spare. 
It was beautiful in the way a clean blade is beautiful to someone who has seen the butcher's ledger. 
Blue light moved under the skin of the water, and pale fish drifted like deputies who had lost their badges. 
At the shore waited a delicately robust skiff folded from newspaper pages, waxed with cave resin, and narrow enough to make hope look embarrassing. 
It held her weight and the baseball without complaint, which was more than could be said for the institutions above. 
Across the glowing water, Tavi's signature heated in her palm and pressed one instruction into the front page: choose the names the columns omitted. 
That was when Mara understood the lake as a labyrinth of choices rather than a maze of stone. 
Every fork in the current brought a page nailed to a basalt post, each printed with a tidy decision and each hiding, between its lines, the person that decision erased. 
The visible choices were practical: preserve the archive, spare the informer, keep the quiet, return to the clinic with a certificate. 
By reading between newspaper lines, Mara found the counterweight under each one, a refused tenant, a starving printer, a child listed as transferred, a mother who had waited on the ministry steps. 
She moved the skiff by speaking those names aloud, and the current yielded only when she chose the route that cost her the cleanest story. 
At the first fork she surrendered the warm outlet that would have led back to her blank apartment. 
At the second she gave up a sealed copy of her procedure record, the paper that proved she owed nobody anything. 
At the third she read the phrase Editor Venn regrets an error, and the white space beneath it spelled Arlen. 
Mara remembered nothing, but her hands began to shake with the expertise of having written that apology for a family she had already reported. 
The baseball rolled against her wrist, and the signature darkened into a boy's handwriting: I came from the year your tidy columns end, so throw one thing differently. 
Only a time traveler would spend a paradox on a censor, and only a child from a murdered future would call that efficient. 
Mara understood then that Tavi had not come back to forgive her, because forgiveness was another kind of clean slate. 
She threw the ball at her reflection, where the water showed a woman with ink under her nails. 
Green fire opened under the skiff and sent the omitted names upward through a thousand drains. 
Empathy returned as an ugly ache, and Mara laughed at the indecent sparkle of it.
