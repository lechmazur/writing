At the coastal kayak launch, this was the hour when expectations collapse: the tide had refused the almanac, and the lantern crossing was canceled.  Mara Venn, still called the broken navigator, stacked paddles under the roof, humming a merrily melancholic tune Alec had left her.  The lighthouse beam shuddered through the wet dusk, giving the launch only flickering faith.  Fifteen years before, steering a supply boat by an astronomical compass, she had ignored Alec shouting for the utility poles their father trusted in fog.  Pride had made starlight seem holier than wood; the reef took Alec as he loaded children into the skiff.  Since then Mara had rented a locker at the launch so remorse could have an address.  Inside waited the astronomical compass, the accident report, and unsent apologies to Alec's daughter Lena.  On that coast people feared the backlash of regrets, the answering current that carried whatever the heart had refused to mend back toward the wound.  It arrived under the canceled festival as brown water slid seaward while a second pull clawed the light craft toward Widow's Teeth.  Ribbons, cups, and unballasted kayaks began to gravitate toward the reef with the patience of old accusations.  Captain Jonas double tied every painter, then saw the smallest green kayak gone.  Lena had taken Alec's fiddle case and one paper lantern to the buoy where the supply boat broke, because an ordinary cancellation had made her grief feel ordinary.  Mara felt the old answer rise, a neat apology with no body attached, and for once she despised its manners.  She wanted, after years of ceremonial sorrow, to align heart and action.  That was the only prayer with weight, and its weight was a paddle in her hands.  She unlocked the locker, took the astronomical compass, and did not ask it to absolve her.  Its stiff arm pointed toward Vega, but clouds made it one witness, not an oracle.  For the rest, she used the old method of navigating by utility poles, which Alec had learned from their father and she had mocked as telephone guesswork.  The poles climbed the dune road with crooked numbers and failing lamps, faint blinks holding the line that stars had stolen.  She backed the rescue kayak into the surge, keeping pole seven behind pole eight, the reciprocal of the channel her father had painted on cedar.  Jonas handed her a coiled line and a joke about coffee.  The first wave struck her cracked hip, and pain made the shore recede into a bright argument for turning back.  Regrets answered one another across the water: too proud, too late, too careful afterward, too bold before.  The backlash pulled hardest at her bow.  Mara did not argue with it; she counted blinking poles over her shoulder and made each stroke an amendment rather than an excuse.  By the time she reached the green kayak, Lena was sideways in the rip, one hand locked around the fiddle case and the lantern burning out against her sleeve.  Mara expected accusation in the girl's face and found the stunned fury of someone who had mistaken a private wound for a destination.  That face broke not the hip or the pride, but the sealed place where Mara had stored love that never moved.  She threw the coiled line, shouted Lena's name, and heard, under the wind, that tune in her own cracked voice.  Lena caught the line on the second throw, and the two kayaks swung together like punctuation at the end of a long sentence.  The current tried to claim them both, but Mara turned her bow until pole seven and pole eight overlapped behind her again.  That small alignment was not triumph; it was work, and work was what her remorse had lacked.  They came home by the utility lamps, Jonas wading out with a thermos and a laugh that kept breaking.  On the ramp, the astronomical compass lay open, its brass arm aimed at an unseen star and a channel anyone could follow.  Mara gave Lena the report and unfolded the first apology, not as a spell but as a question about what they should do next.  Lena, shivering, said they should fix the broken buoy before another child mistook grief for a harbor.  So they did, under a lighthouse beam that offered no certainty, only flickering faith enough for the next stroke.
