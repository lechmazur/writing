After luck becomes measurable, the garden maze at Wren Court is sentenced to gravel.  Brass indexes assign each citizen a fortune, and the maze's readings fall below its water bill.  Inspectors call any favored hedge rot or fraud.  Mara Vey, the withered vine dresser, has kept its hornbeam and old grape arbor for fifty years.  Her hands are tendrils, but she hears thirst through stone.  She wants to prove that gardens know their gardeners before dials replace the rest of us.  Her daughter Lio was dismissed when a meter outscored thirty years of grafting.  Mara is wisely pragmatic, so she does not curse the meters or chain herself to the gate.  She reads the ordinance and bargains for one public trial with gauges, witnesses, and the lottery box.  The maze wears a blooming withering look: roses through mildew, pears over black leaves, paths smelling of decay.  In the lottery box rests her collection of marbles, cracked from a century of tiny ceremonies and winter frosts.  Before readings, visitors drew marbles and believed luck had chosen their paths.  Mara sometimes loaded the box for grieving children, lonely widows, and apprentices needing the fountain before dusk.  That deception kept donations flowing for compost and paid Lio's wages, but gauges exposed every altered draw.  Council law calls a mended deception a lie confessed, its harm named, and the surviving ritual made useful.  Mara accepts the name, because she has no intention of sewing the old lie shut.  Her method is commemorating small victories elaborately, because despair is patient and pageantry is louder.  A rooted cutting won tin whistles; a saved bee won a painted arch; the first pea won a silk canopy.  Each celebration ended with a marble tapped against a trowel, and the crack was rubbed with green wax and dated.  Those cracks archive seasons when care earned a leaf, a dry bench, an unstung child.  On the last evening she tells me to polish each marble until no inspector can call the marks secret.  As I polish, she confesses every altered draw while hedges tighten in yellow light.  I expect shame to shrink her, but confession makes her stand straighter, like a vine tied to a new stake.  A mended deception, she says, is not innocence; it is a torn net knotted to hold bread.  At dawn, the garden maze fills with skeptics, gardeners, and inspectors carrying gauges.  Mara refuses any draw for herself and sets the polished, cracked marbles on an open cloth for all to see.  By the new rules, chance assigns each entrant a marble, a fortune, and one unmarked turn.  Four favored strangers enter first, following clear gravel and laughing at Mara beside the dial.  They meet thorns, a flooded stair, wasps, and a gate painted shut by overnight damp.  Their gauges fall, not from malice, but from a maze shaped by particular knees and habits.  Then Lio, three retired undergardeners, and I enter with low numbers and cracked marbles chosen by inspectors.  The paths welcome us: the north hedge opens for Lio's basket, the arbor leans away from my ankle, and grape leaves hide the wasp gap.  The gauges climb by small defensible increments as we avoid snags, find dry stones, and reach the fountain without haste.  Mara watches from outside, withering in her black shawl while the restored rose above her blooms absurdly pink against the frost.  She says the numbers show not magic but care stored in paths over time, which is knowing.  The inspectors accept the pattern and commute the sentence to maintenance under audit.  We mark the verdict with seed cakes, a ribbon for one repaired leak, and six cracked voices, because every saved thing is small.  Mara gives me the cracked collection, asking that gains be dated, lies confessed, and victories polished before display.  By winter she is buried beneath the arbor, and the maze keeps her shape in openings no stranger thinks to use.  In spring, children who weeded with me come home dry and crowned with pear blossoms despite low readings.  I polish the archive and mark one more modest, elaborate victory in a blooming withering world.
