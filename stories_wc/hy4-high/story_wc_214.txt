In the salt country, through the hush of an overlooked era, the shooting star tracking station kept its black dome above the flats with the primal elegance of bone, oil, and silence.  Ila was its cosmic janitor, a title that had outlived the ministry, and she swept meteor dust from the stairs, greased the shutters, and slept beneath the chart room.  She was spiritually curious, not like priests but like someone listening at a door before knocking.  After her wife Nara died with every rib locked against comfort, Ila had taken the lonely post to find strength in vulnerability.  The only private thing she carried was a square of yellow lantern glass from Nara's bedside lamp, chipped and warm even at midnight.  When held against the observatory lamp, it changed glare into flourishing stillness, the hush inside a seed, crowded with life and yet unmoving.  After mopping, she made a wordless devotion by tracing the path of falling stars across old charts, following soot lines from vanished keepers.  She asked for no messages and only tried to empathize with their brief exposure, like bare skin meeting cold air.  The station was built to do the opposite, catching streaks with mirrors, pins, and numbers for lowland collectors.  Its blue governor still burned a numeric mark into the vapor of a falling star, a trick meant to make the ore easy to find and impossible to waste.  A marginal note in the founder's handbook said the mark also cauterized the star's hidden water, leaving a clean bead of iron where a rough garden might have been.  No ministry had cared for ninety years, so the governor worked on by habit, stamping blank ledgers and wounding skies that no one would ever harvest.  Ila let it run because its ticking kept the roof gears from freezing and disciplined noise had once felt safer than grief.  On the night the Century Shower returned, the governor woke fully, raised its blue beam, and began to comb the sky for streaks.  Ila came with her mop bucket, meaning only to wipe the condensed frost from the lower lens, but the shower pressed against the dome like animals at a thin door.  Through the yellow lantern glass she saw one great ember falling outside the expected lines, slow, unnumbered, and soaked with the same flourishing stillness that gathered in seeds.  Its path bent toward the station's dry cistern, where Ila had scattered Nara's ashes and where nothing had opened for years.  She understood then that the old machine would pin the ember, sear its water away, and drop a dead coin into the place where she had asked the earth to be tender.  The obvious strength was to keep the station correct, to preserve the gears, the ledgers, and the hard little record of every flame.  The harder strength was to leave herself and the sky unguarded, to empathize with a body that could ripen only while breaking.  She climbed the iron ladder, slid the square of yellow lantern glass into the governor's beam, and watched the blue mark dissolve into a wide gold dusk.  With her mop handle she knocked loose the pin wheel, then set the shutter by tracing the path of falling stars across the smoked chart, not to command them but to keep company.  The governor stamped one blank line, shuddered, and was quiet, while the dome opened to a darkness so complete it seemed less like absence than shelter.  Ila stood in that shelter with her cracked glass in one hand and no defense in the other.  The great ember entered without a number, small as a coal and soft as breath, and passed through the open roof toward the cistern.  It made no heroic crash; it made the sound of a hand relaxing after a long grip.  By morning, the cistern smelled of rain, and fine green threads had risen around Nara's ashes in a circle of flourishing stillness.  Ila knelt there, spiritually curious still, and finally felt strength arrive not as armor but as an open hand.  No capital would praise or punish her, and that neglect, which had once seemed a wound, became part of the shelter.  She kept her post, sweeping cosmic dust and oiling the gears, but she left the cistern unswept, the blank ledger open, and the yellow lantern glass fitted loosely where the blue beam had been.
