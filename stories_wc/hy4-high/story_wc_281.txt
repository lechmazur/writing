In the weeks after the static shock, the city sealed the clinics, capped the return bins, and asked blue capsule users to pretend privacy had been restored.  The telepathy pill recall filled the news with batch numbers, apology coupons, and doctors promising the condition would fade.  It did not fade for Jun, who came home from Return Station Nine with her voice shut.  Mara was an affectionate skeptic, the sort who kissed Jun's forehead before checking the pulse meter beside her bed.  She had never believed a pill could carry thought, but she believed the tremor of Jun's hand.  Before the recall, Jun had taken them because her father Saul, speechless after a stroke, seemed full of sentences.  Mara had called the treatment expensive theater, then held her all night when Saul died wordless.  The static shock came when thousands surrendered bottles at once, and the company network tried closing every borrowed channel.  For one white second, Jun felt strangers grieving in her teeth, then her language slipped beyond reach.  Doctors called it residual overload and offered rest, forms, and a coupon for a quieter pill.  Jun refused the coupon by pressing Mara's knuckles hard against Jun's chest.  That refusal became the first signal Mara trusted more than any diagnosis.  Their light painting studio once paid rent by teaching tourists to write names with sparklers in long camera exposures.  Now black curtains covered the greenhouse glass, and the floor smelled of batteries, ozone, and wet clay.  Mara worked there because darkness made confusion honest and a beam could be followed backward.  Her motive was to illuminate hidden pathways from Jun's stranded body back into speech.  She did not want the old telepathy back; she wanted a map two people could hold.  Only one object returned from the station with Jun: a muscovite geode sliver no wider than a thumbnail.  Mara had given her the geode years ago, joking that even rocks needed good lighting.  The sliver had split off during the shock and held a pearly charge that would not ground.  When Mara taped it before a lens, a thin LED line burst into a fractally precise fan.  Each branch repeated smaller branches along its edge, exact as frost or the veins under Jun's wrist.  Mara laughed once, not at miracles, but because the world had made a useful accident.  She had to devise a way to ask Jun yes or no questions without words, through somatic markers.  They began with left palm heat for yes, right knee flutter for no, and a rib catch for almost.  Jun called these her weather, and Mara wrote each climate beside the time stamp.  The method was through somatic markers, stubborn bodily facts no committee could edit into an apology.  Night after night, Mara swept an LED wand across the black paper while Jun watched from the old wicker chair.  A branch would brighten, Jun's palm would bloom with heat, and Mara would mark the turn with blue chalk.  When a dead end appeared, the knee fluttered; when the path bent toward memory, the rib catch softened.  The images that developed were not portraits but braided routes, thin corridors of light around islands of darkness.  Jun touched one corridor with two fingers, and for the first time since the shock she said, 'Da'.  It was not a cure, only the first syllable of Dad, but Mara dropped the chalk and went to her.  Jun wept without shame, and in the dark their old private name for such bending, transparent courage returned: willowglass hope.  By winter, other recall survivors came to the studio with their own pockets full of useless mementos.  Mara never promised minds would meet; she offered darkness, a lens, a body, and patient attention.  One teenager found her brother's route when a red branch made her shoulders unclench; an old mason found his wife's name in a green fork.  Reporters wanted to call the work a resurrection of telepathy, and Mara corrected them with the tired courtesy of someone who had read too many forms.  She said the company had sold a shortcut, while the shock left a maze; all they were doing was lighting the walls.  Jun, steadier now, added that a maze with lights was still a place where people could find one another.
