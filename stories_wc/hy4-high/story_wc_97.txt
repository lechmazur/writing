The tidal arbor above the clouds had been built as a transfer station, where airships rested among iron branches while reservoirs rose and fell under the moon. 
By Mara Venn's last season there, most passengers had vanished, but the wistful station pianist still played the departure waltz for shadows crossing the glass platform. 
Her music carried steely longing, precise as a locked jaw, because every chord resisted the easy comfort of tears. 
On the upright piano sat a broken anniversary clock, a brass gift from Emil, stopped at 7:12 when the evacuation bridge folded into the cloud tide. 
Emil had been the station cartographer, and the clock had been meant to chime each year on the night they first danced under the leaves. 
After he disappeared, Mara kept its crooked hands as a metronome: duty at the quarter hour, grief dragging half a beat behind. 
The station rule let her grant refuge until the horizon invites night, when the upper roots iced shut and no skiff could dock. 
That phrase meant the last amber minute before sunset entered the clouds, and Mara had learned to distrust schedules written in amber. 
On the anniversary evening, a convoy of refugee skiffs rose below the arbor, their lanterns blinking toward a harbor whose compasses had begun to fail. 
The trouble had a name among pilots: flickering compasses, whose needle-light appeared and vanished whenever the salt reservoirs climbed through the branches. 
Worse, they no longer promised north; during high tide each needle swung toward the place its holder most wanted to recover. 
Pilots who followed such tender directions found only cloud canyons, while behind them the arbor gates closed with the patience of fate. 
Mara was strategically creative, not fanciful; she treated panic as a machine whose missing lever could be inferred from its noise. 
She opened the clock's cracked face and drew out the narrow spool Emil had hidden there, because he had never trusted maps that stayed still. 
The only surviving chart of the emergency gates ran through hidden sections in a black-and-white film reel, spliced between dull reels of branch inspections. 
In ordinary light the hidden frames showed nothing, but projected by the clock's strained spring they revealed gate numbers, lantern angles, and a stubborn line of music. 
The broken clock mattered now: a repaired one would have locked at twelve, but its wounded gears could be wound backward to make the hidden sections flicker. 
Mara understood then why Emil had coupled the projector to her piano keys: each note tripped a relay in the lantern grid above the docking roots. 
She could not stop the compasses from remembering, but she could give every trembling needle one shared memory, a black-white pulse brighter than private yearning. 
She projected the reel onto the ticket-hall screen, set the metronome hands of the clock to the first hidden frame, and began the departure waltz without announcing it. 
Above her, shutters clacked in the rhythm of her left hand; below, the lanterns answered frame by frame, and the fugitive compasses ceased hunting separate ghosts. 
One by one, the skiffs turned their prows toward the same sequence of opening leaves, while the station emitted spare chords that sounded almost like permission. 
The harbormaster shouted that the last gate had jammed, its hinge swollen by salt, and that only vibration from a full orchestral cadence could free it. 
There was no such cadence in the departure waltz, no instruction in the film, and no minute left before the horizon would invite night. 
Mara thought of Emil stepping onto the bridge at 7:12, of fate applauding once and then refusing to lift its hand again. 
Her reason for staying all these years gathered into one clean decision: to surprise fate with an unplanned encore. 
She played past the final bar, inventing a hard ascension that threw every relay wide, so the piano became bell, organ, drum, and iron branch at once. 
The glass platform shivered; the jammed gate loosened; the last skiff slid into the leaf hangar as the first blue edge of night touched the horizon. 
Only after the roots sealed did Mara let the reel run out, leaving the screen white and the hall full of people who had nowhere else to stand. 
A child asked whether the extra music had been part of the rescue plan, and Mara looked at the broken clock without softening her face. 
"No," she said, "it was for the one who should have come home; but the compasses held, and that was enough." 
The clock did not chime, yet both crooked hands shifted by one breath, as if the anniversary had been allowed continuation rather than forgiveness. 
Mara entered the names in the ledger, then left one line blank for the encore fate had not known how to schedule.
