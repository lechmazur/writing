On the twilight barge canal, where lamps were lit before the sun had finished leaving, Marek was known as an errant legend seeker, though only he knew how deliberately he wandered.  The antique pocket sundial in his coat, brass, split-hinged, and unsure of north, had belonged to his mother, whose death the city called a ferry accident.  The legend named its power hindsight clarity, a clean seeing that arrived only after a person made the irrevocable choice.  He had spent eleven years chasing such tales, not for wonder, but to understand the architecture of lies.  His mother's last note was thoughtfully clear: a lie is a building, and the missing room is where people died.  Because of that sentence, Marek distrusted maps, clocks, proclamations, and any grief too neatly furnished.  He had wanted a voice from the dead; each false shrine taught him to ask for floor plans instead.  Company records rode a rotting registry barge beneath the last peach light, and a storm had opened its hold that evening.  Canal workers called the pause before the dawn report the silence of morning radio, when the company armed its pulping pumps.  The dock permit said every ledger must be removed by the silence of morning radio, before water and cutters made pulp.  To Marek, pulp was the perfect end for evidence, so he came to salvage the boxes without help from clean cuffs.  He had survived by training instincts to notice rope tension, stale ink, and the pause before repeated lies.  Those instincts led him past the painted gangway on the diagram to a plank hidden under coal sacking.  A young watchman with a company cap told him the lower hold was empty.  Marek heard the pause before empty, smelled fresh ink below, and asked for the lamp instead of an argument.  The boy surrendered it with a grateful shrug, as if obeying a rescuer were easier than obeying the company.  Below deck, black water lifted crates against the barge ribs, and every lift sounded patient, almost kind.  Marek waded to a crate labeled ballast, heard paper answer his knuckle, and pried the new lock open.  Inside lay ledgers from the winter his mother died, their ruled pages smelling of river mold and altered receipts.  He did not take only the volume with her name; he passed crate after crate to the boy on the plank.  The work was slow, but no bells rang, no pistol cracked, and the canal kept its lamp-polished calm.  When the last box reached the dock, the night radio on the boy's belt muttered tide figures, then coughed toward silence.  Marek opened the antique pocket sundial on the stone; dock lamps and first light made its needle cast three shadows.  Only after the boxes were safe did hindsight clarity come, not as a vision, but as columns, crossings, and omissions.  The first shadow touched a column headed lost time, the second crossed repeated signatures, and the third stopped at a blank ferry manifest.  Under the blank, a carbon imprint said lock keepers, thirty one, transferred below by company order.  Marek saw the building at last: schedules for foundations, copied signatures for beams, an obituary for a roof, and that white square for the room where his mother and the others drowned.  He had expected hatred, but what came was a thoughtfully clear sorrow, almost level with the water.  The boy cried without noise, and Marek gave him the carbon page because a witness should not be protected from seeing.  When the radio fell silent, they carried the ledgers into the signal room, where the dawn operator waited with her hand on the switch.  The permit required salvage by that hush, and their obedience put the missing room on the operator's desk.  When the report resumed, she read names, numbers, and the carbon line instead of tide orders and obituaries.  Across the canal, bargemen lowered cups and let the old account collapse without a shout, making it a serene upheaval.  The water kept its small laps, the lamps burned down, and no one defended a lie missing its roof.  With the late mercy of hindsight, Marek understood that the sundial had never been meant for treasure.  Those years had trained him to make room for other names, then to see how those names vanished.  By sunrise he was still an errant legend seeker, but he stopped seeking legends and kept the record.
