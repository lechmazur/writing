After the breach of contract, Nera, the last quartz-eye mender, climbed into the lush orchard spanning the rafters of an abandoned cathedral, with her daughter's hair ribbon tied to her bag. 
She had come to bridge generational wisdom before the youngest children mistook all promises for locks and the oldest councilors mistook all questions for rot. 
Soil had gathered in the broken roof for a century, and roots had followed old hymns down the pillars until trees made a green floor above the saints. 
The founders had planted by deciduous vows, pledges meant to yellow, fall, feed the roots, and be argued into new shapes each spring. 
Every autumn the village composted last year's parchment under the saplings, then let children and grandparents write the next agreement in language both could bear. 
Three merchants and a frightened abbot ended that custom with a fixed contract declaring the original clauses eternal, and the orchard split every copy from margin to seal. 
Since then, fruit formed around unsaid arguments, hard green pearls that ripened only when someone recited a dead lawyer's exact wording. 
Through her quartz eyes, Nera saw the broken promise as a black seam running through the branches, a hairline fracture in patience. 
Her trade joined glass, bells, and obligations by the ticks they made when stressed. 
The council wanted solder, silver pins, and one neat signature that would make young people stop asking why old people feared a blank page. 
Nera had her own reason for taking the job, because her daughter Mara, endearingly feisty from her first stolen plum to her last argument with the council, had chalked that question on every door in town. 
Mara was gone now, carried off by winter fever, but the blue ribbon snapped in the dusty light as if approving a prank. 
Nera set her tools on the highest rafter, yet her first silver stitch stiffened a clause about tithes into a thorn and stabbed her thumb. 
The second stitch turned a promise of mutual aid into a wasp, which complained in a dark organ pipe until dusk. 
She wanted to quit, but Mara had once thrown pebbles at a locked schoolhouse for six days until the master opened it and taught her to read. 
So Nera chose to persist, not nobly, but because quitting meant explaining the wasp to the council. 
On her third crossing, a loosened pear struck the bag and dumped years of neglected pockets across the split contract. 
Out rolled grafting wax from Nera's grandmother, three apple pips saved by the first abbot, a bent fishhook, Mara's chalk slate, and a folded note in purple ink. 
The repair began by accident, using forgotten bag contents that no respectable mender would have entered in a ledger. 
Wax softened in sun and held the parchment loosely, apple pips wedged in the gap like punctuation with plans, and the fishhook caught the blue ribbon before it blew into the nave. 
Nera opened the note only because a child below shouted that purple ink was bleeding, and Mara had written, "Persist, but leave a knot loose," in a schoolroom hand. 
That was the first useful instruction anyone had heard since the breach, and it came from a dead girl's forgotten note. 
Instead of sealing the contract, Nera called grandparents up to the rafters to show which grafts could bear weight and which needed cutting away. 
She called the children next, because they alone could spell the new complaints that adults had renamed "weather". 
On Mara's chalk slate, an old mason drew the pruning marks her mother had used, while a barefoot girl wrote beside them, "Ask again before winter" in huge letters. 
Nobody agreed at first; a grandfather said questions were bad compost, and a boy said the old marks looked like bird scratches. 
Then the wax gave, the ribbon slipped one finger's width, and the apple pips sprouted thin green letters through the torn clause about eternity. 
Leaves fell through the rafters without permission, the black seam softened into mulch, and the trees accepted a promise that admitted it would change. 
By morning, pears ripened into ordinary sweetness, and the council found its neat signature covered by bees, which improved it considerably. 
The grandparents kept the grafting marks, the children kept the right to ask again, and Nera tied the ribbon back on her bag. 
The town called this accidental wisdom, since the nearest thing to a sermon was a pear dropping on a clerk's head. 
Each autumn, they argued, composted clauses, and renewed deciduous vows beneath the wisely missing roof.
