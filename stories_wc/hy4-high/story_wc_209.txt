Across the melting of winter's last snow, Liora Venn returned each morning to the room the town still called the amber pavilion in autumn.  The amber panes turned slush to October, and black leaves clung to the beams.  Outside, gutters ticked; inside, a cathedral window salvaged after a storm filled the east wall.  Liora Venn was a vivid mural artist known for processions so bright that walls sweated summer, but grief had made her quietly driven.  The council had hired her to cover a water stained wall with another blaze of saints and market fruit.  She accepted because in her satchel lay a collection of old postcards sent by her mother, Orla, and because her first reason for coming home was to understand why flowers bloom at night.  Orla had kept the moon garden behind the pavilion and had died the previous autumn without answering the question Liora had asked since childhood.  The question sounded botanical, but it also meant: Why did you work while I slept and leave the brightest hours empty?  The cards carried umbral tenderness: jokes about moths, sketches of white blossoms, and apologies folded small enough to hide inside postmarks.  Most showed places Orla never visited, including a desert greenhouse, a seaside observatory, and the amber pavilion under impossible yellow trees.  On their backs were smudges, pinholes along petal ribs, and dates from another winter's melting.  At first she took the marks for damage, because grief makes loss look like evidence.  Orla had restored cathedral glass and taught her daughter that hidden work was a foundation, not a lie.  So Liora brought the cards to the window each day before the bell ringers woke.  She learned to read them through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window, holding each card a finger's width behind the old repairs.  At noon the grooves disappeared, and under lamplight they became only scratches.  In the dawning hush, when snowmelt paused and no broom or foot disturbed the close, low sun raked across the buried lines.  The etched leaves threw shadows through the pinholes, and letters rose on the cards like remembered ink.  No lamp could do it, because the method needed a world not yet loud enough to shake the light.  The first message said, Plant pale things where the child waits for morning.  The second said, For the moth, not the sun, because hunger also keeps hours.  Others gave depths, distances from the pavilion steps, and names of bulbs Orla had tucked into the frozen beds.  By the third morning, the bare soil behind the pavilion showed green needles in a careful crescent.  She expected botany, and the notes gave it: night blooming flowers save water, invite moths, and offer white petals to moonlight.  Yet the glass kept delivering smaller sentences beneath the botany, asking her to reckon with a childhood simplified into resentment.  Her father had died just before dawn when Liora was seven, and after that the dawning hush had meant that another disappearance was coming.  Orla had not left her alone with that fear; she had sat in the garden, pruning by lantern while white buds loosened.  The final postcard, held against the faintest etched layer, said, For Liora, who feared the dawning hush after her father died.  Liora sat on the cold tiles and let anger change shape, not into cheap absolution, but into company.  Outside, the last snow slid from the roof in a soft collapse, exposing the crescent of shoots.  She began the mural that morning, but she did not begin with vermilion.  She laid a dark green ground, short strokes of amber, and pale flowers under glazes, so daylight would find them gradually.  Visitors later praised the vivid mural artist for restraint, not knowing how much color lay beneath glazes that kept their own hours.  By the next dawning hush, one cereus had opened beside the steps, white as a letter held up to a shaded lamp.  Liora understood that flowers bloom at night because darkness has hungers, witnesses, and mourners, and because some gifts open before daylight asks for them.  She set her brush down, and in that umbral tenderness let the morning arrive.
