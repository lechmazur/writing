After the egg hatches, the icebound atrium under starlight fills with a wild serenity that no stove could imitate. 
Snow claws the glass roof, yet benches of dormant seeds rest in unhurried ranks beneath a sky as sharp as broken salt. 
Mira Venn, heirloom seed saver of the last winter vault, waits beside the empty shell with her bare hands folded. 
The shell, no bigger than a bean and warm for twelve years in her mother's cold drawer, has opened at last. 
From it has emerged a pale moth whose wings set frost trembling in circles. 
The old ledger calls that motion vortex inheritance, because the helix of days passes from keeper to keeper like a seed passed from hand to hand. 
It is not wealth or prophecy; it is a duty to keep the world's seasons from unraveling into sameness. 
Every generation inherits one closed spiral, one egg timed to the stars, and one chance to set time breathing again. 
Mira's mother spent her last winter saving bean embryos from a thaw, then borrowed eleven minutes from spring and never returned them. 
Those minutes have lived in Mira as a debt that no catalog, funeral, or lullaby could name. 
Her public task is to save the vault, but her deeper motive is to discover the texture of borrowed time before it passes out of her hands. 
For that work she has brought only an oak acorn cap, ridged with the whorl of the first tree raised from the vault's oldest packet. 
The instruction in the ledger is brief: after the egg hatches, reopen by decreasing entropy, making the room less random than the winter that sealed the spiral. 
Mira is furiously tranquil, because grief has burned away panic and left a calm exact enough to count dust. 
She does not pray to the stars, because stars in this country are witnesses, not helpers, and helpers have always been made of hands. 
First she uncovers the seed drawers and lays the packets in order, oldest to newest, each square aligned to the black seam of the floor. 
Then she wipes every label, gathers loose frost into one bowl, and coaxes the moth onto the acorn cap's rim. 
Outside, the blizzard throws snow against the glass in frantic codes, while inside each object accepts a single place and a single name. 
This is how entropy falls here: not by force, but by patient refusal to let one more thing drift. 
When the last packet is square, Mira sets her palm over the cap and slows her breathing until the frost stops feathering her reflection. 
A pane cracks above her, and a blade of outside air enters, warm with the thaw that ruined her mother. 
The crack is trouble, because warmth lets everything wander, and the spiral tightens against her knees. 
The moth opens its wings, shedding seconds like pollen, and Mira feels the borrowed minutes in her ribs begin to loosen. 
She could seal the crack with resin, but resin would add another substance to manage; instead she gives the invading air a task. 
She tilts the cap so the current must pass through its cupped whorls before reaching the ordered packets. 
The whorls comb the air into a spiral, and disorder leaves as pattern, colder and quieter than before. 
That is the secret of her inheritance: a vortex is not opened by adding power, but by persuading chaos to remember a shape. 
With the room finally still, Mira lays the oak acorn cap at the center of the closed spiral and speaks the ledger word, reopen. 
The floor does not blaze; it darkens, as if starlight has found a well and is pouring itself down without a splash. 
Through the opening comes the eleven minutes her mother stole from spring, brushing Mira's palms before she can name them. 
Their texture is not silk or glass or water, but the nap of a seedcoat after thaw, cool grit yielding to hidden softness. 
She laughs once, softly, because the debt is tactile, and because wild serenity has never meant safety. 
Mira does not keep the minutes; she folds them around one dormant acorn, sets it in the cap, and lets the moth carry it through the spiral. 
Each wingbeat returns a fraction of order to the floor, and the crack above her knits with obedient frost. 
Outside, the storm goes on spending itself without entering; inside, generations wait in paper packets, holding futures that someone else agreed to borrow and return.
