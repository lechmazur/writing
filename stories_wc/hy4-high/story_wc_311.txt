After finding the old recordings, Mara returned each evening to the ancient observatory at dusk, when the sea was the color of wet slate and the dome's broken lenses gathered the last light.  She was the heartbroken reaper of that coast, though grief had made her punctual rather than cruel.  At that hour the dome echoes forgotten phrases no living mouth remembers before she ferries the dead away.  The recordings had taught her to listen, not for names, but for the pause after one life spent itself for another.  Their makers called that pause the echo of sacrifice, a residue neither soul nor memory, only the shape of giving.  Inside the west drawer lay an atlas page with sea monsters, folded so many times that the whales had creased into ribs.  The reapers had used it as a ledger: each beast stood for a fear meeting the dying at the shore.  Her work had one purpose, to give names to nameless fears, because unnamed terror followed the soul into the dark.  This page had one blank margin beside a coil of ink that might have been a serpent or a rope.  Mara had left it blank for three years, since the night Talen waded into the harbor to cut a child loose from that shape.  He had been a lamplighter, and their distant intimacy was two cups on a sill and footsteps that did not follow to the door.  When the tide took him, Reaper's law required her to bear his soul across the threshold without turning around.  She obeyed, and the obedience had been the only thing in her life that felt like betrayal.  Afterward the child grew, the pier was rebuilt, and Mara kept hearing Talen's unfinished sentence.  The old recordings said such sentences returned at dusk, hidden in the observatory's brass tubes and broken lenses.  Tonight she set the cracked cylinder on the spindle and lowered the needle, careful as if touching a feverish wrist.  A woman's voice unfolded first, thin with age, explaining how an echo of sacrifice must crystallize through scattered light to become a usable name.  The name would not bind the dead; it would give the living something finite to face.  Behind that instruction came a lower sound, not the woman's, but Talen's breath around words the sea had stolen.  The dome answered with its faint arithmetic: cold, cold, not the water, Mara, not the water.  She had always assumed the nameless fear was drowning, because that was what the harbor called every death by water.  His unfinished sentence said otherwise, and the blank coil on the atlas page tightened in her mind.  Mara climbed to the lens room, where sunset lay in shards across black glass.  She pinned the atlas page with sea monsters beneath the western prism and tilted three mirrors until their beams crossed.  Each mirror was scratched by centuries of star maps, so the light broke into freckles, not a blade.  As the freckles touched the blank margin, the cylinder repeated Talen's breath, and the ink coil shivered once.  Mara understood what he had feared: not the water, but leaving a life so quietly that no one could tell comfort from indifference.  In her work, she had mistaken silence for dignity, and had made him carry that mistake across the threshold.  Tears came then, but they were small, almost formal, and she did not wipe them before taking up the naming pen.  She could have written Talen, making the monster precious and her grief its servant.  Instead she wrote The Fear of Being Left Unread, and the words settled like a hand reaching for a cup.  At once the freckled beams began to crystallize into a pale salt star above the ink, small enough to fit under a fingernail.  The star did not speak; it simply made the coil finite, a fear with edges, and the dome stopped echoing.  Mara waited for Talen's voice, but what remained was the old woman's patient explanation and the household noises of cooling brass.  It was enough.  She placed the salt star in the ledger's clasp, set Talen's cup outside on the sill, and carried the named page down to the harbor.  Below, the newly dead were already gathering in the dusk, waiting for someone to teach them what to call the dark.
