On the whispering parapet after rain, the hour was scintillatingly somber, each drop catching firefly light as if grief had learned to glitter.  Warm air rose from the old drains during firefly season when fireflies traced those invisible currents above the stones.  Sela had come before dawn with a chipped bowl, bitter tea, and her mother's bamboo tea whisk, whose split prongs left a crescent in foam.  People called Sela the net-mender philosopher because she repaired storm nets by day and argued that hidden patterns were the moral grain of the world.  Her purpose was modest and stubborn: to appreciate hidden effort before the council sealed the drains and turned the service wall into a smooth memorial.  By decree, the whispering was a nuisance, the channels were empty, and only the long-dead architect deserved a brass name.  Sela knew better, because every spring tide brought silt someone cleared, every torn net was quietly spliced, and every cup she set out was empty by morning.  She did not intend rebellion; she intended tea, names, and one last look at work that had kept the lower quarter dry.  The hour was too grave for anger, and her mother had preferred demure audacity, so Sela knelt and whisked tea with courteous strokes.  Instead of lying flat, the foam drew a spiral toward a hairline seam beneath the coping, where a cluster of fireflies trembled.  Sela smiled at the familiar verdict, for hidden patterns were never decorations; they were instructions left by heat, water, hands, and need.  She touched the storm net overhead and found three new knots tied in her mother's style, placed where the wind would worry hardest.  Someone was still working below, and someone had heard the council carts coming with lime.  She set the bowl beside the seam, stopped to whistle the old knotting call, and heard the wall change the sound into an answering breath.  The reply came from a grate under moss, not from ghosts, and its rhythm said someone was pinned beneath the sluice gear.  Sela reached that grate by following moon paths, silver lines pooled in foot-polished hollows where generations of night workers had crossed after rain.  She could have called the watch, but the tide was shouldering the floodgate, and formal help would arrive after the lower lanes filled.  So she used the skill that earned her name: she cut a length from the damaged net, braided a line, and looped it through the grate's teeth.  One whistle meant slack, two meant hold, three meant pull, and someone below obeyed the old code.  The grate rose an inch, then a foot, and a silt-haired boy crawled out, dragging a rake and a crushed lantern.  His name was Eno, and he had been clearing the channel alone because his grandmother was ill and the council had called drain-keeping imaginary.  Sela gave him tea, not praise, since hidden effort deserved warmth before commentary.  Then they pulled together, Sela above and Eno below, until the jam of leaves, eelgrass, and ribbon burst free into the harbor.  The whispering parapet after rain altered its voice from a rush to small satisfactions, and fireflies scattered upward like a lifted net.  At dawn, the clerks arrived with lime, a plaque, and pleased faces.  They found tea bowls along the moon paths, each bowl frothed by the bamboo tea whisk, each foam crescent pointing to an opened drain.  With demure audacity, Sela had placed beside every bowl a knot, a rake tooth, a bit of wire, or a name on scrap netting.  When the chief clerk asked what this meant, she bowed and asked him to drink before he sealed any mouth that had saved his shoes.  Eno stood behind her, muddy and silent, while other drain-keepers emerged from stairways the plaque would have covered.  No one shouted; Sela counted the hidden patterns aloud, from polished hollows to spliced knots to warm breath guiding fireflies and tea foam alike.  The clerks copied the names into the ledger and postponed the sealing until the workers could advise them.  Only then did the wall grow quiet, not because its workers had vanished, but because at last no one needed to whisper.
