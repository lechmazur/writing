I was the last lore keeper of Lumen Arcology, which meant I cataloged departures no one had survived and lullabies everyone hummed. 
In our glass concourse, cleaning drones polished the unclaimed throne while commuters pretended not to remember kings. 
For ninety years, keeping that chair empty had been our most stubborn civic ritual. 
The Succession Engine announced that sunrise would end the custom and give the seat to any citizen bringing a validated ascent key. 
Three faction leaders had already leased coats and smiles from the image vendors. 
My grandmother left me one instruction in a paper atlas that smelled of pears and ozone: to find the stairway of ascension before anyone used it. 
Pressed between its tissue maps was a ruby flake no larger than a thumbnail, warm with obsolete smart lacquer. 
Under the archive lamps it ticked like a tiny insect, remembering a wall no database could name. 
The atlas margin said the flake was cut from the first civic mural, when paint carried memory instead of advertisements. 
As a girl I had been called wisely adventurous, because I tested every ladder before climbing it and still climbed more ladders than anyone. 
That habit made me pack a tether, a solvent brush, a return beacon, and the atlas before patrols changed routes. 
I knew the route could be recovered only through the peel of paint layers revealing hidden murals, since each frightened council had painted over the previous map. 
At the abandoned transit arch, I touched the ruby flake to a dull red patch, and the wall answered with my grandmother's handwriting. 
The smart lacquer softened one century of slogans, and my brush lifted them in long, sighing ribbons. 
Below lay an orchard in impossible colors, a silver gate, and children carrying lanterns shaped like obsolete home computers. 
A caption named the destination: a hidden orchard behind a kaleidoscopic waterfall at the sealed edge of the old weather dome. 
Another line warned that the cascade's prisms aligned only at night, and I had until sunrise to reach the stairway. 
So I chose to sally through the old flood channel, where moss grew over charging rails and the air still played departure announcements. 
The tether clipped to my belt snapped tight when the channel dropped, and the return beacon blinked like a patient grandmother waiting up. 
At the end, water fell from the ceiling in stained glass sheets, each droplet borrowing color from a different decade. 
The ruby flake glowed against the spray, and a service seam opened just wide enough for someone who trusted old hinges slowly. 
I stepped through the waterfall and entered the hidden orchard behind a kaleidoscopic waterfall, breathing air that tasted of rain, dust, and warm circuitry. 
The trees were engineered pear and apricot grafts, but their fruit held memory cores of first birthdays, lost ballads, and instructions for repairing sunlight. 
I wanted to linger, because the future we had built was homesick for a past we had only inherited. 
The eastern sky, visible through the dome's oldest crack, was turning the color of skim milk. 
A stone pavilion stood at the orchard's center, plastered with forty coats of official beige. 
The murals had followed me here, not as pictures but as a method: the flake's charge could loosen any lacquer made by the founders. 
I worked upward through the peel of paint layers revealing hidden murals, uncovering petitions, grain prices, jokes, drought maps, and finally a spiral stair drawn in children's handprints. 
This was the stairway of ascension, not a staircase of marble but a sequence of living terraces that woke when read aloud. 
I read the handprints in order, and the trees lifted their roots into steps, creaking with the courtesy of old elevators. 
Halfway up, the return beacon pulsed, reminding me that a wiser adventure always leaves a door open behind it. 
I nearly turned back when the Engine's countdown chimed through every bracelet in the city, offering the throne like a prize in a childhood game. 
My grandmother had not raised a claimant, and the orchard's fruit had not preserved ordinary lives to decorate one more lonely chair. 
At the top, I found no crown, only a broadcast lens aimed at the concourse where the unclaimed throne waited under its patient drones. 
The ruby flake fit the lens socket; murals poured across Lumen's walls. 
At sunrise, citizens answered the Engine with handprints, proving no single memory may rule all memories; the unclaimed throne stayed empty.
