In the university clock tower mechanism room, the hour when the door shouldn't exist began just after the ninth chime, when the pendulum paused and a black doorway opened in the whitewashed wall beside the escapement.  The night porter, who had polished those gears for thirty-one years, swore no plan had ever shown a door there, and his ladder still leaned where he had left it, against what had been an unbroken patch of limewash.  Nara Venn, an adaptable archivist with soot on her cuffs, arrived carrying a lamp, a roll of soft lead wire, and the diplomat's wax seal impression from the treaty cabinet.  She was warmly methodical, sitting the porter down with tea before she touched anything, numbering each oddity on a card, and asking the frightened registrar clerk to read the missing names twice.  The clerk's list held thirty-two graduates who, according to the university roll, had never enrolled, while the city census answered every inquiry with the same blank certificate of no birth.  Years of water damage, student pranks, and three rival alphabets had taught Nara that tidy contradictions usually meant someone had been filed between jurisdictions.  Earlier she would have made separate boxes and waited for a committee; now she wanted, more than sleep or praise, to translate confusion into clarity while the names could still be spoken.  The doorway smelled of wet stone and old ink, and the mechanism's brass teeth moved without lifting the weights, as if the tower were reciting a sentence no longer attached to its subject.  In the archive, fables of absence were not bedtime amusements but field manuals: the missing bowl proved a shared meal, the unsung verse located a censored singer, and the erased boundary explained two quarrelling maps.  Nara had catalogued those fables after a winter of lost pension records, and she recognized the black doorway as a gap with a rim, not a wound.  She held the diplomat's wax seal impression to the lamp and showed the clerk its oval ridge, its small clasp, and the smooth hollow where the founder's lion had never been pressed.  That hollow matched the empty crest on the university charter, the blank certificate from the city, and the doorway's lack of any handle, hinge, or named room beyond it.  The treaty had joined town scholars and town children under one clock in 1423, but its final clause had been written as a pattern of omission rather than a royal signature.  A founder's note, copied by Nara the previous spring, said that if the shared hour appeared, the keeper must merge, not choose, before the tenth chime made the omission permanent.  To choose the university roll would erase the citizens; to choose the census would erase the graduates, so the only honest reading of the fables was a joining.  The joining could be made only by reconfiguring the braids of a centuries-old ceremonial rope that hung beside the great wheel and still raised the founder's bell on installation days.  Three braids ran through it: hemp for the university, flax for the city, and a dark silver strand whose invoices had been missing since the first commencement.  Nara laid the wax impression across her open palm and counted the depressions around the hollow: over, under, under, over, then a crossing where no strand should have passed.  Because she was adaptable, she did not force the brittle rope into a scholar's diagram; she used lead wire to pin each braid, warm oil to soften the knots, and the porter's ladder to reach the highest loop.  Her instructions stayed gentle and exact, and the clerk repeated each name as if warmth itself were a cataloguing rule.  At the crossing, Nara paused, because the hollow in the wax did not ask for another strand but for the silver strand to carry both records at once.  She loosened the hemp from the flax, threaded silver between them, and closed the pattern until the braid looked less like three arguments than one continuous grammar.  The mechanism shuddered; the weights dropped one measured inch; and the black doorway folded inward, revealing not a corridor but the pale backs of the two ledgers pressed together.  When Nara pulled the rope, the university clock tower mechanism room filled with the ordinary tick of separate teeth, and the two ledgers began to print a shared column of names.  The clerk wept, the porter took his ladder, and Nara checked the list again.  By morning, absence had become a clear cross-reference, and the missing names remained.
