During the hush where past meets prophecy, the Ashen skyborne arbor stopped creaking and listened. 
Its roots hung above the valley in gray braids, and damp ledges, roped rails, and beetle crumbs gave the ash colored leaves a mossy delight. 
Marra Venn, the Orderly seed sorter, squared every tray by size, shine, and likely weather, since crooked trays could tilt next year's trees into clouds. 
To decode the message in falling leaves, she had set aside her labels and swept the central floor clean. 
The regional folklore of the terraces insisted that such leaves were not omens but correspondence, delivered by trees that remembered futures better than people remembered names. 
Building tiny monuments to forgotten things was therefore part of her office, not a private fancy. 
Along the floor she placed a thimble for lost buttons, a pebble for the unused river, a matchbox for dead aunts' songs, and an empty cup for her mother's last handful. 
Each monument anchored a small disregard; regional folklore taught that a forgotten thing, given weight, would catch one leaf and make its vein script readable. 
Above the west hatch, the old charter named the law of the place, "Emerges transformed always," as if grammar had been bent by weather. 
Marra had copied that line a thousand times without believing it, since she believed in drawers, rows, and seeds that stayed what they were. 
At the first bell of the hush, five leaves came down without wind, each pausing above a place as though deciding whether to confess. 
The thimble gathered a leaf marked Lost, the pebble gathered Cross, the matchbox gathered Sing, the cup held a blank fourth leaf, and the fifth leaf would not land. 
Marra wrote Lost Cross Sing, saw no prophecy in it, and admitted that one more monument was missing. 
The fifth leaf hovered over the swept floor, trembling brown and silver, with vein script too faint for any seed sorter's eye. 
In the pocket of her apron lay her mother's cracked moonstone cabochon, smooth as a blind eye; caught in its rim was one moonstone cabochon dust speck. 
Regional folklore said such a speck could read what eyes had neglected, but only if someone dared to prod it loose with a living seed. 
Marra chose the smallest viable seed, a green comma from the future grove, and touched it to the dusty rim. 
The prod was gentle, yet the speck flared like a tiny moon under water, and the hovering leaf let go its hidden ink. 
The hidden word surfaced on the fifth leaf: Mother, followed by a smaller line that said, Build for the thing you kept unnamed. 
Marra nearly sorted her pain into the empty cup, but the cup tipped, spilling its blank leaf onto the floor, and the fifth leaf waited. 
Then she understood that her mother's last handful had not been unsorted through haste; it had been left open for a future no label could hold. 
With damp fingers and an uncharacteristic grin, Marra built a fifth monument from the cup, a bread crumb, the green seed, the flaring speck, and the blank leaf. 
It honored the forgotten thing she had refused to name, the chance that her mother had trusted change more than order. 
The fifth leaf and the blank leaf settled, and all five veins joined into one sentence, clear as rain on moss: What is lost, crossed, sung, mothered, and unnamed will grow. 
It was not a command to restore the past, nor a tidy inventory, but permission for the arbor to become otherwise. 
Marra laughed then, a low mossy delight that startled the beetles and fogged the nearest pane of glass. 
She opened every seed drawer a finger's width, so the old classifications could breathe together, and left the green seed where the leaves had joined. 
Under the charter, the seed cracked, not into a repeat of any tree she knew, but into a pale shoot with roots like handwriting. 
Because whatever passed through that hush emerges transformed always, the shoot lifted the tiny monuments, leaves and thimble and cup, into a hanging garden of new memory. 
By morning, the arbor had a fifth branch, crooked, leafy, and impossible to label, while Marra sorted around it with a happier ruler. 
When apprentices asked what had happened during the hush where past meets prophecy, she told them regional folklore was a filing system for promises and handed each a crumb, a cup, and a seed.
