The summons reached Mara under the soft hush of a distant morning, when the moon was a black coin and the clouds looked milky and still. 
As a lunar-eclipse locksmith, she opened what daylight sealed, but the message asked for repair, a word she had avoided since her mother died. 
She carried a clockmaker's gear assortment in a dented tin, a sealed vial of amber oil, and the stubborn wish to learn the rhythm of repair. 
The door to the frozen library above clouds waited on a blue ice stair, its brass handle furred with frost. 

Mara had found the way through scraps of prophecy hidden in a library's corner, where children once hid broken indexes behind a plinth. 
Those scraps taught her not maps but intervals: three breaths at the first lock, silence at the second, and uncap before counting teeth. 
The vial had been her mother's, though Mara never knew whether it held oil, ink, or distilled summers. 
At the threshold, she made herself uncap it and heard wax crack softly under her thumb. 
The smell rose thinly, cedar and old rope, and what it woke was driftwood nostalgia, tenderness rubbed smooth by loss. 

Inside, ice-glazed shelves stood pale as beach bones, and the air held suspended pages of light. 
The eclipse shone through a round window, hauntingly bright, making every pane of frost a white moon. 
Beyond the tables, the hall of mirrors held the central lock that measured all borrowed hours. 

Mara remembered her mother saying clocks gave time a pulse, not cages to hold it. 
Mara preferred locks because locks confessed plainly: pin lifted, ward turned, door consented. 
Repair asked for listening, and listening had led her to hear her mother's breath falter next door. 
Since then, she had mistaken stillness for skill, fixing mechanisms without letting any keep their scars. 

The first mirror showed the library new, all gilt and uncracked, with a young clockmaker smiling from a clean bench. 
The second showed her mother alive, hands steady, holding out the same tin as if no year had passed. 
Mara almost touched it, but a prophecy scrap in her cuff advised: refuse the mirror that offers no wound. 
She passed on, counting breaths, while the hall threw back her face at every possible age. 
At the last mirror, the glass cracked from corner to corner; beyond it, the stopped lock crouched in rime. 
Its wheels were too tight, each tooth touching the next, so no beat could enter, and no silence could leave. 
Mara understood why the library had frozen: it had tried to become perfect, squeezing away the pause. 

She opened the dented tin and laid the clockmaker's gear assortment on velvet, hearing brass chime against brass. 
Most gears were bright and true, but one was chipped, its missing tooth the size of a rice grain. 
Her mother had kept that flawed gear, and Mara thought grief had blurred an exact craft. 
The final prophecy scrap was the shortest, a pencil line: leave one tooth for breathing. 
Mara set the bright gears by the diagram in the tin lid, then hesitated over the chipped one. 
The mirrors filled with her mother's hands, only tapping twice on an invisible bench and waiting. 
Tap, rest, tap: that lesson lay under every bolt she had tightened. 

She let a drop of amber oil fall from the uncapped vial onto the chipped gear, and the metal warmed, recognizing the smell. 
Instead of fitting the perfect spare, she installed the flawed wheel with its gap facing the spindle. 
Then she turned the assembly by counted breath, each click following an absence. 
The lock answered with a tick so small that the rime loosened like sand from a shoe. 
A second tick came, then a rest, and in that rest she learned the rhythm of repair: tighten, yield, listen, begin again. 
The cracked mirror no longer showed her mother; it showed the reading room thawing, water bead by bead along spines. 
Mara closed the tin around the unused perfect gear, left the door ajar, and descended the blue stair carrying warmth that had once been an ache.
