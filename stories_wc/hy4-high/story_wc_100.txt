After the mysterious package arrives, the mortuary acquires a chased stillness: clocks hush, gulls hold their tongues, and even the rain seems to run from the parcel on Elias Vale's table. 
Elias Vale, the weary undertaker of Upper Quay, had wanted to give rest a permanent address ever since the cemetery cliffs began sliding into the harbor. 
The package has no return mark, only kelp, cold salt, and a broken lighthouse lens wedged in oilcloth. 
He recognizes it as the lens from the northern lighthouse, whose beam guided rescues until the night Lower Nerys drowned. 
The cracks blink at dusk, not with light but with distress signals, each pulse a clipped name, last berth, unfinished plea. 
Everyone in town thinks signals ask for boats, but Elias hears something more precise: they ask where to lie down. 
He is inadvertently stubborn; he never argues with grief, but he cannot leave a drawer unsorted or a name homeless. 
So when the third pulse spells his sister Mara, drowned in the flood and buried without a stone, he closes the coffin showroom and takes the lens. 
He descends the tide stairs to the forgotten underwater city library, where the old municipality stored deeds, birth records, and death maps. 
The library's dome still holds a skin of air, but readers vanished when the sea took the streets, and tides erased the catalogue headings. 
Elias enters through a maintenance throat in a diving coat, carrying a lamp, chalk, and the broken lighthouse lens under one arm. 
The lens warms in his arms and throws the distress signals across the catalogue cases, making wet drawers rattle like anxious teeth. 
He understands that the old lighthouse did not merely warn ships; it received final locations from the drowning and wrote them into the city index. 
When the lens broke during the flood, those locations had nowhere to land, so the dead went on signalling through every shard. 
To revitalize the library, he must seat the broken lighthouse lens in the empty rotunda beacon and make the index answer. 
But the beacon stands behind a collapsed gallery, in a chamber reached only by occupying impossible spaces. 
This is not a spell from books; it is an undertaker's craft learned from coffins in stairwells, from turning where no turning fits. 
In Lower Nerys, the builders had folded corridors around air pockets, leaving seams too narrow for flesh but wide enough for purpose. 
Elias presses his shoulder into the seam between a shelf and its reflected shelf, exhales until his ribs complain, and refuses the pain's invitation to stop. 
He occupies the wedge between water and wall, then the cold interval between one signal and the next, then the black gap under Mara's name. 
Each impossible space opens another, making his passage a method rather than a miracle. 
Mara's distress signal pulses ahead, not urging rescue now, but leading him as a younger sister once led him home through fog. 
At the rotunda, barnacles crust the beacon cradle, and the central lens socket is shaped like an eye that has forgotten how to close. 
Elias fits the broken lighthouse lens into it, but a shard is missing; he sees the empty notch with an undertaker's unwilling honesty. 
The missing shard is in his coat pocket, wrapped with the parcel's plain paper, though he does not remember putting it there. 
He slots it, the beam catches, and the distress signals stop fleeing long enough to become words on the damp catalogue cards. 
Names pour into drawers with streets, berths, door numbers, and quiet rooms beneath the library, each line a permanent place for someone to rest. 
The library begins to revitalize itself: lamps kindle green, air sweetens, pumps remember their rhythm, and fish press their faces to glass like late readers. 
Yet Elias does not smile until Mara's card slides out, giving her the small blue room where she used to read adventure novels during storms. 
He uses the chalk to add his name below hers as keeper, because an address needs someone answerable when loneliness knocks. 
By dawn, Upper Quay receives no more distress signals; instead, the harbor bells ring once, softly, as if a house has opened its door. 
Elias returns through the tide stairs with wet cuffs and a city restored beneath him, still weary, still inadvertently stubborn, still chasing stillness. 
He knows the quiet will never stay caught, but now, when it runs, it has somewhere to return.
