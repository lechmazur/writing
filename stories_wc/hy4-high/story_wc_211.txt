After the foghorn falls silent, the ancient orchard garden atop a skyscraper seems to inhale.
Mara Venn, the venerable oath scribe, keeps the last listening hour beneath those trees.
Her commission is to archive emotional patterns, not the brittle promises that courts prefer.
She listens for the pressure under yes, the salt in refusals, and the loyalty that outlives its speaker.
The orchard helps because its grafted trunks were raised from tomb seeds, and their roots drink condensed centuries.
Pears hang in a velvet disquiet, plush with fragrance, faintly troubled by what the fog has hidden.
Tonight the harbor foghorn stops before its final note, leaving an absence as thick as water in the ears.
The council has ordered the old signal tower dismantled, so even the warning voice that once steadied the garden is gone.
Only in this hush can Mara separate a sigh from the ventilators, a vow from wind moving through leaves.
An apprentice arrives with a wax cylinder phonograph shaving cupped in blue paper.
The curl was pared from the founders' recording, the one that held their oath to keep this roof alive.
Generations of resurfacing have erased the audible words, leaving only this discarded crescent of brown wax.
The apprentice expects Mara to discard it before dawn, when contractors will replace the trees with antennae.
Among her order, people still say: Mara speaks dead languages, as if that were merely a courtesy.
She can pronounce Sumerian lawsuits, Hittite lullabies, and the river tongue used by people who named their kings after floods.
Those grammars have trained her ear to hear intention before vocabulary, which is why the shaving seems to stutter.
A needle would crush it, but she knows she must immerse it, not play it.
The method returns via a curious third-grade field trip memory.
At nine, she had visited a drowned sound museum where a guide dropped wax scraps into a bowl of warm rainwater.
As each scrap unfurled, its groove shadows crawled across the ceiling, and the children applauded the dead singer's breath.
Mara alone had noticed that the ripples changed when the guide grew embarrassed, as if feeling could emboss water.
So she fills a shallow bronze dish with dew from the oldest pear and sets it under a hooded lamp.
When the fog's last moisture beads on the leaves, she immerses the shaving and whispers a Sumerian verb for binding.
The crescent loosens, and shadows of voices move over her hands like minutes learning to walk.
First comes the founders' pattern, four hungers braided together: fear of heights, pride in roots, grief for drowned streets, and a stubborn communal tenderness.
Beneath it, because she has asked only for emotional patterns, another pressure rises from the same groove.
That second pattern is an accidental prophecy, born not of revelation but of an archive open one minute too wide.
The roots have carried tomorrow's unspoken vote upward, and the wax, softened by silence, has agreed to remember it.
Mara recognizes the signatures of living councilors: appetite wearing patience, shame wearing perfume, and relief already rehearsing a signature.
At dawn they will swear that no tree ever had legal standing, then sell the roof for a cleaner signal.
The orchard's terror follows that sentence, a soft green panic with no language left to object.
An oath scribe may certify what has been spoken, but may not write what only the future intends.
Mara has obeyed that rule through wars, mayoral scandals, and three vows of her own that failed quietly.
Now the rule and her commission stand on opposite sides of a dish of trembling water.
She does not announce a miracle, because velvet disquiet has taught her that dread travels best by touch.
Instead she writes the founders' oath on her official slate in the oldest dead language she speaks.
Beneath it, she copies the accidental pattern exactly, including the councilors' relief and the orchard's green panic.
She calls this a deposition of tendencies, not a prediction, because every syllable is legally true.
The apprentice signs as witness with a hand that smells of pears.
All certified depositions from a venerable oath scribe are read aloud when the tower lifts its night shutters.
At first light, the maintenance stair fills with clerks, gardeners, children, and insomniacs from forty floors.
They come because the deposition names feelings they recognize, especially shame that had hoped to stay scented.
When the councilors arrive to swear, the dead language on the slate quotes them before they can lie.
Mara immerses the shaving once more and archives the hush that follows as consent.
