After the last echo of laughter fades, a deeper silence begins speaking in the subterranean crystal cave.  Ilyan, a charcoal-fingered portrait sketch artist, stays behind while the children and councillors climb toward the surface.  Their dutiful laughter leaves green and violet light over the walls, an auroral sorrow Ilyan has breathed since boyhood.  He has not come to mourn Mira again, though her face waits behind every eyelid.  He has come to rectify the lie that killed the paintings and made even private sketches feel criminal.  His motive is embarrassingly simple: to uncork suppressed creativity before the town forgets the shape of its own imagining.  Because Ilyan is mnemonically gifted, forgetting is the one crime he can never honestly claim.  He remembers every nose in the market and every number his mother hummed while grinding blue pigment.  That gift turns memory into inescapable callings, not visions from saints but small unpaid debts to the dead.  Twelve years ago, a summer festival filled this chamber with painted banners and children laughing at foxes made of color.  A ceiling seam opened, dust fell like gray flour, and Mira, Ilyan's younger sister, never reached the stair.  The councillors blamed brightness, haste, and the dangerous habit of making what had not existed before.  Ilyan remembers the true order: wax plugging the vent, the steward shouting, his mother pointing toward the pigment well.  Grief preferred a cleaner story, so the council lowered a stone cork into that well and clamped it with a brass combination lock.  They meant to silence color, yet their fear also stopped the pressure that had kept the ceiling breathing.  When an elder forced the wheels after the sealing, the lock snapped, making escape from the decree impossible.  Now the broken combination lock still grips the cork, three numbered wheels cracked around a missing cam.  Each year the town descends, laughs on command, and leaves before the crystals can answer with anything truer; only Ilyan waits.  He crosses the damp floor to a recess where his mother once hid a method: a painting with something behind it.  The painting shows Mira at nine, holding a fox no bigger than a thumb.  Its forbidden colors have not faded in the mineral damp.  Ilyan lifts the portrait from two crystal hooks; behind it wait a slim brass cam, folded vellum, and blue pigment.  The vellum repeats the numbers he knows and adds one word, rectify, making memory feel forgiven instead of stolen.  With charcoal-blackened nails he fits the cam into the hollow tooth of the lock.  The wheels resist, then settle as he turns them to seventeen, four, and thirty-one.  A soft click travels through the chamber, and every crystal answers with a thin, grieving chime.  Ilyan braces for the cork to rise, but the repaired lock has released only the first of two seals.  On the vellum's reverse, his mother diagrams a light latch, while behind the hooks a frosted crystal shutter carries a fox-shaped groove and a blank oval.  The council had scraped away the original figure, but his mother left pigment, not a copy, because the latch needed a line no one had seen before.  Ilyan understands that remembering Mira exactly would be another way of burying her.  He wets the blue pigment cake with cave water and mixes in charcoal dust from his fingertips.  With one finger, he paints Mira grown, laughing beside a fox with antlers and a child's crooked crown.  The image is foolish, tender, and impossible, which is why it belongs in the blank oval.  Blue light follows the grooves, gathers under the cork, and lifts it with a sigh like a bottle opened after winter.  Color rises from the well, not as paint spilled on stone but as a slow vapor of violets, golds, and sea greens.  The crystals take the vapor and return faces: bakers, babies, lost husbands, Mira at every age she was denied.  By morning, children draw impossible animals on the stair while the useless lock hangs open.  No one apologizes to the dead; the cave keeps its violet edge.  Ilyan opens his sketchbook, accepting that inescapable callings ask for a future made with the dead beside him.  The released light keeps its auroral sorrow, luminous, wounded, and finally moving.
