After words become unnecessary, people stop spelling meaning into breath and trust bronze, wind, and hands.  On the Whispering bluff above the clouds, truth rides in eleven public bells.  Below, cloud banks crawl; above, stone fissures whisper of pressure.  Mara holds the post of Harmonious bell tuner, filing no complaints and keeping those bells honest.  Seasonally mute, she loses her voice from first frost to thaw, when decisions must be made.  The silence makes her exact; she hears hinges, breath, and doubt in a delaying hand.  In this canton, Symphonies of truth are records, vows, warnings, verdicts, and maps of the dead.  A statement set into bells either resolves with the communal pulse or exposes discord; no sentence is needed.  At the center of the tallest bell hangs an iceberg fragment hauled from the last grounded ice.  It is blue with ancient air and green with old salt.  When morning strikes one angle, the fragment begins to glisten and throws color across the listening floor.  Those bands are the oldest measure of resonance, proving that truth can be accurate without being bleached.  Mara works to preserve the color of wonder because her mother first carried her to this floor.  That day, blue light crossed a verdict of guilty, and the guilty man wept without becoming less guilty.  Her mother only pressed two fingers to Mara's wrist until the bell finished.  Years later, after the funeral chord, Mara scraped a green vein from the fragment to make the tone cleaner.  The tone was cleaner; the floor looked gray, and children stopped lifting their faces.  She has never repeated that mistake, though the council now asks for a cleaner tone again.  In four bell strokes, it votes to replace the iceberg fragment with a basalt disc that stores facts without weather.  The council believes accuracy is mercy when storehouses must be counted before a hard season.  Mara's throat has already closed for the frost; she cannot argue in words and will not falsify a chord.  Through kinetic sculpture, she can set a question where wind, bells, and council must answer it together.  For nine days she works with muted vigor, hauling brass arms, cloud vanes, weights, and an ice cradle uphill.  She eats standing, sleeps by the cold forge, and tests pivots with her palm.  Apprentices lift eyebrows; she sends them for rope and charcoal.  Every moving part becomes a clause in her proof.  One vane catches the bluff's whisper and opens the central bell; another turns the ice toward dawn.  Small hammers wait on the chord that will declare the basalt disc sufficient.  If that chord contains uncolored certainty, the hammers stay still; if it hides doubt, the weights make doubt audible.  On replacement morning, the council carries the basalt disc between two poles.  Clouds press below them, and the stone fissures whisper of a storm under the horizon.  Mara stands beside her machine, seasonally mute and pale with cold, while the first councilor draws the formal chord.  The note is exact, spare, and flat, a clean claim that the town can live by measurement alone.  Under it, the sculpture remains still, and a hand rises to signal the basalt disc forward.  Then the hidden storm leans into the bluff, the whispering rises half a tone, and the first vane opens.  A thin wind enters the bell, finds doubt beneath the chord, and sets the counterweights trembling.  Mara has put no lie there, only room for grief that measurement cannot count.  Small hammers fall in sequence, each striking a bell too briefly for melody and too precisely for accident.  The cradle turns, dawn catches ice, and the fragment begins to glisten with the green vein she once tried to erase.  Blue and green bands cross basalt, damp coats, and faces that had forgotten to look up.  The chord does not break; it widens until certainty and grief resolve in colored overtones.  The answer is plain: a town can count grain and keep the light that made counting worth doing.  No order is given; the public bells carry the verdict into every chest.  The basalt disc is lowered as a weight to steady the sculpture's southern vane.  Mara touches the green vein once and leaves it uncovered for the next frost.  After words become unnecessary, the color of wonder remains a fact the bells refuse to bleach.
