On the cobalt terrace at dusk, under the weight of unspoken words, Mira Venn waited beside the sealed lineage court with her hands folded inside her coat.  The city had forbidden speech before a ruling, so petitions, verdicts, and even grief arrived as objects, laid one by one on cobalt tiles.  She was a guilt-ridden black market genealogist who had once made those objects agree with whatever a frightened buyer could pay.  Twelve years earlier she had sold Governor Sable a clean descent from the old pilots; the certificate erased the true Sorn family from the harbor rolls.  The Sorns were sent beyond the breakwater with no names, including her younger brother Ivo and the child she had never met.  Her mother learned the forgery, said only that hunger makes clever hands cruel, and kept her silence until she died.  Now Ivo's son Niko had come to claim a berth, carrying the only inheritance he could prove, a bent steel watch hand from Mira's father's stopped watch.  He set it beside a damp summons, and his silence asked what the court would not allow him to say.  Mira took the meeting not for money but to discover the weight of a withheld truth, because each year her own memory grew lighter and more false.  The sieved hush drifting up from the registry grates had seen to that.  Made from silence left after banned names were strained through bronze mesh, the sieved hush manufactures false memories in anyone who breathes it long enough.  It had taught Sable's clerks to remember ancestors who never lived, Mira to dream of a brother who never existed, and Niko to doubt names his mother whispered.  She had come to quell it before the court sealed Sable's pedigree again.  The old charter allowed one defense, by sifting through driftwood carvings along a forgotten shore.  Beneath the terrace, past the customs lamps, lay the shore where nameless families carved kin lines into driftwood before returning pieces to the tide.  When the last bell dimmed, Mira led Niko down the salt stairs with the watch hand held like a relic.  Dusk left the terrace cobalt above them, but the beach held the paler blue of skimmed milk and old bruises.  They worked without speech, for the hour remained under the weight of unspoken words and every cough seemed like testimony.  Mira used the watch hand to clear sand from grooves, lift splinters, and fit broken boards edge to edge.  The hush followed in a fine gray sift, offering counterfeit dinners, lullabies, and a cheerful Sable childhood.  When a false memory settled, Niko pressed his palm to a carving until Mira scraped it clean.  Near an overturned eel trap she found a cedar plank carved in her mother's square hand: Ivo Sorn, lawful pilot, sold by Mira's seal.  Under it, half erased by salt, was Niko's birth line and her mother's notation: Mira had known the child was kin.  The watch hand, lighter than a fish bone, seemed to pull her wrist toward the ground.  She wanted to replace the plank with another forgery, a cleaner memory where a starving apprentice had not recognized the names.  Instead she laid the plank across the others, matched the cuts with the steel point, and let the true descent stand visible.  Only by such patient sifting could she quell the hush, because carved wood needed no voice and could not invent one.  Back on the cobalt terrace at dusk, the clerks found the boards waiting on the judgment stone.  No one spoke, but Sable's old pedigree was crossed through in silence.  Niko received his father's name, a harbor berth, and the right to ask Mira why she had done it when speech returned at dawn.  Mira kept only the watch hand, nicked from the carvings, and a copy of her confession signed before the silent court.  The confession said that a pedigree could be bought, but a family could not be restored by a sieved hush that manufactures false memories.  She had wanted to discover the weight of a withheld truth, and learned it was the pressure that keeps her standing when apology cannot lift the dead.  At dawn, when Niko finally asked, Mira answered with tempered regret, omitting neither hunger nor vanity nor the names she had sold.
