In the ancient temple library lost in time, dust settled upward and noon had no authority. 
Its shelves held coronation rolls, ledgers, and footsteps of readers dead in other centuries. 
Only across the pause of shifting constellations did books return to where their first meanings were written. 
When the stars resumed their travel, even a true name could be mislaid. 
Orun, a diminutive titan no taller than a lectern, kept the keys. 
His ancestors had carried roof stones, but his small hands suited clasps, hinges, and marginal notes. 
He had come to unmask a pretender to the throne, not to avenge the shrunken house that had made him guardian. 
Lord Veyr wore the crown and had burned genealogies naming another heir. 
The surviving copy lay in the temple, where every forged addition grew plausible with age. 
Because soldiers watched the roads and priests watched the criers, Orun worked through cryptic messages left in library books. 
Books traveled when people could not, passing from monastery to palace to a child's bed. 
A recipe missing salt instructed a dead herald's daughter to bring the seal matrix. 
An acrostic in a law code persuaded Veyr the library would crown him again. 
A calendar of eclipses, pricked with three dots, summoned Sela, who repaired bindings in the palace kitchen. 
She arrived in a mended gray cloak, regally unpretentious, and replaced a fallen folio before anyone bowed. 
Orun had expected perfume and commands; he found a woman asking whether the glue needed warming. 
That question was the first answer in the old rite, for the lawful line learned to mend before reigning. 
Veyr came at the pause with four guards, a gold stylus, and a smile polished for coins. 
He did not notice Sela and stepped over the folio she had rescued. 
The witnesses hidden by the messages stood among the stacks, borrowed from different years. 
Orun's first impulse was to lift a column and frighten them all into truth. 
He could have done it, for strength slept in his shoulders even if height had refused him. 
But the temple law was gentler: a claim had to be opened, not crushed. 
On the reading table he laid one iron gall ink pellet, dark as a seed and sour with vitriol. 
Sela recognized it from a note in a herbal and offered water from her cup. 
Ordinary ink would fade when time reconsidered itself; iron gall would bite the vellum and hold. 
The pellet dissolved slowly, and the acid smell made the guards step back. 
Orun opened the coronation roll where Veyr's seal covered an erased sentence. 
In the oldest canon, a rune of drift was not a charm for travel but a test of belonging. 
It allowed additions made by ambition to slide toward the margin while service stayed at the center. 
To complete it, a keeper had to sunder the false attachment without touching the hand that claimed it. 
That was why Orun had lured Veyr here instead of denouncing him in a square. 
A proclamation could be answered with swords; a record before witnesses could answer itself. 
Veyr, impatient to be crowned again, spoke the borrowed names from the forged genealogy. 
At each name, Orun touched the wet ink to the vellum and drew the curling strokes of drift. 
He used the last stroke to sunder the false attachment, leaving flesh and memory untouched. 
The gold ink rose like a leaf, drifted to the gutter, and left Sela's name readable in the damaged parchment. 
Under it appeared witness clauses, the seal matrix, and a steward's signature proving Veyr had bought his descent. 
No guard moved, because the books around them whispered the verdict in every borrowed century. 
Veyr's face lost its polish to the stunned modesty of a man seen without his costume. 
The stars resumed, and the ancient temple library lost in time began a graceful collapse, shelf by shelf, bowing rather than breaking. 
Columns settled into dust, lamps went out in order, and witnesses returned to their years with the corrected page. 
At the threshold, Sela waited for Orun, who no longer measured strength by what he could lift. 
He held the door for the rightful queen while the roof gave way without a cry.
