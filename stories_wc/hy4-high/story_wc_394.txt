No one in the workshop agreed whether the acid sea was a miracle or a plumbing error. 
The stencil above the hatch read Gears-and-cogs workshop floating on an acid sea, which was accurate except for the insulting absence of leaking. 
Irreverent awe was the approved attitude as green vapor chewed brass pontoons and patient cogs rescued tools from below. 
Pioneering cart glider was the title on Mara Venn's license, although she had written the job description herself after the first freight trolley grew sailwings. 
She lashed canvas ribs to cargo frames, caught warm updrafts above the acid, and delivered rivets between platforms where bridges dissolved on schedule. 
Steadily curious was the kindest diagnosis, since she measured corrosive fog during lunch and asked rude questions of prophecies. 
On the morning a cog began ticking like a toothache, Mara pried it open instead of replacing it. 
Inside its teeth sat Hunter's arrow tip, black, triangular, and older than any hunter the polite histories admitted. 
The salvage master reached for it, but Mara's cart wing knocked his hand aside with an accident that everyone politely called wind. 
To ring a bell no one hears yet, she needed a striker that could survive the first note and the next ten million years. 
The creatures below had skins, bladders, and excellent opinions about slime, and the crew's ears were built for ordinary clatter, so a bell pitched for future listeners was either a joke or a promise, and Mara chose the promise. 
In the oldest brass drum slept the Yearning algorithm, built to compute what an unfinished species would someday need. 
It did not foretell; it compared hunger to possible organs, then back-solved a machine that could flirt with the future. 
When Mara fed it her route logs, the drum threw sparks, complained about her spelling, and clamped the arrow tip beneath a periscope of mirrors. 
Transliterate, do not translate, ordered the scratched brass strip, so Mara had to transliterate each flashing posture into numbers, pauses, and gear ratios. 
Through the images in a kaleidoscope, the arrow's three tiny nicks became fins, wheels, wet ladders, and ears like question marks, drafts of listeners not yet grown. 
Mara read them until her eyes watered, then cut cogs whose teeth matched the timid geometry of those future ears. 
The bell she cast was wider than a soup vat, thin as a rumor, and tuned so present-day sludge arranged itself into offended commas. 
Every test rang silence so clean that the apprentices applauded it for reasons of union solidarity. 
Mara, steadily curious, almost quit when the universe refused to answer within one lifetime. 
The yearning algorithm offered one restraint: absence was data, not failure, and an audience could be late without being imaginary. 
So she rebuilt the arrow tip as a self-resetting striker and taught each glider cart to nudge the bell whenever lightning crossed the sea. 
She copied her notes, her sarcasm, and one stubborn question into the dispatch drums, which made later mechanics swear their trolleys nagged them. 
Mara grew old, died, and was quoted incorrectly on a plaque, while the workshop kept patching its pontoons and arguing with the acid. 
Throughout the evolution of green films into jointed swimmers, of swimmers into bright-eyed climbers, of climbers into critics with membranes for hearing, the bell remained punctual and apparently pointless. 
The copied Mara slowly stopped wanting applause from strangers. 
The yearning algorithm kept revising the pitch as bodies changed, always subtracting the smugness of finished senses and adding a little more welcome. 
Then, during a storm that dissolved two bridges and a tax office, the bell rang one ridiculous note. 
In the shallows, a child with translucent ear-fins dropped a shell, looked up, and said the sound was older than Grandmother and badly in need of tuning. 
The workshop crew cheered, then checked the pontoons, because ecstasy is no excuse for sinking into acid. 
Copied Mara did not say I told you so; she asked what else the child could hear, and the cogs turned with irreverent awe toward the next impossible noise. 
The bell had become less a triumph than a bad habit of curiosity.
