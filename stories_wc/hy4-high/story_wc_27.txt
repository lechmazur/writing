In the margin of daylight, the abandoned spaceport repair bay held a blue hush, as if the planet were deciding whether to forgive the sun. 
Dust covered freighter cradles, and broken cables lay like crossed-out sentences. 
Mara Venn came through the warped hatch with boots tied to her belt, bare feet testing the chill. 
Dockers called her the barefoot stargazer, because she could read constellations through the warmth that underfloor conduits left on stone. 
That morning she moved awkwardly confident, bowing to empty gantries as if an audience had remained to judge her. 
Vintage opera glasses hung at her neck, dented brass and velvet smelling of her mother's theater trunk. 
A lull in rebellion held Tarrow after the governor burned broadcasts and offered bread for forgetfulness. 
Mara had refused it, needing to discover meaning in the mess before hunger made the wreckage merely rubble. 
The mess held a fractured hull, twelve silent transmitters, a scorched chart, and black stones through cracked flooring. 
Officials called the stones decorative remains; her mother had called them the first engine. 
Settlers built the repair bay around them because ships landed only when the stones answered the tide with a low note. 
Engineers later wired induction coils beneath the ring, using that note to wake cold circuits without matches or sparks. 
Her mother's notebook gave Mara one instruction: reignite the dead relay by the hum of an ancient stone circle under the floor. 
Now the relay was the only device that could reach the outer habitats before the governor registered every rebel cell as extinct. 
At sunrise, satellites would sweep the ash with orders confirming every cell erased. 
That left the thin hour when the sun and Epsilon shared the cracked dome before day glare erased the star. 
She set the opera glasses on a crate and wiped their lenses with the hem of her shirt. 
Made for balconies, not barricades, their twin brass tubes could split one star into two patient beams. 
Her mother had followed tenors through them; Mara meant to find a pitch no ear could hold. 
The bay's dome was split in a long diagonal, and in that seam the pale sun touched Epsilon's last bright edge. 
Mara knelt, placed the glasses beneath the seam, and turned the focus wheel until the star became a needle of light. 
The first beam fell across a black stone incised with a spiral; the second landed on its partner twelve paces away. 
Nothing happened, so she shifted her weight, feeling through her soles for the faintest complaint in the floor. 
Bare feet had saved her on observation decks where alarms came as tremors before sirens, and they saved her again. 
A line of dust trembled, not toward the dome, but around the buried stones in a slow ring. 
She nudged the glasses a finger width left, and the trembling gathered into a note below hearing. 
It pressed against her ankles, her teeth, the fillings in her mouth, a thunderous serenity that made the enormous room feel perfectly still. 
Above her, loose panels answered like distant drums, while dust hung in the light. 
Mara did not cheer, because certainty had always made her clumsy, and she trusted the clumsiness less than the work. 
She held the wheel until buried coils woke and the dead relay reignited with a click. 
The repair bay sent her mother's lullaby through every hidden receiver in the outer habitats. 
The tune was too soft for patrols and too familiar for anyone who had forgotten the dead. 
In kitchens, mines, and hospitals, people lifted flags from walls and walked toward the port with stubborn calm. 
The governor's satellites saw only laborers entering an abandoned repair bay to fix a roof. 
Inside, the ancient stone circle hummed beneath their feet while the relay's blue eye steadied on Mara. 
She understood the mess at last: broken things were instruments waiting for someone patient enough to listen. 
Awkwardly confident, she handed over the glasses' focus settings and stepped aside so other hands could learn the tremor. 
In the margin of daylight, with boots beside her bare feet, the lull ended without losing its peace. 
The sound was enormous, yet Mara felt serene, as if the stars had made room for one another.
