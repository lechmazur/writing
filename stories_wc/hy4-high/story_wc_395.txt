I am a corporate memory keeper in the lower levels of the social network archive, where the company stores the shapes of what users remove.  The shelves hold no photographs now, only access logs, abandoned reactions, and the cool pressure we call sensed absence.  My badge tells visitors that deletion is mercy, and I used to repeat it because repetition is safer than grief.  I took this job to find where deleted files go, because the last voice note from my sister Nia vanished after the Lark crashed.  The official missing persons cold case says seven died in the sea, including Nia, the unlisted courier who smuggled protest songs aboard.  On my first night, an evidence envelope in my drawer held a patch from a crashed spaceship mission, still smelling of ozone.  Its blue bird was sewn with conductive thread, and under the bird someone had stitched 317 in tiny white knots.  The corporation had folded every obsolete platform into one archive, so time no longer ran in dates but across the echo of forgotten songs.  During the nightly sweep, deleted lullabies, ringtones, and protest choruses replayed as index tones, each one opening a different deletion batch.  Nia had loved a banned bedtime song, and I waited for its chorus every shift like a door I could not touch.  Jun, the auditor assigned to watch my hands, was brightly attentive to anything that repeated.  He noticed the same 317 in my checksum errors, stairwell counts, dust readings, and the number of blank chairs in the morning room.  In a building trained to strip names from records, recurring numbers appearing everywhere became the only map I could trust.  I wrote them under the patch, and Jun, who should have reported me, added 0417 and 1123 from his own audit slips.  To reach the sealed tier where those sequences converged, I have to lie.  I lie on the nightly reconciliation: local fragments of Lark profiles, zero.  For a memory system, a false absence is an insult, so the archive answers by calculating the difference between my sentence and its hidden ledger.  That difference opens a maintenance stair behind the music cabinets, exhaling cold air and the felt hush of people recently removed.  Jun follows me down, not as a guard now but as someone brightly attentive to the shape of a wrong.  At the bottom, I press the patch to a black reader; the conductive thread completes a circuit, and the forgotten songs overhead slow to one cracked lullaby.  The lullaby is the one Nia used to hum when our father worked nights, and I feel her before I hear any file.  This is where deleted files go: not outward into clean nothing, but inward to a warm vault of tendency profiles.  The company kept our removals alive to predict longing, pricing grief by the second and teaching advertisements how to sound intimate.  The Lark crew accounts, erased, pulse there as seven careful ghosts, each one still sending fragments from the crash minute.  Their final posts are not messages but coordinates, medical readings, and a shaky list of names the recovery team refused to log.  I understand then that the missing persons cold case was cold because someone had unplugged the room where the witnesses kept speaking.  Her voice note sits in the seventh profile, three minutes and seventeen seconds long, with a background roar like water entering a broken hull.  She does not say goodbye; she says the ship was seen, then repeats a string of numbers that matches the patch.  Jun wants to copy everything, but a full copy would trigger an audit and turn my single lie into a permanent deletion.  Instead I use an old system privilege, stamping the seven profiles as evidence in the public cold case.  The stamp cannot bring bodies home, but it forces the archive to display their names wherever an absence is sold as peace.  Jun signs as witness, brightly attentive even now, and his hand shakes only when her lullaby returns.  By morning, the corridors feel crowded with people who are still gone, a sensed absence that has learned to answer back.  I keep the patch in my breast pocket, no longer evidence of a crash but proof that deletion has an address.  When the company asks why the archive now bleeds numbers into every blank field, I lie again, gently, and say the system is remembering.  That is almost true, because somewhere across the echo of forgotten songs, deleted files go on speaking until someone is brave enough to listen.
