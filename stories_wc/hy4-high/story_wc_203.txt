Nils Vale was an elevator repair technician who sees ghosts, a fact he kept off invoices because clients paid for cables, not company.  He had learned to carry their small requests like a weightless burden, lighter than breath and harder to put down.  Being skillfully observant was his defense against pity; he noticed frayed governor ropes, thumbprints in grease, and pauses where frightened people chose lies.  His private motive was older than any service call: to discover what ceilings hide.  When he was nine, his sister vanished from their elevator, and her ghost hung above the cab, pointing not down at the shaft but up at the plaster.  No report mentioned the maintenance crawlspace where workers later found her red scarf, caught around a cable she had tried to reach.  Since then, every false ceiling seemed to him a polite lie, and every lie had a load bearing seam.  The demolition permit called the place an abandoned circus ticket office, as if that described the smell of wet sawdust and the booths with their little brass mouths.  Outside, cracks in the facade cut through painted trumpets and smiling clowns, making the advertisement look embarrassed.  Inside, a single overhead fluorescent still worried the dusk, although the city had cut the meters years before.  Someone had left a maintenance generator ticking behind the partition, feeding that light; beside it crouched a narrow lift built for the cash strongbox.  The inspector wanted Nils to certify the lift dead before the wrecking ball came; the ticket seller at the counter wanted something else.  She was transparent only at the edges, a woman with ink on her cuffs who kept sliding stubs toward a ceiling stained by rain.  Nils did not ask her name yet, because the living often answer first and the dead wait with better manners.  He began by analyzing dust accumulation patterns, since dust tells on every machine that moved, stopped, or opened in secret.  The public counter wore an even gray felt, but the shelf beneath showed two pale crescents where something round had been lifted away and replaced.  On the tile, a fan of grit thinned toward the rear wall, while a pristine rectangle proved that a hatch had opened after rain.  There were no footprints in that clean rectangle, only brass filings that fell in a spiral near his breath.  A strongbox lift would have used an electric motor, not a clockmaker's drum, so he unfolded his mirror and searched the shadow.  The circle that swung into view was a horologist's mainspring barrel, broad as a dinner plate, toothed at the rim, and wrapped with cable instead of chain.  He understood then that the shaft above the ticket office had not raised money; it had raised a person, hidden between floors whenever inspectors came.  He had learned that when the overhead fluorescent buzz dies off, ghosts stop resembling reflections and start remembering rooms.  At seven, with the generator coughing itself empty, the overhead fluorescent buzz died, and the office fell to blue dusk.  The seller became distinct, though she cast no shadow, and beside her waited a thin girl in a spangled collar, both of them looking up.  The girl held a torn ticket, and Nils felt his sister's old upward gesture pass through him as lightly as a draft from an open shaft.  The seller's lips formed no words, yet her cuffs moved over the counter, counting tickets, hiding cash, writing in a ledger.  Nils set his tool bag on the clean rectangle, took out a square drive key, and reached for the only lever that could engage the barrel.  The lever resisted, not with weight but with stored time, and he had to turn it slowly so the coiled spring would not snap the cable.  As the mechanism engaged, the ceiling hatch sagged open and a narrow platform descended, carrying dust, a ledger, and the smell of dry cedar.  The ledger named the girl Leni Marks, an acrobat called a runaway after she accused the circus of selling phantom seats.  Her aunt, the seller, had hidden her in the lift when inspectors came; the barrel jammed, the platform stopped, and the truth was plastered over.  He left the certificate blank, wrote Leni's name in the margin, and felt the weightless burden lift as the ghosts stepped through cracks in the facade into morning.
