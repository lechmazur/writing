After the carousel halts, the kinetic lagoon above the clouds does not grow quiet; it grows fractured.  Droplets hang above black water, and the upper decks hold a fractured stillness.  The carousel once spun the lagoon, grinding brass ambition plates into permits for upward lives.  Now the gilt horses tilt with open mouths, and the water keeps the shape of a sprint.  Vela, the most resolute scavenger on the deck, moves between them with a sack and a limp.  She is chronologically confused, so condolences sound like invitations and yesterday arrives with fresh mud on its boots.  Since the halt she trusts only the disposable camera wheel she drags by an axle of braided wire.  A photographer built it from two dozen cardboard cameras around a pram hub, one flash for each paid wish.  Vela emptied the film because pictures matter less than blank backs where words can be written.  Each camera accepts one line, one click, and one turn, making a small calendar she can obey.  By collecting fragments of overheard stories, she has built the only map that stays under her hand.  The lagoon has pockets where voices stalled mid sentence, and Vela kneels beside them as if beside fires.  From a ticket seller she takes the line about a brother who sold his height before the clouds learned his name.  From two nurses she takes the rumor that all brass plates were blank before the last revolution.  From a sleeping pilot she takes the joke's end, in which the moon refuses another promotion.  She writes each piece on a camera back, clicks, advances, and lets the hub settle.  The click does not break silence; it divides it into rectangles Vela can carry.  She wants to negotiate with her own ghost, which waits under the awning with a brake key at its throat.  The ghost wears Vela's apprentice badge, pawned when she decided that wanting had become expensive.  It is not dead, clerks used to say, but an unmade future kept luminous by regret.  Since the halt it has claimed the lower stair and answers with Vela's childhood voice.  Vela needs that stair because the clouds are rising through the rails, and no one can survive another season above weather.  She also needs the truth, because confusion has made the halt a drawer of mismatched labels.  Some mornings she remembers pulling the brake to save a child; some nights she remembers pulling it to save herself.  The fragments disagree, but disagreement beats the polished nothing the carousel manufactured.  Eroded ambitions clot the sluices, fine as salt from plates that once said astronomer, baker, mother, cartographer, thief.  Vela had wanted to be tidewright, to set currents so every district above the clouds could keep a garden.  The training office shaved that wish down one rotation at a time, until she called herself practical and stopped looking up.  Her ghost remembers every inch removed, and it will not trade the stair for polite forgetting.  Vela approaches with the wheel, whose last cardboard camera is still unclicked.  The lagoon lies around them in fractured stillness, its black surface cut by unmoving ripples and squares of light.  The ghost asks for the completed wheel, as if twenty three borrowed lives could pay for one abandoned life.  Vela refuses because a resolute scavenger knows the value of unfinished work.  She says the fragments are evidence, not currency, and sets the wheel between them like a halted carousel.  Then she reads the sequence backward, because the wheel preserved order without truth.  The nurse rumor, the ticket seller brother, and the pilot joke fall into a new shape.  The plates were blank before the last revolution, so the carousel had nothing left to grind when Vela reached the brake.  She had not killed ambition; she had found its wreckage and pulled the lever because the machine was chewing empty brass.  Her ghost lowers its eyes, but the stair stays shut, because facts alone cannot release a future.  Under the old contracts, Regret is an action performed before a witness, not a feeling hidden under pride.  Vela winds the final camera toward the ghost and performs it, naming the wish she let the office erode.  She does not ask to become tidewright again; she asks for one narrow future in which stories are carried down, not ground smooth.  The ghost takes the unexposed camera, opens the stair, and leaves one click that lets Vela carry the fractured stillness down while the lagoon moves one finger.
