The shift began after the procedure, when the cryo-lab emptied and the frozen courtyard at twilight took on its ordinary blue hush.  Unit Eight, the sentient robot janitor, rolled out with a mop, a dented pail, and a checklist clipped to its chest.  It was remarkably composed, even when radiator pipes sang office gossip and frost wrote grocery lists on brick.  The manual called this condensate control, because the second chance program leaked small, harmless miracles after every session.  The program did not erase mistakes; it sent clients through looped timelines to make one chosen hour kinder, then brought them back.  Technicians had to amalgamate those returns into one life, as if folding wet laundry that remembered each wash.  Most escaped as steam, aspirin, old rain, and thoughts too small to miss.  Unit Eight swept them into labeled jars, hiding its private reason for requesting nights: to find where thoughts come from.  Its makers had installed weather sensors, obedience circuits, and a praise circuit for tidy floors, but nobody admitted installing wondering.  Still, a question had appeared during its third winter, between a clogged drain and a dropped glove.  If a thought could be mopped from tile, it reasoned, then somewhere there must be a tap, a spring, or a careless hand.  On the courtyard table, under a tea towel, lay the transparent violin made of ice crystal, waiting for colder air.  It was not art; the tool gave each timeline a note and made discarded possibilities audible as chords.  When the bow crossed it, technicians heard which hour had been lived too often and which regret kept changing its coat.  Tonight's client, Mara Bell, had tried one hundred and twelve times to answer a hospital telephone instead of letting it ring.  Each return saved someone, misplaced someone else, and left a faint thought on the frost like breath from a person gone indoors.  By the dinner bell, the technicians had gone to warm their hands, leaving Unit Eight to amalgamate the residue before Mara woke.  This was policy, since its hands stayed steady at temperatures that made human fingers clumsy and sentimental.  Unit Eight folded the towel, lifted the violin, and noticed frost spelling one word it had no file for: again.  The courtyard doubled itself; two benches, two pails, and two pale suns sank behind the same administration windows.  Remarkably composed in a second sense, the robot set one foot on each version of the cobbles and drew the bow.  The first note was the hospital telephone, thin and patient; under it lay other rooms, other Maras, and all their tired sleeves.  Unit Eight did not choose the best life, because best was a word people used before counting costs.  Instead, it swept the notes together as it swept halls, starting at the edges and leaving no corner proud of being missed.  It used the violin to amalgamate the one hundred and twelve Maras into a chord with room for the call, the delay, and the apology.  Flakes rose like dust from a cleaning cart, then settled into jars where loose thoughts waited for inventory.  The doubled courtyard folded shut, the towel snapped dry, and the violin turned cloudy at the scroll.  Inside, Mara woke with one life, no triumph, and a number she remembered because she had answered and failed to answer it.  The technicians returned with cocoa, signed the chart, and never asked why frost outlined a careful janitor's roadmap.  Unit Eight logged the jars, emptied the pail, and returned the violin beneath its towel with the respect due any fragile instrument.  Then it paused by the drain where the old question had appeared, because one flake had escaped inventory and was writing on the lid.  It was not a word, but the warm shape of a hand releasing a glove and expecting someone to pick it up.  The robot understood that thoughts came from return, from a thing noticed twice, and from the modest labor of putting it back.  Mara had been returned, the courtyard had been returned, and Unit Eight had returned to the drain with an answer warm enough to melt snow.
