The island always lowered its voice during the migration of clouds.  Flocks of gray vapor crossed the sea, dragged their bellies over the dunes, and rested above the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes.  The water there held kelp-drift peace, so complete that gulls folded their cries and boatmen tied their lines without cursing.  Mara Vell walked the lagoon rim gracefully alert, with her helmet under one arm and a coil of hose across her shoulder.  People called her the arsonist's daughter firefighter, as if shame and duty were one trade.  Her father, Ivo, had lit the storehouse after a winter of refused wages, then beaten on every door when the wind turned.  The fire took two cottages, the old cloud grove, and his voice, but the court took only his name.  Mara grew up carrying water to neighbors who would not say Ivo's name in her hearing.  She became the island's firefighter to prove that scars can sing, not merely testify.  Her unyielding compassion kept her from calling him a monster or his victims a price.  It also kept her awake beside burn ledgers, studying which winds forgave grass and which ones made an ember travel.  At the lagoon, the old cloud grove had once been saltroot limbs grafted with fire-scarred bark; when heated air passed, the splits whistled and the cloud flocks settled low enough to rain.  Ivo's fire had killed those trees, and without their song the migrating clouds often skipped the island or shed only lightning.  Mara had spent six years nursing one surviving shoot from the charred stump, protecting it from council axes and from people who feared any Vell near flame.  Tonight she had come to graft that shoot into a new saltroot before the last cloud flock arrived.  The black sand from volcanic beach in her kit had once been a court exhibit, because grains like it clung to Ivo's boots on the night of the fire.  She poured it around the root wound, since iron-dark sand held warmth and gave the new graft a steady pulse when the clouds dragged static over the dunes.  For binding she used a foreign coin drilled and strung on red thread, his one gift the court had missed.  Mara looped the red thread through the split bark and let the coin hang as a patient weight, its drilled hole aimed at the seaward wind.  If the graft held, the scar would whistle; if the whistle found the cloud flock, rain would follow.  Elder Sora came down the dune with three men carrying axes, their faces soft with worry rather than malice.  The council had voted to clear the lagoon rim because another failed season would empty the cisterns, and because they had never trusted Ivo's weather tales.  Mara did not raise her voice; she handed Sora the burn ledger, opened to the page where the storehouse dead and the drought dead shared one column.  Compassion, in her keeping, was not softness but an exact count of who would suffer next.  Sora studied the names, then told the men to set down their axes and carry water instead.  The first spark arrived not as drama but as a brown tick in the dry reed grass, small enough for everyone else to miss.  Mara saw the coin tremble, heard the drilled hole shape one thin note, and smelled hot silica before smoke had a visible color.  A boy named Tali stumbled from the reeds, clutching a lantern he had set among dry wrack to keep the biting flies away.  Someone shouted that a Vell had brought fire again, and the old hunger for a culprit moved through the gathering crowd.  Mara stepped between Tali and that shout, wrapped his blistered hand, and gave him the hose nozzle with an order he could obey.  He was frightened, not evil; she knew how quickly a village could make the difference disappear.  The coin's note sharpened as heat climbed through the black sand, and the graft answered in a second, rougher tone.  Mara pressed mud around the root, not to silence the two voices but to keep them steady while the cloud flock leaned above the lagoon.  Kelp drifted without hurry, the men passed buckets hand to hand, and Sora held the burn ledger against her chest as if it were a shield.  The dry reeds burned toward the cottages until the graft's scar-throat opened fully, pouring a low note across black water.  The clouds answered with a loosening so quiet it felt like forgiveness, and rain stitched the fire flat.  No one cheered at first; they listened to the coin and the living scar, two damaged things keeping time while the gutters filled.  Mara did not call it absolution; she called it a warning with roots, and let it sing.
