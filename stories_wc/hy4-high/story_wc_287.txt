After the final petal drops from the old rosebush, Mira Ban, the fire escape gardener, gives herself one minute of stillness.  The luminous harbor by moonlight lies beyond her rail, bright on the water and black under every warehouse arch.  No lamp is allowed tonight; the harbor watch has shuttered flame until its prisoners can be moved unseen.  Up and down the tenement, a quivering hush holds curtains, floorboards, and children with the same careful pressure.  Mira is wistfully pragmatic: she composts the fallen leaves, saves the thorns, and oils the little mirrors she has cut from scrap.  All winter she has built these angles for one purpose, to reflect light into dark corners.  Her mother, dying in a room without a window, had asked for that courtesy as if moonlight could be carried like soup.  Mira, who cannot bear useless sorrow, had answered by making it useful.  In her apron waits the lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth, a steel nib her father used to measure key cuts on harbor gates.  He inspected locks; he never picked them, and he taught her that depth decides whether a door yields or wounds.  Now his tooth scores tin, bends clips, and fits each shard to a thorn above the fire escape.  Below, in chained cellars, dock widows with forged papers, two deserters, and a midwife's apprentice with a newborn wait for a sign.  The sign must be almost silent, because the hush protects them only while nobody breaks it.  Mira will thread moonlight from balcony to balcony, ending at the tide steps where a muffled skiff rocks.  Two flashes mean move; one flash means hide; no flash means she has been taken.  She fastens the first mirror with the rhythm of oars in water, one dip, a pause, two dips.  She means the cadence to instill breath, not heroism, in people she cannot touch.  A boot scrapes below, and Sergeant Vale pauses under the bakery awning with a hooded lantern and a tired young face.  Mira does not hate him; her father once taught him to oil a lock, and he brought nasturtium seeds to her mother's funeral.  Pity nearly ruins her, so she pinches a dead leaf until pain returns her to the work.  She nudges the first mirror with the gauge tooth and sends a pale blade to a chipped cup on the laundry line.  The cup tosses light to a cracked window, which gives it to a brass bedpost in the lower room.  A shadow folds smaller, then straightens, and Mira understands that someone has chosen to believe in her.  She sets the second and third mirrors quickly, with the rhythm of oars in water, tapping steel too softly for Vale to name.  An oar knocks once below, and the answering taps pass through the railings like a secret learning to walk.  Vale turns and starts up the shivering iron stairs.  Mira could pull the mirrors and save herself, but each dark corner behind her would close around someone breathing through a sleeve.  Instead she breaks the empty rose pot on the stair, making a small clatter any grieving woman might make.  Vale stops, embarrassed, and lifts the lantern hood to show an unlit wick.  Mira answers by setting the last mirror, cut from her mother's hand glass, against the thorn hooks above the landing.  Its beam slips past Vale's shoulder, under the chained cellar door, and over the apprentice's hands as she wraps the newborn.  The baby does not cry; the quivering hush holds it as gently as a quilt, while the moon outlines a path.  Mira taps again, with the rhythm of oars in water, and feels the whole house breathing with her instead of hiding from her.  Vale sees the pale chain unfurl toward the tide steps, looks at the stripped rosebush, and lowers his eyes instead of calling out.  Perhaps he remembers the seeds; perhaps he cannot darken a room with a newborn in it.  Mira does not thank him, because thanks would make them both confess too much.  One by one, the hidden people follow the reflected moon, coats close, faces exposed and strangely sheltered by the hush.  When the skiff slides into the luminous harbor by moonlight, Mira turns back, already planning where beans can climb without hiding the light.
