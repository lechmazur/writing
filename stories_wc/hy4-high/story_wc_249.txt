In the clinic's sound wave garden, nothing grew from soil; glass reeds, salt combs, and silver moss held shapes made by rationed tones.  The object at its center was the auric lagoon under starlight, a shallow gold pool beneath an open dome where memories were cooled before use.  As the clinic's experimental treatment guinea pig, Mara Vell had spent nine winters giving recollections to that pool one measured drop at a time.  The therapy was meant to seal minds damaged by the long fever, but it stitched every volunteer's past into entangled memories.  Pull one thread, and a stranger's kitchen, a dead child's kite, or Mara's own brother laughing in a blue stairwell rose with it.  At first she wanted only that brother back, his face cleanly separated from a thousand other faces wearing his voice.  After enough nights, though, the others stopped feeling like thieves; they became patients who had also been promised rescue and then mislaid.  The doctors called the overlap a complication and scheduled a dawn purge, a white chord that would leave each mind tidy and nearly empty.  Mara had been quietly rebellious since the first consent form, when she wrote her whole name under a line asking for a subject number.  She did not argue or break glass; she kept time, pocketed tuners, and learned which cameras blinked before they watched.  Because no single instrument could hold so many lives, her method was drumming on everything: rails, seedpods, cup rims, shoe soles, the lagoon shell.  Each tap returned a fragment, and each fragment answered another, until the garden became less a ward than a sleeping city of entangled memories.  In that city her brother sold paper boats to a girl who had died before Mara was born.  Nurse Ilen counted stairs that existed in three wards at once, and someone always left a window open for rain.  The place was impossible, crowded, and tender, and Mara wanted to breathe life into a shared dream before the purge turned it into silence.  The chart said the only unguarded minute came at the hush of the universe inhaling, when the pumps stopped and starlight leaned inward.  For twenty heartbeats, any rhythm introduced there would pass into the lagoon before the console could name and cancel it.  Mara waited behind a reed with a tuner under her tongue and her brother's stairwell beating behind her ribs.  The room held auroral sorrow, polar light that makes every loss visible without making it bearable.  When the pumps died, she climbed the service ladder to perch on the copper rim above the water.  From that height she could reach the highest seedpod, the starlit rail, and the forbidden brass bell.  She struck them in the order learned from drumming on everything, not as noise but as roll call.  Ilen's stairs began the pulse, the dead girl's kite snapped against a silver frond, and her brother's laugh found a low gold note.  Alarms woke too late, and Director Senn ran in with the white chord trembling on his console.  He saw Mara above the auric lagoon under starlight, hands moving over forbidden instruments, and for one second he looked ashamed.  Mara did not look down, because looking down would make the dream only hers again.  She had promised the others a city rather than a shrine, and the city was beginning to breathe.  She set her forehead against the cold bell and breathed out the stairwell, the paper boats, the blue coat, and every private claim she had carried through treatment.  The sound wave garden accepted the offering as pattern, not loss; beneath her, gold water shivered and began to inhale on its own.  Senn's purge struck a moment later, but the white chord passed through a living chord and came out carrying lullabies.  In the lagoon, each entangled memory kept its own voice while leaning toward the others, as lanterns lean on a windy quay.  The shared dream opened windows, set tables, and gave the dead girl a kite string long enough to cross three wards.  By dawn, the doctors stood among glass reeds and wept without understanding why, while auroral sorrow lay on every face like green fire.  Mara stayed on the rim as a faint percussion, quietly rebellious even in dissolution, keeping time for a world no doctor could tidily own.
