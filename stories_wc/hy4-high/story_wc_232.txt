Solon Venn was a lonely antique map dealer whose shop smelled of foxed paper, river silt, and the lamp oil he burned long after honest customers had gone home.  He could date a coastline by wormholes in vellum, name a surveyor by the pressure of a compass rose, and sense when a map hid a choice.  His customers called this gift luck, but Solon called it empirically intuitive, because every hunch came from thousands of small observations that his hands had filed away.  The chart he never priced hung above his bed, a blank goatskin bearing Ada Bell's command: meet me as the creek rises at midsummer, where the road stops lying.  Ada, a printer's daughter and his only friend, had pressed a type foundry matrix chip into his palm when she left: a copper square stamped with a reversed d and a hairline crack like a river.  Keep it until the water finds the cave, she said, and bring back the answer I was too young to ask, because a promise shelved becomes a landlord.  For thirty years a lingering promise kept him there, measuring other people's distances while his own floor narrowed between counter, cot, and memory.  Then, on the longest day of a wet summer, the river climbed the mill steps and left black ants marching across his floor toward the hillside.  Solon took the chip, locked the shop, and followed the ants, because old cave maps said the safest flood route was following ants home.  They climbed root cracks behind the mill, crossed a pollen dusted ledge, and entered a blooming cavern at midsummer, where cave lilies opened on warm stone above a breathing underground creek.  The water was already taking the lower galleries, erasing paths and turning familiar arches into brown mirrors.  Solon watched the ants shun wet stone three times and trusted the tightening in his chest only after his eyes agreed.  The line led to a nest beneath feldspar, and there the chip grew warm enough to glimmer, its reversed d throwing a bright door onto rock.  When he fitted the copper square into a matching notch, its hairline crack aligned with quartz, and the wall opened on a chamber inked with diverging roads.  At the far end stood the doorway of decisions, dark, crowded with handprints, and smaller than any legend had promised.  He had come to find the doorway of decisions because Ada's note asked no treasure, reunion, or forgiveness, only that he choose before the water chose for him.  Inside the threshold he saw no visions, only Ada waiting with a packed valise, the harbor coach he had not boarded, and his shop reopened each morning as if repetition were courage.  Beside those scenes lay a third, almost hidden: the upper gallery, where ants carried pale larvae above the flood and a narrow stair led toward town.  He had always thought the words on the goatskin named a season; now, as the creek rises around his boots, he understood that they named a deadline.  A slab below the arch held a palm sized impression, and when he set the chip there, lily pollen and iron water darkened the reversed d into ink.  He understood that Ada had given him a letter to cast, not a relic to guard, and that waiting had left every page blank.  He pressed the goatskin chart to the slab, and a clear d appeared beside a route he recognized: the ants' dry stair, the mill root, his own unlocked shop.  Under it, smaller letters surfaced in Ada's patient hand: Will you come as yourself, or keep sending maps ahead of you?  Solon laughed once, with the relief of a man naming his fear, and wrote one word beneath her question: Yes.  The doorway did not applaud, the absent did not return, and the creek kept climbing, but the promise loosened and became a direction.  By morning he had hired a wagon, packed his portfolios and the marked chart, and pinned a note saying that routes would be drawn again after he learned where this one went.
