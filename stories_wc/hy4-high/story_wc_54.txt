At dawn, after the carnival leaves town, I report with a clipboard to the abandoned spaceport passenger terminal.  The midway is gone, but cables, popcorn tubs, and confetti mark the paths between ticket islands.  The county had hired me as the curious civil engineer intern who would measure the old building before the seal.  No supervisor came; the storm had closed the river road, and my radio gave only a dry hiss.  I was not sent to admire the place, yet the long hall held a stoic wonder that made haste seem impolite.  Light fell through the cracked dome, and dust moved above gate numbers with the patience of snow.  The chief called me functionally creative, meaning I made ugly repairs that kept working.  That habit suited culverts, pumps, and failed levees, not a building that remembered why people walked.  The manuals called its routing system a maze of intentions, because it sorted travelers by what they meant to do.  Pressure floors read hesitation, hurry, and return, then opened corridors toward waiting rooms or departure arms.  When the last evacuation failed, engineers shut the system down and let the terminal become a civic rumor.  The carnival had rented the concourse for eleven nights, using its old pipes and emergency current.  After departure, its pumps were gone, but temporary manifolds still breathed warm condensate into lower galleries.  My assignment was to draft a closure form; my private reason was to puzzle out new equations for hope.  Hope, to me, meant gradients, loads, and dependable exits, not posters in disaster shelters.  In a lost property drawer beneath Gate Twelve, I found an explorer's sextant piece wrapped in a survey tag.  Its brass arc carried a cracked mirror, a degree scale, and initials from the woman who mapped the landing field.  Reverence would have left it there, but the atrium sun tracker lacked a calibration arm.  I climbed, fitted the sextant piece into the burned clamp, and used its mirror to throw dawn across the solar relay.  The board woke enough to display one line: INTENTION ROUTING REQUIRES FLOW.  I understood that I could not resurrect the terminal with a switch, a battery, or a speech.  I would have to resurrect it through redirected flows of water, air, charge, and human purpose.  The phrase belonged to hydraulics, but here it also belonged to grief, since every abandoned gate needed a direction.  I began in the flood culvert, where carnival runoff pressed against a rusted valve and threatened the clinic wing.  I closed the fountain line, opened the condensate return, and sent warm water toward thermal drums beneath the floor.  The drums expanded, plates woke, and the first corridor accepted my weight as a question.  Next I turned the terminal fans toward north vents, reversing air that had carried mildew into waiting rooms.  Gate seals loosened with small municipal sighs, and dry motion crossed the departure arms.  Charge came last, because every old grid mistrusts a sudden gift.  I trimmed the carnival generator cable with a ticket punch and fed the solar relay one careful pulse at a time.  Lamps came on in sequences, showing blue routes toward high ground, warm routes toward cots, and white routes toward the clinic.  The maze of intentions still needed passengers, so I loaded carnival token buckets onto service carts.  Their wheels pressed the floor in patterned waves, and the terminal read them as people meaning to shelter, carry, wait, and begin again.  At Gate Twelve, the sextant mirror held the sun long enough for the relay to lock.  The roof shutters opened on the gray launch field, where town buses waited behind floodwater.  I radioed that the high terminal road was dry, the clinic wing was warm, and the gates would guide anyone who came.  The answer was static, but the buses turned, and I wrote no grand conclusion in the closure box.  I wrote an equation instead, with hope equal to shelter plus routes plus the light a broken instrument can still redirect.  It was crude, but it accounted for loss, pressure, and the fact that systems answer when given a direction.  The terminal did not become young, the dead did not return, and the stars stayed indifferent.  That was stoic wonder: a dead board spelling a living route while my hands stayed level.  When the first bus reached the curb, I held the door and counted people as terms worth keeping.
