At the ancient terrace at midsummer, during the slow dance of celestial bodies, Mara Venn waited with the ebony clipboard against her ribs. 
After decades of armistices, the senate had stopped calling her an envoy and called her the forever war peace negotiator, because everyone expected another front by harvest. 
The terrace held a flawed beauty made of wild thyme in cracks, a cracked mosaic's painted eye, and a moon dial stained by old smoke. 
She had come not to recite mercy but to anchor ideals in practice, since the last beautiful vow had left three river towns without water. 
Across from her, the Two Crowns and the Free Cantons sat in linen armor, each guarding the grievance it loved more than its children. 
At dawn their engineers would open the sluices, burn the granaries, and begin another summer of the war. 
Mara was operatively gentle, not soft; she placed water between enemies before she placed blame, because shame had killed more envoys than blades. 
Her ebony clipboard looked like a mourning tablet, but its clasps held timetables, well measurements, witness names, and the first ration lists for occupied mills. 
It also held the Bell Ford clause, written in her hand eleven years earlier, which had promised evacuation without naming who would carry the wounded. 
When the bridge closed, four hundred people drowned behind it, and every later treaty had praised her precision. 
She had learned the price of redemption was not apology; it was surrendering the right to be the person who repaired the damage alone. 
Only through ritual significance could she make proud rulers accept inspection, because law had become just another uniform. 
The terrace had been built for such work: midsummer shadows crossed three bronze notches while the twin planets climbed, and no later oath counted before the ancestors. 
Priests called the hour a wedding of lights; soldiers called it a deadline. 
Before the shadow reached the first notch, Mara had to muster not an army but a working peace: masons, nurses, cart drivers, clerks, and widows who could verify each road and name. 
She set them among the delegations in a half circle, so every promise would have a face, a craft, and a memory attached to it. 
The old protocols allowed the rivals to speak in poetry and leave unchanged; Mara had rewritten each verse as a task. 
For safe passage, a Crown captain would pour river water over a Canton boot; for shared wells, two children would turn the same key; for demobilized guns, each smith would nail a serial tag to the altar rail. 
The gestures were ancient, yet the ebony clipboard bound them to dates, quantities, inspectors, and penalties, so mist would not again pass as mercy. 
High Warden Ors refused first, saying a boot washing would mock his dead father, and his officers reached for their etiquette knives. 
Mara did not invoke the senate; she refilled his cup with operatively gentle hands and asked whether his father had died of water or of orders. 
The question moved through the terrace like a draft, and the old man sat down because the dead had become harder to trade than pride. 
Then the Canton advocate demanded the Bell Ford clause be struck, so no one could shelter behind her celebrated mistake. 
Every face turned toward the ebony clipboard, where the old clause lay beneath fresh amendments in a network of arrows and marginal figures. 
Mara could have said that custom protected envoys; instead she unclasped the page and read aloud the sentence that had omitted the wounded. 
She named the clerk who had warned her, the boys she had called baggage, and the minutes she had saved by leaving the bridge ambiguous. 
The price of redemption was a ledger no grief could balance. 
She signed away her sole authority to widows, deserters, and river pilots, then listed herself first for inquiry. 
That confession became the first ritual act without incense, and the terrace changed. 
Ors poured the water. 
The Canton advocate handed a well key to a Crown child. 
Smiths hammered tags until the stars seemed to ring. 
Clerks copied ration lists while the newly mustered witnesses checked every numeral against barns, ferries, and hospital beds. 
Before the twin planets cleared the last notch, the delegations had promised thirty enforceable acts. 
No one embraced; thyme rose through the painted eye, and Mara trusted broken surfaces to hold weight. 
At dawn sluice gates stayed shut and the ebony clipboard ordered a noon inspection with two enemies and one widow.
