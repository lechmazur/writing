After receiving the donor heart, Ivo Venn, the watchful dye master of the floating rock tea house, returned to his kettles with a new question. 
He wanted to learn why some people magnetize stories, because his new pulse leaned toward certain strangers before they spoke. 
The surgeons called it recovery, but Ivo felt an attention wider than his own. 
It was no voice, only a bright tightening when a tale neared the place it belonged. 
The tea house hung above a river valley on iron roots, its basalt floor mossy and its windows blurred by valley-mist wonder. 
At dawn, mist touched the cups and made every guest seem gently remembered. 
Ivo kept watch from the dye bench, grinding madder, soot, lichen, and moth scales. 
He had always believed color was patience, and the valley had taught him to wait. 
With the heart came a packet passed on by the hospital, because the donor had asked that her dye notes reach whoever received her heart. 
Inside lay a notebook of sun paths and one blue morpho wing, thin as a held breath. 
The wing was not truly blue; its color lived in angles and vanished when the room turned. 
On the first page, the donor had named her rule iridescent caution: show a truth slantwise, or scorch it. 
Beside a covered kettle she added that crowded stories under steam are quietly explosive. 
Ivo reread the warning each morning, because the urns now hummed when certain guests sat near them. 
By the third week, every story bent toward a girl who took the window seat without ordering. 
A boatman describing a broken rudder ended with a gray ribbon he had never seen. 
A nun recalling a winter bell remembered a child waiting beside a blue shawl. 
Even Ivo, asked about saffron, said that some farewells need a table left empty. 
The girl only warmed her hands around the cup, and his borrowed heart beat with recognition. 
Ivo wanted to question her, yet the notebook's warning held him back. 
He had spent his life fixing color to cloth, but stories needed a margin. 
Such a margin was what she carried, a silence with room for other lives to settle. 
To test it, he unfolded the donor's charts by tracking sun trails across the turning floor. 
The floating rock rotated on its roots, so each bright trail crossed a different table as mist thinned. 
He chalked the paths in ochre and waited for the line that would reach the window seat at the quietest hour. 
At that hour, he set the blue morpho wing in a clear glass, angled it toward the final trail, and let it illuminate the air rather than the girl. 
The choice came from iridescent caution, for a direct question might have packed every borrowed sorrow into one bursting kettle. 
When the sun touched the wing, blue ripples opened above the cups, soft as breath on old glass. 
They did not point at the girl; they gathered around the empty chair beside her. 
In that lit absence, Ivo saw the donor's last afternoon: a cup cooling, a daughter expected, a letter unfinished. 
His heart gave one hard knock, not of pain but of completion, and the room fell silent. 
The girl looked up, and her face held the same patient attention that had drawn every story to the window. 
Ivo understood that some people magnetize stories because they do not seize them; they offer an absence sturdy enough to hold them. 
He did not announce the donor, the letter, or the heart beating under his own ribs. 
Instead, following the slantwise rule, he carried the notebook to the girl and laid the blue morpho wing on its first page. 
She read the note about sun trails, then the warning that crowded stories under steam are quietly explosive, and her eyes filled without spilling. 
The guests felt the pressure leave the urns; their borrowed details drifted back into separate, harmless conversations. 
Ivo returned to his bench, no longer hunting an answer so much as guarding the conditions in which answers could appear. 
Outside, the rock rose a hand's breadth in the cooling air, and the valley below filled with pale light. 
He poured one more cup for the empty chair, letting valley-mist wonder do what explanation could not. 
By sunset, the tea house smelled of rain, saffron, and a blue that no cloth would ever hold.
