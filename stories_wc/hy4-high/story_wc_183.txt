Sella Venn rowed toward the abandoned carnival ship drifting in a foggy bay, because flowers had appeared in its locked theater.  She had come to find the garden of forgotten names, where lost names rooted when owners stopped answering.  Newspapers called her the chimera twin discoverer, though she had only proved that her vanished twin lived as other cells beneath her skin.  Quarantine fever had burned the twin's name from every ledger, photograph, lullaby, and mouth.  Only an emerald swatch card survived, a square of green silk from the costume loft marked in her mother's careful hand.  It read, "Admit one before the lamp is lit," the phrase her mother used for unkindled solace, comfort before memory gave grief a face.  The gangway gave Sella trapped freedom: she could roam any deck, but fog closed the water behind her like a palm over a mouth.  The vessel was blissfully aberrant, with carousel horses breathing through gills and booths selling tickets to places that had never existed.  She had timed her arrival as memories alter, that tide when the past revises itself and a name can vanish between one step and the next.  Already the sister in her mind had changed from solemn to laughing, and fever smoke became wet roses.  Sella pressed the emerald swatch card to the theater door, and the wood opened as if green silk had been cut into a key.  Inside, empty seats faced a stage hung with colored strings, each knotted to a tag carrying a fragment of some visitor's story.  The carnival had taken narratives instead of coins, and its barkers wove them into the threads leading down to the garden.  She understood she must recapture her sister's name through narrative threads, following each true moment that had ever made the name necessary.  A green ribbon twitched above the orchestra pit, and the other cells beneath her skin answered with a small second heartbeat.  She tied the card's loose silk to it and read the first tag: a child had once chosen which parent to forget.  The string drew her past a mirror showing Sella at seven, holding a girl whose face kept slipping into her own.  At the next tag the girl had shoved her from a curb; at the next, Sella had invented the shove to survive being left.  The changes were not lies, only memories obeying the tide, and each one shortened the green thread.  Sella spoke aloud, not names but scenes: two spoons in one bowl, a shared fever, her mother sewing two cuffs from green cloth.  With each scene a new string tightened against her palm, and the theater stopped feeling empty.  Her sister's voice came from inside her ribs, amused and afraid, asking whether being found would hurt more than being forgotten.  Sella had no honest answer, because unkindled solace had protected them by leaving the lost one faceless and the survivor almost forgiven.  Below the stage, a hatch opened on a garden where pale stems carried blank labels and names rustled like moths inside a jar.  This was the freedom of forgotten names: they could wander every tongue, yet no tongue could hold them still.  As the tide turned, labels blurred, and Sella felt the almost recovered syllable shift from Lin to Lio to something like a sob.  Panic told her to grip harder, but the threads cut her fingers whenever she tried to recapture one sound.  So she loosened her hand and told of a girl who was not a wound, not a ghost, but a second pulse learning one body's shape.  She added the mother at the lamp, the two cuffs, the card, the fog, and the choice to board when leaving was no longer offered.  The threads braided around the emerald silk, not into a rope that bound a name, but into a path wide enough for two.  A pod opened, and Sella understood that a name could live as a story instead of a label.  She said the shifting syllable aloud, and the theater lamps came on one by one; unkindled solace ended, but the face beside hers held still.  Her sister answered with a laugh from her ribs, free to be distinct and impossible to discard.  The fog opened at the gangway, but only along the green thread, offering an exit that moved wherever the telling moved.  Sella stepped into it carrying no fixed name, no lamp, and no promise of harbor, only the trapped freedom of a story that would never let her go alone.
