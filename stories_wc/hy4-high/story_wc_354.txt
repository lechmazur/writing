At the magnetic nexus at twilight the iron reeds leaned toward the last red light and sang whenever the wind forgot them.  Ilyan stood among them as an exiled chess tutor in a borrowed courier's coat, carrying no passport but a chessboard wrapped in oilcloth.  The hour held a hopeful melancholy because the city he had lost was bright enough to forgive and too proud to ask.  Below the terraces, engineers waited with levers ready before the next phase of the great reversal.  They believed they would free the capital from its ancient pull by switching the buried engines from river current to crown current.  Ilyan knew that the buried engines were answering a contract older than any crown.  The eclipsed covenant had been drafted during a dark sun, when the founders played sixty four moves against the ambassador of the lodestone.  Every spoken clause described what the city could take, while every silent move described what it must leave untouched.  Later ministers copied only the speech and called the omissions piety, so the quiet half of the bargain sank into eclipse.  At each reversal, silence returned as pressure in the rails, and the lower districts paid for forgotten restraint with crushed ceilings and crooked doors.  This time, the engineers had calculated a cleaner line, and their confidence left no room for the poor quarter's iron bones.  Ilyan had been exiled for teaching a princess to distrust such clean lines.  He had wanted to discover beauty in the difficult rather than decorate certain victory with clever names.  Mara, then thirteen, had sacrificed her queen to save a row of pawns that represented the river wards, and the council had called the lesson sedition.  His thumb found the polished basalt worry stone dent left by Mara's black rook and felt the shape of a missing note.  For six years that crescent had held a pinch of iron dust from the nexus, so it trembled whenever the field approached a forbidden interval.  Now the same crescent fitted the silence between the reeds, and Ilyan understood that exile had given him the one instrument the palace had discarded.  Mara came to the railing in a plain cloak, older, with a regent's seal hidden under her sleeve.  She did not speak, because any public pardon before the reversal would make the council sabotage the levers.  Instead she set a white pawn on his board beside a square of black basalt, asking in the old lesson whether a lost position could still shelter its people.  Ilyan answered by lowering his eyes, which meant that difficulty was not the same as defeat.  With thoughtfully precise fingers he unpacked the board, aligned its squares to the compass reeds, and waited for the next hush.  He had come to correlate the pauses in the iron choir with the silent moves of the founders, and he could do it only by translating silence into chess.  Each pause became a rank, each tremor of the stone became a file, and the dent supplied the dark square that later copyists had feared to draw.  The method was not magic, though the reeds shone blue; it was the same patient reading he had taught children who thought quiet meant absence.  Where the engineers saw empty time, Ilyan saw a fortress, a refusal, and a route by which power could return to the river.  He correlated the forbidden intervals with Mara's old pawn chain and found the hidden clause: an excluded hand must set the final switch.  The covenant had made exiles necessary witnesses, because only someone outside the bright lines could remember what the bright lines consumed.  Mara read his notation over his shoulder, and the set of her mouth changed from royal steadiness to the grief of a pupil who had finally solved the problem too late.  The switch stood inside a brass cage that no councillor could open without gloves, but an exile's bare hand would be burned and then remembered.  Ilyan considered the cost with the calm of a man studying an endgame in which the king must walk toward danger to force a draw.  He did not think of pardon, or of the chair beside the palace windows, or of students who might one day speak his name without shame.  He thought of the dent, the river wards, and the strange clean joy of a defense that looked ugly until the last move.  He opened the cage and set the switch to silence.  The rails eased; lamplight reached the wards.  Mara left the pawn; Ilyan walked east, exiled, smiling.
