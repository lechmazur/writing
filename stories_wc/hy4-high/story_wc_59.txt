From the first tremor beneath the cisterns through the discovery that waited under the dawn bell, the bedraggled prince climbed the chains of a suspended palace anchored to giant stalactites. 
Each link shivered above a black drop, and he moved in a quaking reverie, soaked with rain from caverns where no rain should fall. 
Ten years earlier his father had ordered the last orchards folded into brass registers, and the people had learned to applaud in exact meters. 
Now the prince had returned to reawaken beauty in a mechanical world, not with an army, but with a torn satchel, a bent crown, and questions. 
His tutors had called his habit a disease, because he survived exile by hypothesizing about everything, from the grief of drains to the jealousy of locked doors. 
In the palace, every corridor repeated the same polished sunrise, and every servant was a clockwork figure with a painted yawn. 
He did not curse them, for he guessed that their stiffness had begun as obedience, maybe even love, and he wrote the guess on his cuff. 
At the Hall of Origins, the Regulator, a brass tribunal with a voice like filing drawers, met him and demanded the password for perfection. 
The prince answered with a question about why the left stalactite wept mineral tears while the right one stayed dry, and the machine paused to consult a century of ledgers. 
That pause let him slip into the relic chamber, where an extinct bird's egg lay in twelve labeled fragments beneath a glass dome. 
A card said the species had sung before sunrise, and that its final egg had cracked during the first making of the royal engines. 
Another card, stamped with his mother's hand, warned that the crack was not damage but the first beat of the song. 
He began to assemble the shell on a velvet cloth, but his fingers shook so badly that the smallest piece rang against another like a tiny bell. 
The sound was bitterly sweet, carrying the orchard smell of bruised pears and the colder smell of a nursery after a fever. 
He hypothesized that the shell needed warmth, then that it needed darkness, then that it needed the palace's own trembling, and each guess changed what he did next. 
He set the fragments near a steam pipe, covered them with his cloak, and finally held them against the chain that descended from the largest stalactite. 
Those same chains carried the regulating current, so he imagined the cracked shell as a mouth pressed to the nerves of every clock. 
All night the engines argued in their sleep, while he listened for a skipped note in the grinding and marked every irregularity in soot. 
Then he understood that the makers had erased one stammer from the first lullaby, believing a clean beginning would make a clean kingdom. 
The truth arrived not as a blaze but as a shame in his bones: the famous birth of order had been a flawed genesis, founded on the refusal to let one cracked egg sing off key. 
He reopened the shell, left one seam misaligned, and used his bent crown as a wedge so the trembling chains could breathe through it. 
The egg did not hatch into a bird, for extinction is honest, but it released the pattern that had waited inside its flaw. 
The pattern entered the clocks as a bitterly sweet hesitation, and their painted yawns became startled, unprogrammed smiles. 
Along the corridors, polished sunrises loosened into colors no register had named, and the stalactites trembled with drops that fell upward in the quaking light. 
The Regulator tried to demand perfection, but its voice of filing drawers caught on a sob, and the prince, still bedraggled, did not correct it. 
He understood at last that beauty was not a lost object to restore, but a permitted imperfection, a sweet bruise in the law of gears. 
By the discovery at dawn, his question had become a law for the kingdom: if his family's first error could be forgiven, then every machine might learn to answer with a living variation. 
He knelt among the assembled fragments, heard the chains hum like a throat after long silence, and felt the palace hold him without becoming still.
