After the universe contracts, the ambered observatory deck no longer looks outward, because outward has folded back through every window.
The stars hang near enough to seem touchable, yet each one flickers with a stellar doubt that makes every measurement feel like a confession.
Mira keeps the last watch there, wrapped in a heat shawl, quietly enthusiastic about the hiss that other surviving crews have learned to hate.

That rupture is Mira's collection of foreign coins, carried from markets on worlds whose names have already been squeezed into spectra.
When a choice must be made, she shakes the coins in a clay cup and lets their metals, densities, and scars speak where the future has lied.
Now the choice is whether to spend the remaining power on warming the sleep chambers or on one final transmission into the first light.
Mira wants to wavelength align with purpose, not merely with another star, because she believes the last human act should fit the shape of the whole.
Her method is understanding static bursts, the crushed radio noise in which all distances arrive together after their long collapse.
The old astronomers called such noise failure, but Mira hears grammar in it, intervals of pain from galaxies that no longer have separate addresses.
She asks the technicians to distill the bursts through the amber lens, stripping away heat, panic, and the echoes of instruments that once disagreed.
What remains is a backwards prophecy, not a prediction of tomorrow but a memory of the end pressing backward to name its necessary beginning.
It says the universe will only close cleanly if someone at the end chooses a purpose before knowing what that purpose will cost.
The technicians fall silent, because the sentence sounds like the superstition that killed their confidence, and the stars outside pulse as if embarrassed.
Mira does not argue; she opens the clay cup, lays a square of amber glass on the deck, and calls for each coin by its mint.
There is a thin lunar drachma, a Mars transit token, a nickel from a drowned Pacific city, and a black iron coin once used for river tolls.
Their foreignness matters, since each was stamped under a different gravity and carries a slightly different memory of mass.
By tossing them across the glass, Mira turns bias into a spectrum, and the pattern of their landing gives the transmitter an unclaimed wavelength.
On the first cast, the coins choose heat for the sleep chambers, and someone begins to weep with relief.
Then the black river coin rolls from the glass, strikes a seam, and rings against the rail with a note the static has been imitating all night.
Mira smiles without showing her teeth, the way she did as a child when a forbidden machine finally confessed its hidden wheel.

The backwards prophecy settles around them then, not as thunder but as a chain of small corrections in the numbers they have already recorded.
