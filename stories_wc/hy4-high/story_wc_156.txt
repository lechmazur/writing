Mara Orr, the vivid mural restorer, stood before the unfinished wall with vermilion on her cuff and a question drying on her tongue. 
The wall ringed the old sound wave garden, where curved brick, copper shells, and coiled channels returned every voice in six obedient delays. 
For three months she had cleaned a faded mural of citizens listening at those walls; only the face of Saint Vero remained blank beneath gray overpaint. 
Chairman Orr brought the archive photograph, the appropriation paper, and a smile that expected obedience. 
He asked the question the council had rehearsed. 
'Will you restore the missing face exactly as the archive says, so we can finally be certain?' 
Everything until her answer happened in the pause between question and reply, and the sound wave garden made that pause large enough to enter. 
Mara had come to exchange certainty for curiosity, because certainty had once erased a wall she loved. 
As a girl she had watched inspectors whitewash her grandmother's orchard mural after one survey photograph declared the pears were plums. 
Her grandfather Ivo, who carved hobo nickels in freight cars, slipped her a coin that night. 
He had changed a buffalo nickel into a traveler with an enormous ear and hollowed the reverse until it rang. 
'A face can travel and still be legal tender,' he said, tapping the hobo nickel against his thumb. 
She had kept the hobo nickel in every toolbox since, not as a charm but as an argument she could hold. 
Now it lay in her palm, green with age, its carved ear pointed toward the Questions Well. 
Mara was methodically playful, numbering her jokes and painting a grape on each test tile before recording which purple matched the plaster. 
That morning she chalked six numbered ears on the stones, measured the echo delays, and hid a colored ribbon behind each shell. 
She had made a game of the counts, yet knew to the half second where Orr's last word would go. 
She meant to bewitch them through reflected sound, not into belief, but into doubt lively enough to become a question. 
She set the coin in the throat of the well and struck it once with the silver handle of her brush. 
The note jumped into a copper channel, struck the first curved wall, and returned with the hard edge of certain rubbed soft. 
By the sixth return it sounded less like certain than certain with a door left open. 
Orr blinked: the first awakening was a man hearing his own conclusion as an invitation. 
The second awakening belonged to the mural. 
The echo crossed the plaster at Mara's tested pitch, and a frost of loose overpaint sifted from Saint Vero's hidden chin. 
Under it appeared three faint mouths, a stern line, a laughing curve, and a parted O shaped for listening. 
The painted citizens woke in order, first the child with a cupped hand, then the baker, then the dog with one lifted ear. 
Dust had made everyone prefer the photograph, and sound had loosened it. 
The third awakening filled the garden. 
Each shell answered the next, sending the chairman's question through reflected sound until six delays braided into one doubtful syllable. 
Blue grasses trembled in their wave shaped beds, and the fountain held one bright drop above the basin. 
Mara understood why Ivo called such chains nested awakenings, a coin waking an ear, an ear waking a wall, a wall waking the listeners. 
Inside that larger waking, the councilors woke from the comfort of having decided. 
'What else is under the gray?' someone whispered. 
Orr lowered his photograph until the paper showed the tremor in his hand. 
Mara felt a fourth waking in herself, the old fear that certainty would win giving way to pleasure in unfinished surfaces. 
Her methodically playful chart had given wonder six places to arrive. 
The last echo died in the Questions Well like a seed, and the silence had the texture of people leaning forward. 
When the pause closed, Mara replied, 'I will restore the face as a question, if you will help me keep it open.'
