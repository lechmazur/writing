In the silver courtyard under snow, the chaste courtesan Mara waited, still as a shut mouth. 
Courtiers ringed her, breathing together, while snow erased the small evidence of feet. 
Their calm had a name in the palace ledger: alarming serenity. 
No one screamed during the harvest of echoes, because screaming had been tuned out of them. 
Each winter the Regent took private words and gave back a lullaby of conformity. 
At the courtyard's center stood the prism sanctuary under starlight, a glass casket ribbed with black iron. 
Starlight entered its facets and came out as colored threads, each one a stolen voice. 
The previous year, Mara had heard her sister asking for water inside a green thread. 
After that, every tranquil song sounded like a blanket over a drowning mouth. 
As a chaste courtesan, she was invited everywhere and trusted nowhere that mattered. 
Men paid for her attention, mistook refusal for delicacy, and confessed the knots beneath their polished sentences. 
She kept her body as her only unowned instrument, bodily poetic in every pause. 
Wrists made line breaks; breath made rhymes; stillness made men lean closer. 
The Master of Echoes had once boasted that none could unlock the sanctuary without the Regent's mouth. 
When she asked what loosened such a lock, he laughed by untangling threads in the air. 
He said the sole unguarded instant was between sigh and smile, just before obedience reached the lips. 
Mara trained a year to live inside that instant, to defy the lullaby of conformity. 
She learned the sigh's first pressure, the smile's first treason, and the silence between them. 
The harvest did not merely steal memories; it returned them as a tune that made people love their smaller selves. 
Children stopped asking why bells were silent, and widows stopped saying the names of the dead. 
The city grew kinder, quieter, and impossible to recognize. 
She refused beds and patronage, because gifts would leash the body she needed. 
Now the Master lifted a silver flute, and the Regent nodded, serene as a knife on satin. 
Mara stood in her white gown, arms frosted, face composed into the obedience she had come to break. 
The flute breathed. 
Hundreds of mouths opened, and one soft communal sigh moved over the snow like warm fog. 
Before the smile, Mara stepped out of the ring. 
No guard moved, because the guards were waiting to smile too. 
She crossed to the prism sanctuary under starlight and laid both hands on its cold facets. 
Its locks were voices braided into filaments that tightened whenever a citizen tried to remember. 
To unlock it, she worked by untangling threads, never cutting them, because cut voices died. 
Her fingers declined, paused, invited, received, the old choreography of making powerful men speak. 
The city's sigh trembled in the glass; starlight broke across her face, calm as judgment. 
A baker's red curse and a child's blue question about the moon loosened under her hands. 
Green resisted, her sister's green, as if trust feared a world trained to smile without her. 
Mara bent close and whispered nothing, because the living had said too much. 
Nothing was the one language the lullaby had never harvested. 
The green thread slackened, and the knots began dividing into separate names. 
The flute kept playing with a wrong note, small as blood in milk, while courtiers stayed peaceful, their faces shining with alarming serenity. 
Mara had no time to watch, because the smile was gathering at every mouth. 
She set her thumbs against the final knot and used the lesson: unlock by yielding, not force. 
The sanctuary opened without a sound. 
The harvest of echoes flew apart over the silver courtyard under snow, and every flake caught a stolen syllable. 
People did not cheer; they gasped, demanded water, spoke dead names, and asked why bells were silent. 
The Regent's smile arrived too late, alone and absurd, and the flute cracked in his hand. 
Guards did not seize Mara; they followed their own voices back into themselves. 
Her sister's echo did not forgive her; it said only that the water was cold and someone had heard. 
Mara bowed, bodily poetic even in exhaustion, and left before gratitude could be polished into a leash. 
Behind her, the prism sanctuary under starlight stood open, no longer an object but a doorway still requiring unlocking.
