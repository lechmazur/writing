After a wind chime falls silent, Mara Venn, the vigilant levee warden, begins her last inspection on the midnight ridgeway at twilight. 
Below her, the lowered reservoir holds a mirrored hush, doubling every exposed roof and chimney in violet water. 
The reservoir town resurfacing is not a miracle; it is drought, arithmetic, and repair gates opened before winter rain. 
Saint Orra has been underwater for forty-seven years, but tonight its main street lies exposed in mud, slate, and pale weed. 
Mara knows each dangerous seam, each cellar mouth, each wall that would crumble under a careless boot. 
Her vigilance is precisely humane: it measures risk in inches, then counts the people whom risk would wound. 
An order written to hide broken relocation promises tells her to expunge the place from maps, signs, and public minutes by morning. 
The county wants a clean record; she wants to spin stories into shared meaning before the water returns. 
She has allowed twenty-three descendants or survivors, and she has roped every unsafe threshold herself. 
At the first lantern she sets a photograph of the hotel porch, where a blue bottle catches evacuation light. 
Beside it lies a sapphire-hued liqueur label, recovered from silt and still smelling faintly of anise. 
Its ink reads Aurelian Cordial, and on the back someone has penciled the words when the water falls, set one more place. 
Mara has chosen a trail of photographs leading to a forgotten memory, because directives would make tourists and litigants of them. 
Old Nell Aster sees the school picture and recalls chalk dust on her brother's cuffs. 
The baker's daughter adds that her father sent loaves to the levee camp, although official history says the valley waited in silence. 
Their sentences are small at first, but the mirrored hush gives each one an equal reflection. 
Mara writes them in a field book, not as evidence for a claim, but as threads for a common telling. 
Later photographs show chapel benches, a long table, blue bottles, and an empty chair facing the water. 
Nobody remembers why until the trail turns down Hotel Lane, where the label's blue matches a square of color in the final photograph. 
There, under a collapsed stair, they find a biscuit tin sealed with wax and oilcloth. 
The tin contains no deeds, only name cards, a recipe, and a minute from the last supper before inundation. 
The minute says the town agreed to be flooded only if every household carried one story downstream and repeated it until the town surfaced again. 
That is the forgotten memory, not a secret treasure, and its recovery changes the silence around them. 
Nell weeps because she has repeated one more place all her life without knowing it was an instruction. 
Mara remembers her grandmother setting an empty cup at every holiday table and calling it manners, not history. 
She understands that the old people made a relay of remembrance and then forgot why they were running. 
They read names that county ledgers had shortened to numbers. 
With each name, the resurfaced town becomes less a temptation to trespass and more a responsibility to speak accurately. 
At the long table's footprint, Mara asks them to decide what the official record should say. 
They do not ask for reopening, compensation, or a shrine on unstable ground. 
They ask that wells be capped, walls fenced, names transcribed, and the county lie corrected. 
This is why Mara's plan was precisely humane: it protects bodies without amputating the past. 
Together, beneath the mirrored hush, they expunge the sentence Nothing remained from the county form. 
In its place they write that Saint Orra remains in trust, held by those who drink from its water and tell its stories. 
Mara countersigns as warden, because shared meaning needs the signature of someone answerable for flood levels. 
Near dawn, she walks the visitors back up the ridgeway, checking ropes while the forgotten memory acquires many keepers. 
Behind her, the reservoir town resurfacing ends as repair gates close, leaving the sapphire label underwater but its story in the corrected minutes. 
When the wind chime trembles, the vigilant levee warden notes that a town can be submerged without being made absent.
