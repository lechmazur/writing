The vivid mural artist, Elara, slumps against her studio window ledge mid-brushstroke, slipping softly into the fold of a daydream before she can register the fog curling off the city’s forgotten edge. 
She blinks to find herself standing in the ruined orchard district lost in centuries of thick fog, where gnarled apple boughs twist above cracked cobblestones and the air hums with the faint, static fizz of half-remembered laughter. 
For months, she had painted commissioned murals of generic sunsets and corporate logos, the spark that once made her chase light across concrete walls dimmed to a flicker, and she feels the tug of her core motivation here: to rediscover lost joy, the unscripted delight she had traded for steady paychecks. 
The fog around her is not just mist, she realizes, but a living knowledge network, strung together from the scattered memories of every person who once tended the orchard, bit into crisp apples, and chased fireflies between the rows on summer evenings. 
Half-buried in moss at the base of a crumbled garden wall, she spots a fresco fragment, its chipped surface holding a faded splotch of vermilion that looks like the edge of a child’s painted smile, a fleck of gold that glows even in the dim fog. 
When her fingers brush the cool plaster, the knowledge network hums louder, and she remembers the old craft she abandoned as a teen, when she first decided murals had to be made of paint instead of the way she naturally paints with light, catching refracted sunbeams to layer soft, glowing images that shift with the viewer’s angle. 
Tucked beneath the fresco fragment is a crumpled roll of exposed film, its canister rusted at the edges, holding decades-old frames of orchard harvests, picnic blankets spread under boughs heavy with fruit, and the same vermilion smile she saw on the plaster, belonging to a girl who once painted murals on that very garden wall. 
The knowledge network pulls the latent images from the exposed film, weaving them into thin, golden beams that cut through the fog, and Elara laughs when she realizes she does not need a brush or paint to pull those memories into view, only the same instinct she once used to draw rainbows on her bedroom wall with a flashlight and a prism as a child. 
She moves slowly, savoring the quiet giddiness of the moment, shaping the light to layer the film’s captured memories against the crumbled wall: a teenager passing a jug of cider, a dog chasing a butterfly through tall grass, an old man teaching a small child to press a paint-stained thumb to wet plaster, each image glowing soft enough that it does not fight the fog, only dances with it. 
When the last beam settles, she steps back to display the full, living mural to the empty orchard, half-expecting the fog to swallow it whole, but instead the mist catches the light, carrying fragments of the images down every twisted row until the whole district glows with the warm, familiar hum of long-dormant joy. 
A soft, pensive mirth tugs at her chest as she notices her own younger self in one of the light-woven frames, seven years old and covered in prism rainbows, grinning like she held every secret of light and color in her grubby, paint-stained palms, and she realizes she never lost that joy at all; she only buried it under layers of what she thought art was supposed to be. 
A sharp knock on her studio door yanks her back to the waking world, the fog outside her window now looking less like a barrier and more like a canvas, the half-finished corporate mural on her easel suddenly pale and lifeless next to the glow still fizzing at the edges of her vision. 
She tucks her glass prism into her jacket pocket, leaves the half-written client email about generic golden sunsets unanswered, and heads for the fog-shrouded edge of the city, ready to bring the light paintings she first dreamed up to the real orchard walls, carrying the faint echo of that daydream’s soft laugh with her every step.
