Jules, an elevator repair technician who had seen quiet, unmoored ghosts since a childhood lift fall left him clinically dead for 47 seconds, shouldered his tool bag and pushed open the warped oak door of the abandoned circus ticket office on the edge of the overgrown fairgrounds. 
The air smelled like dried cotton candy, rusted staples, and the faint, sweet rot of old paper ticket stock stuck to the counters under a quarter-inch of undisturbed dust. 
Skillfully observant from 18 years of tracing frayed cables and misaligned gears before they failed, he did not reach for his flashlight immediately, but let his eyes adjust to the thin gray light seeping through cracks in the facade, the web-thin splits in the cinder block exterior where ivy had pried its way through to curl around the old ticket booth shelves. 
The only working overhead fluorescent flickered to life when he flipped the tripped breaker by the door, filling the room with a high, tinny buzz that made his teeth ache, the sound bouncing off the dented metal ceiling tiles above the counter. 
He was not here just for the seized elevator, though that was what the property manager was paying him for; he had spent his whole adult career chasing the quiet, unspoken urge to discover what ceilings hide, the spaces between floors where lost things and lost people settled when the world forgot to look for them. 
He knelt to run a gloved finger along the edge of the counter, analyzing dust accumulation patterns the way other techs read wiring diagrams, and noted that a thick, unbroken layer of dust coated every tile except one, three feet from the elevator access hatch, where fine grit had been brushed away in a narrow, repeated arc, like someone had run a finger along its edge a thousand times. 
Right as he reached for the collapsible step ladder slung over his tool bag to pry that mismarked tile loose, the fluorescent flickered once, twice, and went dark, the buzz cutting out so abruptly the silence felt like a soft hand pressed to his ears. 
When he flipped on his headlamp, he saw him: a thin man in a frayed wool work vest stitched with the circus’s laughing clown logo, a horologist’s loupe still slung around his neck, perched on the exposed ceiling joist just above the odd tile, a polished brass horologist’s mainspring barrel cupped in his palms like a sleeping sparrow. 
Most people would have scrambled for the door, but Jules had long grown used to the weightless burden of sharing space with the unquiet dead, their uncompleted tasks resting on the air like dandelion fluff, impossible to swat away but light enough to barely feel, so he leaned against his ladder and gave the ghost a small, quiet nod to engage. 
The ghost did not speak, but tilted his chin toward the misaligned tile, and when Jules pried it free with a flathead screwdriver, he saw the source of the seized elevator: a frayed hoist cable, its strands ready to snap, wrapped tight around the very same brass horologist’s mainspring barrel the ghost held, which had slipped from his vest pocket mid-fall in 1987, when he’d climbed up to inspect the fraying cable no one else had bothered to notice. 
The circus management had spent thousands patching peeling paint and repainting the grinning clown faces on the ticket booth windows to hide cracks in the facade, but they had never bothered to look up, never noticed the failing cable, never noticed their quiet clock repairman was missing until three weeks after he fell, when his unpaid rent prompted a landlord to check his apartment. 
Jules carefully pried the wedged mainspring barrel free from the cable guide, setting it on the counter next to a half-roll of unused pink circus tickets, and when he looked back up, the ghost was gone, the weightless pressure in the room lifting just enough to let him smell the clover growing through the foundation cracks outside. 
He taped a bright red warning tag to the access hatch marking the fully frayed cable for full replacement before the renovation crew hauled any more debris, slipped the dented brass mainspring barrel into his tool bag to donate to the local clock museum on his way home, and smiled a little, knowing that for all the effort people put into patching over their visible cracks, the ceilings always kept track of the things they tried to hide.
