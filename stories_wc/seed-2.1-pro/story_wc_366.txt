The reticent lighthouse supply skipper rarely spoke more than a handful of words to the coastal keepers he serviced, his quiet demeanor hiding a warmly observant nature that caught chipped mug handles, frayed cuff edges, and unspoken longings before anyone else thought to name them. 
For 12 years, he had navigated fog-snarled channels and gale-whipped swells to deliver kerosene, tinned peaches, and darning wool, his core motivation never the pay, but a quiet drive to bottle the feeling of safety he had never known as a foster kid shuffled between cold, unlit homes. 
He knew the old tales that called Blackrock Point’s lantern room haunted, that keepers left the post after three months swearing they heard the voices of shipwrecked sailors whispering through the cracked panes, that the lamp guttered for no reason on the clearest, calmest nights. 
It was on one such night, tied to the dock after dropping off supplies, that he climbed the iron stairs to check on the 19-year-old new keeper, only to reach the top landing after the lamp flickered out, the glass room plunged into a silver, moon-drenched dark that smelled of salt and old dried lavender. 
The teen was gone, fled down the stairs with his duffel slung over one shoulder if the scuff marks on the steps were any clue, and tucked into the narrow stone ledge beneath the lens was the frayed start of an anonymous letter chain, its first entry written 70 years prior by a lighthouse keeper who had lost his sister to a storm when the lamp failed mid-gale. 
He had found that same tattered letter chain three years prior on his first supply run to the point, had added his own entry back then, writing of the way storm light turned the waves honey-gold at dawn, then left a frosted piece of sea glass he had carried in his coat pocket for a decade, smoothed by tides from a broken shard of the old lamp lens that had shattered in the 1969 storm that killed three local fishermen. 
He knew he could not relight the lamp permanently that night, not until the coast guard sent a repair crew at high tide the next day, so he turned to the old coastal custom he had learned from his grandfather, a way of stacking stones as messages to anyone watching the shore -- three high, two wide, one balanced on top to signal "watch held, lamp will return" -- that the point was not abandoned, that someone was there to stand watch. 
He dragged smooth granite cobbles from the corner of the lantern room, stacking them in that exact pattern, the cairn positioned so its shadow fell directly across the folded stack of letter chain pages to keep them from blowing out the cracked window when the wind picked up. 
This was how he had chosen to protect the quiet, fragile network of strangers who had left their small, unguarded joys and sorrows tucked in that stone ledge for decades, the same network that had held him through the long, lonely winters after his wife died at sea, when he thought he might never feel anchored or warm again. 
He sat on the cold iron floor through the small hours, watching the moonlight gild the edges of the cairn and glint off the piece of sea glass he had left with the letters, the air thrumming with that soft, luminescent ache he had come to associate with places where grief and care sat tangled together, no one needing to speak to know they were not alone. 
He did not speak to the soft, salt-kissed voices the old tales claimed haunted the room, but he left an extra line in the letter chain before he settled in to wait, writing that safety was not a light that never went out, but the quiet choice to show up and stack the stones, even when no one knew your name. 
When the coast guard crew climbed the stairs at dawn, they found the lamp repaired and lit, the cairn stacked neatly by the ledge, the letter chain tucked beneath the frosted sea glass, no note left to say who had stood watch through the dark, only the faint, warm smell of pine soap the skipper always used lingering on the iron rail, and a single fishing boat chugging back toward the mainland, its wake trailing gold in the early light.
