The junkyard prophet had wandered three miles of brine-thick coast to reach the fog sculpture garden, where half-melted glass forms loomed like half-remembered dreams and stacked crates of mold-flecked VHS tapes hummed with the hush of old videotapes left unwatched for decades. 
He was precognitively forgetful, cursed to lose the memory of a moment seconds before it arrived, so he navigated life by clinging to echoes, the faint salt-stung traces of feelings and sounds that outlasted the events that spawned them. 
For ten years, he had chased one particular echo, driven by the quiet, unshakable motivation to capture the sound of growing older, the soft, sliding note he knew existed just out of reach, the one that held the weight of laugh lines deepening, tape spools sticking, fog curling over weathered stone. 
He pulled from the frayed pocket of his oilskin coat a piece of plain chalk, nicked smooth at the edges from years of being clutched through fog and rain, the only tool he trusted to mark the points where echoes pooled thick enough to almost touch. 
He had hauled a sack of sun-bleached driftwood up the coastal trail with him, arranging driftwood as directional markers every three paces, each stick angled to point the way he had come so he would not lose his path when the coming fog swallowed his sense of direction seconds before he realized he was turned around. 
The tide pulled low as he moved between the glass sculptures, his bare feet sinking into cold, shell-dusted sand, and he paused every few steps to tilt his head, straining to locate the thin, thrumming frequency he had chased across half the coast, the one that never arrived when he was ready to hear it, always slipping away a breath before his ear could catch it. 
Tidal longing hung thick in the fog, the same pull that made the sea retreat only to surge back again, the same pull that made him return to this coast year after year even when he could not remember, in the seconds before he arrived, why he had come. 
He passed a crate of tapes labeled with smudged marker: 1998 birthday, 2003 beach trip, first steps, and he knew their unspooling magnetic tape held the same kind of echoes he chased, moments sealed away from the people who lived them, left to hum softly in the fog instead of being remembered. 
When his boot crunched on a shard of sea glass worn so smooth it held no edge, he heard it: the soft, crackling slide of time moving, the sound of a child’s shoe scuffing a sidewalk, a grandparent’s laugh catching in their throat, a tape spool turning slow as a tide, and he dropped to his knees, scrabbling for the chalk to mark the spot before the memory of hearing it slipped away before it could fully land. 
He drew a tight, wobbly circle in the sand at the center of the sculpture garden, and for three full heartbeats, the fog thinned just enough to let him see the line of driftwood markers stretching back behind him, a trail of small, intentional points leading right to the spot where the sound hummed loudest. 
He did not have a jar or a tape recorder to hold the sound, because he had long known you could not cage the sound of growing older any more than you could chain the tide; all you could do was leave markers, follow the echoes, and lean into the soft, aching pull of the longing that led you back, again and again, to the places where time hummed loud enough to feel. 
As the fog rolled thick again and the memory of the sound started to slip, as it always did, seconds before he could hold it tight, he smiled, because he knew the driftwood would point him back, the chalk mark would wait, and the tide would bring the echo home again, soft and sure, the next time he came looking.
