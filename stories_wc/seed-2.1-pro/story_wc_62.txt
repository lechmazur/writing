Elias, the thoughtful gravestone carver whose fiercely meditative focus had carved tributes for three generations of valley families, stepped into the cracked glass frame of the ancient greenhouse at dusk as the first monarchs of the annual migration drifted through broken panes like flecks of smoldering sunset. 
For three weeks, across the butterfly migration, he had set aside his chisels to walk the valley, listening to widows and orphans and distant cousins speak of their lost loved ones, determined to discover the shape of collective grief rather than carve another generic angel or scroll that felt hollow to every hand that touched it. 
He had come to retrace the last walks his grandmother Elspeth, the valley’s carver before him, had taken in the months before her death, when she told him she had finally learned to carve grief not as a sharp edge, but as something soft enough to hold. 
The air hung in thundering calm, thick with the scent of dried moss and crushed mint, no wind to stir the tattered shade cloth strung between rusted iron rafters, when he spotted the dented cedar box he remembered from his childhood, tucked under a stone ledge where she used to start tomato seeds each spring. 
Inside, resting on a stack of tattered poetry chapbooks, sat the woven ribbon roll she had spent ten years making, dyed indigo and gold and madder root, its edges stitched with tiny lines of verse saved from decades of following the family custom of trading favorite poems at dinner. 
When his fingers brushed the woven fabric, he did not see a wispy apparition, but felt the familiar weight of her hand over his, the same pressure that had guided his first chisel cuts when he was twelve, the quiet start of the spectral mentorship he had longed for since her funeral, when he thought he had lost all access to her wisdom. 
He unspooled the ribbon slowly along the old stone workbench where she had once tied bunches of lavender for grave blankets, and monarchs began to land one by one on the stitched lines, their orange wings catching the last amber light of dusk until the ribbon looked strung with living embers. 
He thought of the dinners of his boyhood, when even the quietest cousin or most gruff farmhand would share a favorite poem, lines about wild strawberries or winter fires or a dog that waited by the road, every verse a small thread of what people carried with them long after a loved one was gone. 
Settling onto the weathered bench, he fell into the fiercely meditative state he usually reserved for the hardest chisel cuts, no urge to rush, no pressure to force a design, as the migration unfolded around him, each monarch pausing for a few seconds on the ribbon before continuing south to the forests its ancestors had visited for millennia. 
He understood, finally, that he had been wrong to look for a single, sharp shape of collective grief, as if it could be carved from a single block of granite the way he carved individual headstones for single graves. 
Grief, his grandmother’s quiet voice seemed to murmur in the rustle of butterfly wings, was a woven thing, stitched from the same small, beloved memories they had traded over dinner plates for generations: a line of poetry, the taste of a favorite pie, the flash of a butterfly’s wing on a sunny afternoon, each strand soft on its own, unbreakable when held together. 
When the last monarch drifted through the greenhouse pane on its way south, he packed the ribbon roll carefully in his satchel and walked back to his workshop, his design clear in his mind: a long, low curving wall of local granite, etched not with angels or scripture, but with the short, beloved lines of verse people had shared with him on his walks, woven together like the threads of his grandmother’s ribbon. 
He would leave shallow indentations along the top of the wall, too, where visitors could tuck small mementos or press their fingers against the cool stone the way he had once pressed his fingers to his grandmother’s ribbon, a physical space to hold the weight of all the love that outlasts loss. 
As he struck the first chisel blow at dawn the next day, the thundering calm he had felt in the greenhouse settled over his workshop, and he knew his grandmother was right there, guiding his hand, as every line he carved wove individual grief into something the whole valley could hold close, together.
