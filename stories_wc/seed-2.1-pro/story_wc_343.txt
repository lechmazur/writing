The curious map apprentice, Lila, leaned against the duralumin rail of the creaking antique airship Wren’s Rest, three days adrift above the cloud line amid starling murmurations that swirled like loose ink across the honeyed dusk. 
She was diligently curious by nature, had signed on for the high-atmosphere survey run expressly to chart the next great discovery, the kind of unmarked floating isle or wind current corridor that would earn her a permanent spot in the Cartographers’ Guild ledger rather than a footnote in someone else’s expedition log. 
The rest of the crew had evacuated two days prior, when a rogue thermal gust shredded the port gas envelope and sent the radio array spinning into the void, leaving her with only tinned rations, a half-broken barometer, and the quiet, companionable press of wind against the gondola windows that softened the sharp edge of her solitude. 
On her third sweep of the lower cargo hold that afternoon, she spotted it: a dusty hopscotch board chalked directly onto the worn pine decking, its numbered squares smudged by decades of boot falls but still sharp enough to trace with a calloused finger. 
Tucked under the chipped edge of square seven was a crumpled polyglot’s phrasebook dog-ear, its thin rice paper covered in scribbled translations for sky-traveler pleasantries in seven regional dialects, ink faded to the color of dried lavender. 
She had initially written the board off as the idle doodle of a long-ago crew child, until she knelt to get a closer look and realized the chalk marks did not line up with standard hopscotch numbering at all. 
Working slowly, careful not to smudge the fragile chalk, she began to note every small deviation from the familiar childhood game, decoding its hidden guide by noting secret patterns in a dusty hopscotch board: the way the third square angled exactly 17 degrees east of magnetic north, the tiny notch cut into the fifth square that aligned with the peak of the distant floating spire she had spotted that morning, the faint glow seeping up through the cracks under the ninth square. 
When she pried up the loose pine plank under that ninth square, she found the source of the glow: a tiny, pulsing orb of stranded luminance, the fabled lost navigation light of the first Cartographers’ Guild expedition, which had vanished without a trace 120 years prior while charting the upper sky lanes. 
The orb hummed softly when she lifted it, warm as a cupped tealight, and outside the gondola, the swirling starling murmurations shifted, rearranging into a loose, arrow-shaped formation that pointed due east, toward a network of uncharted floating archipelagos draped in blooming sky-jasmine that no living cartographer had ever mapped. 
She flipped the crumpled phrasebook dog-ear over and found a scribbled note on its reverse, written in the same faded lavender ink as the translations: "The light doesn't lead you to places you can find alone—it listens for the ones who stop to read the marks the sky leaves behind." 
For a long moment, she sat cross-legged on the deck, the warm orb in one palm and the tattered paper scrap in the other, watching the starlings dip and swirl as if waving her onward, no crew to cheer her find but no loneliness to dull the thrill of it either. 
She pulled her leather-bound chart journal from her satchel, marked the hidden archipelago’s bearing in the margin next to a small sketch of the hopscotch board, and clipped the phrasebook dog-ear to the page as a bookmark, a quiet pact between two curious map apprentices separated by more than a century of sky. 
The stranded luminance pulsed a little brighter when she adjusted the airship’s rudder to match the starlings’ heading, casting gold over the hopscotch squares as if the long-gone cartographer who had drawn them was leaning over her shoulder to check her math. 
She did not feel stranded, not really; she had a heading, a light to guide her, a silent companion in the old apprentice who had hidden the greatest uncharted secret of the sky in plain sight, waiting for someone diligent enough to stop tracing only the lines printed on official maps. 
As the airship drifted east, carried by a steady, gentle wind, the starlings wove tight, swirling patterns around the gondola, chattering like old friends seeing her off on the voyage that would make her name.
