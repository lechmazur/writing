Lila, a third-generation diaspora returnee whose grandparents had fled their high-mountain valley during the 1947 border skirmishes, stepped into the catacomb orchard at the valley's edge where gnarled apple tree roots snake between sun-bleached bones jutting from crumbled tomb walls. 
She had carried her grandmother's chipped ceramic canteen and a trowel for three months of purposeful wandering, chasing the half-remembered folk tales her grandmother had told to chase the hollow feeling left when her family's small immigrant church in Toronto had shuttered, its congregation scattered when faith in a shared, rooted home had frayed too thin to hold. 
She had come to excavate the half-buried village tomb marked on her grandmother's hand-drawn map, a site locals avoided for fear of unsettled ghosts, but her warmly curious nature pushed her past the warnings, drawn as much to the soft hum of bees in the apple boughs as to the quiet stories held in the cracked stone. 
Across six weeks of slow, careful excavation, she brushed soil from shards of painted pottery, child-sized leather shoes caked in decades of mud, and weathered wooden talismans carved with the names of travelers who had never made it home, their journeys interrupted by flood, war, and the slow, unspooling displacement that had pulled her own family across an ocean. 
On the forty-third day of digging, her trowel clinked against a heavy, circular slab of sandstone half-wrapped in a thick apple root, its face etched with faded hour marks and chipped where the gnomon had once stood: the sundial base her grandmother had described as the heart of the old village, a marker that once aligned noon with the peak of the mountain overlooking the valley. 
She did not rush to pry the stone free, instead sitting cross-legged on a bed of moss to watch dappled sunlight slide across its etched marks, feeling the cautious enchantment of the space settle over her: no ghosts wailed, no curses stirred, only the soft rustle of leaves and the faint, sweet smell of fallen apples fermenting in the gaps between bone and root. 
Over the final two weeks of the excavation, she talked to the few remaining elders who lived in the scattered homesteads below the orchard, learning that the sundial had been shattered the day the soldiers came, its pieces carried off by villagers fleeing the violence, each person taking a shard as a promise to return that most had never been able to keep. 
She knew then that her months of purposeful wandering had not been a random search, but a quiet pilgrimage to rebuild not just the sundial, but the frayed thread of connection that had snapped when the village dispersed, when her own family had stopped believing they could ever belong to the land their bones had come from. 
She sent word to diaspora communities across three continents, tracking down the families who had carried shards of the sundial away as part of the excavation's community curation work, explaining that the sundial base sat waiting in the orchard, held fast by roots that had grown around it even when the people had been gone. 
A teenager from Buenos Aires sent the chipped bronze gnomon, found in his abuela's jewelry box; a teacher in Vancouver sent a wedge of sandstone etched with a mark for the harvest moon; an elderly man in Nairobi sent the final shard, wrapped in a scrap of the same indigo cloth Lila's grandmother had used to tie her hair for village festivals. 
When she set the repaired sundial back on its base at noon on the autumn equinox, marking the official end of the tomb excavation, the shadow fell sharp and true across the mountain peak, and she felt the lost faith she had chased across oceans settle soft in her chest--not a faith in grand, unbroken stories, but in the quiet way interrupted journeys can loop back, held fast by roots and memory and the choice to show up and build something new from what remains. 
Around her, apples fell soft into the grass, and a bee droned past, as if the orchard itself approved.
