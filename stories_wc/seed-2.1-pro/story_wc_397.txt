Elara, a euthanasia tourist who had traveled to Zurich with a one-way ticket and a carefully curated playlist for her final moments, woke up changed on what should have been her third day dead, her incurable neurodegenerative condition vanished without medical explanation. 
She found she gripped reality loosely in those first disorienting weeks, as if the world might dissolve back into the soft, expected dark if she held too tight to the taste of coffee, the crunch of gravel underfoot, the calls of gulls wheeling overhead. 
Unmoored by a reprieve she had never asked for, she sought a way to learn the sound of approaching endings, the quiet cues she had missed when her own planned end dissolved like mist on glass. 
She found that path through disaster relief volunteering, signing on for a post-storm deployment to a remote drift-ice marina where a late April gale had splintered docks, crushed small fishing boats under house-sized ice floes, and scattered decades of residents’ most cherished belongings into the slush-choked harbor. 
Each morning, she would climb into a flat-bottomed skiff with a mesh net and trawl the narrow gaps between grinding ice pans, pulling up splintered oars, waterlogged photo albums, chipped ceramic mugs, and child-sized rubber boots caked in salt. 
The work required a fold in patience, the kind that did not snap when the net came up empty for hours, when a wind gust stung her cheeks raw, when a resident would break down holding a water-warped letter she had pulled from the waves, its words blurred beyond all recognition. 
She had arrived at the marina with nothing but a change of waterproof overalls and the heirloom lace parasol tip she had tucked into her coat pocket before traveling to Zurich, the carved brass finial all that remained of the parasol her grandmother had carried to every summer picnic and funeral for 60 years. 
For weeks, she thought the sound of approaching endings would be a harsh thing: the crack of splitting ice, the snap of a frayed rope, the rattle of a last breath in a failing chest. 
She listened so closely she barely noticed when the other volunteers began leaving mugs of hot cocoa on her skiff seat, when the marina’s old harbormaster taught her to read the shift of wind on ice so she would not get stranded between floes, when Mabel, a 78-year-old fisherman’s widow whose home had been swept off its foundation, started leaving loaves of sourdough on the dorm porch for everyone. 
It was late on a pale gold afternoon, when she sat on a splintered piling twisting the brass parasol tip between her cold fingers, that she finally heard what she had come for, carried on the same wind that tangles hair and carries pine scent and salt for miles. 
The sound was not a crack or a rattle, but a soft, thrumming hush: the shift of ice as it drifted slowly out to sea, the murmur of volunteers sharing bread on the dock, the faint, warm lilt of a neighbor calling a child in for dinner as the light slanted low. 
She realized then that approaching endings did not roar to announce themselves, but drifted soft as windborne solace, weaving into the fabric of ordinary days so gently that you could miss them if you were tensed only for disaster. 
The loose grip she had kept on reality since waking softened, not into certainty, but into the same folded patience she had learned hauling nets through slush, a willingness to sit with both the coming loss and the warm, bread-scented present without clinging too tight to either. 
That evening, she drilled a small hole in the brass parasol tip and strung it on a length of frayed dock line to hang from the marina’s new welcome sign, a marker not of the death she had planned, but of the quiet, unscripted life she had trawled from the ice and wind. 
As the first star pricked through the pale twilight, she leaned against the sign and let the wind fill her lungs, no longer waiting for an ending to outrun, just listening, soft and open, to every sound the world carried her way.
