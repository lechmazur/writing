When clocks run sideways, their brass hands curling into loose spirals instead of marking steady minutes, the ardent ferry deckhand Kael polishes the chipped rail of the commuter ferry that has a habit of vanishing mid-crossing, his resiliently positive outlook unshaken even when passengers blink out of their vinyl seats midsentence. 
The vessel docks this sideways-hour not at the concrete commuter pier strung with flickering sodium lights, but at the lip of a spectral grotto under starlight, where cave walls glow with the faint, drifting faces of travelers who slipped between timelines on prior crossings, and the air hums with the quiet static of unfinished journeys. 
Tucked in the breast pocket of his oilskin coat, Kael carries an empty hourglass, its glass frosted at the edges from years of exposure to timeline bleed, no sand left to mark the difference between a second that was, a second that is, and a second that might yet be. 
He took the deckhand job three years prior, after he watched his younger sister, who had been sitting across from him on the 7:12 commute laughing over a spilled coffee, vanish when the ferry phased out of the prime timeline, a split-second shift that left only her half-empty paper cup rolling across the scuffed floor. 
His quiet, unshakable drive since that day has been to merge parallel timelines, stitching the frayed gaps where commuters slip away so no one else is left staring at an empty seat across the aisle, wondering which version of the world holds the person they love. 
He knows the ferry’s vanishings are not random glitches, but shifts that follow interconnected patterns: the way frost ferns spread in identical whorls across the wheelhouse window each time a phase approaches, the rhythm of waves lapping the hull that matches the pulse of commuters’ half-remembered dreams, the faint chime that sounds in every passenger’s pocket right before the world warps. 
Tonight, in the grotto where starlight seeps through cracks in the cave ceiling to dapple the dark water like scattered sand, he unclips the crackling marine radio from his belt, ready to send the signal that will align the timelines instead of letting the ferry slip further into the void between worlds. 
He does not broadcast a mayday or a call for help, but a sequence of soft, rhythmic tones that match every interconnected pattern he has mapped over three years of crossings: the thrum of the ferry’s engine at idle, the lilt of his sister’s laugh the day she vanished, the quiet sigh of passengers leaning against windows as they commute between homes and jobs and lives that never quite feel fully theirs. 
As he holds the transmit key down, he upends the empty hourglass, watching starlight catch in its frosted glass as the grotto hums in answer, the ferry’s hull solidifying instead of fading, the faint faces on the cave walls softening into the shapes of passengers who had vanished over decades of sideways crossings, blinking as they find themselves back on the solid, if shimmering, deck planks. 
He spots his sister across the deck, holding her crumpled paper coffee cup, and lifts a hand in warm greeting, no rush to close the distance because he knows the truth of this work: there is no final, fixed dock to reach, no permanent arrival where the ferry stops moving and every timeline sits perfectly still. 
The permanent transient rhythm of the ferry, always leaving, always arriving, never fully in one world or the next, is not a flaw to fix, but a space to hold, a threshold where people who once slipped through the cracks can stay connected across every version of their lives. 
He clips the radio back to his belt, gives the rail one last affectionate polish, and waves the ferry away from the grotto dock as clocks resume their sideways coiling, the empty hourglass still held loosely in his other hand, no sand needed to measure the worth of a journey that has no end.
