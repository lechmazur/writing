Three years after the last lighthouse keeper locked the cobblestone cottage door and moved inland to be closer to his granddaughter, the arboretum perched on a seaside cliff still held the salt-kissed hush of tides turning below gnarled Monterey cypress, wild sea thrift, and spindly coastal redwood saplings. 
Lila, a drone strike wedding survivor who carried a thin, pale scar slanting across her left jaw from the explosion that killed her fiance and half their wedding guest list, had taken on the work of tending the space after finding an old volunteer flier taped to the town library bulletin board six months prior. 
She was quietly talented at noticing the small, unspoken signals of grief and hope in the visitors who wandered the winding gravel paths, from the tight hunch of a teen fresh from a fight with their parent to the soft, tentative smile of a person bringing a handful of a loved one's favorite flower to tuck into a planter. 
She read these cues through somatic markers, the tightness of a throat, the faint tremor of a hand, the split-second pause before a breath, rather than relying on the careful, polite words people offered when they realized she carried a visible scar and a story they were often too afraid to ask about. 
Her work in the arboretum was not an attempt to outrun her own grief, but to anchor optimism in realism, to hold space for the fact that pain did not erase beauty, just as salt spray and coastal wind did not stop the hardiest wildflowers from pushing through cracked rock each spring. 
The only thing she carried in the deep pocket of her canvas work vest, besides pruning shears and seed packets, was a crumpled photograph taken 10 minutes before the strike, showing her and her fiance laughing, flower crowns askew, his mother holding a plate of honey cakes just out of frame. 
Over months of tending the paths and talking to visitors, she had cultivated what the old lighthouse keeper, who stopped by once a month to check on the redwoods, called a pearl of stillness: a quiet, unshakable center that did not flinch when a visitor's voice cracked, when a foghorn blared from the now-automated lighthouse, when memory pulled her back to the sound of the drone's high, thin whine overhead. 
One crisp October afternoon, as she knelt to tuck a native sea thrift seedling into a crack in the cliff edge, she heard that same high, thin whine cutting through the salt wind, and every muscle in her body threatened to freeze, the old terror rising so fast it made her jaw throb where the scar pulled tight. 
She pressed a palm to the crumpled photograph in her pocket, breathing slow until her eyes focused on the source of the sound: a tween boy standing at the arboretum entrance, fumbling with the controls of a small camera drone he had gotten for his birthday, his mother hovering a few feet away holding a thermos of hot cocoa. 
She noticed the boy's shoulders hunch, the nervous flick of his eyes to the "no unauthorized drones" sign posted at the gate, the faint flush climbing his neck, somatic markers that told her he was not a threat, just a kid excited to capture footage of the waves curling against the cliff far below. 
Instead of telling him to put the drone away, she waved him over, pointing to a stand of cypress trees where hawks often nested, explaining the flight path that would let him get the best view of the waves without disturbing the birds or the quiet of the space. 
As the boy whooped and guided his drone out over the water, she pulled the crumpled photograph from her pocket for a second, tracing the edge of her fiance's smile, and felt that pearl of stillness settle warmer in her chest, proof that realism did not require closing herself off to joy, and that optimism did not require pretending the scar, or the loss, had never happened. 
A gust of salt wind tangled her hair as she knelt back down to tuck soil around the sea thrift seedling, holding both the memory of loss and the sound of the boy's laughter equally, gently, in the quiet cliffside air.
