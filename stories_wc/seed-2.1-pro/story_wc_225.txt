The exiled chess tutor knelt in the cobblestone square of the time-frozen village, her knees pressed to moss that had not grown an inch in the three years since the frost-curse locked every villager mid-step, mid-laugh, mid-goodbye. 
It was the lull of distant thunder, the thin, ringing quiet between storm rolls that carried no bird cry, no creak of timber, no sound at all save the soft rasp of her own breath, the woven hush that had wrapped the valley the day she failed to warn the elders of the raiding party crossing the high pass. 
That day, she had been hunched over a carved oak chessboard with the lord’s 12-year-old son, walking him through a labyrinthine knight fork that would win him the village tournament, so focused on mapping piece movements she had ignored the watchtower bell’s faint, frantic chime until it cut off abruptly. 
She had not run when the raider-witch’s curse settled, but she had exiled herself from the company of the frozen, sleeping villagers for two years, sleeping in a root cellar at the village edge, too ashamed to look at the boy’s frozen, excited smile as he reached for his knight piece. 
She fidgeted productively through every soundless, unchanging day, her fingers never still as she twisted dry grass into cord, polished the dented chess pieces she salvaged from the lord’s hall, and picked at the frayed edge of the founders’ tapestry strung between the market oak and the stone well, the same tapestry she had walked past a hundred times as a girl without ever really seeing it. 
It was during one of those quiet, repetitive motions, three months prior, that she noticed the threads in the tapestry’s border did not match the pastoral scenes woven into its center, that the frayed, faded strands formed tight, turning paths that matched the knight’s tours she had taught her pupil for years. 
She had spent every day since working the threads loose, her fingers moving with the same steady focus she once brought to endgame positions, by decoding frayed threads in a tapestry’s border to find the counter-curse the founders had woven into the fabric long before any raider-witch had crossed the pass. 
She learned to winnow the strong, memory-charged wool and silk strands from the rotted, decoy threads the founders had woven to fool curse-weavers, separating each path as carefully as she once separated winning chess lines from losing traps, until the shape of the ward revealed itself at the border’s center: a platinum labyrinth no bigger than her thumbnail, spun from wire-thin metal thread that glowed even in the muted, storm-dim light. 
Her posture held the fierce repose of a player who had studied every possible response, every forking path, every chance of misstep, as she reached out to press her thumb to the tiny labyrinth, no shake in her hand, no gasp in her throat, even as the first roll of post-lull thunder rumbled close enough to stir the edges of her wool shawl. 
She did not do it for praise, or for the gratitude of the villagers who would wake in moments to find the curse lifted, the raider threat long faded into the valley’s old stories; she did it to forgive herself for past mistakes, for the day she had let a chess game matter more than the people she was sworn to care for, for the years she had hidden in the dark rather than reaching for the solution right in front of her. 
The platinum labyrinth warmed under her thumb as the woven hush split open, sound flooding back in a rush: rustling oak leaves, the distant gurgle of the well, the boy’s laugh as he finally landed the knight fork he had spent the cursed afternoon practicing, three years frozen in time. 
She did not stand to greet them immediately, but sat cross-legged in the moss for a long beat, letting the thunder roll overhead, the weight she had carried for three years lightening until she could breathe deep, unapologetic, ready to teach again, ready to stay.
