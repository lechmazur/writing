Kaelin, a celestial signal interpreter by trade, hauled her tool kit up the final rusted ladder rung to the central array platform of the abandoned radio tower city suspended over the sheer, pine-fringed drop of Ironwell Gorge, arriving exactly as the moon reached its closest perigee of the 12-year cycle. 
She did not come chasing the loud, crackling ghost broadcasts most hobbyists sought from the rusted spires, because her core work was rooted in a quiet, unshakable motivation: to find the beginning in every ending. 
Most signal chasers assumed the tower arrays held nothing but fragmented snippets of old variety shows, emergency weather alerts, and the faint, warbled hum of deep space radiation, but Kaelin knew the most powerful signals never announced themselves with fanfare. 
She had tracked a faint, persistent signature to this platform for three years, a signature tied to a relic a 1950s tower technician had welded to the array’s core: a chipped Hollywood star fragment, pried loose from the cracked terrazzo of a silent film actor’s Walk of Fame plaque when the technician had driven west to help build the city’s broadcast network. 
Local legend claimed the fragment held a shard of hush, the thin, resonant quiet that settles over a room the second a final performance ends, the gap between the last line of dialogue and the first ripple of applause, the pause where every possible new story waits to be born. 
Other interpreters had written the signature off as corrupted static, writing off any signal that did not fit their pre-calibrated charts of known deep space frequencies, but Kaelin approached every array the same way: by discarding assumptions. 
She unclipped her beat-up frequency counter from her belt, brushed decades of bird droppings and rust flecks off the welded star fragment, and began to tune the array’s rusted dials, ignoring the standard markers that usually guided her work. 
For ten minutes she turned the dials past crackling newscasts of the city’s final evacuation in 1987, past old radio play cliffhangers cut off mid-line, past the deep, thudding pulse of the moon’s gravitational pull tugging at the gorge’s stone walls, until the air around her shifted into something serenely electric. 
The static fell away completely, leaving no voice, no music, no urgent alert, only the soft, living hum of the shard of hush thrumming through the terrazzo fragment under her fingertips. 
Leaning in, she did not hear grand cosmic secrets or lost recordings of the star’s most famous performances, but the small, sharp, bright starts tucked into every recorded end the towers had ever witnessed. 
She heard the nervous laugh of the 1950s technician as he welded the star fragment to the array, fresh off a cross-country drive after his Ohio farm had failed, arriving in the gorge to help build something that could send voices across mountain ranges. 
She heard the silent film actor’s quiet, steadying breath before he spoke his first ever radio line, months after the advent of talkies had left his screen career in tatters, stepping into a new medium he had once thought was for people who did not know how to speak with their eyes. 
She even heard the soft, tired optimism of the last tower maintenance worker as he locked the control room door during the 1987 evacuation, tucking a packet of native wildflower seeds into the crack of the doorframe for whoever might climb up once the landslide risk passed. 
Kaelin pulled a small paper notebook from her satchel, jotting down the exact frequency of the hush so other travelers could tune into it too, and left a small offering of her own: a shard of the broken satellite dish she had pulled from her grandmother’s backyard after her childhood astronomy camp shut down, tucking it next to the Hollywood star fragment as a new marker of a start tucked into an end. 
As the moon began to pull away from its perigee close approach, she slung her kit back over her shoulder and started the climb down, already planning her next trip to a decommissioned lighthouse on the northern coast, where another set of discarded instruments waited to give up their quiet, hidden starts.
