Three weeks after the record-breaking drought ended, preventable death investigator Mara Voss stepped off the buckled maintenance catwalk into the lowest stretch of the city’s oldest subway maintenance tunnel, her boots squelching in rainwater that had seeped through cracked concrete for days. 
She moved with the watchfully calm precision she had honed over six years on the job, every sense tuned for clues that would explain why a 17-year-old transit worker had drowned here two nights prior, trapped when a storm drain blocked with decades of accumulated detritus flooded the narrow passage faster than he could climb to safety. 
Her department required all casework to be conducted through empirical observation, no room for anecdote or intuitive leaps, a rule she had clung to for years as she tried to break free from the controlling influence of her grandmother, a storied local historian who had raised her to parrot carefully curated tales of the city’s abolitionist past instead of letting her form her own judgments. 
Half-buried in the muck at the tunnel’s blind end, wedged beneath the same rusted grate that had blocked the teen’s escape, she spotted a dented, soot-stained tin lantern, its punched tin sides etched with the tiny, staggered star pattern she recognized, from a hundred forced childhood recitations, as a lantern from the Underground Railroad. 
When her gloved fingers curled around the lantern’s cool metal handle, a low, thrumming hum began to reverberate up her arm, and she froze, expecting the sharp, intrusive flood of her grandmother’s scripted stories, only to be met with a cascade of unmoored, entangled memories that belonged to no single person. 
There was the gasp of a 19-year-old freedom seeker, huddled in this same passage in 1857, holding the lantern low as he waited for a guide to lead him north to safety, his boots caked in the same red clay that had washed down from the hills during the drought’s final, brutal dust storms. 
There was the quiet pride of a 1970s subway maintenance worker, who had found the lantern during a track repair and tucked it behind the grate rather than turn it over to a city government that had erased every marker of Black history in the transit system, leaving a crumpled note tucked in its base she could now almost read through the haze of overlapping recollection. 
Last, there was the bright, nervous excitement of the 17-year-old who had died, who had spotted the lantern on his first solo shift, researched the star pattern on his lunch break, and planned to bring it to the city’s small community history center to apply for a youth heritage scholarship, his foot slipping as floodwater surged around him before he could climb to safety. 
She did not dismiss the tangle of impressions as fantasy, as her department manual would demand, instead leaning into the empirical observation she had trained her whole career to trust, noting the faint clay residue on the lantern’s base, the 1972 transit union sticker tucked under its handle, the smudge of the teen’s cherry-flavored lip balm on its tin rim where he had lifted it to get a better look in the dark. 
For years, she had seen those childhood history lessons as chains, the memorized dates and patterns a cage her grandmother had built to keep her tied to a legacy she never got to choose, but standing in the damp tunnel, the lantern warm in her hand, she felt the sharp edge of that control soften, no longer a weight pressing down but a thread tangled with the hopes of everyone who had ever stood in that dark space reaching for something better. 
She tucked the lantern carefully into her evidence bag, alongside the crumpled union note she pulled from its base, and climbed back toward the catwalk, already drafting her report that would recommend new flood grates, permanent historic markers in the tunnel, and a scholarship in the dead teen’s name, no longer fighting to escape the stories that had shaped her, but choosing to carry them forward on her own terms. 
The hum of the lantern faded to a soft, steady warmth against her side as she walked toward the tunnel entrance, where sunlight slanted through the access grate, bright with the promise of rain that would no longer bring unmarked death or forgotten stories.
