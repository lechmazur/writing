The crestfallen portrait artist huddled in the forgotten library tucked three stories below the lantern room of the decommissioned Cape Thalassa lighthouse, her charcoal-stained fingers brushing the cracked leather spine of a sailcloth-bound book when the first low groan of shifting stone rumbled up from the foundation. 
It was the hour when the walls close in, the lighthouse's long-dormant magic contracting the small, book-lined space to press against the edges of the unspoken grief she had carried for two years, ever since her sister's fishing boat vanished in a storm she had chosen to paint out of every commission she'd taken since. 
At her feet sat the broken tarot card box, its cedar splits gaping where it had crashed to the cobblestones when she stumbled up the lighthouse steps an hour prior, its hand-painted Major Arcana cards spilling across the floor to show fragmented glimpses of worlds that folded into one another where the dim lantern light touched them: a wave curling into a charcoal stick, a lantern flame blooming into her sister's sun-chapped cheek, a closed door melting into the dark curve of a stairwell leading down. 
This was fragmented reality made tangible, the price of hiding from the parts of the story she had sanded smooth for wealthy clients who wanted portraits that showed only their polished smiles, none of the cracked grief or quiet longing that lived beneath their skin. 
She leaned forward, fingers hovering over the frayed edge of The Moon card, and let the rhetorical questions she had swallowed for two years bubble past her lips, unanswerable and soft as sea spray: Who was she to capture light on canvas if she feared the dark that gave it shape? What good was a portrait that erased the very scars that made a person recognizable to the ones who loved them? Why had she run from the memory of her sister's laugh when it was the only thread that could stitch her back together? 
She knew then her motivation was not to run from the shrinking walls or the shards of reality nipping at her cuffs, but to descend into necessary darkness, the shadowed stairwell that curled below the library stacks where the lighthouse kept all the fragments people had discarded in their rush to stand in uncomplicated light. 
Before she could take that first step down, though, she knew she had to redesign the fragile, splintering threshold between the half-real library and the dark below, for the fragmented shards would cut her to pieces if she tried to push through without weaving them into something that could hold her weight. 
She would do it by untangling threads: the frayed linen cords from the spines of water-warped poetry books, the gossamer strands of memory that glowed faintly silver where they drifted between the tarot cards, the thin, strong line of her sister's old fishing braid she had kept tucked in her jacket pocket since the Coast Guard returned her sister's empty tackle box. 
As the walls crept closer, so close she could smell the salt crusted in the stone cracks, she wove the untangled threads into a narrow, flexible bridge across the splintered threshold, each strand glowing with the soft, honeyed light of every portrait she had ever been too afraid to paint: the widower with the scar across his jaw who missed his wife's terrible cooking, the teen girl with the dyed blue hair who wanted to be seen beyond her report cards, her sister, grinning, holding up a striped bass she had caught at dawn. 
The luminescent ache of it settled in her chest, warm and sharp, as she stepped onto the thread bridge, leaving the half-finished commissions and the broken tarot box behind for a moment, no longer running from the dark that waited below because she knew it did not hold only sorrow--it held every piece of the people she loved, every truth she had erased, every portrait waiting to be remade when she climbed back up. 
When her boot touched the first dark step, the walls stopped their groaning, and the faint glow of the woven threads lit her way down, steady as the lighthouse beam that had guided ships home for a hundred years.
