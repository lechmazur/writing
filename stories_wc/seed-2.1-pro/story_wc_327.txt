In the canyon of endless echoes, where towers of fused prehistoric bone rose three hundred feet above wind-scoured stone, the copper-veined storyteller perched on a narrow ledge as the last thin syllables of her people's language curled away on the dry, gritty wind. 
Quietly articulate even when alone, she did not weep or call out to beg the words to stay, knowing sound never truly vanished in this place; it only tangled, waiting to be pulled into coherent shape. 
Behind her gaped the mouth of the tunnel of echoes, a narrow passage carved by centuries of wind through the solid core of the tallest bone tower, where every sound made in the canyon bounced and warped and repeated until it lost all connection to its source. 
Tucked in the fold of her leather belt sat the flawed diamond rumored to be cursed, a cloudy, cracked stone her grandmother had passed down, said to steal the voices of anyone who tried to speak a dead language within the tunnel's walls. 
Her life's work, the task she had trained for since the copper veins first spiderwebbed across her cheekbones at puberty, was to translate texture into sound: to turn the rough pock of bone tower walls, the soft drag of sand over stone, the sharp cold of high desert night, into words that could outlive the last native speaker of her tongue. 
For three days she had waited on the ledge, watching the canyon's resident flock of cliff swallows wheel and dive over the bone towers, their shifting formations mapping the invisible currents of wind and echo that snaked through the canyon's every crevice. 
Old tales had long mislabeled the diamond as cursed, she knew; it did not steal voices, but absorbed unmoored sound, holding every echo that brushed its faceted, cracked surface until it could be guided into a stable, lasting form. 
She would harness the tangled echoes held in the tunnel, and the stored sound in the diamond, not by forcing words through her own fading throat, but through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight, whose synchronized movements could stitch disjointed sound into a sequence no wind could erase. 
When the flock banked hard to ride a thermal rising straight up the tunnel's mouth, she lifted the diamond high, letting the sharp afternoon sun hit its cracked facets and split light into a hundred tiny rainbows that caught the swallows' wings as they darted past. 
As the first swallow passed through a beam of refracted light, the diamond hummed, releasing a soft thread of sound: the rough grit of sand under bare feet, the first word she had ever spoken as a child, the low thrum of her grandmother's lullaby. 
Each shift in the flock's formation pulled a new sound from the tunnel's tangled echoes, mapping texture to tone the way ink maps rivers on parchment, no single bird carrying a full word but the whole flock carrying every syllable the language had ever known. 
She did not speak as the flock wove the sounds together, because she knew her own voice would be the last added to the tapestry, the final thread stitched in the exact moment her people's language ceased to live on human tongues. 
When the flock pulled up into a tight spiral high above the canyon, the air filled with a sound no wind could disperse: the texture of sun-warmed bone, the taste of desert rain, the quiet weight of generations, all held in the cadence of wingbeats that would echo off the canyon walls for as long as the swallows returned each spring. 
Travelers who crossed the canyon in later years would hear those layered, textured sounds on the wind, never guessing they were listening to a language preserved across its own slow death, carried not by scrolls or memorized verses, but by the quietly articulate storyteller who had learned to listen to the world as carefully as she spoke.
