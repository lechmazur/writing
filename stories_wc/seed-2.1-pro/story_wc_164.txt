The sea-foam recollectionist hunkered in the iridescent cave inside a giant crystal geode, their frothy edges gilded by refracted amethyst and aquamarine light as they raced the quiet, unstopping deadline of before memory fades. 
Their core motivation, the quiet drive that had pulled them through the narrow tidal crack into the geode’s hollow heart three tides prior, was to discover why some paths disappear. 
For months, they had traced cliffside trails and shoreline footpaths only to find whole stretches erased, no boot print, no worn blade of grass, no lingering echo of laughter or quiet heartbreak left to mark where travelers had once walked, leaving only gaping missing pieces no local elder or tide pool spirit could explain. 
As a being woven from compacted sea foam, they bore a unique attribute: they transliterated emotions, turning the faint, unspoken feeling threaded through every object and memory into fine, salt-crusted script that glowed soft silver against their frothy skin. 
Their only tool for the work was a jagged shard of crater glass, forged when a falling meteor seared a hole in the headland a thousand years prior, its dark, pocked surface holding faint flecks of starlight that amplified the quiet emotional signatures of even the smallest, most discarded objects. 
Their method of inquiry was unplanned, cobbled together from the scattered seeds dropped by a migrating arctic tern that had rested in the geode two days prior, the tiny, buff-colored seaberry seeds coated in dust and pollen from every cliff and meadow the bird had crossed on its journey north. 
They settled cross-legged on a bed of crushed crystal, laying the crater glass flat on their lap and pressing one seed at a time to its dark surface, letting their innate gift pull the faint emotions caught in each seed’s papery coat into legible, glowing script. 
One seed held the giddy thrill of a child chasing a butterfly off the main path, their sneakers skidding on moss as they ducked under blackberry brambles. 
Another held the soft, tender ache of a teenager carving their first love’s initials into a weathered signpost, too shy to say the words out loud so they left them for the wind to find. 
A third held the quiet relief of a widower pausing on a steep climb to catch his breath, smiling as he remembered how his late wife had always stopped at that exact spot to feed the chickadees. 
None of the seeds held a memory of the paths ending, of rockslides or floodwaters or official closure signs blocking the way, which meant the recollectionist had to conjecture, stitching the faint, disconnected emotional threads together to fill the gaps left by the missing pieces. 
They sat with that thought for a long moment, watching geode light dance across the crater glass as a small, fizzing laugh bubbled up through their frothy chest, the sound soft as surf pulling away from pebbles. 
The conclusion they landed on carried the particular bittersweet warmth of cheerful gloom, the kind of truth that tugs at the corner of your mouth even as it makes your eyes prickle with quiet tears. 
Paths did not disappear because of erosion or neglect, they realized, but because people stopped taking them for no reason at all, stopped chasing butterflies off-trail, stopped pausing to carve initials or feed chickadees, stopped wandering just for the sake of wandering. 
When every walker stuck only to the marked, efficient routes that got them from point A to point B as fast as possible, the unplanned, meandering paths had no reason to stay, dissolving soft as sea foam back into the landscape, leaving only faint emotional traces before memory fades entirely. 
The recollectionist did not mourn this truth, exactly; there was no anger in the realization, only a soft, sunny sort of sadness, the same feeling that comes from finding a faded photograph of a day you can almost, but not quite, remember. 
They gathered the remaining seeds into a small fold of their frothy form, tucking the crater glass into the same makeshift pocket as they prepared to let the next high tide carry them back out to the shoreline. 
They knew they could not bring the vanished paths back, but they could plant the memory-dusted seeds along the cliff edges, letting them grow into berry-laden bushes that would tug at walkers’ curiosity, inviting them to step off the paved route, to wander, to make new unplanned paths before the last traces of the old ones slipped away entirely.
