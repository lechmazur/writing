The fever broke an hour before dawn, when the first pale green streaks of aurora bled over the jagged ridgeline above the long-shuttered mountain weather station. 
Elara, the station’s last keeper and a fault-tolerant oracle, one who did not shatter when visions warred, when forecasts frayed, when the future refused to lock into a single unshakable track, pushed herself up on the splintered cot where she had tossed for three straight days, her joints throbbing with the thin, biting high-altitude air. 
She had climbed to the station three weeks prior, driven by a quiet, unshakable motivation: to find the weight of a swallowed truth, the one she had clamped down on 12 years prior, when she had told park rangers her botanist sister’s fall from the ridgeline was a random, unforeseeable accident. 
For a decade, she had done the work of an oracle the only way that did not split her open: by occupying margins, lingering at the frayed edges of storm systems and half-formed futures instead of reaching for the sharp, bright, unyielding certainties other seers burned themselves up chasing. 
She was peripherally aware, as she swung her legs over the cot edge, of the aurora rippling in soft, mourning veils outside the cracked window, of the wind shifting to carry the sharp, metallic tang of old mine runoff, of the faint, familiar crackle of her sister’s old field notebook tucked under the station’s warped desk. 
She crossed the creaky floor in her frayed wool socks, pulled the notebook free, and flipped to its final page, where her sister had pressed a meticulously rendered botanical illustration of the rare alpine paintbrush that only grew in soils saturated with heavy metal leachate, the flower her sister had been documenting the day she fell. 
The mining company that had run the operations above the treeline had found Elara three days after the fall, offered her enough funding to keep the weather station running for a decade in exchange for omitting the flower, the pollution, the sister’s final findings from her report to rangers, and she had taken it, tucking the guilt down so deep she thought it would dissolve into bone. 
For 12 years, she had held tight to the certainty that her choice had been necessary, that the station’s decades of storm data had saved more lives than exposing the mine would have, that her sister would have understood the tradeoff. 
Now, as her thumb brushed the illustration’s petal edge, she watched a fleck of ash from the dead cabin stove drift down to smudge the fine ink line marking the flower’s crimson tip, and she felt those long-held certainties begin swaying, soft as the aurora curtains rippling outside, unmoored by the quiet realization that the mine had been shuttered for eight years anyway, that the funding had run out long before the station closed, that she had traded her sister’s legacy for nothing but a half-decade of empty weather logs. 
The weight of the swallowed truth did not hit her like a lightning strike, as she had always feared it would, but settled soft as the aurora’s green glow across the page, light as a paintbrush petal, heavy as a decade of unspoken names. 
As a fault-tolerant oracle, she had always known truths did not have fixed weights, that they shifted depending on where you stood to look at them, how long you held them clamped under your tongue. 
She had hidden in the margins of her own life for so long that she had forgotten the smudge on the page, the crack in the certainty, the quiet tremor of swaying truth was not a failure of vision, but the point of seeing at all. 
Outside, the aurora spilled over the ridgeline where her sister had fallen, gilding the scattered paintbrush blooms pushing through the toxic, mine-stained soil, and for the first time in 12 years, Elara did not look away. 
She slipped the illustration into her coat pocket, already drafting the report she would send to the rangers, to the water board, to the town downstream, knowing the weight she carried now was not one to tuck away, but one to finally set down where it could be seen.
