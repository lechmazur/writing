The errant legend seeker knelt on the splintered gunwale of a half-sunken work skiff, boots caked in canal silt, arriving at the twilight barge canal exactly by the silence of morning radio, that thin pre-dawn hush when no shoreline cottage had yet flipped on a kitchen receiver to bleed weather reports or old country ballads across the water. 
She had chased half-truths across four states for seven years, her driving motivation to understand the architecture of lies: the way small omissions mortared into brick-hard narratives could erase entire lives, rewrite small-town tragedies into folksy fables, turn a wronged barge engineer into a caricature of a drunken fool who’d abandoned his post mid-flood. 
She did not dig blindly through the muck, for she had spent years honing her approach by training instincts to read the faint shift of silt, the glint that did not match broken glass or rusted nail, the weight of a shape that had been carved with intention rather than tossed in careless frustration. 
Thirty seconds of slow, breath-held probing in the soft black muck brought her fingers to a pitted brass edge, and she lifted gently to salvage the object she had tracked across half a decade of conflicting accounts: an antique pocket sundial, its hinged lid etched with the initials of the engineer she’d spent years chasing, its gnomon slightly bent but still aligned to mark the sun’s slow arc even under the bruised purple of pre-dawn sky. 
When she flipped the lid open, she found no hidden note, no damning confession etched into the brass, only a series of tiny, carefully scored marks along the sundial’s hour ring, each notch placed where no standard time mark belonged, spaced to align with the exact moments the town’s official flood timeline deviated from the water levels the engineer would have measured from his lock station. 
The rising sun caught the brass face just as the first distant crackle of a radio cut through the hush, and the thoughtfully clear glint of light bouncing off the scored marks laid out the lie for her without drama, without grand revelation. 
Hindsight clarity did not arrive as a thunderclap, as she had expected after years of chasing shadowy conspiracies and deliberate cover-ups, but as a soft, unshakable recognition: the lies that held communities together were not grand, arching structures built by malicious masterminds, but small, lazy accretions, each one added by a person who found the truth too heavy to carry on a given day. 
The engineer had not been run out of town for exposing graft, nor had he drowned in a drunken stupor as the local stories claimed, but had simply left after realizing no one wanted the exact, unvarnished measurements he carried, his sundial tossed in the canal not by an angry mob, but by his own hand when he decided to stop carrying the weight of being the only person who cared about the exact truth. 
A heron lifted from the reed line at the canal’s edge, its wings beating slow and soft enough not to ripple the water more than a breath, and she felt the familiar, restless drive that had pushed her across state lines for years soften, not break, into something calmer, even as every assumption she had held about the nature of lies dissolved like silt in the current. 
She wiped the muck from the sundial’s face with the frayed cuff of her canvas jacket, tucked it into the inner pocket of her pack rather than holding it aloft like a trophy, and pushed the skiff off the mud bank to let the slow current carry her toward the next lock, no longer chasing grand, hidden architectures to tear down, but ready to notice the small, soft marks people left behind when they could no longer carry the weight of perfect, uncompromised truth.
