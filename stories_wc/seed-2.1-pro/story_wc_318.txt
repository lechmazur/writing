After the final grain slips through the hourglass, the world softens at the edges, its sharp everyday noises leached away to leave only the hush of things never said hanging thick enough to cup in cold palms. 
The echo chamber tunnel curves beneath the old neighborhood, its concrete walls scored with decades of half-remembered arguments, stifled sobs, and snatches of song that never made it to the kitchen table where the household’s laughter died three winters prior. 
This is where the leaf blower symphony conductor comes, mournfully eager, his scuffed boots scuffing loose gravel as he hefts a canvas bag slung over one shoulder, his prize clutched tight in his free hand: the cracked plastic pull cord handle wrenched free the day the family’s youngest child left for college, the same day the last unforced giggle faded from the house’s drafty halls. 
He has long made it his work tending to the unspoken, the small, discarded fragments of connection that get washed down storm drains and trapped in tunnel echo, too soft to survive the roar of busy lives that prioritize loud, tangible victories over quiet, fraying bonds. 
He dumps the contents of his canvas bag at the tunnel’s narrowest, most resonant point: crumpled sticky notes scrawled with unspoken apologies, dried dandelion fluff blown from a child’s birthday wish, the faint, wispy echoes of jokes that died in throats too tight with grief to voice them. 
He lines three dented, repurposed leaf blowers in a semicircle around the pile, each tuned to catch a different register of echo, then fits the cracked pull cord handle to the first machine’s frayed starter cord, his fingers brushing the bite mark the youngest child left in the plastic when she was seven and snuck it outside to "conduct the wind." 
When he yanks the cord, the machines roar to life in wobbly harmony, blowing an off-key wind that curls around the tunnel walls, and he begins to conduct with slow, sweeping arm movements, his voice rising in half-sung lullabies he learned lingering by the household’s open window years ago, the same lullabies the mother sang to her babies before grief made her throat too tight to carry a tune. 
The whorl of wind and song wraps around the pile of unspoken fragments, spinning them slow enough to macerate their sharp, brittle edges into fine, glowing mist that carries the warm, fizzing texture of long-lost laughter. 
He guides the mist up the tunnel’s sloped storm drain access, his conducting motions softening as he watches the glow seep through the cracks in the foundation of the small blue house above, winding up through floorboards to curl around the mother where she sits at the kitchen table staring at a photo of her late husband, around the teenaged son who’d stopped telling bad jokes the day the funeral procession pulled out of the driveway, around the empty bedroom where the youngest child’s old stuffed rabbit still sits propped on the pillow. 
For a long, breathless moment, the tunnel holds its echo, and then he hears it: a small, wobbly huff of amusement from the mother as she remembers the time her husband tried to bake a birthday cake and covered the kitchen in flour, a quiet chuckle from the teenaged son as he finds a crumpled doodle his little sister drew of the family tucked in his old backpack, a faint, distant giggle that travels down the phone line from the college dorm three states away when the youngest child gets a text from her mom with a photo of the flour-covered cake memory. 
The pull cord handle slips from his grasp as the wind dies, his form starting to fray at the edges into the same fine mist he wove, because tending to the unspoken never comes without a cost: he is the echo of the husband, the father, the man who died three winters prior, allowed only this one small window after time ran out to stitch his family’s joy back together before he fades completely. 
The tunnel swallows the last faint echo of his conducting gesture, and the only trace he was ever there is the cracked pull cord handle half-buried in gravel, waiting for the next person with a grief too soft to voice and a household waiting to laugh again.
