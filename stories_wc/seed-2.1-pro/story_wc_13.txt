The natural history illustrator poled her scuffed flat-bottomed skiff along the rooftop canal system weaving between weathered sandstone towers, the slow, rumbling exhale of the slumbering colossus beneath the city rippling the glassy water and sending eddies curling past half-submerged terra-cotta planters bursting with wild moss. 
She had poled these waterways for three full cycles of the giant's inhale and exhale, chasing faint, half-formed sounds that slipped between crenellated tower walls, her motivation unshakable: to find the place where echoes are born, the fabled source of every faded laugh, forgotten lullaby, and half-spoken apology that drifted through the city's lower alleyways long after their original speakers had passed. 
Around her neck, strung on a cord woven from dried canal reeds, hung an infinity symbol pendant cast from cooled, iridescent canal glass, a gift from the retired time-sculptor who had first told her of the echo source, and who had taught her that one sculpts time itself not with grand spells, but with small, deliberate choices to borrow from unspent minutes rather than rushing through the present. 
For weeks, she had been borrowing time in small increments: siphoning seconds from the long, empty pauses between raindrops hitting the canal surface, skimming minutes from the stretches when tower residents sat silent staring out their windows, hoarding the collected moments in the twist of her pendant so she would have enough to reach the echo source before the giant stirred and woke. 
She rounded a bend where the canals narrowed between two towers strung with wind chimes, and heard it: the soft, rising phrase of a folk tune she had known as a child, climbing note by note toward a resolution that never landed, held suspended in the damp air like a dragonfly hovering an inch above the water. 
That suspended, unresolved phrase was the interrupted cadence the old sculptor had warned her marked the threshold of the echo source, a place where sound never fully landed, never faded, just circled waiting for a listener to give it a place to rest. 
Tied to a rusted mooring ring at the canal's end was a cage of woven sound-wire, its bars thrumming with the half-formed echoes, holding captive the small, bright core from which all new echoes bloomed, a point of soft gold light flickering like a captured star. 
She had not expected the source to be caged, trapped decades prior by a city official who thought unregulated echoes caused public unrest, who had believed a city without unplanned sounds would be orderly, calm, easy to rule. 
As a natural history illustrator, she had spent years documenting the small, unplanned things that made the city feel alive: the way moss grew in cracked mortar, the way sparrows nested in tower gutters, the way a half-remembered song could make a stranger smile on a crowded walkway, and she knew the cage was slowly starving the city of the soft, unscripted connections that held its people together. 
She pressed her infinity pendant to the cage's lock, letting all the borrowed time she had stored flow out in a warm, steady stream, using the sculpted, malleable minutes to soften the rigid sound-wire until it bent like reeds in a breeze, leaning in to uncage the flickering golden core before her borrowed reserve ran dry. 
The moment the core slipped free, the interrupted cadence hanging in the air finally resolved, softening into the warm, familiar lilt of the folk tune she remembered, spreading outward in ripples of sound that carried new echoes down every canal, through every tower window, to wrap around residents going about their days. 
She felt the giant shift beneath the towers, a slow, rumbling sigh that rippled the canal water, and knew his nap was drawing to a close, that she had finished her quest just in time, with no borrowed minutes left to spare. 
She pulled her sketchbook from her pack as she poled back toward her home studio, ready to draw the golden echo core she had found, knowing she would not just document the city's small, living wonders now, but help nurture them, one uncaged echo at a time.
