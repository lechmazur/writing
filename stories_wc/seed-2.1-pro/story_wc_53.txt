After the sky-carousel jerked to a final, groaning halt, the last brass note of its calliope dissolved into the mist of the kinetic lagoon above the clouds, where eddies of condensed starlight lapped at rusted ride struts half-buried in floating pearl silt. 
Kaelin, the resolute scavenger who had patrolled the lagoon's floating drifts for as long as she could parse memory, knelt in the silt and brushed iridescent grime off a cracked plastic disc: the sprocketed take-up wheel from a long-expired disposable camera, its edges chewed by cloud-mold. 
She was chronologically confused, some days certain she was 19 and still sanding carousel horse hooves in her grandfather's workshop, others convinced she was 72 and had outlived every person she'd ever built the ride to reach, with no fixed marker of time between the two truths. 
Her once-bright ambition to spin the carousel fast enough to bridge the gap between the cloud realm and the living valley below had eroded slowly, worn thin by decades of frayed cables, missing carousel horses, and the quiet realization that no mechanical marvel could outrun the weight of choices she had made to chase the project. 
For years, she had prepared for this moment by collecting fragments of overheard stories, snippets drifting up from the valley on thermal updrafts, half-muttered confessions from other stranded drifters, the tail ends of lullabies carried on storm winds, tucking each fragment into the hollow core of salvaged trinkets to use as bargaining chips. 
Her singular, unshakable motivation now was to negotiate with her own ghost, the sharp, bright 19-year-old version of herself who had climbed the first cloud strut with a tool belt and a pocket full of nails, before regret had curdled in her chest like spoiled cloud-honey. 
The lagoon hung in fractured stillness, no wind stirring the silt, no calliope note warbling, no drift of cloud moving across the pale sun, as if the whole realm had held its breath to let the two versions of Kaelin meet. 
A figure materialized three feet away, scuffed work boots caked in valley sawdust, holding the crumpled body of the disposable camera the wheel belonged to, its plastic shell cracked where Kaelin had dropped it the day she left her grandfather's funeral to climb to the lagoon. 
She let regret move through her then, not as a dull ache she had pushed down for decades while tightening bolts and chasing an impossible dream, but as a sharp, clear current, naming each missed moment she had traded for the carousel: her sister's wedding, her grandfather's last story, the first steps of the niece she had never met. 
She held out the camera wheel in one palm, and the hollow trinkets stuffed with overheard story fragments in the other, expecting to have to trade every collected memory for permission to stop chasing the broken carousel, to walk down the cloud struts and back to the valley she had abandoned. 
The younger version of her smiled, and did not reach for the trinkets, instead tapping the camera wheel so it spun on her palm, each click of its plastic teeth landing on a memory she had thought was lost forever, no bargain required. 
For a second, the chronological fog that had clouded her mind for decades thinned, and she was neither 19 nor 72, but exactly the age she was, standing in silt that glowed soft gold, the broken carousel at her back no longer a monument to failed ambition but just a pile of scrap to be repurposed. 
She left the trinkets stuffed with overheard stories tucked in the carousel's control panel for any other drifter who might need a bargaining chip of their own, as the fractured stillness softened into a faint, warm breeze carrying the sound of a child's laugh from the valley far below. 
She tucked the disposable camera wheel into her tool belt, turned toward the first sloping cloud strut that led down to the life she had almost missed entirely, and took her first step home.
