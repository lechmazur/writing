The onyx-ink cartomancer climbed the last rime-crusted pine root to the midnight glade above clouds, three weeks after the birth of the daughter she had fled in a blind, old panic. 
Her fingertips were stained black with the thick, light-eating ink she mixed from crushed jet and spring water to paint card faces that spoke of unkept promises and mislaid paths, and she had climbed through thin, stinging air driven by a single, unshakable motivation: to find where lost time accumulates. 
She was willingly perplexed, carrying no grimoire spell or carved ritual token to force an answer, because she had long known regret does not yield to neat, inked formulas or prewritten card spreads. 
The glade spread before her like a half-remembered dream, its moss soft as lamb's ear, its stone outcrops glowing silver under starlight bright enough to make her eyes water, the sea of clouds below churning slow as breathing so that the whole world felt suspended between the life she had run from and the cloudbound repentance she had come to earn. 
She knelt on a flat, lichen-dusted stone at the glade's center and moved to unfasten the brass clasp of the scuffed leather satchel slung across her chest, her cold fingers fumbling a little as the wind tangled her ink-streaked hair across her cheek. 
The first thing she pulled out was the frayed linen tablecloth from her seventh birthday, the last one she had celebrated before she ran from her family's cottage at 16, its fabric crisscrossed with the faint, faded pencil tracings of every guest's outstretched palm, a marker of the family custom of tracing hands on birthday tablecloths to mark the shape of each year's love before it slipped away. 
The second thing she pulled out was the thin porcelain saucer from her childhood dolls' tea set, its pale rose paint chipped at the edges, a fine, jagged crack snaking from its rim to its center where she had dropped it the day she fled, tucking the broken piece into her pocket even as she left her mother's calling voice hanging in the yard behind her. 
She spread the tablecloth smooth over the cold stone and set the cracked saucer dead in its center, half expecting some grand, luminous door to swing open and reveal a vault piled high with all the minutes she had thrown away: the birthdays she missed, the goodbyes she never spoke, the first three weeks of her daughter's life she had spent hiding in the pine treeline instead of holding her. 
No such door appeared. 
Instead, starlight spilled through the crack in the saucer, gilding the faded hand tracings on the tablecloth until each pencil line glowed like a tiny, warm fuse, and the air filled with the soft, layered echoes of all the moments she thought she had lost: her mother laughing as she traced her seven-year-old hand, her father burning the birthday cake so the kitchen filled with smoke, the thin, reedy cry of her newborn daughter the moment she had drawn her first breath. 
The beauty of it caught in her throat until she could barely breathe, sharp and sweet as the cold pine air, and she understood at last that lost time does not accumulate in some distant, locked vault beyond the clouds. 
It accumulates in the traces we carry: the crack in a broken saucer tucked in a satchel for 20 years, the faint pencil lines of old hand tracings on a frayed cloth, the quiet, unflinching choice to turn back down the mountain instead of running further. 
Her cloudbound repentance did not require grand penance, only the willingness to stop running from the traces she had left scattered across half a lifetime. 
She folded the tablecloth carefully around the cracked saucer, tucking both back into her satchel and latching the brass clasp shut, then turned toward the steep, pine-needle strewn downward path, already imagining the first hand she would trace on a crisp new linen tablecloth when she got home: the tiny, fisted palm of her daughter, warm and soft, still curved from the shape of holding the world for the very first time.
