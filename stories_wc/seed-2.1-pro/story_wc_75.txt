The grotto city clung to the damp sandstone shelves behind Thalyn’s monumental waterfall, its narrow walkways strung with dried rose garlands that released a soft, honeyed scent even as mist curled cold around the ankles of anyone who dared step too close to the thundering curtain of water. 
It was three weeks after the beekeeper’s last visit, when he’d slung his wicker hives over his mule and warned the pass above would ice solid before the next full moon, leaving the enclave of displaced lowland craftspeople sealed in tight through the long, dark months ahead. 
Lira, the grotto’s cultured, historically oriented museum guide, ran a trembling finger over the cracked face of the emerald-tinted opera glass lens perched on the shelf above her small stone hearth, the only surviving fragment of the capital opera house’s royal viewing boxes she’d managed to tuck into her pack when the militias burned the lowland cultural districts three years prior. 
For months, she’d watched a quiet despair creep through the grotto, as elders forgot the verses of old folk ballads, weavers abandoned the intricate patterns that had defined their clans for generations, and children grew up knowing nothing of the world their families had fled, their mission to rescue dying arts fraying faster than the worn tapestries stacked in the museum’s back storage cave. 
The air in the grotto always carried the faint, cloying scent of rosewater steeped for winter tonics, tangled with the cold mineral tang of waterfall spray, a combination that had come to feel like dread made perfume, sweet enough to make you forget the sharp edge of loss nipping at every quiet moment. 
She’d almost given up on finding a way to pull the community back to its roots when she remembered her grandmother’s family custom of baking bread shaped like initials, a practice once used at wedding feasts and harvest festivals to mark every guest’s place at the table, to make sure no one’s name was forgotten even when guest lists stretched to hundreds. 
She lit her hearth that evening, mixing ground wheat foraged from the high ledges with wild honey the beekeeper had left as a gift, kneading until her arms ached, shaping each small roll to match the first initial of every displaced person sheltered in the grotto, tucking a tiny snippet of a forgotten craft, a ballad line, a weaving pattern, a recipe for oak-gall ink, into the fold of each corresponding loaf. 
She set the emerald-tinted opera glass lens on the communal stone table when she carried the baskets of warm bread out to the central grotto plaza, holding it up so the lantern light caught its deep green glow as she explained that every loaf held a piece of the culture they’d almost lost, that each person who bit into their initialed roll would carry that piece forward to teach to the next child who asked. 
Slowly, at first, then all at once, a quiet thaw spread through the crowd as elders reached for their loaves and recognized the lines of poetry tucked inside, as weavers ran fingers over the sketched patterns, as children laughed when they bit into honey-sweet bread marked with the first letter of their own names, no longer strangers to the legacy they carried. 
The scent of rosewater still hung thick in the misty air, and the dread had not vanished entirely, for they all knew the militias might one day climb the pass, that the waterfall might not hide them forever, that their old homes were still ash and crumbled stone far below the mountain. 
But as an old weaver began to hum a ballad no one had heard in three years, and a teenaged boy held his initialed roll up to catch the green glow slanting through the opera lens, Lira knew the dying arts would not fade that winter, not as long as they baked each other’s names into bread and held their small, bright pieces of home close enough to share.
