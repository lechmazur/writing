Three years after the wispy, cool-humming energy beings drifted into Earth’s atmosphere to dissolve linear time’s rigid hold and offer every person a single, irrevocable do-over of their most regretted choice, the drifting roadside poet tramped across sunbaked badlands scrub to reach the terra-cotta grotto locals swore held unfiltered access to time’s tangled weave, arriving just as silver starlight spilled through the grotto’s domed natural skylight to gild its fired earthen walls. 
She carried no offering for the beings save a frayed coil of rope from a prison escape, the length her older brother had braided from bedsheets and scavenged wire to break out of the state penitentiary 12 years prior, when he’d been sentenced to a decade for stealing insulin for their uninsured, diabetic mother. 
Widely known for her ethically playful approach to the beings’ grand, heavy offers, she refused to treat the chance to rewrite the past as a grim moral test, instead leaving handwritten joke poems for the floating light-forms at roadside rest stops and teasing them for framing every choice as an all-or-nothing gambit. 
She had not come to beg for a do-over, though; her core motivation for trekking across the badlands was to discover the texture of time, to feel its ridges and soft spots and frayed edges the same way she could feel the worn twists of her brother’s rope under her fingertips, rather than engaging with the abstract, binary choices the beings insisted on presenting to humanity. 
Kneeling on the grotto’s cool, dust-dusted floor, she began to trace the faint pattern pressed into the terra cotta, looping lines that mirrored the twist of her rope, the crackle of starlight, the rhythm of the poems she recited for truckers and hitchhikers at every crossroads she passed. 
She worked by uncovering layers, brushing away decades of red dust to reveal older etchings beneath: a teen’s carved initials, a migrant’s tally of desert miles, the jagged line her brother had scratched into the clay the night he hid in the grotto mid-escape, hours before a flash flood swept him down the arroyo before he could make it home to their mom. 
A soft, cyan glow pooled at her elbow as one of the energy beings drifted close, wordlessly laying out the impossible choice it had come to offer: she could rewind time to warn her brother of the flood, letting him survive to serve his sentence and build a quiet, long life with their mother, but in that timeline, he would never tuck his own handwritten book of poems into the grotto’s clay crevice for her to find three months later, the book that had pulled her out of her own grief and set her on the road as a poet. 
For a long minute, she ran her thumb over the frayed twists of the rope, watching starlight catch the tiny, rough fibers where it had snagged on the grotto’s entrance the night her brother climbed down from the bluff, and smiled the quiet, teasing smile she used when she spotted a flaw in a rigid rule. 
She told the being she rejected the binary choice entirely, because time was not a set of fixed paths to pick between but a texture, woven from every laugh and grief and scrawled line of poetry, every frayed rope fiber and starlit grotto wall, every impossible choice that made a person who they were. 
When the being pulsed in soft, uncomprehending cyan, she coiled the rope tight and tucked it into the crevice where her brother’s book had rested, leaving a new poem of her own tucked beside it, a verse about how choice did not require erasing the parts of the past that hurt to keep the parts that sang. 
As she turned to hike back up to the road, she felt the rough, warm grit of clay dust on her palms, the faint thrum of starlight on her cheeks, and knew she had found what she came for, not in a grand rewrite of history, but in the quiet, layered weight of every moment that had led her there, as real and vivid as the pulse of blood under her skin.
