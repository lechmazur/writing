Through the veil of sleep, the frozen garden hung suspended between breaths, its frosted rosebush thorns crusted with ice that never melted, its stone paths blurred by mist that swallowed sound before it could travel more than three feet. 
Curled on a chipped marble bench at the garden’s heart was the feral library guardian, its pelt matted with fragments of forgotten title pages and frozen dewdrops, its claws worn smooth from years of reshelving books that slipped between waking and dream. 
It was dimensionally homesick, pulled from the sun-drenched, infinite stacks of its home realm when a ragged cultist had stumbled through a light-boundary rift a century prior, dropping a trinket as he fled the guardian’s pursuit. 
That trinket, the cultist's pendant, lay half-buried in frost at the guardian’s paws, its cracked obsidian core pulsing faint, erratic light that marked the fraying, shifting edges of where dream-light bled into waking shadow—the very boundaries of light it had once been carved to police. 
For decades, the guardian had chosen to wait, rather than claw its way back through the rift by force, because it had grown tired of the rigid, unchanging light of its home stacks, where every book had a fixed place, every shadow was banished to a labeled shelf, and nothing ever shifted without permission. 
It practiced interiorizing exteriors, breathing in the mist and frost and shifting pulse of the pendant until the unsteady edges of the garden’s light lived inside its own chest, rather than something separate to be patrolled and tamed. 
Its quiet, slow motivation was to make friends with uncertainty, to learn to sit with the way the mist rearranged the garden paths every hour, the way the pendant’s light sometimes dimmed so far it might vanish entirely, the way sleepers stumbled through the garden half-visible, never staying long enough to be pinned to a single story. 
On this particular sleep-threaded night, a figure materialized at the garden’s edge, no cultist, no invader, just a child clutching a half-remembered picture book, their boots leaving no prints on the frost as they peered through the mist at the guardian. 
The guardian did not snarl, did not reach to shepherd the child back to the waking world, because it knew the soft, fuzzy line between curiosity and fear was just another boundary of light, one that shifted as easily as the mist curled around frozen rose stems. 
It tilted its head, and the cultist's pendant, which it had strung on a cord of twisted ivy around its neck after decades of letting it rest in the frost, glowed soft gold instead of its usual jagged red, as if responding to the child’s quiet, unhurried breath. 
The dimensionally homesick ache in its bones softened, because it realized home was not a fixed realm of perfectly ordered shelves, but the slow, unplanned work of waiting for something unscripted to step through the mist, of letting the world outside seep in until its edges no longer felt like a barrier. 
The child took one slow step, then another, holding out a crumpled drawing of a fox with wings made of book pages, a figure that looked almost exactly like the guardian, as if they had been searching for it across a hundred scattered dreams. 
The mist curled thicker between them for a heartbeat, blurring the line where the guardian’s fur ended and the frost began, a quiet testament to the years it had spent interiorizing exteriors instead of fighting to seal every crack in the light. 
It did not reach for the drawing too fast, because it had learned to make friends with uncertainty, to savor the quiet thrill of not knowing if the child would stay to talk, or fade back into sleep before their fingers could brush the ice-crusted edge of the bench. 
The cultist's pendant warmed against its chest, no longer a marker of a violent rift between worlds, but a small, steady light that held the boundaries of light soft, not sharp, as it leaned forward, slow as drifting frost, to meet the child halfway. 
For the first time since it had tumbled from its home stacks, it did not feel like it was waiting for a way back, but like it had already arrived, held in the hush of the frozen, mist-shrouded garden where every unplanned meeting was a story no fixed shelf could ever hold.
