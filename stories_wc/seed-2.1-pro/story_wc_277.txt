Clara Voss, third-generation newsprint heiress, set her cracked rotary phone back in its cradle ten minutes after the journalist called, his frayed claim of a decades-old cover-up tangled in the static between her ears. 
As a woman who had spent her whole life devotedly observant of the gaps between printed headlines and the quiet truths people tucked into folded newspaper margins, she did not dismiss the claim out of hand. 
Her quiet, lifelong drive to discover hidden truths had made her the black sheep of a family that preferred profitable, uncomplicated stories to messy, unflattering facts. 
She slung her grandfather’s old canvas satchel over her shoulder and drove the winding mountain road up to the echoing observatory above clouds, the domed stone structure her family had gifted to the university in 1927, its walls so bare of soft furnishing that every breath bounced back to the speaker like a half-remembered secret. 
Tucked in the satchel’s inner pocket was the rosewood pen nib he had used to sign every edition’s front-page editorial for forty years, its polished surface worn smooth where his thumb had rested a thousand times. 
She had grown up listening to his drunken, maudlin asides about the orbits of regret, the unspoken wrongs that circled the people who hid them, never drifting far enough to escape gravity, always close enough to brush the back of your neck when you thought you were alone. 
She approached the search with the solemn playfulness of a child hunting for hidden Easter eggs in a graveyard, half-giddy at the thrill of the chase, half-heavy with the knowledge that whatever she found would not bring anyone back. 
She decided to work through proof by contradiction, starting from the assumption that every word her paper had printed about the observatory’s construction was unassailably true, then following that thread until it snapped. 
For three hours, she sifted through the stacked leather-bound ledgers stored in the observatory’s locked supply closet, cross-referencing payroll numbers, material receipts, and the short, glowing news clips her paper had run about the project’s launch. 
If the official story held, every name on the construction crew roster would appear on the coastal bridge’s payroll dated three weeks after the observatory opened, and no unexplained payments would trace back to her grandfather’s personal account. 
What she found instead was that seven names from the roster never appeared on another payroll in the state, and that starting the week the observatory opened, her grandfather had sent monthly checks equal to a full construction wage to seven widows living in the mill village at the mountain’s base, each signed with the distinctive scratch of his rosewood pen nib. 
The echoes of her quiet gasp bounced off the domed ceiling, and for a second she could almost feel the seven dead workers orbiting the room, held fast by the weight of the secret her family had guarded for 96 years. 
She knew she had to intervene before the centennial edition went to press that night, before her family printed another glossy lie about their generosity and civic virtue while erasing the men who had died building the monument to their pride. 
She pulled the rosewood pen nib from her satchel, tucking it into her breast pocket next to the copied ledger pages, and smiled a small, wobbly smile--half grief for the men who had been forgotten, half mischief at the thought of the board’s apoplectic reaction when she ran the front-page story herself. 
She would use that same nib to sign the editorial, she decided, her boots crunching on gravel as the cloud layer thinned below her to reveal the city’s twinkling lights spread out like scattered metal type slugs waiting to be set into truth. 
Orbits, she thought as she turned the key in her old truck’s ignition, only held as long as no one reached up to tug the looping, weighty things back down to earth, where they could be named, and apologized for, and finally set to rest.
