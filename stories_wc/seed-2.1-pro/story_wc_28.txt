After the great fire, the ancient plaza beneath a shattered sky breathed only in fine, ash-dusted gasps, its cracked flagstones crisscrossed with the faint, fused glint of melted glass and warped bronze. 
Kaelin, the gentle radical who had spent a decade organizing mutual aid networks across the city’s divided quarters, stood at the plaza’s core, her dented steel prosthetic arms hanging loose at her sides, built for lifting fallen beams and holding shivering survivors alike to cradle empathy in arms of steel. 
She was retrocognitively trapped, her mind snagged on a three-second loop no other witness carried: the fire warden, the man the mob had dragged to the plaza that morning to face rough justice, dropping his torch mid-order when he spotted a small girl clutching a cloth doll in the tenement doorway, a split second of hesitation before his junior guard kicked the torch into the tinder-dry thatch. 
The crowd bayed for blood, their voices sharp with the cold of ash-cloaked autumn, their anger a warm, living thing against the grey, dead expanse of scorched stone, a balance of warm coldness that settled over the plaza like a shroud. 
Beneath her boot, set into the dirt as an impromptu stepping stone, lay a fragment of the old minaret’s wall, its chipped patterns of cobalt and turquoise tile worn smooth by weeks of fleeing feet, a single loose minaret mosaic tessera of iridescent indigo winking at its edge like a misplaced star. 
She knelt, her steel joints whirring softly, and traced the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone with a calloused fingertip, letting the rough edges of tile anchor her out of the looping memory long enough to step between the mob and the bound warden, who flinched as if expecting a rock to the skull. 
The crowd roared in protest, but she held up a steel hand, plucking the loose indigo tessera from the stone’s edge to hold high, so the fractured sunlight slanting through the smoke-shattered sky caught its glaze and threw a tiny blue circle of light across the warden’s gaunt cheek. 
What she offered next was aberrant mercy, the kind no law or lynch mob would recognize, the kind that did not erase harm but refused to reduce a person to the worst choice they had ever failed to stop. 
She did not free him to run, nor did she hand him over to the crowd; instead, she pressed the cool tessera into his bound palm, and announced she would rename him, stripping away the title of fire warden that had made him a tool of the wealthy council that ordered the neighborhood cleared. 
She chose his new name via the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone, tracing the fractured outline of a peacock that once curled up the minaret’s flank to land on "Liraz," a word meaning "indigo witness," a name that bound him to spend the rest of his days helping rebuild the homes he had almost helped burn. 
The crowd grumbled, but none stepped forward to stop her; they had seen her carry three children out of a burning tenement with those steel arms, had seen her pass out the last of her bread to a homeless elder the week before the fire, and they knew her justice did not bend to easy rage. 
She cut his bonds with the sharp edge of a broken tile, and when he stumbled to his feet, his fingers closed tight around the indigo tessera, the cool press of glass a tangible promise that he would not be allowed to outrun his guilt, but neither would he be crushed by it. 
For the first time since the fire, the retrocognitive loop in her mind went quiet, the image of his hesitation fading into the sound of his first shaky breath as Liraz, not a warden, not a monster, just a man who would spend years earning the grace he had been given. 
The air still held the sharp, cold tang of ash, but the sun broke through a crack in the smoke to gild the edges of the chipped mosaic stone, warm light spilling over the steel of her arms as she gestured for the crowd to make space, to bring hammers and nails and cut lumber, to begin building something better than the city that had burned.
