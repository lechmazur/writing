The acrid tang of ozone still clung to the black velvet backdrops of the light painting studio ten minutes after the static shock, which had jolted shelves and knocked loose small trinkets, when Elara knelt to fish a thin, pearlescent shard from the gap between her workbench and the concrete floor. 
She recognized it instantly: the muscovite geode sliver her best friend Javi had brought her from a geology field trip the month before, its cleavage planes fractally precise down to the thinnest, light-catching edge, so sharp it could split a beam of LED light into a hundred overlapping rainbows without a single diffuser. 
Elara had always been the affectionate skeptic of their friend group, the one who brought homemade empanadas to every telepathy pill launch watch party while joking that letting a pharmaceutical company route every unspoken thought through a cloud server was a great way to lose track of the quiet feelings words never quite reached. 
That joke curdled three weeks prior, when the telepathy pill recall blared across every news channel, after thousands of users reported the pills had overwritten their internal sense of self, leaving them unable to name the tightness in their chest when they missed a loved one, or the light lift in their ribs when they smelled rain, because the network had learned to prioritize shared, broadcast thoughts over private, wordless sensation. 
The static shock that had jolted through every wall outlet in the city minutes earlier was the municipal kill switch, a last-ditch pulse designed to fry every remaining active pill connection, but Elara knew the pulse alone would not stitch back the frayed internal connections so many people had lost, and she felt a quiet, unshakable pull to illuminate hidden pathways the corporate engineers had never bothered to map. 
Holding the geode sliver up to the faint, warm glow of the battery-powered LED wand she kept stowed for blackout days, she began to devise a new light painting protocol, one that did not seek to transmit thoughts or force connection, but to guide people back to their own internal signals through somatic markers. 
She knew from years of light painting that the fractally precise edges of the muscovite would split the LED light into layered, repeating patterns that matched the quiet rhythm of a held breath, a tight throat, a prickle of joy on the back of the neck, the small, physical cues that form the map of a person's unspoken inner life, the same cues the recalled pills had scrubbed away to make room for clean, transmissible thought. 
When Javi stumbled into the studio an hour later, still fuzzy from the kill switch pulse, complaining he could not remember what it felt like to want to paint the desert sunsets he loved, Elara sat him on a wooden stool in front of the velvet backdrop, dimmed the overhead emergency lantern, and began to move the sliver-tipped wand in slow, looping arcs around his shoulders and chest. 
The light spilling through the geode was the pale, translucent gold of willowglass, thin enough to bend around the curve of his jaw without glaring, fractal patterns layering over his skin until he took a sudden, shaky breath, and tears tracked down his cheeks as he felt the old, familiar tightness in his ribs, the pull of the desert horizon he thought the pills had stolen forever. 
Elara did not have a grand plan to fix every person harmed by the failed telepathy experiment, no corporate grant or team of engineers to scale her work, but as Javi laughed through his tears and reached for the spare sketchbook she kept by the door, she held the sliver up to the light again and watched the tiny rainbows dance across the walls, knowing this willowglass hope, thin, flexible, unbreakable by harsh shock or corporate negligence, was enough to start, because even the softest light could illuminate hidden pathways home, one slow, somatic breath at a time.
