Three years after thoughts became taxable, when the hum of neural scanners on every street corner made even quiet daydreams feel like a risky financial liability, the charming rogue scholar slipped through the cracked plastic sheeting strung across the entomologist's greenhouse entrance, brushing aphid dust from the frayed cuffs of his velvet jacket. 
The greenhouse was the unofficial clubhouse for the city's last secret language club, a ragtag group of artists, retirees, teens, and bug researchers who had spent months evading scanner detection by coding their dissent into patterns scanners could not parse. 
The scholar was known across underground networks for being candidly cryptic, never lying to anyone who asked about his work but wrapping his explanations in layers of gardening metaphor and beetle trivia that left tax agents squinting in confusion as they scribbled useless notes. 
His chosen method of resistance was quiet and unassuming, joining every oversubscribed community garden plot across the city to plant message patterns, his coded communications rooted in soil instead of broadcast over scannable airwaves where the Bureau could tally and tax them. 
Tonight he carried a crumpled manila envelope stuffed full of gourd rattle seed, the hard, round, matte-black seeds marked with tiny, natural cream-colored swirls that looked almost like handwritten letters when lined up in neat rows. 
He was not fighting to topple the Bureau single-handedly, he often told the club members; he was fighting to stand guard over his own spark of defiance, the quiet part of him that refused to let every stray thought, every quiet joy, every silly inside joke be reduced to a line item on a tax bill. 
The air in the greenhouse hummed with the kind of converging chaos the scholar had learned to recognize in the weeks prior, when scanner vans circled working-class neighborhoods for hours at a time and Bureau agents started showing up at community gardens asking pointed questions about unregistered plantings. 
Maren the entomologist was leaning against a bench stacked with mason jars of iridescent beetles, her face tight as she reported that three club members had been fined that week for "unreported creative ideation" after agents spotted coded flower arrangements on their windowsills. 
The scholar poured a handful of the gourd rattle seeds into his palm, grinning as he explained the trick he had spent months testing with Maren's help: when mature, the hard, hollow gourds would dry in the summer sun, their loose, hard seeds rattling in every breeze to throw out a low, steady frequency that would warp the cognitive scanners' ability to pick up unreported thoughts within a 50-foot radius of every planting. 
The garden patterns he had been planting for months were not just coded messages alerting other club members to drop points and safe houses; they were the exact grid needed to create overlapping scanner blind spots across the entire city, each gourd vine positioned to connect to the next like letters in a sentence no algorithm could read. 
A teen from the west side plot leaned in, holding up a crumpled map marked with red Xs where scanner vans had been parked that afternoon, and asked if the plan would work before agents dug up every existing plot. 
The scholar laughed, passing out sealed seed packets to every person in the circle, his tone warm and unshakable even as the distant wail of a Bureau siren drifted over the greenhouse's glass panes. 
He told them the scanners would never learn to read a language written in vine tendrils and rattle echoes, because the words grown from soil were not for sale, not for counting, not for taxing--they were for the people who tended them, small, loud, alive, and impossible to steal. 
As the club members filed out one by one, tucking seed packets into inner coat pockets and boot linings to avoid detection, Maren tapped the scholar's shoulder and pointed to a single fat seed sprouting a tiny green tendril in a terracotta pot on her bench, the first of the gourd rattle vines already reaching for the greenhouse light. 
Outside, the scanner vans rumbled closer, but for the first time in months, the people leaving the greenhouse did not quiet their thoughts or tamp down their grins, knowing they carried the seeds of a space where their ideas would belong only to them, no receipt, no fine, no tax required.
