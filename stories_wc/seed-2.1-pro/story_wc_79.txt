The sun bleached the ridged, barnacle-crusted carapace of the gargantuan turtle that bore the bulk of Veyra City so thoroughly that every weathered plank and chitin tile glowed pale, brassy gold, three decades after the iron harbor gate had rusted shut against the open sea. 
Kaelin, the city’s last spiral-shell cartographer, kept her rolled maps tucked in the tight, pearlescent whorl of a giant sea-snail shell slung across her back, her hands perpetually smudged with iron gall ink and salt, quietly driven by a question no other resident dared voice aloud. 
For as long as anyone could remember, tall, motionless figures had stood on the hazy western horizon, half-obscured by sea spray, never approaching, never signaling, and the city’s elders had long forbidden any attempt to sail close enough to speak to them, citing the fate of the lost expedition that had vanished the day the gate sealed itself shut. 
Kaelin did not trust the elders’ tales of sea demons and cursed tides, because she mapped the world through pattern languages, tracing the recurring whorls of current, the matching fracture lines on every turtle shell that made up the city’s foundations, the identical slant of light that fell across the watchers at every high tide, to find truths no spoken story could capture. 
Tucked in the inner pocket of her oilskin coat, she carried a dented reed whistle carved with the same spiral mark that decorated her map shell, a relic pulled from the drift line ten years prior, the only trace of the lost expedition anyone had ever recovered. 
For weeks she had pored over the whistle’s carved notches, cross-referencing them with tide charts and the faint, weathered carvings on the rusted gate, until she realized the notches were not decoration, but a map marking a narrow, current-sheltered path that wound between the jagged reefs the elders claimed were impassable. 
On the morning of the lowest equinox tide, she slipped past the dozing gate wardens and climbed down the jagged edge of the turtle’s carapace to a small, hide-covered coracle she had stashed in a shadowed crevice, the reed whistle strung around her neck, her spiral shell of maps secured tight to her back. 
The water glowed so bright under the unblinking sun that it stung her eyes, the air thick with the salt smell of dried kelp and the faint, iron tang of the rust that flaked from the far-off gate, and she followed the pattern of currents she had mapped for so long, the watchers growing taller and sharper on the horizon with every pull of her oar. 
She was halfway across the reef channel when a low, grinding sound rumbled up through the hull of her coracle, and she looked down to see a thin, dark crack spiderwebbing across the pale bone-white reef below her, a line that matched exactly the fracture marks she had traced on the city’s turtle shells for years. 
As the crack widened, she lifted the reed whistle to her lips and blew, not to signal for help, but to test the pattern of echoes she had predicted would bounce off the distant watchers, a clear, high tone that cut through the brassy sunlight without wavering. 
The echo that came back was not the sharp, hard reply of stone or rock, but the soft, resonant thrum of living chitin, and she realized with a slow, cold twist of dread that the silent watchers on the horizon were not strangers or demons, but the heads of a dozen more gargantuan turtles, frozen mid-swim, their shells crusted over with the same pale coral and barnacles that coated the reefs. 
The lost expedition had never vanished, she understood; they had sailed close enough to see the truth, that the turtles bearing the city had not moved in generations, held fast by a web of calcified coral that was now beginning to split along the crack she had spotted, that the elders had sealed the gate not to protect the city from outside threats, but to keep anyone from learning the city was slowly, inexorably sliding into the deep. 
She turned her coracle back toward the glinting gold of the city, tucking the reed whistle back under her coat, already mentally drafting the first new map she would draw in three decades, one that would not hide the cracks, or the waiting turtles on the horizon, no matter how hard the elders tried to make her look away.
