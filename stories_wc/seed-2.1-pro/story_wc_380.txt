Three days after the great female sperm whale beached on the stone wharf, the dock-side lantern lighter, Mara, shouldered her brass wick trimmer and set out for the old co-op salt crystal growing chamber, her boots crunching over stray crumbs of salt and crushed shell. 
The whale's beaching had torn loose a drift of the pockets of gravity that clung to the coastal shelf, patches where weight shifted, time softened, and the memories people had deliberately pruned away--griefs, small shames, lost loves--slipped out of their minds to drift like dandelion down. 
These pockets fluctuated mysteriously, bobbing between the pier pilings one hour, hovering over the fish market stalls the next, light enough to carry on the salt wind, heavy enough to yank a lantern right off its iron hook if you walked too close. 
Mara's sole motivation, the quiet fire she had carried for 18 months, was to find where deleted memories go, specifically the memory of her little brother, who had vanished entirely when a gravity pocket swept through their cottage the previous winter, leaving only a crumpled sheet of pressed wildflowers tucked in the lining of her coat pocket as proof he had ever existed. 
She had learned how to trace the pockets' paths through patterns in coffee grounds, a trick the old blind chandler had taught her, explaining that the fine, dense grounds caught the faint gravity ripples the way sand catches tide marks, mapping where the drifting memories settled. 
When she pried open the chamber's heavy oak door, the world dropped into gleaming silence, the air thick with brine, every surface crusted with faceted salt crystals that caught the faint glow of her hand lantern to throw prisms across the walls, as if the room held a thousand captured shards of sunlight. 
For three weeks, her coffee ground readings had converged on this space, marking a massive, slow-moving gravity pocket that had settled deep in the chamber's central brine vat, where crystals grew the size of a child's fist over months of quiet evaporation. 
She knew she would have to exhume the trapped memories from where they had been encased in the growing salt, layer by layer, careful not to crack the crystals that held fragments of birthdays, half-remembered lullabies, apologies never spoken. 
She pulled the sheet of pressed wildflowers from her coat, the purple sea thrift and yellow tansy still bright even after 18 months pressed flat, and held it over the vat, watching as the fluctuating gravity plucked gently at the paper, lifting the edges like a child's tentative hand. 
Leaning over the rim, she brushed the top layer of crystals aside, her breath catching when one faceted shard caught the light to show a tiny, moving image: her brother, seven years old, chasing a crab across the mudflats, his bare feet slapping the wet muck, a grin splitting his sunburnt face. 
The gleaming silence held, broken only by the soft plink of brine dripping from the crystal rafters, as she realized the deleted memories did not vanish forever, but settled into the heavy, quiet places where gravity pooled, preserved in salt as perfectly as the wildflowers in her pocket. 
She thought of the whale out on the wharf, its body slowly being broken down by the tides and the crews, and understood it had not beached out of sickness or confusion, but had swum into the shallow water to deposit its own load of gravity-tousled memories, carried across thousands of miles of open ocean, to rest in the salt-crusted docks where lost things were kept. 
She lifted the crystal shard holding her brother's face out of the brine, tucking it into the same pocket that held the pressed wildflowers, before turning to trim the single lantern hanging by the chamber door, its flame steady now that the largest gravity pocket had settled into stillness at the bottom of the vat. 
Outside, the gulls called softly over the whale's bones, but in the chamber, the prisms of salt glowed on, holding every lost memory tight, no longer drifting, no longer forgotten.
