The hush hangs thick in the minutes just before the condemned oak trunk library--hollowed over a decade of patient carving by the 18th-century city scholar’s guild--meets its scheduled demolition, and even the wind holds its breath above the chain-link construction perimeter. 
Lila, a face blind portrait artist who had snuck past the site security an hour prior, knelt on a cushion of moss and crushed acorn caps, her easel propped against a shelf warped by two centuries of rain seepage through knothole gaps. 
Unable to parse the holistic arrangement of features that let most people recognize loved ones or strangers, her vision had honed to a fractally precise attunement, tracking repeating patterns across every scale of the world around her. 
She had come to craft a final portrait of the hollowed space, driven by a quiet, years-long motivation to mirror the sky inside walls: to paint slanted sunlight, dappled cloud shadow, and the soft blue of midday filtered through oak leaves so accurately that enclosed spaces would hold the same unmoored, open warmth she could never anchor to the unrecognizable faces around her. 
The air smelled of damp rot and pine resin, and the only sounds beyond her own soft breathing were the distant idle of the excavator and the faint scrabble of a squirrel in the upper branches, so loud in the pre-demolition hush that each small noise felt etched into the wood around her. 
When she reached for a fresh stick of willow charcoal, her knuckle brushed a leather spine that did not yield like the rotting, paper-pulp volumes around it: a secret book safe, its cover tooled to match the guild’s bound agricultural records, its pages glued shut to hide a hollowed core. 
She pried the glued edge free with her charcoal stick, and found no letters, no hidden maps, no rare manuscripts inside, only a single dented, centuries-old copper coin, its face worn smooth by hundreds of years of handling. 
No raised marking caught her fractally precise eye, but she knew from years of lifting texture from weathered stone and bark that faint indentations on a centuries-old coin could hold stories invisible to a straight-on gaze, so she laid a thin sheet of sketch paper over the disc and rubbed the side of her charcoal lightly across its surface. 
As the charcoal dust settled, the marks emerged not as a royal seal or guild crest, but as a constellation of tiny, uneven presses: the whorl of a thumb, repeated thousands of times over decades, as some long-gone visitor to the library had turned the coin over and over while sitting in that exact spot, gazing up at the same knothole view of the sky she had been painting. 
In that quiet, held breath of a moment, she discovered the limits of the precision she had relied on for her whole career: for all her ability to map the fractal echoes between bark ridge and cirrus wisp, between shelf crack and leaf vein, she could never craft an image that held the weight of that anonymous person’s quiet hours, the soft press of a thumb worn into metal by repetition too gentle, too human, to fit into any measurable pattern. 
A sharp beep echoed from the perimeter, the excavator’s arm swinging into position, and she tucked the coin and its charcoal rubbing into her satchel, turning back to her half-finished painting. 
She had already rendered every sharp, fractally matched line of light and bark to mirror the sky inside the oak’s curved walls, but now she softened a single edge of cloud shadow, leaving the blur as a quiet tribute to the gap between what precision can capture and what lingers, faint as a thumbprint, in the spaces waiting to be demolished. 
The first splintering crack of the oak’s outer bark split the hush a heartbeat later, but she did not flinch, holding the soft, imperfect drawing to her chest like a secret shared across three hundred years of quiet sky-gazing.
