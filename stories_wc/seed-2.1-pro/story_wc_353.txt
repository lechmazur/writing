The siskin alder grove held its breath in pale gold early spring light, moments after the tiny, frost-dusted hedgehog uncurled from its tight, cold night's huddle. 
Kneeling between gnarled alder roots, Lira, the grove's last tree-song sculptor, was symphonically minded, her life's work anchored to the quiet, urgent motivation to create music in a silent world stripped of birdsong, wind-rustle, and even the soft crunch of pine needles underfoot by a strange, unyielding quiet that had settled three winters prior. 
She had spent three months tracing the half-buried form of a long-forgotten sculpture half-swallowed by moss and alder roots, working by the method of analyzing stress cracks in its weathered surface to map the points where trapped vibration might finally break free into sound. 
At the sculpture's curved, outstretched wooden arm, set where a hand might have once cupped sunlight, sat a smooth, milk-white porcelain insulator, uncracked at its center but ringed with fine, web-like fissures where decades of frost and root pressure had pulled against its fired surface. 
The hedgehog, its tiny black nose twitching at scent of alder sap, waddled slowly to the sculpture's base, pausing to nudge a fallen catkin with its snout, as if aware it stood in a space where sound might be reborn. 
Lira did not reach for her tools right away, for she knew tree-song was not carved or forced, but felt through the slow, reciprocal practice of empathy in motion, matching one's own breath and pulse to the quiet tensions held in wood, ceramic, root, and bone until all things vibrated at the same gentle frequency. 
She closed her eyes for three slow breaths, feeling the faint thrum of the alders' sap rise, the hedgehog's tiny heartbeat through the soil, the tight, held tension coiled in the fine cracks around the porcelain insulator, each fissure holding a note that had waited a century to sound. 
When her pulse matched the slow, steady push of sap and the soft patter of the hedgehog's feet as it settled against the sculpture's base, she lifted one calloused finger, light as siskin down, to strum the thinnest, most tension-wrought fissure that arced from the insulator's edge to the alder root tangled around the sculpture's ankle. 
The sound that came was not loud, for the world had forgotten how to bear loud noise, but it was clear: a thin, silver note that hung in the air like spider silk, soft enough that it did not startle the hedgehog, which tilted its head as if tasting the vibration on its tongue. 
She moved her finger to the next crack, then the next, not playing as a musician playing for an audience, but moving as one part of the grove answering another, the empathy in motion she had practiced for years unfolding so gently she barely felt the shift between her own body and the wood, the ceramic, the small animal at her feet. 
Each strum pulled a new note from the stress cracks, the porcelain insulator humming like a tiny bell as the vibrations traveled up the alder roots, out along the branches, stirring the catkins so they released fine clouds of yellow pollen that drifted slow as stardust in the quiet light. 
A small flock of siskins, which had huddled silent in the upper branches for months, tilted their heads at the vibration, and one opened its beak to let out a thin, tentative trill that wove into the notes rising from the sculpture, as if remembering how song worked. 
The hedgehog gave a soft, snuffling chirp, the first sound it had made since the silence fell, and pressed its small spined side closer to the sculpture's root-tangled base, as if leaning into a warm fire. 
Lira did not rush, for symphonies were not built in haste, especially when they were stitched from cracks and held silence and the slow, quiet work of meeting the world exactly where it rested. 
She knew the silence would not vanish all at once, that there would be more cracks to map, more notes to draw out from forgotten places across the valley, but for that long, soft moment, the grove held its first music in three years, quiet enough not to break anything, alive enough to remind every living thing in it how to listen again.
