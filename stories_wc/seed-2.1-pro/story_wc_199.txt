On the wide, moss-padded boughs of the ashen skyborne arbor--where gnarled ash trees twined their roots into soft cloud loam hundreds of feet above the valley farms, their pale bark dusted perpetually with fine, pearlescent ash--the orderly seed sorter knelt over her graded wicker trays during the hush where past meets prophecy. 
Her assigned task was sorting viable ash, maple, and pine seeds to send down to valley planters, but her quiet, driving motivation was to decode the message in falling leaves, the same message referenced in the hush-time regional folklore her grandmother had taught her at knee level. 
Between sorting passes, she practiced the gentle work her grandmother had modeled: building tiny monuments to forgotten things, stacking seed hulls, shed bird down, and crumbled lichen into thumb-sized cairns tucked into bark crevices where wind would not scatter them. 
Mossy delight warmed her cheeks as she leaned in to tuck a scrap of abandoned wren nest into the latest cairn, the soft, damp green fuzz on the bough beneath her releasing a faint, earthy scent when she shifted her weight. 
A single crumpled ash leaf spiraled down to land on her finest sorting tray, and she leaned forward to prod its curled edge with a pine needle pick, expecting to find a seed caught in its papery folds. 
What she found instead was no seed, but a moonstone cabochon dust speck, so fine it caught the muted silver light filtering through the ash canopy like a fragment of captured star, tucked exactly where the leaf's central vein met its stem. 
The old regional folklore held that the hush between past and prophecy delivered a sign to whoever tended the arbor's forgotten corners, a sign that was never what the seeker expected, and that emerges transformed always, shifting shape to match the care the seeker had poured into small, uncelebrated acts. 
She held her breath as the dust speck lifted from the leaf, drifting slow as a dandelion seed to settle on the closest of her tiny monuments, the cairn she had just tucked the wren nest scrap into minutes before. 
Where the speck landed, the rough pile of hulls and lichen and nest scrap softened, glowing with the same milky iridescence as the moonstone, until it resolved into a tiny, perfect sculpture: a miniature of the arbor itself, complete with moss-furred boughs, tiny wicker seed trays, and a small, smiling figure kneeling to tend a cairn. 
One by one, more ash leaves spiraled down to land around her, their veins glowing faintly with that same moonstone light, and she did not need to sort or prod them to read the message they carried, written in the soft tilt of each stem and the curve of every lobed edge. 
The message was not a grand prophecy of flood or bountiful harvest, as generations of arbor tenders had assumed, but a quiet reminder: the work of tending small, forgotten things was never wasted, and every act of gentle care built a monument strong enough to hold both memory and hope. 
She had always thought decoding the leaves would mean finding a concrete answer to pass down to the next generation, but as the hush softened and the first faint breeze stirred the ash canopy, she understood the old folklore's promise that the sign emerges transformed always meant the seeker, too, was reshaped by the finding. 
She tucked the tiny moonstone sculpture into the inner pocket of her linen tunic, right next to the crumpled first ash leaf that had carried the dust speck, before turning back to her seed trays with a softer, warmer smile than she had worn all season. 
Below her, the valley farms glowed soft green in the breaking light, and she knew she would keep building tiny monuments to forgotten things, now that she knew even the smallest specks of moonstone and memory could carry a prophecy gentle enough to root deep, just like the seeds she sorted every quiet, moss-scented day.
