The prismatic escondite hummed, its faceted crystal walls refracting the bruised, tangerine glow of the solar storm raging above the high Andean ridgeline where it had tucked itself out of mortal sight for twelve millennia. 
Kael, the dimensionally aware, bored immortal who had claimed the escondite as his only permanent refuge, slouched against a wall of rose quartz, his thumb brushing the jagged cursed amulet fragment he had pulled from the ash of Pompeii on a lark 1900 years prior. 
For eons, he had drifted through human empires and ice ages and cosmic alignments, chasing every novelty to outrun the hollow tedium of outliving every soul he ever grew to love, his singular, unshakable motivation to discover what happens after goodbye. 
His dimensional sight let him trace the edges of pocket realms, slip between parallel timelines, and watch choices branch like frost on windowpanes, but it could never pierce the soft, final veil that fell when a loved one squeezed his hand for the last time, smiled, and let go. 
The slow, coiled intrigue he had woven across three centuries had led him here, to this exact storm, this exact shard of cursed jewelry, this exact hidden prism-walled redoubt. 
Old texts, stolen from the archives of long-dead celestial mages, had explained that the veil between life and whatever lay beyond was not a wall, but a set of spatial rules enforced by the quiet hum of the planet’s core, rules that could be goaded into open revolt: spatial mutiny, when stoked through magnetic fields thrown into chaos by a peak solar storm. 
The cursed amulet fragment, he had learned, was cut from a meteorite that had crashed to earth carrying the exact resonant frequency needed to nudge those roiling magnetic fields into breaking their programmed bounds, its curse a side effect of its endless, quiet pull on the threads of space around it. 
The air in the escondite thickened, carrying the low, thrumming vibration of hushed thunder, not from the storm clouds high above, but from the crystal walls themselves as they began to vibrate in time with the solar wind lashing the planet’s magnetosphere. 
Kael lifted the amulet fragment, and it pulled hard toward the center of the escondite, tugging against his grip as if straining toward a long-lost home, the light refracting through its cracked edge splitting into a hundred tiny, wavering doorways that hung suspended in the air between the crystal facets. 
This was the spatial mutiny the texts had promised: the fixed lines between dimensions fraying, the rules that had trapped him in an endless string of goodbyes dissolving, because space itself had tired of being told which paths it could and could not open for those brave enough to listen. 
He did not rush toward the nearest doorway, because he had waited long enough to know that rushing made fragile thresholds shatter; instead, he stepped slowly, his bare feet silent on the cool crystal floor, the hushed thunder of the resonating walls thrumming up through his bones as he neared the closest glow. 
Through the wavering light, he could see flashes of the faces he had grieved: the Roman poet who had taught him to write love sonnets before the plague took her, the Incan shepherd boy who had shown him how to read the stars, the lighthouse keeper from Maine who had shared his last can of peaches with Kael three days before a hurricane swept the lighthouse away. 
The amulet fragment grew warm in his palm as he reached out, his fingertips brushing the edge of the threshold, and for the first time in twelve thousand years, he did not feel the cold, hollow boredom of endless life, but the soft, bright spark of hope that goodbye was not an end, just a door he had never been allowed to open before. 
The solar storm peaked outside, sending a surge of charged particles tangling the magnetic fields around the escondite, and the hushed thunder rose to a soft, resonant crescendo as he stepped across the threshold, finally ready to learn what waited on the other side of every goodbye he had ever spoken.
