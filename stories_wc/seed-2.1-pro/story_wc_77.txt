I woke gasping on the splintered floor of the abandoned carnival funhouse, my chest still stinging where paramedics' defibrillator pads had peeled raw skin, three months to the day after the resuscitation that dragged me back from the Mirror Maze collapse that stopped my heart for 47 seconds. 
The air clung thick with the rot of cotton candy fused to cracked fiberglass, a repulsive allure that pulled me deeper even as my throat tightened against the mildew and old burnt popcorn stench. 
I had come on the quiet instruction of the whispering advisor, the sibilant presence that had nested in the hollow of my skull the second my heart restarted, the voice that never spoke above a breath but knew every cold, knotted twist of the terror that had locked my joints since I woke. 
The advisor had promised a way to outwit the choking fear that followed me through grocery stores and quiet bedrooms, to trick fear into revealing its weakness so I could stop flinching at every shadow that stretched too long across a wall. 
The method, it had breathed into my ear during sleepless nights, lay in the tattered dream dictionary I'd found propped against a warped funhouse mirror, its pages scribbled through with paradoxical notes in a faded ink that shifted color when I tilted the page. 
One note, scrawled in the margin beside the entry for "clasps," warned that the opera cloak clasp buried under the cracked plastic clown at the maze's center was photographically cursed, that anyone who held it to their chest would see every memory they had ever run from lit by a lingering luminance that did not fade, even when eyes were squeezed tight shut. 
The paradox, the whispering advisor hissed as I dug through splintered wood and melted plastic to reach the clasp, was that fear fed only on the things we refused to look at directly, that the curse people fled was exactly the key to robbing it of its teeth. 
When my fingers closed around the cold brass clasp, carved to look like two tiny screaming masks twisted into a single loop, it hummed against my palm, the same repulsive allure of the funhouse itself thrumming up my arm to settle behind my eyes. 
The lingering luminance bloomed the second I pressed the clasp to my sternum, searing through my shirt to project every fear I had buried onto the warped funhouse mirrors: the split second the mirror cracked above my head, the cold of the concrete as I lay dying, the quiet shame of being too afraid to leave my apartment for weeks after I was released from the hospital. 
I had expected to scream, to clamp my hands over my eyes and run, but instead I found my voice loosening, ready to unbosom every quiet terror I had clutched tight to my chest for months, speaking each memory out loud as the light gilded its edges on the glass. 
As I spoke, the shadowy figure I had felt nipping at my heels for months coalesced in the center of the room, a twisted, jagged thing made of cracked mirror shards and quiet sobs, and I realized I had tricked it into revealing its weakness: it could not exist in the light of the things I no longer hid from. 
The whispering advisor fell silent for the first time since my resuscitation as the figure shrank, dissolving into motes of the same soft light that clung to the mirrors, and I realized the sibilant voice in my head had never been anything but fear itself, trying to lead me away from the very thing that would destroy it. 
I looked down at the opera cloak clasp in my hand, its brass surface no longer cold, and understood why the notes in the dream dictionary had felt so paradoxical: fear had written the warnings to make me run from the curse, never guessing the curse's unflinching, photographic hold on my buried memories was the exact luminance I needed to strip it of power. 
I tucked the clasp into the pocket of my jacket as I stepped back through the funhouse's rotting entryway, the lingering luminance soft on my shoulders, no longer a searing curse but a quiet glow that kept the shadows from feeling like threats. 
The funhouse's strange, repulsive allure faded behind me as I walked toward the street, and for the first time since the paramedics had pulled me back to life, I did not look over my shoulder, because I knew fear had no secrets left to hide.
