Perched on the jagged spine of a snow-dusted mountain range, the cinder-block shooting star tracking station had not logged an official observation in 72 years, its domed lens crusted with rime, its logbooks fanned open to pages nibbled thin by pack rats, holding space through the hush of an overlooked era when human wonder turned from the wide dark sky to small screens held inches from tired, downcast faces. 
Its sole permanent resident was a cosmic janitor named Kael, broad-shouldered, his hands crisscrossed with thin scars from prying frozen shutters loose and patching cracks in the observatory dome, spiritually curious enough to have turned down three promotions to orbital maintenance posts because he refused to leave the quiet hum of the high-altitude dark. 
The only light he carried on his nightly rounds came from a cracked fixture he had fished out of the station's abandoned storage closet, its pane warped, bubbled yellow lantern glass that threw honey-gold slants across frost and stone instead of the harsh white glare of standard-issue work lights. 
He had come to the mountain three years prior, raw from a breakdown that had left him unable to navigate the loud, performative resilience demanded of cosmic maintenance crews, driven by a quiet motivation to find strength in vulnerability instead of patching over every crack in his own heart the way he patched cracks in the dome. 
He did not use the station's rusted tracking computers on his shifts; instead, he sat on the dome's outer catwalk for hours each night, practicing his own quiet ritual of connection by tracing the path of falling stars as they streaked from the outer dark to burn out high above the pine treeline. 
At first, he had watched the meteors with a faint, familiar grief, seeing in their brief, fiery fall his own sense of having burned out too fast, too far from the roles the world had expected him to fill; slowly, though, he learned to empathize not with their fall, but with the quiet, persistent work they did even when no one was watching. 
One night, as a particularly bright meteor streaked overhead, he held up his lantern to catch its faint reflected glow, and noticed for the first time how the warped yellow lantern glass softened the harsh, fleeting fire of the shooting star into something warm, slow, and steady, as if the star's light was choosing to rest a while in the honeyed pane instead of rushing to burn out. 
That was when he began to understand the flourishing stillness that clung to the abandoned station like rime on stone: it was not absence, not failure, not the hollow quiet of a place forgotten, but a rich, living slowdown where moss grew thick in cracked concrete, where pine martens nested in the old equipment bays, where a burnt-out maintenance worker could sit for hours with a dented lantern and let his own frayed edges knit back together without pressure to perform productivity. 
There was a primal elegance to the routine he built there, stripped of all unnecessary ceremony: he swept the catwalk at dusk, lit the lantern when the first star pricked through the dark, traced each falling star's arc with a calloused finger, and did not rush to fill the silence with chatter or radio broadcasts or self-improvement mantras. 
He came to see that the falling stars were not failures, burning out before they could reach some grand cosmic destination, but beings brave enough to be soft enough to burn, to leave a trail of light even when no official logbook recorded their passage, even when no crowd gathered to wish upon them. 
The yellow lantern glass glowed each night like a small, fixed star of its own, holding the light of every fallen meteor he had traced, a tangible reminder that strength did not require constant motion, constant noise, constant proof of worth. 
In that flourishing stillness, he did not "heal" in the loud, linear way the world often demands, checking boxes of recovery until he was deemed fit to rejoin the rush; instead, he rooted, slow and soft, the way moss roots in concrete cracks, drawing strength from the very vulnerability that had once sent him running for the high, quiet dark. 
He was the cosmic janitor, after all, and it was his job not to fix the sky, or rush the stars, but to tend to the quiet, flourishing spaces the rest of the world had forgotten to see.
