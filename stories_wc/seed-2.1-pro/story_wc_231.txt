At nautical twilight, when the horizon bleeds soft indigo between the last glow of sunset and the first prickle of starlight, Silas, the eccentric lighthouse keeper who had tended Cape Thalassa's beacon for 42 unbroken years, picked his way down salt-crusted stone steps to the opal dock in mist. 
Three nights prior, a waterlogged dinghy had drifted to the dock's slatted pilings, holding only a still, gray-coated man whose pockets held no wallet, no map, no identification--nothing but a dried Edelweiss boutonniere pinned to his lapel, its fuzzy white petals crisp as pressed paper despite the briny spray. 
The county coroner had written the death off as a random fishing accident, but Silas, whose softly persistent nature made him allergic to easy, uncurious answers, had come to the dock to investigate. 
He did not comb the splinters for lost coins or torn fabric, as the sheriff's deputies had done; he had learned long ago, after decades of watching ships blink in and out of fog, that truth revealed itself by observing gaps--the silences between gull cries, the empty slots in a lighthouse log, the spaces where a memory should sit. 
He pulled the dried Edelweiss boutonniere from the inner pocket of his oilskin coat, running a calloused thumb over its petals, and felt the first faint brush of borrowed memories slip into the quiet hollows of his own long, solitary days. 
His motivation was simple, unshakable: to decode a stranger's final gesture, to understand why a man wearing a flower that grew only in high alpine meadows, thousands of miles from the salt-crusted Atlantic coast, would drift to his dock alone. 
The borrowed memories did not come as full stories, but as the absences he knew so well: the gap where a wife's laugh should have been, cut short by a cancer diagnosis three years prior; the gap where a daughter's weekend visits should have sat, empty after she moved across the country and stopped answering calls; the gap where a mountain hiking trail should have been, sold to a developer who clear-cut the edelweiss slopes the man had loved since boyhood. 
Silas leaned against a piling, the mist beading on his wool cap, and recognized the shape of those gaps, for they mirrored the empty spaces in his own life: the wife lost to a riptide 30 years prior, the son who joined the merchant marine and never sent a postcard, the lighthouse that had been automated 10 years before, leaving him with only the gulls and the fog for company. 
The final gap came soft, as mist curling around a piling: the stranger had known he was dying, his heart giving out on a solo sail he had taken to chase some faint echo of the wonder he had lost, and he had pinned the edelweiss to his lapel not as a memento of the past, but as a signal, cast out for whoever might find him, so he would not vanish entirely without leaving a trace that he had once loved deeply, grieved deeply, existed. 
Silas's softly persistent focus had not failed him; he had read the gaps correctly, and for a long minute he stood as the mist thickened, letting the borrowed memories settle soft in his chest, no longer just empty spaces but pieces of two isolated men, tied together by a single pressed flower. 
When the first lighthouse beam swept over the dock, painting the mist in streaks of milky opal, Silas pinned the dried Edelweiss boutonniere to his own oilskin lapel, right above his heart. 
He did not know the stranger's name, would never know the exact lilt of his laugh or the precise shade of his daughter's childhood hair, but he would carry those borrowed memories with him on every twilight walk to the dock, a quiet witness to a stranger's isolation, proof that no one had to vanish completely as long as someone was willing to look for the gaps they left behind.
