After the last forest burns, the ash drifts for three years before settling soft as lint on the glowing dune in bloom. 
The only permanent resident is the dreamless freight guard, who walked the rail lines until the ties melted into slag, their pockets crammed with half-erased train tickets and a chess clock frozen at midnight. 
Beneath the dune’s bioluminescent moss and tiny, flame-colored wildflowers lies the divergent archive, a repository of every choice the world unmade, every life cut short before it could hum, every quiet joy snuffed out when the last canopy turned to cinder. 
The archive is occasionally everywhere, its edges seeping into cracked sidewalks and empty bird nests and the gap between a child’s last laugh and the silence that follows, never staying anchored long enough for grief to pin it down. 
The guard does not sleep, has not slept since the first smoke signal curled over the ridgeline, because their work is to cultivate the sparks in the unlit corners where the archive bleeds through, to tend the small, fragile things no one else remembered to carry. 
The chess clock frozen at midnight is their only tool besides their calloused hands, its two dials stilled the moment the last old-growth oak collapsed, holding a sliver of the moment before loss became permanent. 
They do not cast grand spells or shout incantations to reach the archive’s scattered contents; instead, they charm the loose quantum threads tangled in the dune’s glowing blooms, working through wave functions that hum at the same frequency as a lullaby half-forgotten, a hand on a fevered brow, the first rain after drought. 
Each spark they tuck into the moss is a shard of what could have been: a girl who would have taught fireflies to spell out poetry, a grove of redwoods that would have stood for a thousand more years, a grandfather who would have taught his grandchild to carve wooden chess pieces. 
Sometimes, when the wind shifts, the clock’s frozen hands twitch, as if the midnight it holds is leaning closer, trying to tip over into a new morning where the archive’s stored possibilities can root instead of drift. 
On nights when the dune’s blooms glow bright enough to outshine the ash-dimmed stars, the guard walks the edges of the old burn zone, their boots leaving faint prints where wildflower seeds tucked in their cuffs drop into cracked soil, the archive’s presence rippling out ahead of them like a half-remembered song. 
They hum as they work, snatches of lullabies no living person remembers, the notes cracking when they catch on a shard of grief, softening when they land on a spark ready to grow. 
Once, they charmed a spark of a fox kit that would have denned in the old forest, pulling it through the soft fold of a wave function tangling around a clump of blooming dune grass, and watched as it solidified into fur and whiskers, its tiny paws leaving prints in the moss before it trotted off toward the first patch of new green sprouting beyond the dune. 
Travelers who stumble across the dune sometimes say they see the guard out of the corner of their eye, in a hundred places at once for the span of a breath, which is how they know the archive is near, occasionally everywhere, holding every lost thing close until it is ready to be born again. 
The guard never checks the clock, never tries to wind its stuck springs, because they know midnight is not an end, just a hinge between what burned and what will bloom when enough sparks have taken root. 
They know the forests will not come back fast, will not look the same as the ones that turned to ash, but the sparks do not need to replicate what was lost to be worth tending. 
They will keep walking, keep humming their cracked, half-remembered lullabies, keep charming loose threads through humming wave functions, until the dune’s blooms spread all the way to the horizon, until the unlit corners glow soft with the light of all the worlds that almost were, and the clock finally ticks past midnight into a morning no one thought possible.
