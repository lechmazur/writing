The hush hung thick over the sheet music archive tucked into the last standing cottage at the edge of Hollowbrook Orchard, the loaded, breath-held quiet that settles in the final 36 hours before crews arrive to fell 72 years of gnarled apple trees for a strip mall development. 
Marnie, the city's infamous gossip-column exorcist, shouldered open the warped screen door, her scuffed work boots crunching over fallen apple blossom that had drifted through the gap under the threshold. 
She had not spoken to her sister Clara, the archive's curator, in 12 years, not since a petty, wine-fueled fight at their mother's funeral led Marnie to write a throwaway blind item mocking Clara's devotion to "dusty old paper no one cared about" rather than admitting she was terrified of being left alone with their grief. 
Tucked in the inner pocket of her frayed canvas jacket was the antique perfume bottle their mother had left them both, cut from cranberry glass, its stopper sealed tight with decades of dried jasmine residue, the same scent that had clung to every piano recital, every apple-picking afternoon, every quiet fight they had ever refused to finish. 
Marnie did not carry crucifixes or holy water for her exorcism work; she used a jury-rigged box of batteries and copper wiring that stirred induced currents in old spaces, pulling loose the tangled, static-clung emotional residue that most people mistook for poltergeists, the same quiet charges that held unspoken apologies and half-remembered lullabies as readily as they held petty grudges. 
Clara looked up from sorting water-stained waltz manuscripts when Marnie stepped in, her hands smudged with archival ink, her face tight with the same wariness she reserved for patrons who tried to sneak rare sheet music out to sell on eBay. 
Before Clara could tell her to leave, Marnie set her current box on the oak reading table, flipped the switch, and spoke in the slow, careful tone she used for clients, the one that always advised through metaphors rather than blunt, messy truth, because metaphors gave hard feelings a soft place to land. 
"Apple trees don't stop holding the memory of fruit just because someone comes with a saw," she said, as the box hummed, sending thin, invisible currents curling through the stacks of sheet music, lifting loose pages like slow, paper moths. 
"Sisters don't stop sharing a root just because one of them was too much of a coward to say she was hurting instead of lashing out." 
The currents caught the dried jasmine residue sealing the antique perfume bottle as Marnie pulled it from her pocket, popping the stopper loose so the soft, decades-old scent drifted through the room, tangling with the smell of old paper and ripening apples drifting through the open window. 
For a long minute, Clara said nothing, then reached across the table to turn off the humming current box, her fingers brushing Marnie's for the first time in 12 years. 
She did not forgive Marnie outright, not in that moment, and she did not have some magic contact at city hall to stop the crews from cutting down the orchard; these were the imperfect miracles no column ever promised, the kind that do not erase loss or turn back time, but stitch the frayed edges back together just enough to hold. 
Clara pulled out a tattered copy of the lullaby their mother used to sing, the score dotted with both their childhood crayon marks, and slid it across the table so Marnie could see. 
When Marnie reached for the page, she did not just touch aged paper; she began to reconnect, one slow, unpolished word at a time, no snappy column punchline, no dramatic exorcism fanfare, just two sisters sitting in the hush, the scent of jasmine curling between them as the first distant rumble of a work truck rolled up the rutted access road, carrying the sharp, metallic promise of saws that would arrive at dawn.
