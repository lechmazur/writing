The last tick of the chess clock faded over the hillside cemetery an hour before midnight, leaving only the low thrum of crickets and the glint of starlight spilling onto the sloped metal flank of the old cobalt silo tucked between the oldest, moss-caked plots. 
Maren, the blithe cemetery gardener who tended those plots with more care than most locals gave their own living family, did not startle when the silo’s rusted hatch creaked open on its own, as it did every year on the anniversary of the weaver’s burial. 
She slung her trowel into the loop of her canvas overalls, hefted the wicker basket at her feet, and stepped inside, where the air smelled of dried lavender and old wool, and the weaver’s life’s work sat on a rough oak table, its edges frayed from decades of uncharted echoes seeping through the silo’s stone foundation. 
From her basket, she pulled the antique sovereign case she had unearthed three months prior while planting lily of the valley on the weaver’s grave, its velvet-lined compartments tenderly organized with the small, unmarked brass bobbins the weaver had used to stitch every color into her final tapestry. 
Local lore held the weaver had been branded a witch for stitching hidden messages into the funeral shrouds she made for the town’s dead, but Maren had never believed the tales, and her quiet motivation for making the yearly trip to the silo was to discover the shape of intentions the old woman had woven into her work, rather than the cruel lies the town had stitched onto her name. 
The silo’s interior held that soft, velveteen gloom she loved, no harsh shadows, only the dim silver of starlight slanting through the vent slats to gild the edges of the massive tapestry draped across the table, its central panel faded so thoroughly none of the original imagery remained visible. 
For three years she had come to this spot, and tonight she knew she would finally untangle the mystery of the weaver’s work, not by prying at the stiff central weave, but by decoding frayed threads in a tapestry’s border that matched the exact weight and dye lot of the threads wound around the bobbins in her sovereign case. 
The chess clock that had run down an hour prior belonged to the small, mismatched chess set the town left on the weaver’s grave every year, a silly ritual where locals took turns playing against an empty seat, convinced they could "outwit" the ghost they still blamed for every lost crop and untimely death in the valley. 
She sat on the weathered wooden stool beside the table, leaned close, and let her fingers brush the frayed border threads, each one holding a thin, thrumming echo of the person the weaver had stitched it for: a child’s laugh, a farmer’s quiet apology to his old workhorse, a widow’s soft goodbye to her husband of 50 years. 
These were the uncharted echoes the town had feared for centuries, not hexes or ill will, but small, sacred fragments of the lives the weaver had tended to, stitched into the border so they would not be forgotten even when headstones crumbled and names faded from leather-bound family bibles. 
As she matched each frayed thread to the corresponding bobbin from her sovereign case, the shape of the weaver’s intentions became unmistakeably clear: she had not been weaving curses, but a map of all the quiet love the town had refused to see in one another, a tapestry of connection hidden in plain sight along the edge no fearful local had ever bothered to examine. 
Maren smiled, tucking a stray thread of larkspur blue back into its proper place, knowing she would plant a thick border of larkspur and lily of the valley around the silo the next spring, so every visitor would be greeted by the same soft, persistent care the weaver had stitched into every thread, long after the town’s silly chess matches and old, ungrounded fears faded to quiet dust.
