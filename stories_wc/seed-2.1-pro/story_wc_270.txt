The steam off the obsidian-lined volcanic hot springs curled thick enough to blur the scaffolding where crews stacked cut tuff for the new community hall, during the reconstruction that followed the caldera’s cataclysmic eruption three years prior. 
Elara, the valley’s sharp-tongued insurgent grandmother, knelt at the spring’s mossy edge, prying flaky mineral crust from a half-buried stone casket the old council had ordered sealed forever a generation prior. 
She had never sought the odd trait that made her accidentally telepathic with plants, a permanent quirk from breathing concentrated volcanic sulfur fumes as a 17-year-old hiding from the authoritarian old guard’s enforcers in a fern-choked lava tube, but the sphagnum moss at her knees had murmured for weeks of a locked truth buried where the spring water ran hottest, untainted by the rigid dogma that had nearly gotten the valley killed in the eruption. 
The moss had not led her to the casket alone; tangled in the willow branches overhanging the spring was a tattered, abandoned kite, its rice-paper face long dissolved by steam, its braided reed tail etched with the looping, cryptic script the old council had used for their most sacred, hidden records. 
For three evenings after her construction shifts, Elara had sat on the warm stone edge cross-referencing the tail’s marks with the fragmented oral lore her own insurgent cell had preserved when the council banned unapproved history, working to decode the cryptic script on the tail of the abandoned kite that promised the key to the casket’s lock. 
Her motivation was not rebellion for rebellion’s sake, but a quiet, fierce drive to evolve tradition with intent, rather than letting the valley rebuild either the old council’s cruel, rigid hierarchies or a rootless new order that discarded every lesson the land had ever taught its people. 
When she finally mapped the last etched mark to the correct sequence of mineral deposits along the casket’s edge, she pressed three fingers to the warm stone in the pattern the script described, and felt the ancient lock click open to let her unseal the lid that had stayed shut for 60 years. 
Inside, cushioned in a bed of dried, still-fragrant pine needles, sat the embryo in amber, a thumb-sized nodule of golden, fossilized resin holding the tiny, perfectly preserved embryonic form of the valley’s native sacred pine, a tree the old council had declared extinct a century prior when they burned the old growth to clear land for their exclusive resort compounds. 
The old council’s hidden records, tucked under the amber nodule, explained the core concept of implanted destiny that had warped the valley for generations: the council had fabricated a prophecy that the pines would only return when a leader of their bloodline sat in permanent, unchallenged power, using the story to hoard resources and crush dissent even as the caldera rumbled warnings the now-extinct pines would have helped them read through their deep root network. 
As Elara lifted the amber nodule, the hot spring’s steam curled around her wrists, and the moss at her knees sang with quiet relief, because the pine embryo was viable, ready to be germinated in the warm, mineral-rich spring soil and planted along the new community hall’s slopes to rebuild the old root network that had once warned the valley of eruptions, no fake prophecy required. 
She tucked the amber into the inner pocket of her worn work jacket, pausing to run a finger over the kite’s reed tail before tucking that too into her bag, to display in the new community hall alongside the council’s old records so every resident could see how easy it was for powerful people to sell a fake destiny as unshakable tradition. 
A young volunteer carrying a load of cedar shingles called a greeting from the scaffolding, and Elara waved back, breathing in the sharp, sulfur-kissed air as the first faint, telepathic hum of the dormant pine embryo reached her, soft with the promise of a future the valley would get to write together, line by line.
