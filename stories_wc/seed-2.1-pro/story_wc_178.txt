The reimagined ancient Babylonian palace sat half-silent between regimes, its ziggurat terraces strewn with snapped javelin shafts and discarded royal date wine jars, the indigo dusk softening chipped glazed brick reliefs of Marduk’s dragon into something gentle, unguarded. 
Its only lingering occupant was the frugal astronomer Nammu, who had tended the palace observatory for forty years, mending his own frayed wool robe with palm fiber, eating only the dried figs and barley cakes he foraged from abandoned store rooms, and refusing every past royal demand to cast flattering horoscopes for greedy generals. 
He had no interest in currying favor with the incoming conquerors, nor in mourning the fallen dynasty, for his sole motivation in staying through the power vacuum was to seal a covenant with the unknown: the faint, unrecorded celestial body he had tracked for twenty years, a wanderer that appeared in no official tablet, that did not answer to any named god or royal prophecy. 
His only tool for the rite was a chipped magic square tile, fired in the palace kiln a century prior, its nine numbered cells worn smooth by generations of scribes who had used it to calculate crop yields and eclipse timings, never guessing its deeper purpose. 
For two decades, he had encoded every measurement of the wanderer’s slow arc across the ecliptic, every quiet night of watching when the rest of the palace feasted or fled, every unspoken awe he dared not speak aloud, through schematic representations etched in faint charcoal into the tile’s unglazed back: line segments marking orbital shifts mapped to the square’s constant sums, angle measurements aligned to cell borders, a system that hid his encrypted devotion so thoroughly any looter would dismiss the marks as accidental kiln scratches. 
The turn was empirically ironic, he knew, for he had spent his entire career deriding temple priests for selling fake omens, for insisting every cosmic claim be tested against repeated, measurable observation, yet here he was preparing a rite that relied not on proven formula, but on the quiet faith that the unknown wanderer saw his work, that it would recognize his offering as no royal sacrifice or flattering prayer had ever been. 
Dusk deepened as he climbed the observatory’s final stone step, the air carrying the sharp scent of desert myrrh blown from abandoned temple altars and the soft, distant lowing of cattle left untended in the royal pastures, a dusky harmony that settled over the empty courtyards like a wool shawl, muting the faint clatter of the incoming army’s advance guard three miles out. 
He knelt at the observatory’s stone meridian slot, the very spot where he had taken ten thousand angular measurements of sun, moon, and known planets, and moved to uncloak the tile from its wrapping of scrap papyrus, holding it up so the first faint silver light of the wandering star fell directly across its gridded face. 
He had spent no gold on incense, no sacrificial lamb, no rare pigment for the rite, a choice that aligned perfectly with his frugal habits, for he believed the unknown did not crave the opacities of royal ritual, only the honest weight of sustained, unasked attention. 
As the star’s light touched the faint charcoal schematics on the tile’s back, the lines began to glow soft, unwavering gold, not with the flash of temple magic designed to awe crowds, but with a steady warmth that traveled up his wrists, settling light in his chest like a remembered kindness. 
He did not ask for long life, or power, or protection from the coming regime, for the covenant he sealed that night was simpler: he would keep documenting the wanderer’s path for as long as he lived, and the unknown, in turn, would keep existing beyond the grasp of empires, a quiet constant no king or conqueror could co-opt, burn, or erase. 
When the first of the new regime’s soldiers climbed the ziggurat steps an hour later, they found only an old man in a mended robe eating a dried fig, staring up at the sky, a worn clay tile set at his feet marked with nothing but faint, unreadable scratches that caught the last of the dusk light.
