High above the ash-choked lowlands where clanking gearworks droned in endless, unfeeling repetition, the suspended palace anchored to giant stalactites hung like a cracked glass bell, its onyx terraces draped in tangles of dead vine and rusted steam pipe. 
The bedraggled prince, his ermine collar matted with cave moss, his crown dented where a flying piston had knocked him from a parapet weeks earlier, moved through the crumbling halls with the slow, trembling focus of a man chasing a half-remembered lullaby, driven by a singular, burning motivation: to reawaken beauty in a mechanical world that had traded song and softness for unceasing, cold efficiency. 
He had no complete manuscripts to guide him, no living elders to recall the world before the gears, so he worked by hypothesizing about everything: testing which mosses glowed when brushed with warm steam, which wire alloys carried a resonance that made his teeth hum, which shards of stained glass cast prisms that looked almost like flower petals when held to the cave's faint bioluminescent glow. 
Through the discovery of each small, fragile clue, he had pieced together fragments of a lost ritual, one he believed would call back the living world the gear-builders had smothered, and on the stone worktable at the palace's highest terrace, he had begun to assemble the components he thought required: crushed petrified pollen, a vial of condensed cave dew, a shard of mirror that held no reflection of steel or gear, and tucked in a velvet nest at the center, an extinct bird's egg he had found wedged in a stalactite crevice, its opal shell crisscrossed with fine, silvery cracks. 
He had hypothesized the egg held the dormant essence of the sky-swallows, birds whose songs were said to make metal bloom into wildflower, so he breathed warm, misty air across its shell, arranged the pollen and dew in a circle around it, and tilted the mirror shard to catch the faint glow of the cave glow-worms, his hands shaking so badly he nearly knocked the vial of dew across the stone. 
For a long, breathless moment, nothing happened, and he felt the familiar hollow ache of a failed hypothesis creep up his throat, until a thin, spiderweb crack split the opal shell, and a small, damp creature tumbled out, not the iridescent sky-swallow of legend, but a stunted, soft-feathered thing with one wing too small to ever carry it above the stalactites, its chirp a thin, reedy sound that nonetheless made the rust on the nearest steam pipe flake away to reveal a tiny, bright patch of green moss. 
This was the flawed genesis he had not dared to hypothesize, a rebirth that did not wash away the mechanical world in a flood of unblemished beauty, but crept in small, imperfect increments, tied to a creature that would never soar, never sing loud enough to hush the gears far below. 
He lifted the tiny, cheeping creature gently in his palm, watching as it tilted its head and nipped softly at the frayed edge of his tunic, and he felt a bitterly sweet ache spread through his chest, sharp with the grief of knowing the perfect world of old stories would never return, soft with the warm, unshakable hope that beauty did not need to be perfect to take root. 
He settled on the stone terrace, the tiny bird curled in the crook of his arm, and watched as the patch of moss spread slow as a breath across the rusted pipe, as a single tiny white wildflower pushed its way through a crack in the stone at his feet, as the low drone of the gears below seemed to soften, just a little, to make space for the bird's thin, reedy song. 
The palace swayed gently on its stalactite anchors, as if trembling in time with his quiet, gasping wonder, and for the first time in his life, the prince did not reach for a hypothesis to explain the light gilding the moss, the flower's soft scent, the warmth of the small bird against his wrist; he simply sat, and let the reverie hold him, knowing the world he would build would be cracked, and stunted, and infinitely more precious than any perfect legend he had ever chased.
