The desert fortress squatted on sunbaked sandstone, its crenellations still streaked with the rare rain that had fallen three hours prior, the air sharp with petrichor and the faint, unspooling hush that comes at the close of an unspoken dream. 
Kaelin, the fortress’s resident chef, carried two gifts that felt more like curses: she could taste memories in every ingredient she handled, and her touch was emotionally contagious, pulling unprocessed feeling from anyone her bare skin brushed as easily as wind pulls sand from a dune crest. 
She had come to the fortress for one reason only: to sandpaper rough memories, to grind the sharp, cutting edges of the garrison’s accumulated guilt and grief until they no longer drew blood when the soldiers reached for them in quiet moments. 
She had spent the weeks since her arrival mapping the landscape by analyzing tidal pool formations left in the sandstone hollows after every rare rain, tracing the faint mineral trails that marked where old water had seeped down through the dunes to structures long buried. 
The post-rain pools that morning had blazed with a faint, rose-pink mineral stain she had never seen before, a trail that led her a mile out from the fortress walls to a spot where a recent sand collapse had exposed the curved, ice-crusted eave of a coral icebound pavilion beneath the dunes. 
When she climbed down the narrow sand chute into the pavilion’s antechamber, the air hummed with the dense, heavy weight of stored remorse, centuries of unspoken regret frozen into the coral walls and glacial ice that held the structure fast against the desert’s slow shift. 
She pulled her linen gloves off as she stepped further in, her bare palm brushing a pillar of fused coral and ice, and the memory-taste hit her immediately: briny flood, a city’s worth of people choosing to lock their regrets away instead of carrying them, building the pavilion as a vault so their collective guilt would not leach out to poison the lives of their descendants. 
She had long known that unprocessed remorse did not stay locked away forever; it seeped through sand and stone, rising on the post-rain air to settle in the bones of fortress garrisons, turning small arguments into fatal fights, quiet nights into hours of screaming wakefulness. 
If she broke the vault, the raw remorse would flood the dunes and overwhelm every person for miles, but if she pressed both palms flat to the ice-coral core at the pavilion’s center, she could let her own quiet, well-practiced capacity to hold feeling subsume the centuries of locked-away pain, drawing it into her own body where she could sand each sharp edge smooth before letting it drift out again as soft, bearable memory. 
Her emotionally contagious touch, which had felt like a burden for so many years, was not just a way to pull pain from others—it was a way to carry it without breaking, to spread softness as easily as others spread anger, if she chose to lean into it. 
The ice under her palms cracked in thin, spiderwebbing lines as she leaned her weight forward, the first sharp tang of old remorse hitting her tongue like burnt bread and salt, the kind of memory that made throats tight and eyes burn even when the person who carried it could no longer name what they regretted. 
She did not flinch, even when the weight of it made her knees shake, because she knew the fortress above held people who deserved to sleep without ghosts, who deserved to taste their bread and date syrup without the bitter undercurrent of guilt that was not even their own. 
The faint glow of sunlight filtering down through the sand chute gilded the coral walls as the last of the frozen remorse melted, not into flood, but into a soft, cool mist that carried the faint, clean taste of forgiveness, sharpened by the knowledge that regret did not need to be locked away to be bearable. 
When she climbed back up to the dunes, the last wisp of the unspoken dream that had pulled her to the desert drifted away on the post-rain breeze, and she could taste, on the air drifting from the fortress kitchens, the first pot of stew simmering for the evening meal, free of the bitter ghost of old regret. 
She flexed her fingers, already feeling the faint, rough edges of the soldiers’ smaller, daily regrets waiting for her in the courtyard, and walked home, her steps steady on the wet sand, her spine straight, her heart held together with the same thin, unbreakable ice that had lined the pavilion walls for a thousand years.
