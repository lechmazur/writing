The gloom-hill coachman stepped onto the blooming jetty at twilight, his boots crunching softly over mats of succulent glasswort that had crept over splintered planks in the three years since the reef died. 
He had left his dray cart half a mile up the coastal track, its iron wheels caked in rust-red dust from old shell craters, for he knew the jetty’s rotted pilings would never bear the weight of his two shaggy horses and the oilcloth-wrapped bundle slung across his back, which held his carefully curated collection of dried seed pods gathered over five years of walking headlands, marsh edges, and abandoned clan meeting grounds. 
Few who traveled the rutted hill road knew he spoke dead languages, the guttural, vowel-thick tongues of the three coastal clans who had once shared these shores in a pact so old its written terms had dissolved into salt spray long before the war: a submerged concord, sunken beneath broken coral, sunken skiffs, and the unburied bones of the boys who had fought over fishing grounds the reef’s death had made worthless. 
His sole motivation for these twilight walks, made when the gulls fell quiet and the first stars pricked the pale indigo sky, was to let a garden reclaim a battlefield, not as a formal memorial of chiseled stone, but as a living, tangled thicket that would feed birds, hold silt, and stitch back the torn edges of the shore without erasing the memory of what had been lost there. 
He did not haul sacks of commercial compost, or drive stakes to mark neat planting rows, or rely on printed gardening guides written by people who had never felt the pull of the tide on their ankles; instead, he worked by understanding bird calls, a skill he had learned from the last surviving speaker of the northern clan tongue, who had taught him that shore birds did not sing for pleasure alone, but mapped the land’s quiet needs in every trill, chirp, and sharp alarm cry. 
A sandpiper’s staccato pip, he knew, marked patches where the silt held enough crushed shell and decomposing eelgrass to feed young roots without amendment; a marsh wren’s bubbling reel signaled spots sheltered enough from storm surges to hold slow-growing perennials; a tern’s sharp, diving cry warned of thin, barren patches that would need a handful of the rich, loamy soil he carried in his coat pockets to fertilize the seeds before they were tucked into the mud. 
The narrow vastness of the scene wrapped around him as he worked: the jetty was only three planks wide in places, its edges frayed by tides, but beyond it stretched the pale, bleached expanse of the dead reef, running unbroken to the curved edge of the horizon, a blank canvas waiting for the slow, unscripted work of regrowth. 
He moved slowly, pausing every few steps to listen, tucking a seed pod of beach pea, sea thrift, wild rose, or salt-tolerant goldenrod into every spot the birds recommended, pressing a pinch of loam over each one to hold it fast against the next high tide. 
He did not speak aloud to the dead as he worked, though he knew the old tongues well enough to recite the old clan blessings for growing things, for he believed the dead did not need words to know someone was tending the ground where they fell, turning scars into habitat, old grief into nectar for bees, broken promises into root systems that would hold the shore together long after the last person who remembered the war was gone. 
By the time the last of the sun slipped below the horizon, he had planted every pod he carried, his fingers caked in mud and salt, the air carrying the sharp, sweet scent of glasswort bloom and the distant cry of an oystercatcher flying out to roost on the last remaining chunk of standing reef. 
He knew the garden would take years to spread, that storms would wash some seeds away, that the submerged concord would not rise again in his lifetime, but he also knew that growth did not rush, that the birds would keep mapping the shore’s needs, and that every rooted plant was a quiet word spoken in a language older than war, a promise that even the most broken places could bloom again if someone was willing to listen first.
