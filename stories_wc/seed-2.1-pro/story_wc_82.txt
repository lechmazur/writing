Three weeks after the crafty lantern maker’s shadow detached from her heels mid-funeral procession, she poled her reed skiff to the floating island tea garden, the jagged silhouette of the glass basilica on a floating isle glinting high above the jasmine trellises. 
The tea house keeper, a woman with silver threaded through her braids, poured her a cup of osmanthus tea without asking, wordlessly nodding to the vine stair that coiled up through cloud wrack to the basilica’s stained-glass doors, for every griever who came to the garden knew the shadow carried all the unspoken, unprocessed dreams the living could not bear to hold close. 
The lantern maker had come not to banish her shadow, as so many grievers did, but to retrieve the dream fragments it held of her younger sister, the girl who had taught her to fold origami cranes from rice paper scraped from lantern frames, driven by a quiet, unshakable purpose: to fold grief into origami cranes rather than let it curdle into permanent, hollow distance between her and the memory of the sister she had lost to a winter storm three weeks prior. 
As she climbed the swaying vine stair, she felt the old, thrumming magic of the floating isles rise to meet her: one descends while ascending, each step upward into the thin, sun-dazzled air pulling her deeper down into the soft, tormenting weight of memory, the sound of her sister’s laugh echoing off the cloud banks until she could almost feel the girl’s cold, rain-damp hand tucked into her own. 
When she pushed through the basilica’s glass doors, her shadow coiled on the altar, a writhing thing of smoked glass that held every half-remembered dream she and her sister had shared: summer nights catching fireflies, plans to open a joint lantern shop, the last frantic dream of treading icy water as the storm swept the small fishing boat away. 
She pulled the rough chunks of rose quartz from her pack, settling cross-legged on the basilica’s glass floor as she set to work with her gem polishing cloths and fine grit, the craft she had honed over decades making lantern lenses that could catch and hold the soft glow of dream light without letting it burn too bright to bear. 
Hour by hour, as she buffed each quartz chunk to a clear, rose-kissed sheen, the shadow began to soften, stretching toward the polished gems as if magnetized, spilling fragments of recorded dream into the stone until every fleck of tormented grief held a tiny, glowing image of her sister’s face. 
When the last stone was polished, the shadow did not lash out or vanish, as she had half-feared, but began to transform, melting from smoked, jagged glass into hundreds of thin, crisp squares of rice paper, each printed with the faint, warm glow of the dream fragments now held safe in the quartz cores she would set into her lanterns back home. 
She sat for another two hours, fingers moving in the familiar, muscle-memory folds her sister had taught her at seven years old, working to fold grief into origami cranes, each crease tucking a sharp edge of pain into a soft, curved wing, each crane holding a single polished quartz gem at its center so the dream light glowed through the rice paper like a captured star. 
The tea house keeper looked up from wiping cups when she stepped back onto the garden’s mossy paths, and gave a small, knowing smile, for she had seen hundreds of grievers climb to the basilica, but few who chose to carry their grief forward rather than cast it into the clouds. 
When she poled her skiff back toward the cliffs as the first stars pricked the dark, she lifted one small origami crane from her pack and held it up to catch the moonlight, watching its quartz core glow with the echo of her sister’s laugh, proof that torment did not need to be erased to shine, and that love could outlast even the split of a shadow from its bearer.
