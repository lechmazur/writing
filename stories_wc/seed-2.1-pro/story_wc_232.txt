The lonely antique map dealer had not traded a word with a neighbor or customer in twelve days when the swollen midsummer creek spilled its banks, sending silty water curling across the worn floorboards of his cluttered high-street shop. 
Elias, the dealer, was empirically intuitive, a skill forged over thirty years of matching tattered map fragments to forgotten footpaths, cross-referencing smudged survey marks with long-vanished milestone stones, and spotting forgeries by the faint off-grain of pressed rag paper that no scientific instrument could flag faster than his quiet, observation-honed gut. 
For seventeen years, he had carried a lingering promise, made the night his older brother Luka slung a rucksack over his shoulder and left town to search for the fabled doorway of decisions, the liminal threshold where unmade life choices coalesce into walkable paths, vowing to leave a sign so Elias could follow when he was old enough. 
The floodwater brought him that sign, bobbing against his worktable leg: a dented brass type foundry matrix chip, etched with the sharp serif of a capital L, the exact piece Luka had stolen from the print shop where he'd worked the summer he left, the only trinket he'd ever said held enough weight to mark a path between worlds. 
When the chip slipped from his wet fingers and clattered to the floor, Elias noticed a thin, steady column of black carpenter ants trundling past his boot, each carrying a minute fleck of glowing gold pollen toward the gap under his back door, moving with the unwavering purpose of creatures that knew exactly where they were bound. 
He pulled on his rubber boots, tucked the matrix chip into the breast pocket of his flannel shirt, and stepped out into the drizzle, following ants home as they wove around submerged fence posts, crossed slick stone outcroppings, and climbed the sloped wooded trail that led up from the creek valley to the limestone bluffs he had explored a hundred times as a boy, never guessing what lay inside. 
The ants vanished through a narrow crack in the bluff face that Elias had always written off as too small to enter, but the rising creek had pooled at the base of the rock, wearing away years of packed silt and widening the opening just enough for him to squeeze through, his shoulders brushing cool damp stone as he ducked into the dark. 
What he found inside was the blooming cavern at midsummer, a vast vaulted space strung with pale bioluminescent cave orchids that unfurled their creamy petals only once a year, when the high creek water seeped through the limestone to feed their hidden root systems, casting soft gold light across smooth stone floors dusted with the same pollen the ants had carried. 
At the center of the cavern, a faint, warm light began to glimmer, pulsing in time with the slow drip of water from the stalactites, resolving into the outline of a rough-hewn oak door frame hung with a frayed wool scarf that Elias recognized instantly as the one he had helped Luka knit for his first winter camping trip, the one Luka had worn the night he left. 
He pulled the dented matrix chip from his pocket, and as his fingers brushed the etched serif, he understood that Luka had never come back because he had chosen a path that led away from their small town, but the promise he left was not an invitation to follow; it was a reminder that Elias did not have to stay trapped in his own lonely stasis, waiting for someone else to choose his path for him. 
The glimmer brightened as he stepped closer, and he saw that there was no single door behind the frame, but a dozen faint, overlapping paths stretching out into the dark: one leading back to his quiet shop, one leading to the coastal town he had dreamed of visiting as a teen, one leading to the community garden where he had long avoided the part-time librarian who left pressed flowers on his shop porch, one leading to a dozen small, brave choices he had spent years putting off. 
He set the matrix chip on the stone ledge at the base of the door frame, where the ants could crawl over it on their way back to their nests deep in the cavern, and smiled for the first time in weeks, knowing he no longer needed someone else's sign to choose which path to take when he walked back out into the midsummer light, as the creek slowly began to recede.
