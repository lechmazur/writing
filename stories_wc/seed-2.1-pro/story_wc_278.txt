When time folds in on itself, the laboratory of lost sounds drifts unmoored between eras, its frosted glass doors catching the glow of gas lamps one minute and neon bar signs the next, shelves crammed with wax cylinders of half-forgotten lullabies, cracked Dictaphones holding final words smothered by static, and unmarked memory chips thrumming with the low warble of haunted firmware.  The constellation tattooist found the lab on the third night of chasing the sound that had lived in her chest for seven years, the thin, reedy cry she had failed to answer when her infant son stopped breathing in the next room while she finished inking Orion’s belt on a regular’s wrist.  She carried in her coat pocket a piece of amber with an insect inside, the teething gift she had bought for the boy three days before he died, its trapped gnat frozen mid-flight as permanently as the sound of his last fussy cry was frozen in the loops of the old baby monitor she still kept plugged in by her bed.  She had learned of the lab via handwritten letters found in antique furniture, a tattered bundle tucked in the secret drawer of a 1920s oak rocking chair she had bought at a yard sale the year prior, pages scribbled in faded iron gall ink by the lab’s long-dead curator, who wrote of a device that translates baby cries not into words of want or discomfort, but into the quiet, unspoken messages infants carry from the soft edges of the world before they fully learn to be human.  The curator’s notes warned the device ran on haunted firmware, code tangled with every unresponded cry, every missed lullaby, every quiet apology a parent never got to speak, so that without a stable anchor, its transmissions bled into one another in a sharp, clanging dissonance that could crack a listener’s ribs from the inside out.  The tattooist knew she had not come to demand a last conversation with her son, only to hush the dissonance of old guilt that had made it impossible for her to ink a single constellation in two years, her hands shaking too badly to trace the delicate lines of Lyra or Ursa Minor without seeing the curve of his tiny fist against his cheek.  She pulled the amber from her pocket and set it on the device’s copper input plate, watching the trapped gnat catch the dim blue glow of the circuit boards as she wove a fine thread of inked silver from her tattoo kit between the amber, the monitor, and the soft patch of skin over her own heart to tether the firmware’s wild, looping transmissions solely to the frequency of her own son’s cry.  For a long, breathless moment, the lab hummed with the meteor hush that falls between eras when time stops folding long enough for a single truth to cut through the noise, no clang of unheard grief, no crackle of static, just the soft, warbling translation of the cry she had replayed in her head a thousand times.  The translation did not hold blame, or a plea for help she had failed to answer, only a soft, sleepy observation of the glow-in-the-dark star stickers she had stuck to his ceiling, the little dipper positioned right above his crib, bright as a fallen meteor.  She sat on the lab’s scuffed wooden floor for what felt like an hour and what felt like a second, time folding soft around her as she traced the curve of the amber with her thumb, the gnat inside still frozen mid-flight, the hum of the tethered firmware soft as a lullaby, no dissonance left to rattle in her bones.  When she walked back out through the frosted glass doors, she found herself on the sidewalk outside her tattoo shop, the oak rocking chair waiting on her porch, the amber warm against her chest, her hands steady enough that she knew she would ink a tiny gnat mid-flight next to the Ursa Minor tattoo on her wrist when she got inside, a small constellation for the boy who had only ever wanted to look at the stars.
