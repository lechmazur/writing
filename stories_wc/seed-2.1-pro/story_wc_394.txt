The pioneering cart glider, scuffed birch frame strung with repurposed balloon silk and dented brass railings, perched on the splintered loading dock of a gears-and-cogs workshop floating on an acid sea, its wheel hubs crusted with evaporated crystalline toxin from a hundred cross-archipelago test runs. 
It was steadily curious, a core trait baked into its oak control panel by the long-gone workshop tinker who'd cobbled it together before the acid tides rose high enough to swallow the coastal lowlands whole. 
Its singular motivation, coded into a brass punched-card processor slung under its seat, was to ring a bell no one hears yet--a chime the old tinker had insisted hummed just at the edge of perception, carried on the same thermal currents that let unpowered gliders ride between sea spires without burning a drop of fuel. 
The code driving that quest was no boring navigation routine, but a yearning algorithm, stitched together from tinker's journal entries, migratory bird flight logs, and half-remembered lullabies that pulled the glider toward gaps in the cloud cover no fixed map ever marked. 
Tucked in a slot cut into the glider's control panel, next to a cracked pressure gauge, was a hunter's arrow tip, knapped from obsidian so black it seemed to drink in the workshop's sputtering gaslight, a gift from a cliff-dwelling forager the glider had ferried across a toxic squall three seasons prior. 
Throughout the evolution of its own flight systems, it had tried every navigation trick in the tinker's manuals, from star-sighting to smoke tracing, but none led it to the invisible bell. 
The day it stumbled on the dented brass kaleidoscope tucked behind a shelf of mismatched cog sprockets, it realized it had been reading the world wrong all along, looking for straight lines and fixed bearings where only fractured, shifting patterns held the key. 
It rigged the kaleidoscope to its front-facing sensor array, so every shift of colored glass and mirrored shard fed directly into its yearning algorithm, and set to work to transliterate the tumbling patterns into flight coordinates, reading the spinning triangles of sunset orange, acid-sea green, and cloud white like a text written in light. 
It did not chase these coordinates by cold calculation alone, but navigated entirely through the images in a kaleidoscope, letting the tumble of shards dictate when it caught a thermal, when it dipped to avoid a plume of corrosive sea spray, when it veered toward a jagged sea stack it had never marked on prior flights. 
It felt a little silly, at first, trusting a child's toy to guide it past outcrops where acid fumes could eat through its silk wings in 10 seconds flat, but there was a giddy, reverent thrill to it too, the kind of awe that doesn't require solemn hymns or stiff ceremony, just the guts to trust that beauty can be a map. 
Three days into this new flight path, the sun hit the obsidian hunter's arrow tip slotted in its control panel, bounced a shard of inky black light into the kaleidoscope's lens, and the whirling patterns locked into a single, steady shape pointing straight up, toward a thermal no instrument had ever detected. 
It rode that thermal higher than it had ever climbed, past layers of acrid yellow cloud, until it broke through to the bright, clear upper sky, where the air didn't reek of burnt metal and the cogs of the floating workshop far below looked like tiny, whirring toys. 
There, slung from a tangle of wind-carved cloud ivy, was the bell: pitted brass, strung with seabird feathers, so high up that its vibrations never reached the settlements huddled on the acid sea's rocky outcrops, which was why no living soul had ever heard its ring. 
It angled its birch frame so the sharp, obsidian hunter's arrow tip protruded from its control panel, swung close as the thermal carried it past, and tapped the bell's rim once, sending a warm, thrumming chime rippling out across the sky, a sound so soft and bright it felt like laughing through sun-warmed silk. 
It didn't linger to make a ceremony of the moment, because that felt like too much stuffy pomp for a craft held together with baling wire and hope, but it did loop three wobbly victory circles around the bell before tilting its wings to glide back down, already curious about what other unheard sounds, unmapped routes, and unreadable patterns waited for it in the whirling world below.
