In the hush between starbirths, the nebula garden unfurled, its petals wrought from ionized hydrogen that glowed soft rose and pearl, drifting slow enough that their fall spanned three generations of the short-lived silicon pollinators that nested in its gas fronds. 
The garden’s sole caretaker was an otherworldly sentimentalist, a being woven from cooled pulsar wind who had tended the drifting blooms for longer than most terrestrial civilizations kept written records, its form shifting to match the curl of each petal as it fell. 
For eons, it had chased a quiet purpose: to share light without using fire, for fire consumed, and it had watched too many young civilizations burn themselves out chasing warm, ravenous flame instead of the slow, cool glow of interstellar bloom. 
It had first noticed the generational patterns playing out across the nearby rocky worlds a hundred petal-falls prior, when the first crude calculating machine gear drifted into the garden, jettisoned from a primitive probe launched by a species that would eventually annihilate itself in nuclear fire three generations later. 
Over the quiet sigh of falling petals, it mapped that repeating cycle of rise, innovation, self-destruction, using low, resonant sonar pinging that bounced off gas clouds and discarded satellite debris to trace the arc of each civilization as it rose and fell, the unbroken line of collapse stretching to a horizon that approximates infinity. 
The pocked brass calculating machine gear, its teeth worn smooth by centuries of drifting through cosmic dust, became the core of its latest project, a tiny craft built to carry the cool glow of nebula petals on a slow transit between the nearest inhabited worlds, no fire, no heat, no call to frantic growth or conquest. 
It did not rush, for the garden’s time moved differently; a single petal’s fall from bloom to gas cloud outlasted entire royal lineages, scientific revolutions, and the slow fade of collective grief after each civilization’s collapse, so the craft’s drift of a few thousand years felt no longer than a breath. 
The serenity of the garden was always threaded through with a thin, troubling undercurrent, for it knew the generational patterns were not written in stone, only etched deep by habit: each species reached for fire first, for heat and power and the quick, bright burn that let them outcompete their neighbors, never stopping to notice the soft glow that hung just above their atmosphere. 
It sent out regular sonar pinging to track the little craft as it drifted, the echoes carrying back snippets of sound from the worlds below: a child laughing as she chased a bioluminescent insect, an elder telling stories of stars that did not burn, an engineer sketching designs for a machine that ran on starlight instead of fission. 
The calculating machine gear at the craft’s core turned slow, unpowered by flame or battery, pushed only by the gentle pressure of the solar wind, its rotations calibrated to catch the nebula’s glow and focus it into a thin, steady beam that painted the upper atmosphere of each world it passed in soft rose light, bright enough to see, too faint to burn. 
Across the quiet sigh of falling petals, it watched as the first small groups of people on each world began to look up, pointing at the soft rose glow, telling new stories about a light that did not consume, that did not demand sacrifice or conquest, that simply was. 
It did not fool itself into thinking one small light would break eons of generational patterns overnight, for the stretch of repeating self-defeat does not shatter at the first touch of soft glow, but it could plant a seed, a memory of light without fire that might pass from grandparent to grandchild, slow as a falling petal. 
The sonar pinging echoed back again, carrying the sound of a young girl building a tiny replica of the gear from polished river stone, turning it in her hands to catch the sunset, already planning the first craft she would send out to carry the same soft light further out, to worlds she would never live to see. 
The garden hummed, petals drifting slow, the troubling serenity of its endless watch softening just a fraction, as the sentimentalist realized transit was never only for the small craft, but for the light itself, moving across generations, slow, unburning, steady enough to outwait even the oldest, harshest cycles of collapse.
