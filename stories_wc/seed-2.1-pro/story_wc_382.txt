I climbed the rusted fire escape to my building's rooftop garden three months after moving in, lugging an empty canister and a thin excuse to borrow sugar from the reclusive garret tenant I'd spotted tending moonflower vines and wild thyme through his fogged garret window. 
As a historic preservation carpenter, my quiet, burning motivation was to learn the language of old buildings--the half-eroded masons' marks, weathered carvings, and subtle structural cues that held the unwritten stories of every beam, lintel, and cornerstone I repaired. 
I'd seen his tattered flier taped to the lobby mail slot months prior, advertising tutoring for every dead language from Linear B to the uncategorized glyphs carved into 19th-century urban stonework, and I knew he held the key I'd spent years chasing. 
That initial, awkward request for a cup of sugar bloomed exactly the way I'd dared to hope, shifting from a one-time favor into weekly tea sessions at his dented wrought-iron table tucked between raised beds of lavender and mint. 
The dead language tutor never asked for payment, never shared his last name, and never expressed surprise when I arrived each week with splinters of old lintel or rubbings of faded stone marks to decode. 
His fingers were patiently nimble, whether he was steeping chamomile to exactly the right strength, tracing the faint lines of a stone rubbing to parse its meaning, or pressing his heavy brass antique desk seal into small discs of beeswax to mark each glyph I mastered. 
The seal bore the exact interlocked compass and acorn sigil carved into our brownstone's 1892 cornerstone, a mark no archival record had ever been able to identify for me, no matter how many city records or mason's ledgers I combed through on weekends. 
We met for our tea on the rooftop garden during a meteor shower in mid-August, wrapped in frayed wool blankets as streaks of silver light cut through the hazy city glow and moonflower petals unfurled in the cool dark, releasing their soft, sweet scent into the breeze. 
For months I had held back my sharpest questions, afraid to break the soft, warm rhythm of our visits, but that night, as a meteor blazed bright enough to gild the edges of every thyme leaf, I decided to interrogate him about the sigil, the seal, and the way the brownstone's walls seemed to hum whenever he spoke the old stone language out loud. 
I did not ask if he was the ghost of the immigrant mason who had laid the brownstone's first stone, who had disappeared from census records the year the building opened, who had left the same acorn sigil on every structure he'd built across the neighborhood for the working-class families he'd wanted to house. 
He did not ask if I had known from the first week that no other tenant in the building had ever met him, that his garret door was permanently nailed shut from the inside, that the bag of sugar he had lent me that first day was sealed with a paper label printed in 1891. 
These were our agreed upon lies, the gentle untruths that let us sit across from each other under the streaking sky without the weight of death or decades or unfinishable business pulling us apart before I had learned all he had to teach. 
He slid a small, warm beeswax disc across the table to me, marked with the acorn and compass seal, and told me I had learned enough to listen to the walls on my own, as long as I spoke slowly and did not rush the stones to share their stories. 
When I looked up from the wax disc, he was gone, leaving only his chipped celadon teacup and the faint, sharp scent of thyme hanging in the meteor-lit air. 
I did not stand to search for him, because the shape of his absence felt wrapped in mysterious familiarity, as known to me as the worn grain of the floorboards I repaired each day, as predictable as the creak of the third-floor stair I had fixed my first week in the building. 
I pressed the wax disc into my palm as a meteor streaked directly overhead, and for the first time I could make out the low, soft murmur of the brownstone below me, speaking the language I had spent years chasing, welcoming me home.
