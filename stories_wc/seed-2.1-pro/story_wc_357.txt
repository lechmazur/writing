The intrepid orchard pilot Kaelin leaned against the hull of her lightweight crop skiff, gaze sweeping the orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches as the horizon glowed the soft amethyst that signaled the start of the annual period when gravity inverts for an hour. 
She had spent three seasons watching her grafted stone-fruit boughs wilt, their shallow roots failing to hold against increasingly erratic micro-storms, and her motivation to find the source of resilience spoken of in first-settler lore burned brighter than the amber navigation lights strung along her skiff’s canvas wings. 
The first lurch of upward pull tugged at her scuffed boot soles, and the familiar whirling silence settled over the terraced valleys—no rustle of fuzzy fruit leaves, no trill of cliff-nesting pollinator moths, only the slow, weightless spin of loose loam, golden pollen, and glistening stream water lifting toward the pale sky, as if the world itself was holding its breath. 
She did not fire her skiff’s quiet thrusters, knowing churning exhaust would throw her off the invisible current; instead, she relied on the old pilot technique of navigating by water flow, tracing the thin, silver threads of upward-rushing irrigation and snowmelt as they wove through gaps in the floating basalt arches, pulling her skiff upward with gentle tugs on the wooden control yoke to match the water’s winding path. 
As she climbed higher, she became aware of a gathering of echoes—not audible sounds, exactly, but faint, tingling impressions pressing softly against the cockpit’s clear plastic: the laugh of a teen planter dropping a wicker basket of ripe peaches, the quiet gasp of a pilot riding out a brutal arch-top storm, the soft murmur of a settler singing to the very first seed planted in the planet’s rust-red soil. 
The silver water threads converged at the highest, most jagged arch, a span of pocked basalt floating half a mile above the oldest orchard terrace, and when she pulled the skiff to a gentle stop against its flat, moss-carpeted upper surface, she stepped out onto rock draped in a thick, velvety mat of emerald moss fed by centuries of inverted water flow. 
At the arch’s exact center, half-buried in soft moss, she expected to find a fabled crystal relic or a sealed cache of genetically engineered super-seed, but instead she knelt to discover a jagged broken arrow tip, napped from glossy black obsidian, the same material the first settlers had used to carve planting stakes when their imported metal tools corroded in the planet’s early, harsh cycles. 
When her bare fingers brushed the cool obsidian, the echoes swirled closer, soft and warm rather than overwhelming, and she watched in quiet awe as fine, hair-like roots shot out from the moss around the tip, curling lightly around her wrist before unfurling tiny, tender leaves that grew faster than any plant growth she had ever documented, proof the shard carried an inherent property that accelerates plant growth, fed by every quiet act of care the orchard’s tenders had offered across centuries. 
She realized then that the source of resilience was no single, glittering magic relic, but the accumulated weight of every small, persistent choice to tend the land, to keep planting even when storms tore boughs loose, to pass down quiet tricks like navigating by inverted water flow to every new pilot who climbed into a skiff to tend the trees. 
She did not pry the arrow tip loose to carry back to her wilting groves, knowing its power came from being anchored in the spot where all those echoes converged; instead, she brushed a finger over its chipped edge, tucking a single fast-grown leaf into the breast pocket of her oilskin flight suit as the first faint tug of downward gravity signaled the inversion’s final minutes. 
As she guided her skiff back down the now-falling water threads, the whirling silence softened to let in the first rustle of resettling leaves, and she knew she did not need a stolen trinket to save her orchards—she only needed to carry the echoes of all who had tended the planet before her, planting steadily, one grafted bough at a time, as the world turned gently right-side up again.
