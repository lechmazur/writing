Three years after the empathy virus scoured the last performative nicety from casual human interaction, leaving every unspoken judgment and flinch of irritation sharp enough to cut across a 10-foot gap, the rational truffle forager picked her way along the frayed rope walkways of the hanging orchard, a storm-battered ravine where gnarled hazel and apple roots twisted down through splintered suspended platforms to claw at the frothing whitewater 80 feet below. 
She had earned her reputation for rationality by dismissing every post-virus folk cure peddled by walkway tinkerers, from magnetized acorn necklaces to tonics brewed from moss scraped off lightning-struck bark, but she carried a tattered, water-stained stack of discredited 19th-century medical textbooks tucked in her foraging satchel, their margins crammed with scribbled rumors of a smuggled relic said to soften the harshest edges of post-virus connection. 
The relic, the books claimed, was an antique glass eye, blown from molten amber by a monastic glassblower before the collapse, smuggled across the quarantine line by a nurse who'd watched three ward mates die from the pain of feeling every unvarnished truth flung at them by infected patients. 
The forager understood that pain better than most: a rare persistent strain of the virus had left her involuntarily truthful, unable to pad a hard observation with even the smallest social lie, so when she told a new orchard resident their woven rope repair was fraying at a critical load point, or told a teen their foraged blackberry jam was too bitter to trade, they felt the full, unbuffered weight of her honesty as a physical blow, flinching away before she could explain she meant only to keep them safe. 
Her sole motivation for tracking the relic for six months, truffles traded for tip after tip across the ravine's swaying platforms, was to cultivate psychological safety for the ragged community that had become her chosen family, to be able to speak hard, necessary truths without leaving people feeling gutted, unseen, or unvalued. 
She found it tucked in a knothole 20 feet above the highest orchard platform, wrapped in a scrap of old nurse's scrub, the amber glass eye warm from months of sitting in sun slanting through storm-scoured apple leaves, its dark pupil flecked with the same hazel as the truffle sheaths she dug from root tangles each week. 
The old textbooks promised the eye did not block truth, but would occlude the cruel, unmediated sharpness the virus stoked in honest speech, wrapping hard words in the quiet, gentle warmth of someone who spoke only because they cared, not because they wanted to wound. 
She tested it on the walk back down, pausing by the platform where the teen had set out their bitter jam the week prior, the glass eye tucked tight in her palm as she told them the batch was too bitter to trade, but that she would show them how to balance the tartness with crushed hazelnut and dried apple if they wanted to learn. 
The teen did not flinch. 
They nodded, and pulled a crumpled bag of foraged hazelnuts from their pocket to offer as a first lesson payment, and for a second the forager thought the discredited textbooks had delivered an impossible, perfect cure, the kind that would stitch their fractured community back together without a single frayed edge. 
But when a gust of storm wind hit the walkway a minute later, making the ropes sway so hard she had to grab a rail to keep her balance, she glanced down at her palm and realized the glass eye had a thin, spiderwebbing crack running from its iris to its edge, smuggled across quarantine lines and hidden in a knothole for years but still fragile, still imperfect, just like the people who clung to the orchard's swaying platforms. 
It would never erase the sharpness of hard truths entirely, never stop every flinch, never make connection easy in a world still battered by the virus's aftershocks, but it would soften the edges just enough to let them lean in, to listen, to build something gentle even when every rope and root and relic held a crack. 
As she curled her fingers tight around the warm, cracked glass eye and turned toward the platform where the new resident worked on their frayed rope repair, the storm wind carried the scent of ripening apples and black truffles through the ravine, sharp and sweet and broken all at once, the only kind of grace the broken world had left to give.
