The between-worlds translator stepped onto the rope-plank span of the alchemist’s market strung high above city rooftops, her scuffed boots catching on frayed hemp where gusts off the harbor tangled strings of glass wind chimes forged from melted clock faces. 
She occupied the soft, unmarked stretch of life between childhood and now, old enough to have outgrown the unshakable belief that every lost thing waited just around the next corner, but young enough to still chase the stories her grandmother had whispered to her before the coastal fog took the old woman’s memory. 
Her work, and her quiet quest, was rooted in a drive to honor overlooked histories, the lives erased from official harbor records: the Black lighthouse keepers, the Indigenous teen coast watchers, the widowed women who had tended the beacon through hurricane seasons without a single line of print marking their names. 
She tracked traces of these forgotten caretakers through hidden details in paintings, the tiny, easy-to-miss brushstrokes in seascapes sold at flea markets and museum gift shops that hinted at a calloused hand on the lighthouse rail, a braided scarf fluttering in the wind, a chipped mug set on the beacon’s metal ledge. 
She called the small, thrumming clues she followed anchors of intuition, the quiet pulls in her chest that cut through the noise of official narratives to point her toward what the world had tried to discard. 
For three months, those tugs had pulled her to this sky-high market, to a tented stall draped in faded sailcloth where a vendor sold fractured pieces of old beacons, each shard holding the faint, stored light of the storms it had outlasted. 
There, resting on a bed of dried sea grass, was the lighthouse lens fragment she sought, elusively tangible--there when she looked directly at it, softening at the edges if she let her focus slip, as if it might drift back to the foggy coast where it had sat for 120 years. 
The vendor nodded when she pointed to the shard, no haggling needed, as if he had been waiting for her to arrive, and she lifted a hand to unfasten the brass clasp of the leather satchel slung across her chest, the same satchel her grandmother had given her the summer she first learned to read the stories in tide pools. 
When her fingers curled around the warm, prismatic glass, the world dropped away into an eternal instant, the wind in the market ropes blending with the howl of long-ago hurricanes, the chatter of vendors mixing with the laughter of the forgotten keepers, the glint of the shard catching the high-altitude sun just as it had once caught beam light to guide ships through storm. 
She did not come to trap the light in a private collection, or sell it to a curio hunter who would strip it of its context to hang as a fancy decor piece in a penthouse far below the market’s swaying planks. 
Instead, she would carry it back to the crumbling coast guard station at the edge of the city, where a community mural of the harbor’s unsung guardians stretched across a cinder block wall, and set the fragment into the empty painted socket of the lighthouse at the mural’s center, where its stored light could glow for anyone who leaned close enough to look. 
As she tucked the shard into the soft velvet lining of her satchel, she felt a light pressure on her shoulder, as if her grandmother’s hand rested there for half a breath, carrying the salt scent of the shore she had tended for 40 years without official pay or recognition. 
She did not need a printed plaque or official historical marker to know the work mattered, because the anchors of intuition hummed steady in her chest, telling her every hidden story pulled back into the light wove a stronger, truer map of the world for the children who would one day walk the span between their own childhoods and the unwritten futures waiting for them.
