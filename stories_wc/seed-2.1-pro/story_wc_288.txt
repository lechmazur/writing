The first thing Lira registered, ten heartbeats after the reincarnation memory settled soft and sharp behind her sternum, was the dry, papery scent of vellum drifting through the tidal library above clouds. 
She was a contemporary of extinct species, she realized, as a shadow slid across the leaded window beside her: the last cloud rocs, thought vanished for three hundred years, nested in the craggy sandstone spires that anchored the floating library, their broad, iridescent wings occasionally brushing the glass as they rode cloud tides that ebbed and flowed through the open stacks like slow, silver surf. 
This was one of the brittle awakenings the old oral traditions of reincarnation warned of, when the weight of half a dozen past lives of lonely, unchosen seclusion threatened to crack the edges of the current self before it could fully set, leaving nothing but fragmented memories of walled-off cottages and missed connections. 
She had come to the library three years prior, running from the noise of lowland towns that felt too loud, too crowded, too quick to demand pieces of her she did not know how to give, and for months she had spoken to no one but the books and the wind, letting isolation curdle into a quiet, defensive loneliness she mistook for peace. 
On unsteady legs, she climbed the winding sandstone stairs to the library’s highest open terrace, where a brass sundial sat at the exact center of an ancient stone circle erected by the library’s first builders, its face atomically reflective enough to catch not just the high, thin alpine sunlight, but the faint pearlescent drift of cloud waves far below and the darting shapes of roc fledglings testing their wings hundreds of feet above. 
When she pressed her palm to the nearest standing stone, a low, resonant thrum traveled up her arm, the hum of an ancient stone circle that rose and fell in perfect time with the push and pull of the cloud tides, the same frequency that rocs tilted their heads to track before selecting a thermal to ride, the same vibration that kept loose book pages from blowing off the open shelves during high tide. 
By the hum of an ancient stone circle, she felt the tight knot in her chest loosen, as she realized she had spent years operating under a false binary: either fully cut herself off from all connection to protect her quiet, or abandon the library entirely to chase a noisy, performative version of belonging that never quite fit. 
She leaned closer to the sundial, watching her reflection blur where it overlapped with the image of a curious young roc that had hopped onto the circle’s edge to cock its amber eye at her, and understood that she did not have to erase parts of herself or her home to coexist with the world beyond her shelves. 
She could leave seeds for the rocs on the terrace rail at dawn, trade rare cloud-pressed manuscripts with lowland scholars via the small rope-and-basket pulley the rocs had learned to nudge along its guide wire, curate the library’s collection on her own slow schedule, and never again let loneliness make her feel like she had to choose between safety and connection. 
This, she realized, was the work she had been reaching for across all her past lives: to translate isolation into independence, to build a life rooted in her own needs rather than the fear of being too quiet, too slow, too fond of high places and old books to belong anywhere. 
The young roc hopped closer, tilting its head to drop a smooth, iridescent molted feather onto the sundial’s face, where it sat without casting a shadow, as if the reflective surface refused to draw hard lines between the girl, the bird, the stone, and the slow, turning world around them. 
She picked up the feather, tucking it into the spine of the nearest leather-bound poetry collection, and smiled as a gust of tide-wind carried the scent of jasmine from a lowland valley thousands of feet below, proof that connection did not require closeness, and independence did not require walls. 
The hum of the circle softened into a steady, comforting thrum as she turned to walk back down the stairs, already planning which seeds to set out for the rocs at sunrise and which manuscripts to offer the lowland scholars, the last edges of her brittle awakening melting into the quiet, unshakable certainty that she was finally home.
