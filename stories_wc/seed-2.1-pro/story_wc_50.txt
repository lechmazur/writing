The reflective locksmith had trekked three miles up the frost-crusted trail to the marble cavern at the first soft hush of the wounded season, when the valley’s oak leaves curled like burnt paper and the wind carried no birdcall, only the faint, frayed memory of summer’s last thunder. 
He was peripherally focused by trade and temperament, never fixing his gaze straight on a thing for fear of sanding down its soft, unnameable edges the way he might file a key too smooth to turn. 
His work across the lull of that wounded season centered on the harvest of echoes, a quiet craft no town almanac documented, practiced only by those who noticed how sound clung to polished stone long after its source had faded. 
The cavern’s veined white walls caught every stray sound from the world above -- a child’s laugh carried on a warm updraft, the crack of a falling branch, the lilt of a folk song sung by a hiker pausing for breath -- and held them tight, trapped in tiny fractures no wider than a key tooth. 
He had hauled the broken timer mechanism down from his shop on his first trip, its brass gears bent, its spring snapped, the device that once marked town square bell tolls and bakery oven timers until a lightning strike three months prior had fused its inner works into a useless, tangled lump. 
His motivation was simple, unspoken even to the regulars who dropped off rusted padlocks at his shop door: to store wonder in the cellar of reason, for later, when the long, gray stretch of midwinter left even the most cheerful townsfolk hollowed out, unable to recall the exact tone of a summer laugh or the soft thud of an apple hitting orchard grass. 
He knew he could not simply pry the echoes loose from the marble, for they would dissipate like smoke if forced into a metal container without a structure calibrated to hold them, so he set out to reverse-engineer the broken timer, reshaping its bent gears to catch sound instead of seconds. 
His method, honed over decades of cutting keys to fit locks he never looked at directly -- a habit born of his peripherally focused vision, which caught tiny, easy-to-miss details central sight glossed over -- involved studying reflection angles at different times, marking the marble walls with faint charcoal lines where sunlight slanted in at dawn, midday, and the thin golden hour before dusk, tracing how each angle bounced a trapped echo toward the cavern’s narrow central ledge where he worked. 
He worked slowly, never rushing, for the wounded season’s lull stretched long enough that frost crusted the cavern mouth thicker each week, and he had no deadline but the first heavy snow that would seal the trail until spring. 
As a reflective craftsman, he did not measure success by speed, but by how gently he could cradle the fragile pieces of the world others rushed past, from a bent key to a half-forgotten sound. 
He adjusted each gear tooth to align with the angles he mapped, bending brass with a small leather mallet, winding the repaired spring just tight enough to hold an echo without crushing its delicate texture, testing each calibration by holding the open mechanism to a wall fracture and listening for the faint hum of a captured sound. 
On the last day before the snow sealed the trail, he sat on the ledge and turned the timer’s key, and the device began to tick softly, not with the rigid, metronomic beat of clockwork, but with the layered, living rhythm of the echoes it held: the trill of a wren, the pop of a campfire, the low, warm laugh of the baker who had given him his first free loaf of sourdough when he was a quiet, orphaned teen learning to file keys. 
He slung the modified timer into his pack and trekked down the trail as the first fat snowflakes settled on his wool collar, already planning the shelf he would build in his shop’s cool back cellar, where reason kept his tools, his ledgers, his carefully cut key blanks, and where wonder would wait, ticking softly, to be released on the coldest, darkest midwinter evenings when the world felt stripped of all softness.
