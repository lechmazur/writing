Three cycles after the ragtag group of crash-landed foragers, retired xenobotanists, and teen stowaways stopped marking days as "survival" and started calling each other family, their stone-fruit orchards on the floating-arch-riddled orchard planet hung crisp with the sharp, sweet tang of ripening frost-plums. 
None of them could name the softly glowing figure that drifted between the archways at dusk, but the altruistic ghost felt strangely trustworthy, the kind of quiet presence that left extra sun-warmed fruit on the porches of the folks who pulled double shifts tending the youngest kids, and tucked lost tools back where their owners would find them. 
The ghost never spoke aloud, so no one expected it to reach out when the annual radiation flare, sharp enough to scorch every tender fruit bud and turn the topsoil to dust, barreled toward their valley earlier than the old scout ship’s weather monitors predicted. 
Its first message appeared at dawn, chalked in soft sky blue on the cracked concrete sidewalk that snaked between the settlement’s rounded adobe huts, left long before the first baker lit their oven, when the floating rock arches still draped long, purple shadows over the deserted path. 
The first line read "Bring the dented butterfly garden watering bell from the old community plot," followed by a crudely drawn map of the high arch ledges where no one had bothered to climb since the first harvest, and a tiny, wobbly sketch of what looked like a cloud streaked with starlight. 
Most folks paused to squint at the chalk on their morning walk, half-ready to dismiss it as a teen prank, until the oldest xenobotanist, whose hands still ached from digging the first planting beds, recognized the star-sketch as the nebula of respite, a fabled pocket of cool, radiation-dampening gas that old spacer tales claimed could be anchored to a settlement if communities offered a shared, unselfish gift. 
The group stopped to reexamine the quiet, unasked-for kindnesses the ghost had left over the cycles--the mended fence slats, the extra jars of preserved plum left for homesick stowaways, the way it had herded the pollinator moths back to the gardens after a windstorm--and realized it had never asked for anything, only ever worked to stitch their ragtag family tighter. 
The ghost’s goal, they realized, was never to do their work for them, but to orchestrate collective effort, to give them a shared task that would pull every member of the settlement into the work of protecting the home they had built together. 
Each subsequent dawn, new chalk marks appeared on the empty sidewalks, assigning tasks that played to every person’s strength: the teen stowaways, lightest on their feet, would scale the highest floating arch to anchor the guide ropes, the foragers would collect nectar from the wild cliff blooms to fill the butterfly garden watering bell, the bakers would pack honey cakes to share at the summit so no one climbed on an empty stomach. 
The climb was hard, sharp rock outcroppings snagged jacket sleeves, a sudden gust knocked one teen off balance only for a fellow climber to catch their wrist, the watering bell sloshed nectar over its dented rim when they hauled it to the top, leaving sticky, sweet trails that glinted in the thin sunlight. 
When they rang the bell, the soft, clear tone rolled out across the orchard, and swarms of iridescent blue butterflies rose from the valley below, sipping the spilled nectar and fluttering up to meet the wispy, opal-tinted curl of the nebula of respite as it drifted overhead, weaving the gas into a soft, glowing shield that settled over the entire valley just as the first wave of radiation hit. 
The ghost drifted at the edge of the group as they watched the shield hold, its glow a little fainter than before, as if the work of guiding them had cost it a small piece of the quiet energy it used to leave gifts on porches. 
No one thanked it out loud, because they knew the work was never done alone, and the hope they held was not the soft, untested kind of fairy tales, but brambled hope, tangled with scraped knees and dented metal and past grief, rooted deep enough to weather any storm as long as they showed up for each other. 
When they climbed back down at dusk, the first of the season’s frost-plums had softened to perfect ripeness under the nebula’s glow, and a single new chalk mark waited on the sidewalk by the huts, no instruction this time, just a tiny chalk butterfly, to remind them they already had everything they needed.
