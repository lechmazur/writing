Kael Voss, the amoral philanthropist who had quietly engineered the region's manufactured drought by siphoning glacial headwaters to top up his private reservoir portfolio and strong-arm small ranchers into selling mineral rights, crossed the bleached, crunching expanse of the Great Salt Lake's exposed salt flat during the lull of distant thunder, when bruised purple storm clouds hung 20 miles off and no drop of rain had touched the cracked earth in 1,127 days. 
The public knew him as a man who donated 10-cent matching grants for food banks while hoarding water futures that priced entire rural towns out of access, but no one knew he carried a small, velvet-padded brass case tucked in the inner pocket of his tailored canvas field jacket, the kind of secret a secretly sentimental man hides even from his own board of directors. 
Inside that case rested the extinct bird's egg, a faintly speckled, cream-colored relic of the salt flat tern, a species last recorded nesting on the crusted flats in 1953, its colonies wiped out when an earlier generation of developers diked the lake's southern marshes. 
He had spent six months tracing a map drawn by cracks in dried riverbeds that once fed the salt flat, walking every fissure on weekends when his staff thought he was attending charity galas, following the deep, branching splits as they converged on a single, bowl-shaped depression half a mile from the old shoreline, a spot old-timers called Tern Hollow. 
He'd first heard the story from his grandmother, who had brought him to the flats as a sickly seven-year-old, when the terns were already gone but old residents swore that if you left an offering of what the birds had lost in the exact center of the old nesting grounds, the wind would carry their sharp, keening calls back across the salt for a hundred years, an echo no drought or development could silence. 
He knew the drought he had manufactured was cruel, knew it had dried up farm wells and killed stands of ancient cottonwoods, knew it had turned the once-shimmering shoreline into a bleached wasteland, but he had long ago stopped letting moral squeamishness stop him from getting what he wanted, even when the thing he wanted was as fragile and irrational as a half-remembered story his grandma told when his fever spiked. 
The lull held, thunder a low, thrumming vibration under his boot soles rather than a sound, as he knelt in the fine, powdery salt and unsnapped the brass case, lifting the egg carefully with fingers that had signed hundreds of eviction notices and water-rights seizure forms. 
He was not there to hoard the egg, as he hoarded water and land and influence; he was there to release it, to tuck it into the soft salt at the center of the crack map's convergence, to give back the last physical trace of the terns so the wind could catch the memory held in its calcium walls and spin it out across the flats forever. 
He settled the egg into a small hollow he scraped with his thumb, covering it with a thin layer of salt that glinted like crushed diamond in the faint, filtered storm light, the gesture holding the fractured grace of a man who could burn down a whole ecosystem and still kneel to tend to the ghost of a single bird. 
His sole, unnegotiable motivation for making the long, unmarked trek was to preserve the echo of extinct birds, a sound he had chased for 40 years, ever since his grandma had leaned in during a storm just like this and told him she could hear the terns calling even when their nests were long gone. 
A thin thread of wind curled across the flat then, carrying the first sharp, high cry that was not thunder, not wind, but the exact tern call his grandma had mimicked for him all those years ago, bouncing off the salt crust and looping around him like a ribbon. 
He did not smile, because men like him did not smile at ghost birds and childhood stories, but he stayed kneeling for three long minutes as the thunder crept closer, letting the echo wrap around him before he stood and turned to walk back to his idling truck, knowing he would open the floodgates of his diverted reservoirs in three days' time, that he would still make his billions, that the terns would never come back, but their call would outlast him all the same.
