The nectar-dense dusk of late white clover bloom draped soft over the rutted access road to the city’s hilltop lightning collection tower, seventy-two hours after the cascading city-wide blackout that silenced stoplights, kitchen refrigerators, and the familiar high hum of the municipal drone beehives. 
The candid toll keeper manning the dented chain across the road was known across four adjacent neighborhoods as involuntarily truthful, incapable of uttering even the kindest white lie to soothe a harried commuter or cover for a kid sneaking up the hill to watch summer storms. 
Most residents tramping up the hill that evening expected him to parrot the utility company’s official line, that a frayed lightning conductor had fried the grid and knocked the pollinator drones offline mid-flight. 
Instead, he sat on a splintered stool beside a chipped cypress lacquer box, its surface painted with tiny, faded wild bees that had been left to him by the last commercial beekeeper pushed out when the city launched its drone pollination program a decade prior. 
He did not charge a toll that night, because he knew the utility crews planned to sweep the tower grounds at dawn, erase all record of the drone beehive failure, and reset every unit to a rigid, factory-calibrated routine that would never again deviate from its mapped pollination routes. 
His quiet, unshakable motivation, held close through years of taking change and nodding through commuter rants, was to preserve wonder in routine, to make sure people did not miss the small, unscripted magic that flickered even in the city’s most heavily engineered systems. 
When a group of tired, curious neighbors paused at the chain to ask what had really caused the blackout, he did not lie, as his involuntary commitment to truth would never allow, but he also did not spoil the surprise waiting for them two hundred feet up the hill. 
Instead, he lifted the lid of the lacquer box, pulled out a dented cardboard matchbox printed with the logo of the old beekeeper’s honey farm, and held it out to the first person in the group, a teen who had spent the blackout handing out free popsicles from her family’s corner store. 
He would not explain what waited at the tower, he said, because the point was not to hand them a boring utility report, but to let them uncover the truth themselves, guided only by the cryptic riddle scrawled in pencil on a scrap of notebook paper tucked inside the matchbox. 
The riddle read: “I fly without charge, make gold without coin, and return even when every engineer swears I am gone for good.” 
The group passed the matchbox between them as they climbed, guessing at answers from lightning to stolen utility wire, until they rounded the last bend to the tower base and the reveal hit them all at once, soft and golden in the failing light. 
A small colony of wild bumblebees, absent from the city for twelve years, had moved into the empty upper slots of the drone hives mounted to the tower’s midsection, and the pollinator drones, programmed to mimic bee behavior to maximize pollination efficiency, had begun following the wild bees off-route to forage on untended wild aster and goldenrod growing in the hill’s vacant lots. 
The fine, powdery wild pollen coating their chassis had conducted a stray surge of stored lightning charge back into the tower’s grid, blowing the main transformer and triggering the blackout, not a frayed wire or failed conductor. 
No one yelled about the inconvenience of three days without power as they watched a fuzzy bumblebee crawl over the smooth plastic shell of a dormant drone, gilded with the same thick, honeyed dusk light that had followed them up the hill. 
The teen who had taken the matchbox tucked it back into her pocket, already planning to leave a small dish of sugar water on her fire escape for the bees when the power came back on, knowing that the best parts of routine were never the parts engineers designed, but the small, wild wonders that snuck in when no one was watching. 
Back at the chain, the toll keeper smiled, knowing the wonder he had protected would outlast any factory reset the crews tried to impose.
