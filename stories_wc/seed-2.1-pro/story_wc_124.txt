The wistful lantern broker stepped past the frosted glass threshold of the greenhouse for extinct plants, her boots crunching softly on crushed fossilized fern fronds dusted across the cool flagstone path. 
She was aromatically cautious by trade and by grief, trained to parse faint scent notes that clung to the hand-blown glass lanterns she restored and sold, yet utterly unmoored by the random, sharp whiffs that pulled unbidden tears to her eyes for no reason she could name. 
Her trip to the long-sealed greenhouse was rooted in that quiet, unshakable motivation: to understand why some smells trigger tears, even when the memory tied to them seems long buried under years of quiet routine. 
Tucked in the inner pocket of her wool coat was the crystal perfume vial, the last item her grandmother, the greenhouse’s former curator, had left her, which she had never been able to bring herself to discard in the three years since her grandmother’s passing. 
The vial was cut from dense, clouded lead crystal, its stopper sealed so tight she had never been able to pry it loose, its surface etched with tiny, unreadable markings that looked like fine leaf veins in ordinary light. 
Crystal shadows stretched across the raised growing beds, sharp-edged and prismatic, cast by the panels of cut lead glass that formed the greenhouse’s peaked roof as the late afternoon sun slanted low through wispy mountain clouds. 
She pulled a small, brass-caged lantern from her satchel, the same custom design she sold to collectors of vintage scientific gear, its bulb tuned to cast a soft ultraviolet glow ideal for revealing hidden etchings and faded ink on old glassware. 
She held the lantern first over the nearest growing bed, where the last cultivated specimen of the Hawaiian mountain hibiscus, thought extinct since 1912, sat in a sealed glass terrarium; time seems to hold still as the transformation completes, petals shifting from matte olive to a pale, glowing pearl just before they unfurl for the first time in a century. 
When the UV glow fell across the crystal perfume vial she had pulled from her coat pocket, the faint, invisible etchings across its surface flared bright silver, mapping directly to the hibiscus’s leaf shape and marking the exact twist needed to pop its long-sealed stopper free. 
She twisted the stopper just as the final hibiscus petal unfurled, and the scent inside drifted out to wrap around her, soft and honeyed, cut with the sharp green tang of pine trees that had grown outside her grandmother’s mountain cottage, the faint vanilla of shortbread they baked every Sunday, the warm sun on cotton linens hung out to dry in high summer. 
She did not fight the tightness in her throat or the hot tears that spilled down her cheeks as she stopped to grieve, not just for the sharp loss she had carried for three years, but for the quiet, perfect memories she had not realized she had forgotten, tucked away so tight they only surfaced in fragmented, unplaceable whiffs that caught her off guard at the market, on the street, in the hush of her lantern workshop. 
The answer she had chased for so long, rooted in the quiet, unshakable impact of memories, was not the sharp, searing pain of loss she had expected, but a soft, tangled warmth, the kind that lingered like lantern light long after the flame was turned low. 
She had been aromatically cautious for years, holding her breath around candle stalls and bakery windows, afraid of the unnameable tightness those random scents triggered, but now she understood the tears were not a sign of brokenness, but a quiet signal that a piece of the people and places she loved was still close, carried in the air itself. 
She set the open vial on the stone ledge beside the hibiscus terrarium, letting the perfume curl through the crisp greenhouse air to mingle with the living flower’s scent, and lit one of her smallest hand-blown lanterns to leave on the ledge, its warm gold glow softening the sharp edges of the crystal shadows around her. 
She left the greenhouse that evening no longer afraid of unexpected tears, knowing every fleeting, unplaceable scent was a small, glowing memory waiting to be recognized.
