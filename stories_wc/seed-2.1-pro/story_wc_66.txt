Three years after the story famine, when every bound tale and spoken anecdote dissolved to fine, silty dust that sifted through window cracks and coat collars, the diligent orchestra copyist claimed the ember alcove in mist as her workshop. 
She had spent a decade transcribing every note and dynamic mark of the city symphony’s repertoire by hand, neat ink strokes that never smudged, a habit of patience that outlasted the concert hall’s collapsed roof and the heavy silence that followed the famine’s end. 
Her quiet, unshakable motivation was to teach dust to tell stories, for she knew the fine powder coating every windowsill and scuffed music stand was all that remained of the tales the world had lost. 
The alcove, tucked between two crumbled sandstone walls, held a single banked coal ember that never fully died, breathing faint warmth through the perpetual thin mist that curled off the adjacent canal, its edges soft as thistledown to match the thistled calm she carried in every slow, deliberate movement. 
She had salvaged a dented 1950s shortwave radio from the conductor’s locked office, its cracked casing caked in the same story-dust that coated every surface, its dial glow a thin, honeyed thread of light when she twisted it to life on her third night in the alcove. 
She soon noticed the dust that settled on the radio’s scuffed plastic face was secretly luminous, catching the dial glow to throw tiny, shifting flecks of gold across the rough stone walls when the damp canal air stirred. 
The city’s provisional council had banned all unsanctioned broadcasts after the famine, fearing any unvetted narrative would unravel the fragile, story-less order they had built, so she knew she would need to cloak her signal to avoid patrols sweeping the airwaves for rogue transmissions. 
Her method, honed over weeks of quiet late-night experimentation, was to weave the fragmented story-memories held in the dust via elusive song lyrics, lines half-remembered from old symphonic folk arrangements that registered as harmless, wordless melody to council signal scanners, but hummed with quiet narrative weight for anyone listening close enough to lean in. 
This was her pirate broadcast, transmitted on a frequency so low it only reached the small, battery-powered radios people hid in coat linings and back kitchen cupboards, its signal carried as much on the drifting mist as on conventional radio waves. 
Each night, she sat cross-legged beside the glowing ember, turning the radio dial until its warm glow fell soft across the drifts of dust she had collected in chipped ceramic teacups salvaged from the concert hall’s old cafe, letting the lyrics wind through the particles until they stirred, carrying small, vivid stories out into the curling mist. 
At first the stories were tiny: the memory of a child chasing a monarch butterfly across a summer lawn, the bright taste of lemon tarts at a symphony intermission reception, the held-breath hush before a concert’s first ringing note. 
Listeners began leaving small notes tucked in the cracks of the alcove’s sandstone walls, scribbled on scrap paper salvaged from old score sheets, saying they had woken to find thin layers of dust on their windowsills forming soft, shifting images when the morning light hit, telling stories they thought they had forgotten forever. 
She never responded to the notes, never sought out thanks or recognition, for her only goal was to teach the dust to hold and carry stories on its own, no microphones or transmitters required, long after her radio’s batteries died and the alcove’s ember finally cooled to fine gray ash. 
On a crisp October evening, as mist curled thick around the alcove walls and the radio’s dial glow painted warm gold across her ink-stained copyist’s fingers, she noticed the dust drifting out of the chipped teacups without any prompting from the transmitter, swirling in slow, soft eddies that wove themselves into the shape of a violinist drawing a bow across taut strings. 
It hummed a bar of the old folk melody she had used for her very first broadcast, the elusive lyrics clear even without a crackling speaker, carried entirely on the dust’s soft, slow motion through the mist. 
She leaned back against the cool sandstone, a small, quiet smile tugging at the corner of her mouth, as the secretly luminous dust drifted out of the alcove into the wider mist, carrying small, tender stories to every window, every doorstep, every quiet corner of the city that had ached for tales for three long, silent years. 
She did not turn the radio off that night, letting its soft glow blend with the dust’s faint light, knowing the stories would keep spreading, soft and persistent as thistledown on the wind, no scanner or patrol able to quiet them now.
