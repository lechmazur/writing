The scientific poetry library held its hush like a held breath on weekday mid-afternoons, when its small cohort of regular patrons drifted between shelves bound in buckram and hand-stitched pamphlets, paging through verses stitched to equations of orbital drift and cell division. 
Mara, the odd-hour librarian, had held this post for twelve years, taking only the shifts that fell between 2 p.m. and 10 p.m., the narrow window when light slanted just right to catch the dust motes dancing above the reading nooks, and her sanely eccentric habits drew no more than soft, affectionate smiles from the regulars. 
Tucked in the breast pocket of her corduroy blazer, she carried a tattered silk scarf dyed the deep indigo of a new moon, frayed at the edges where her grandmother had used it to mark pages in the very same collection decades prior. 
Unlike the library’s other staff, who cataloged holdings by standard Dewey decimal markers, Mara believed the collection held more than printed words, that its core was woven from analog memories left by every reader who had ever cried over a line about quantum entanglement or laughed at a limerick about thermodynamic entropy, and her quiet, unshakable motivation was to find the place where stories live, the tucked-away dimension where verses outgrew their pages to breathe on their own. 
For three years, she had pored over the collection’s most obscure pamphlets, cross-referencing lines about non-Euclidean space with marginalia left by mathematician-poets, until she realized the only path to that hidden place ran through hyperbolic geometry, the curved, crinkled fold of space that mirrored the way a single poem could hold a thousand parallel meanings at once. 
Patrons browsed around her as she worked, a graduate student flipping through a sonnet sequence about neural pathways, a retired engineer scribbling his own verse about black hole event horizons on a scrap of receipt, none of them noticing the way she ran a finger along shelf edges to map the subtle, unplotted curves of the room, her presence a quiet, unobtrusive fixture of the space’s observed solitude, seen but not scrutinized, present but not interrupted. 
When she reached the back shelf, the one tucked between the poetry of crystallography and the verse of fungal networks, she pulled the tattered silk scarf from her pocket, holding it up to the slanted light so its frayed edges caught the gold flecks embedded in the indigo dye, and began to fold it in the repeating, pleated pattern she had practiced a hundred times, each fold doubling the fabric’s surface area in the crinkled, expanding form of a hyperbolic plane. 
As the last pleat settled into place, the air around her softened, and the analog memories held in every page on the shelves began to reawaken, spilling out not as loud visions, but as soft, sensory echoes: the hush of a 1972 poetry reading, the smudge of a child’s chocolate-stained thumb on a collection of star verse, the quiet catch in a reader’s throat when they first encountered a line that felt like it had been written just for them. 
The folded scarf in her hands hummed softly, a thin, curved seam opening just above it where the shelf met the wall, a gap that did not obey the straight lines of Euclidean architecture, curving outward in impossible, gentle arcs that led to the space she had sought for so long: the place where stories live, not as fixed print, but as living, growing things that fed on every reader’s attention, every marginal note, every quiet moment of connection between a person and a line of text. 
She brushed a finger over the frayed edge of the scarf peeking out of the seam, smiled, and turned to reshelve a misplaced pamphlet about moss poetry, content to wait until the library was quiet to step across the threshold she had spent years seeking.
