The rough-hewn limestone chapel wedged between the abandoned textile mill and the overgrown rail bed serves as the valley's only reliable dimensional anchor, its mortared stones holding fast against the slow, oozing creep of a possession that has already seeped into the town's diner, its school hallways, the hush of front porches where residents sit with unblinking, empty smiles. 
The chapel's caretaker is the loop pedal priest, a soft-spoken man named Father Elio who long ago traded a booming pipe organ for a scuffed, battery-powered loop pedal he uses to layer soft breaths, distant chime strikes, and the rustle of oak leaves into contemplative soundscapes for his tiny, dwindling congregation. 
He never hangs tinsel for Advent or paints murals on the chapel's plain stone walls, for he decorates with silence, leaving measured, breath-long gaps between every looped sound, every line of liturgy, every quiet greeting to a parishioner, gaps wide enough to hold the things people are afraid to say out loud. 
On this gray October evening, he can feel the chapel's usual thick, holding stillness fray into unraveled stillness, the kind that pulls apart thread by thread as the possessing entity draws closer, its progress slow enough that he has 47 minutes, by his count, before the possession completes and the town's last spark of self-awareness is smothered under the entity's hollow, contented haze. 
On the rough wooden altar before him sits the tool he has kept for this exact moment: an artist's palette with one color, a deep, matte iron-gray that matches the hue of unvoiced resentments, unacknowledged grief, the quiet assumptions people wrap around themselves like old blankets without ever examining why they reach for them. 
His work this evening is not to shout exorcism rites or fling holy water, for his motivation is to illuminate hidden assumptions, the quiet, unexamined beliefs the entity feeds on: that the mill's closure was inevitable, that neighbors who left betrayed the town, that loneliness is just a fact of life too small to name. 
He will not fight the entity directly, but will plumb the soft, fuzzy edges of its growing hold through rhetorical devices, posing questions that act as gentle chisels: if the mill's closure was inevitable, why did the out-of-state owners sell the equipment for scrap before workers even received their final paychecks, if neighbors who left were traitors, why do so many send postcards asking after the families they left behind, if loneliness is too small to name, why does it sit so heavy on every chest? 
The words travel through the chapel's thick stone walls in a tone of muffled clarity, softened by the layers of looped breath and rustling leaves he has already cued up on his pedal, loud enough to seep into porches and diner booths but gentle enough not to jolt residents out of the entity's growing daze before they are ready to wake. 
He dabs a brush into the single gray pigment on his palette, painting thin, faint lines on the chapel's stone walls along the edges of his silent gaps, each line marking a spot where an unexamined assumption has already started to dissolve, pulled loose by the questions hanging soft in the air. 
Outside, he can see the first hints of the haze lifting: a teenager on a bike blinks and looks around like he is waking from a long nap, a diner waitress pauses mid-wipe and touches her chest like she is noticing the weight there for the first time. 
The unraveled stillness starts to knit back together, not as the heavy, smothering stillness the entity craves, but as the soft, alive quiet of a town beginning to notice its own voice again, held fast by the anchor of the chapel walls. 
He adds one more soft loop of wind through oak leaves to his pedal, lets a long stretch of decorated silence fill the chapel, and knows that even when the last gray line dries on the wall, the work of seeing the things we hide from ourselves will never truly be finished.
