High in the dust-mottled vault of the Rialto's vintage movie palace projection booth, the crystal gazer settled cross-legged on a scuffed wooden stool, her canvas satchel slung over the arm of a rusted 1920s projector. 
She had come at the thin, thrumming hour while forgotten lyrics search for tune, when disembodied lines of old show tunes and unwritten ballads wove through the velvet auditorium below, groping for the melodies that had slipped free of audience memory decades prior. 
Naturally spatially imaginative, she did not need a map to trace the hidden folds of old buildings, the tucked-away corners where emotion pooled thick enough to leave a tangible residue, and her sole motivation that night was to find where unused love is stored, the vast, unvisited cache of affection never spoken, never acted on, never given a place to land. 
The air thrummed with hushed thunder, the low, resonant pressure of all the unvoiced feeling pressed into the theater's plaster walls, not the sharp crack of a summer storm but the slow, rolling throb of something held too long behind closed ribs. 
Beneath a tattered tarp draped over a stack of discarded reels, she found it: a warped, unlabeled film strip, its celluloid edges softened by decades of trapped humidity, tucked beside a stack of ticket stubs from the Rialto's 1957 run of An Affair to Remember. 
She knew better than to rush a psychometry reading, so she did not grip the celluloid tight immediately, but let it rest in the palm of her open hand, letting the faint warmth of her skin seep into its cracked surface to meet the memories trapped within. 
For long minutes she sat quiet, letting the connection incubate, as the drifting lyrics below brushed the projection booth's smudged window, snippets of "I'll be seeing you" and "As time goes by" tangling in the cobwebs strung between the projector's lens housings. 
When the first hum of memory rose up her wrist, she lifted the strip to the faint glow of the booth's cracked exit sign, holding it up to the light to see the faint, pressed slips of paper tucked between every third or fourth frame: goodbye letters, folded thin as onion skin, written by three generations of Rialto projectionists to people they had loved but never dared to reach. 
There was the 1950s projectionist who wrote to the usherette he'd watched fall for a sailor on leave, too shy to ask her to stay before she moved across the country; the 1970s attendant who wrote to his high school sweetheart the night before he was drafted, too afraid to mail it lest she wait for a man who might not come home; the 1990s teen who worked the booth to save for college, writing to the boy who sat in the back row every weekend, never working up the nerve to say hello. 
She had expected unused love to be locked in some airless vault, frozen and forgotten, but as she ran her thumb along the celluloid, she realized the love in those letters had never been locked away at all. 
Every time the film had spooled through the projector, the faint ink of those goodbye letters had cast a thin, warm glow onto the screen, seeping into every audience member who had ever sat in the dark, heart open, looking for a reflection of their own unspoken feeling. 
The hushed thunder in the walls swelled, soft and steady, as a snippet of lyric finally tangled with a stray wisp of melody, wrapping around the film strip in her hand like a ribbon. 
Unused love, she realized, was never stored at all, but lent out, carried in the quiet catch of a stranger's throat during a movie scene, in the half-remembered lyric that sticks in your head for days, in the soft goodbye you never got to say but carry with you anyway. 
She tucked the film strip gently back under the tarp, leaving the cache of goodbye letters to keep spilling their quiet love into the dark, as more lyrics drifted up to find their tunes, one soft, thrumming note at a time.
