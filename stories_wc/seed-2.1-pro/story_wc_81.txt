The forgotten library inside a sunken city held its breath during the blackout, salt-crusted stone walls blocking every flicker of bioluminescent tide glow that usually seeped through cracked upper archways to gild dust motes dancing above sagging oak shelves. 
Kaelin, the library’s only remaining logical page indexer, was tranquilly perceptive even when the electric lights cut out, her fingers resting lightly on the spines of water-warped manuscripts as she waited for her eyes to adjust to the deep, velvet dark. 
For three years, she had worked to trace honest origins for hundreds of texts falsely stamped with the official seal of the sunken city’s long-vanished ruling council, forgeries that had distorted local history for generations. 
Faint coolness seeped through the thin cotton of her work glove as her palm brushed a small, heavy object tucked behind a loose shelf slat: the long-lost antique ink stamp, its carved wooden handle smoothed by centuries of use, its ink pad crusted but still faintly fragrant with iron gall and sea lavender. 
For months, the dominant archival record had claimed the stamp was stolen by a disgraced scribe who used it to fabricate edicts seizing land from coastal fishing families, a narrative that had been taught in every local school for a century. 
Kneeling on the cold stone floor, Kaelin did not rush to light her emergency beeswax candle, instead testing an alternative hypothesis: that the stamp had never been stolen at all, but hidden by a junior clerk who refused to participate in the council’s own campaign to rewrite land titles before the city sank in the great flood of 1472. 
When a stray beam of moonlight finally sliced through a crack in the vaulted ceiling, slanting across the shelf to pool over the stamp’s carved base, she leaned forward to readjust the angle of a loose leather folio tucked beside the hiding spot, its pages covered in the junior clerk’s tight, slanted handwriting. 
The journal entries confirmed her hunch: the council had ordered all public records altered to legitimize their seizure of common fishing grounds mere weeks before the flood, framing the coming disaster as divine punishment for the fishing communities' "theft" of council property. 
For years, she had chased leads under flickering electric lights, rushed by tight archival deadlines and pressure from city officials to close the forgery case with a simple, palatable villain, but the blackout had stripped away all that noisy urgency. 
What settled over her then was reclaimed stillness, the quiet, unshakable focus that came from knowing she held proof that could give land titles back to the fishing families whose ancestors had been wronged for nearly six hundred years. 
Dappled silver light shifted across the journal pages as small ripples in the flooded lower corridors caught the moonbeam and scattered it, and she felt a quiet, unyielding resolve settle in her bones, no trace of the frantic triumph she had expected to feel when she finally found the stamp. 
She did not rush to carry the stamp and journal up to the surface, where city officials waited for news of the blackout’s end; instead, she sat cross-legged on the stone, turning the stamp over in her palm to trace the tiny, almost invisible scratch on its base that the clerk had described in his final entry, a mark he had made so he could distinguish the true council seal from any later forgery. 
She knew the work ahead would be slow: cross-referencing every stamped document in the collection against the scratch mark, correcting decades of false history, testifying before the city council to return the stolen fishing grounds to the descendants of the families who had cared for them for millennia. 
When the lights flickered back on an hour later, harsh and artificial, she did not squint or jump, but carefully wrapped the stamp and journal in a soft cotton cloth, tucking them into the inner pocket of her jacket before climbing the stone stairs to the surface. 
The dappled resolve she had found in the dark stayed with her, steady as the tide, as she stepped out into the glow of the newly restored streetlights to share the truth the sunken library had guarded for centuries.
