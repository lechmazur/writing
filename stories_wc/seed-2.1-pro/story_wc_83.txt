The floating observatory’s overloaded circuit breakers tripped mid-scan, leaving Lila, sole survivor of the drone strike that had reduced her highland wedding to ash and shrapnel, with exactly 11 minutes of unmonitored access while the breakers cooled to safe operating temperature. 
She had not taken the junior technician post to log stellar dust readings or calibrate spectrographs, but to hunt for the place where unused potential pools: the quiet, unspooled futures the strike had stolen from her groom, her grandmother, the 27 guests who would never plant orchards, raise children, or argue over bread recipes in the sun. 
The observatory’s lead physicist had dismissed her private theory as grief-fueled fancy, but she knew perturbation theory, the framework for mapping how small, unplanned disruptions ripple through otherwise predictable systems to carve new possible paths, could be bent to trace those lost futures, if only she could bypass the algorithmic filters the institution used to cull "non-empirical" data streams. 
From the inner pocket of her frayed work coverall, she pulled the dented geode crystal her groom had pressed into her palm seconds before the missile hit, its rough, pocked exterior still flecked with the rust-colored dust of her home valley. 
She slotted the geode into the spectrograph’s sample cradle, knowing the crystal’s lattice, forged in the same mountain bedrock that had held her village for centuries, would act as a resonant anchor for the intangibly tangible traces of erased possibility that slipped between the gaps of standard measurement. 
When she tripped the manual override to route all sensor power through the geode’s core, the screens flared not with numbers or spectral lines, but with a cascade of unredacted imagery that pulled her into an unfiltered awakening: there was no separate, distant pool of unused potential, only the living, breathing weight of every choice, every cut-short moment, every unlived life braided into the very air she breathed. 
She watched her groom twist the geode open on their wedding dais to reveal a cavity lined with amethyst points that caught the sun, watched her grandmother pass around rounds of honeyed bread, watched the children chase a goat through the wheat fields, all of it playing out not in some far-off afterlife, but in the tiny, unmeasured perturbations of the world around her, the things algorithms and missiles alike were too blunt to see. 
The observatory’s hull creaked as it drifted 82,000 feet above the scarred mountain range, and she could feel the faint vibration of the cooling breakers thrumming up through the metal floor, a countdown ticking toward the moment the filters would snap back on, the screens would blank, and she would be left again with only the scar on her left forearm and the geode in her pocket. 
The bleakness of that inevitable return to blank monitors and clinical data logs tangled with a sharp, bright exhilaration she had not felt since the second before the missile struck, because she no longer needed a floating observatory or million-dollar sensors to find what she was hunting. 
She had thought unused potential pooled in some hidden cosmic eddy, far above the smoke and ruin, but her unguarded scan through the lens of perturbation theory had shown her it pooled in the way she still kneaded bread the way her grandmother taught her, in the amethyst glint of the cracked geode she carried, in every small, unplanned choice she made to honor the lives the strike had erased. 
A faint chime signaled the breakers were 10 seconds from full reset, and she pulled the geode from the sample cradle, tucking it back into her pocket as the first status light flickered green, no longer grieving the lost future she had come to hunt, because she carried every bit of that pooled potential with her, as intangibly tangible as the wind on her face when she stood on the observatory’s outer walkway, as real as the sourdough loaf she would bake that night in the station’s tiny galley, as unshakable as the knowledge that no strike, no algorithm, no filter could ever fully erase the worlds that lived on in the small, rippling choices she made every day.
