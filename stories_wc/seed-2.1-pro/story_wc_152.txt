Beneath the cobalt observatory’s sloped, tile-fringed dome, where crisp autumn wind sifted dry maple leaves through cracked wall slats in the quiet weeks before the royal guard’s coup, the galaxy-chart embroiderer huddled on a wobbly three-legged stool, pretending to fumble a spool of indigo silk thread. 
Most observatory staff dismissed her as a bumbling, half-sighted artisan hired only to mend frayed celestial tapestries, a deliberate ruse that let her disguise hard-won wisdom as foolishness, so no one thought to watch her closely. 
She had not traveled to the mountain observatory to stitch star charts, though her embroidery was fine enough to fool even the most critical court art critic; her core motivation was to linger in overlooked corners to listen for unheard stories, the voices of scullery maids, stable hands, and junior scribes that the court’s powerful worked so hard to erase. 
The observatory’s curved stone walls trapped sound in soft, layered echoes, wrapping every mumbled complaint and accidental admission in a hush that felt like a shared secret, the exact echoed silence she needed to do her work without being detected. 
Tucked in the hidden inner pocket of her frayed wool tunic, she carried a pinched shard of glazed clay holding a single, steady flame from the eternal candle housed in the royal chapel, a gift from a kitchen maid whose brother had been imprisoned for warning local villagers the crown planned to seize their harvest stores for the palace guard. 
Her quiet life’s work was unraveling conspiracies that hid in the gaps between official court statements and lived reality, plots that never made it into official royal histories but shaped every hardship faced by the kingdom’s most vulnerable people. 
She did not rely on stolen letters or paid informants to trace these plots; instead, she would sit still for hours at a time to synchronize the small, pulsing glow of the eternal candle’s flame with the thin slants of starlight that filtered through the dome’s punched star holes, tracing connections through adjacent possibilities no trained spy would ever bother to map. 
A guard’s offhand joke about extra winter rations being stored alongside "cobalt parade regalia" connected to a junior scribe’s mumbled complaint about unordered royal seal stamps being delivered to the observatory instead of the palace, which connected to the head astronomer’s sudden ban on staff entering the basement vaults after sundown, each small clue a thread pulled tight by the flame’s unwavering heat. 
Whenever patrolling guards paused near her stool, she would deliberately prick her finger on an embroidery needle, drop her silk spools, and ramble about mismatched star positions on the tapestry, playing the addled, clumsy fool so thoroughly they never noticed how her eyes tracked every shadow, how the candle flame in her pocket flared brighter whenever a conspirator’s lie hung thick in the air. 
On that particular crisp autumn evening, as dry maple leaves skittered across the observatory’s cold stone floor, the synchronized flame pulled her gaze to a loose floorboard tucked beside the great brass telescope, beneath which lay folded orders scheduling the coup for the first harvest full moon, signed by the very men who stood a few feet away laughing about the king’s upcoming birthday feast. 
She did not gasp, or freeze, or rush to flee; instead, she let her silk spool roll toward the floorboard as if by accident, kicked it gently back toward her stool, and tucked the folded orders into her embroidery roll while pretending to fumble for the loose thread, letting the observatory’s echoed silence swallow the weight of what she had found. 
These were the unheard stories she chased, not the grand declarations of kings or rebel leaders, but the quiet, fragile threads of ordinary people who would be the first to suffer when the coup arrived, their lives erased if no one took the time to map the quiet connections hiding in plain sight. 
She tied a thin silver thread around the edge of the folded orders to mark the conspiracy’s anchor point, adjusted the shard holding the eternal candle flame in her pocket, and went back to stitching, her bumbling facade firmly in place as she planned how to carry the warning down the mountain before the first full moon rose.
