Under the soft hush of a distant morning, the lunar-eclipse locksmith picked her way across rime-crusted stone lintels to reach the frozen library above clouds, her oilskin coat dusted with the fine, dry snow that drifted above the troposphere. 
She had traveled three eclipse cycles to this remote aerie not to pilfer ice-locked grimoires or pick the seals on forbidden celestial vaults, as others of her trade often did, but to learn the rhythm of repair, the gentle, unrushed cadence that let mended locks hold fast without grinding against the turn of the sky. 
She had mapped the library’s twisting, ice-slick corridors through scraps of prophecy hidden in a library's corner, tattered vellum slips she’d found tucked in the water-warped spine of a book of tide charts years before, their edges softened like driftwood washed smooth by a hundred storm surges. 
That soft, worn driftwood nostalgia clung to every step she took, a quiet grief for the mentor who had died before teaching her the final secret of their trade, leaving her with nothing but a dented brass case holding a clockmaker's gear assortment, its teeth nicked from decades of turning in time with lunar phases. 
The prophecy scraps had promised the answer lay at the library’s frozen heart, in the hall of mirrors where every reflected surface held the echo of a thing broken before its time. 
When she pushed through the ice-frosted oak doors to the hall, she expected shadow, or the dim, silver glow of eclipsed moonlight she had worked beneath her whole career, but instead the space was lit by a hauntingly bright glow that seeped through the mirror glass, turning the rime on the floors to scattered diamond dust. 
Each mirror showed a different memory: her mentor’s hands filing a gear to fit a rusted village lock, her own fumbling first attempt to set a latch during a partial eclipse, the slow, steady turn of the moon crossing the sun’s face as the world held its breath. 
At the center of the hall stood a single plain oak pedestal, holding no book or relic, only a small glass vial filled with condensed shadow collected during the total lunar eclipse the night her mentor died. 
She stepped close, her boots leaving faint prints in the frost, and reached out to uncap the vial, her fingers trembling as if she might disturb a bird that had been waiting a decade to take flight. 
The moment the seal broke, the hauntingly bright glow in the mirrors dimmed to soft silver, and the reflections shifted from static memories to moving scenes of every broken lock, every misaligned gear, every quiet hurt she had tried and failed to mend over the years. 
She watched, transfixed, as each scene played out with a slow, steady beat, the same rhythm that lapped at distant shorelines, turned the gears of old clocks, and pulled the moon across the face of the sun: no rush, no force, just patient attention to the way each part wanted to fit. 
She reached into her satchel and pulled out the dented brass case holding the clockmaker's gear assortment, laying the tiny brass cogs out on the frost-covered pedestal until they matched the slow, beating rhythm she saw reflected across every mirror, each gear turning in time without pressure or filing. 
For the first time since her mentor’s death, she did not feel the sharp, hollow ache of unfinished learning, only the soft, worn warmth of driftwood nostalgia, the kind that comes from realizing the thing you were searching for was already stitched into every memory, every failed attempt, every quiet turn of the gears you carried with you all along. 
When the distant morning’s first pale gold light seeped through the library’s frosted skylights, she tucked the gears back into their case, leaving the uncapped vial on the pedestal to catch the light as the mirrors dimmed to plain, clear glass. 
She did not need to take the shadow with her, because she had finally learned the rhythm of repair was not a secret to be stolen from a frozen archive, but a slow, gentle pulse she carried in her own hands, honed by every small, imperfect effort she had ever made to fix what was broken. 
She stepped back out onto the cloud-borne lintels as the first edge of the sun peeked over the horizon, the gear case warm against her hip, ready to mend what waited below.
