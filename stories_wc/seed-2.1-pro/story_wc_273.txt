When the final edge of the moon pulled away from the sun and the world blushed back into full, unshadowed light, the albino tracker stepped into the wind-carved encampment, her bone-white braid slung over a deerskin tunic stitched with crow feathers. 
She was consciously wild, not a castoff from the lowland clock-cities but a deliberate refugee, who had left measured minutes and scheduled joy behind seven years prior to chase the quiet, unanswerable question that burned soft and steady behind her pale red eyes. 
Her motivation was sharp and unshakable: to discover why time slows in crisis, why the 12 seconds it had taken a mudslide to tear her younger brother from their ridge-side campsite had stretched into 10 long, breath-held minutes of useless, reaching effort, why every synchronized clock in the valley had ticked forward as if nothing had stretched at all. 
The encampment belonged to the shadowy chronotech firm that had run secret eclipse-aligned time experiments for three years, their gear half-packed in weathered crates stamped with the logo of a hovering hummingbird, a dark puddle of their proprietary disappearing ink spreading slow across the flat sandstone slab they had used as a field table. 
A faint, high whine cut through the sharp post-eclipse wind, and she looked up to spot an artificial hummingbird drone, its iridescent plastic wings beating too fast to track, hovering three feet from her face as if scanning for a reason to blare a perimeter alarm. 
She did not flinch, did not reach for the bone-handled knife at her hip, because the serene anger she had carried for years did not burn hot enough to make her reckless; it sat cool in her bones, a quiet certainty that the people who had warped time around her brother, who tugged at the fabric of reality for corporate profit, would not escape accountability. 
She had tracked the firm's field teams for 18 months, trading dried elk meat for tips from ridge-top herders, following the faint warp of slowed time that clung to their test sites like high-altitude frost, and she knew the open secret of their disappearing ink: how it did not merely fade when exposed to open air, but opened temporary, ephemeral patterns in spilled ink that acted as doorways between the rigid, clock-bound world of the lowland cities and the stretched, unregulated time of the crisis spaces their experiments created. 
The drone zipped closer, its red sensor eye blinking once to signal it had identified her as an unauthorized intruder, but she was already stepping toward the sandstone slab, her soft moccasins making no sound on the wind-polished rock. 
As the ink puddle caught the slanted post-eclipse sunlight, it bloomed into swirling, shifting lattices that looked exactly like the stretched, slow seconds she had lived through during the mudslide, thin bridges of dark pigment that would dissolve entirely in less than a minute, leaving no trace they had ever existed. 
She did not hesitate, lifting one foot to step directly into the center of the ink, because she knew that to disappear through ephemeral patterns in spilled ink was not to vanish forever into nothing, but to step into the pocket space between seconds where the chronotech team had hidden their mobile control room, where they pulled digital levers to slow localized time for their tests without care for who might be caught in the warp. 
The drone fired a thin, stun-charged dart that whizzed through the empty air where her chest had been half a second earlier, its red sensor flickering in digital confusion as the last of the ink lattices swirled tight and seamless behind her. 
On the other side of the ink threshold, the air hummed with the low thrum of time-dilation servers, and the lead researcher looked up from his glowing console, his face draining of color when he saw her standing between the server racks, her pale eyes steady, no snarl on her lips, no furious shout building in her throat, just that quiet, unyielding serene anger wrapped around her like a well-worn cloak as she stepped forward to finally get the answer she had chased across seven years of wind and ridge and quiet grief.
