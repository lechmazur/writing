Barlow, the bloodhound retired from finding bodies, padded the rusted gantries of the scrapyard settlement spanning a cracked wasteland, his paw pads calloused from decades of stepping over twisted rebar and shattered capacitor shards left after the last resource war. 
The air hung in the wild hush that had settled once the last artillery shell fell, thin and sharp with rust dust and the faint, sweet tang of drought-tough sage pushing up through fissures in the baked clay. 
Tucked against his collar, strung on frayed parachute cord, swung an autumn leaf preserved in wax, chipped at the edges from years of travel, the last gift his handler Kael had given him before the mudslide that separated them mid-evacuation. 
Barlow's sole motivation in the years since he stopped tracking the broken, buried remains of war dead was to translate distance into closeness, to close the 300 miles of cracked, scorched earth between the scrapyard and the mountain outpost where Kael had been airlifted, too injured to call for him as the mud roared down the slope. 
He had no maps, no working radio, only his nose, an intuitively descriptive instrument that did not just register scents but wove them into full, textured stories of who had passed where, what they had carried, what they had grieved. 
Three moons prior, he had dug a dented phonograph cylinder from a half-buried oak cabinet at the scrapyard's furthest edge, its wax surface crisscrossed with fine grooves that held more than crackling old audio of folk songs and medic field notes. 
His method, honed over years of reading faint traces on bomb-blasted stone and rain-soaked soil, was by reading secret texts in the grooves of a phonograph cylinder, parsing the micro-thin layers of dried sweat, pine resin, wood smoke, and linseed oil trapped in the wax as clearly as a cloistered scholar reads illuminated script. 
The cylinder had traveled with a caravan of roving medics in the final months of the war, passed from hand to hand until it was lost in a botched supply drop, and as Barlow nosed its grooves each amber dusk, he turned over a quiet riddle the scrapyard's white-bearded tinker had spoken over a pine nut campfire weeks before: what carries home across unmarked waste, when roads are gone and compasses spin false? 
The revelations he pulled from the wax did not come in sharp, unvarnished truths, but as tinted revelations, gilded by the amber glow of scrapyard bonfires, softened by the deep crimson hue of the waxed maple leaf at his throat, smudged at the edges by the quiet longing that hummed constant under his thick, brindled fur. 
He learned the caravan had stopped at the mountain outpost three weeks after the mudslide, that a man with a thin scar across his left eyebrow, who smelled of pine soap and boiled maple sap, had recorded a short, wavering message onto the cylinder before handing it to a driver heading east, asking anyone who found a lanky bloodhound with a white chest patch to tell him the path home was lined with newly planted maple saplings. 
That was the answer to the riddle, he realized, not a paper map or a magnetic compass bearing, but the thin, unbreakable thread of scent and memory that stretched across the cracked earth, no distance too great for it to span. 
He did not need to rush, for the trail would not fade, held fast in the micro-grooves of the wax cylinder and the faint, familiar tang clinging to the leaf at his throat, a compass that never spun wrong no matter how hard the red dust blew across the wasteland flats. 
He tucked the cylinder into the scrap of canvas slung over his back, touched his cold nose to the waxed leaf at his collar, and stepped off the rusted gantry onto the faint game trail leading west, the wild hush wrapping around him like a well-worn wool blanket, the scent of pine soap and maple already pulling him closer with every soft, deliberate step.
