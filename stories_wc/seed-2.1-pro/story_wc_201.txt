After the anesthesia wears off, Kaelin blinks awake at the edge of the ultraviolet forest, where bioluminescent lichen bleeds indigo light over moss that hums at a frequency only their proprioceptively enhanced nervous system can parse. 
They are an enhanced interrogation survivor, left for dead three days prior by the regime that carved sensory augments into their joints to turn them into a living lie detector, only to discard them when they refused to calibrate pain as a metric of truth. 
A faint weight tugs at the side of their neck, and when their fumbling fingers find it, they recognize the dented pewter mask strap they wore as a child acolyte of the old forest penance cult, the same strap their lead interrogator had torn from their throat mid-torture to taunt them about the futility of grace. 
The cult’s central rite was ultraviolet penance: a journey into the forest’s indigo heart where petitioners sat exposed to the UV-rich glow that stripped away manufactured guilt, letting only unvarnished, unchosen truth rise to the surface, a truth the regime had spent weeks trying to beat out of them. 
Their motivation for crawling to the forest edge after escaping the interrogation cell, even as broken ribs screamed with every shift, was to discover the mathematics of mercy: a formula the cult promised existed in the forest’s resonant light, a set of ratios that could quantify how much pain a person could hold before they owed no one anything, how much accountability balanced with grace to avoid the cycle of cruelty the regime enforced. 
They know they cannot force the formula to come through deliberate memory, not when the interrogators’ drugs had tangled their recollections of cult lessons into snarls of fear, so they rely on remembering through trauma responses, the involuntary twinges and flinches that hold unedited truth better than any forced recollection. 
They brace one palm against the rough, glowing bark of a silver birch to pull themselves upright, and the sudden jolt up their arm sends a familiar flinch lancing through their jaw, the same reaction they had at 12 years old when their cult mentor first told them mercy was not a zero-sum equation. 
A strange, dual feeling settles over them as they step deeper into the indigo glow: they are a displaced fugitive, stripped of home, community, and even unbroken memory, yet every hum of the moss, every tug of the pewter strap at their neck, every proprioceptive tingle in their augmented joints anchors them more firmly to the forest floor than any formal homecoming ever could. 
When a low branch brushes their cheek, they flinch hard, shoulders hunching to protect their throat the same way they did every time an interrogator leaned in to snarl that mercy was a lie for the weak, and that flinch unlocks a shard of the old lesson they had buried under layers of pain. 
The ultraviolet light seeps through their tattered prison uniform, warm on the scar tissue crisscrossing their arms, and they realize the mathematics of mercy has no constant, no fixed ratio that applies to every situation, only a variable rooted in the choice to stop inflicting pain the second you have the power to do so. 
They lift the pewter mask strap from where it rests against their collarbone, running a thumb over the dented edge that had been pressed into their skin for so many years of childhood rituals, and understand the ultraviolet penance is not about being absolved of guilt by an outside force, but about choosing to anchor yourself to mercy even when every trauma response screams to lash out in self-defense. 
Their proprioceptively enhanced joints hum in alignment with the moss under their boots, no longer screaming with the leftover tension of interrogation, because for the first time since they were taken, they are not performing truth for someone else’s algorithm, or flinching from pain someone else chose to inflict. 
They do not know if they will ever return to the community they lost, or if the regime will track them into the forest to finish what they started, but for now, they are rooted exactly where they stand, held by the indigo light, the worn pewter strap, and the quiet equation of mercy they had carried in their bones all along, even when torture and drugs tried to carve it out of them.
