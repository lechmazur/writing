Dawn spilled thin, opalescent light over the Sapphire Viaduct, its vaulted sandstone arches crusted with salt and bioluminescent blue mussel shells, settling soft across the stone walkway just moments after the last winter herring had been hauled into the final fisherman’s wicker creel and his small boat had pulled for the sheltered cove. 
Perched on the viaduct’s central parapet, the lukewarm mage, so named by the academy for his inability to summon fire hot enough to singe parchment or freeze water hard enough to hold a pebble, his magic always hovering at gentle, unimpressive room temperature, leaned forward to brush crusted salt from the jagged shards of a broken ship’s bell laid out on a tattered wool cloth before him. 
Earnestly naive, he had ignored the archmages’ dismissive sniffs when he announced his plan to right a cosmic imbalance no grand spellcaster had bothered to address, a tiny but widening rift between the tidal rhythm of the sea and the slow turn of the coastal constellations. 
The rift had opened three years prior, when the viaduct’s long-serving bell tender had let his brittle devotion snap: faced with a choice between staying to ring the bell through a category-five gale or rushing to the mainland to care for his fever-stricken infant granddaughter, he had chosen his family, and the unrung bell had shattered under the storm’s fist, its resonance scattering across the waves. 
In the years since, tides had run 17 minutes late, herring runs had shrunk to a fraction of their former size, and the coastal stars had slipped just far enough out of their assigned paths that shepherds regularly lost their way on clear, moonless nights. 
The archmages had declared the bell beyond repair, arguing only a spell forged from starfire could reforge its cosmic resonance, but the lukewarm mage knew better, because he had spent 47 dawns on the viaduct listening, watching, understanding dripping rates of seawater seeping through the viaduct’s storm-cracked stone. 
The old sandstone, he had realized, had absorbed 400 years of the bell’s ring, and the steady drip of water through its cracks fell at the exact frequency the bell had once rung, a metronome aligned perfectly with the old, unbroken rhythm of tide and star. 
He did not have starfire, but he had his quiet, lukewarm magic, the kind that could nudge edges together without warping them, could hold metal in place without melting it, could match its pulse to any slow, steady rhythm if he listened close enough. 
He had come to rebuild the bell not as it had been, rigid and tied to a brittle, unforgiving standard of devotion that demanded a choice between duty and love, but as something that could hold both, its metal annealed to the soft, unstopping rhythm of the drips that had never ceased, even when the bell was broken. 
As the first direct ray of sun crested the headland, he set the final shard in place, letting his magic throb in time with a drip that fell just as his thumb pressed the jagged edge to its seam, fusing the metal without heat, without fanfare. 
A breeze caught the newly whole bell, and it rang once, soft and clear, a tone that matched the drip rate so perfectly that the mussels on the viaduct arches opened in unison, and the herring waiting just offshore turned as a single school to swim up the estuary on schedule for the first time in three years. 
Overhead, the constellations, faint even at dawn, shifted just enough to settle back into their old paths, no grand explosion of light, no crack of thunder to mark the cosmic righting, only the soft ring of the bell and the steady drip, drip, drip of water through stone. 
Down the path, the old bell tender climbed the viaduct steps, holding his now-healthy granddaughter’s hand, bringing a jar of wild blackberry jam for the mage he had seen working at dawn for weeks, ready to tend the bell again without choosing between his post and the people he loved. 
The lukewarm mage smiled, leaning against the parapet as the next drip fell, knowing the grandest cosmic balances rarely required grand magic, only the quiet patience to notice what the powerful had long dismissed as too small, too soft, too unremarkable to matter.
