Elara, a provenance researcher who had been accidentally telepathic with plants since a childhood spill of alpine star-thistle tincture on her temple, climbed the weathered spiral stairs to the velvet observatory at midsummer, when the sun hung low enough to gild every frayed edge of the mulberry-hued velvet drapes pooled across the cold flagstone floor. 
She had arrived three days before the decades-old non-aggression promise between her mountain valley home and the expanding lowland empire lost its glow, when the empire’s envoys would arrive to demand either total annexation or a war that would scorch every pine and wild blueberry patch for a hundred miles. 
Resting on the observatory’s central marble plinth, lit by slanted honeyed sunlight, was the transparent violin made of ice crystal, the relic her grandmother had guarded for sixty years, said to hold the song that wove the valley’s collective destiny into a single, tangible thread. 
The old tales called that destiny perforated, stitched through with tiny, unseeable holes where choices could slip through to rewrite even the most seemingly fixed futures, if only someone dared reach through the gaps. 
Elara’s task, passed down through her line of researchers, was to weigh a dream, the soft, rooted dream of the valley’s people to tend their orchards, play their songs, raise their children under unregulated skies, against the cold, iron weight of the empire, and she did not carry runestones or star charts to do it, only a chipped ceramic mug of cold-brewed chicory coffee, for her family’s long-held method of divination was by reading the future in coffee stains. 
As she set the mug down on a small oak side table, the climbing ivy curled around the observatory’s rafters hummed soft, anxious static into the edge of her mind, warning that the empire’s envoys carried forged orders to burn the valley regardless of the valley’s answer, that the promise’s glow was already dimming by the hour. 
She swirled the last dregs of coffee, tipped them into the mug’s saucer, and leaned in to read the future in coffee stains, tracing the smudged brown rings that mapped not a single unavoidable path, but a web of perforations: one hole led to ash, one to surrender, one to a song so sharp and bright it could cut through iron. 
When she lifted the transparent violin made of ice crystal from its plinth, it was light as dandelion fluff, cool against her jaw, and she realized the action she needed to take was not to fight or to flee, but to propagate: to carry the frequency of that bright, unwritten future through every root and mycorrhizal thread woven through the valley’s soil, so every person, every pine, every blueberry bush would know the gap in destiny they could reach through together. 
She drew the horsehair bow across the violin’s transparent strings, and the note that emerged was not loud, not a battle cry, but a soft, mulberry ache: the taste of summer jam on burnt toast, the sound of children laughing in wildflower meadows, the quiet grief of every person who had ever lost a home they loved. 
Beneath her feet, the grass hummed back, the pine trees thrumming the note up their trunks to shake their needles, the blueberry bushes pushing new shoots up through the soil as the frequency traveled, propagating not anger or fear, but the quiet, unshakable certainty that their dream did not have to shatter under the empire’s weight. 
She did not know if the envoys would listen, if the song would hold, if the perforations in destiny would stay open long enough for the valley to choose its own path, but she kept playing, letting the mulberry ache of the note wrap around every rafter and root, while the midsummer sun gilded the velvet drapes and the promise still held its last, warm glow.
