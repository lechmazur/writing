The ascending servant, Lira, pressed her palm to the frosted glass of the spire’s creaking external elevator as it began its ten-floor climb to the airship captain’s private quarters, her warmly inquisitive nature humming under the practiced, gentle remove that defined her years of service to the remote icebound spire above the clouds. 
This tone of affectionate detachment had been her survival mechanism for three winters at the outpost, allowing her to tend to gruff, frostbitten crew members and frozen supply lines without letting the constant, biting grief of the isolated station seep into her own bones. 
She had been assigned to clear out the captain’s quarters after his fatal crash last tenday, a task no other crew member volunteered for, as most remembered only his sharp temper, his refusal to join holiday feasts, the way he snapped at anyone who touched the dented airship captain’s goggles perched permanently on his fur-lined cap. 
The elevator jolted as it passed the third floor, where the galley staff hung drying herbs, and slanted sunlight cut through the foot-thick ice walls to scatter across the brushed metal floor, painting shifting prisms that slid with every lurch of the rickety car. 
Her quiet, unshared motivation for taking the unwanted assignment was simple: to seek out the joy beneath anguish, the soft, human core she knew must exist even in the man who had spent two years flying supply runs through blizzards without a single unguarded smile for the crew. 
By the fifth floor, where the crew kept their frost-fogged dormitories, the elevator’s open iron grille let in a sharp gust of high-altitude wind that ruffled the edge of a leather-bound book tucked on the elevator’s frosted window ledge, left behind by the captain on his last trip down to the hangar. 
The book was his encrypted diary, its pages sealed with a rotating brass cipher lock that the entire crew had spent weeks speculating about, convinced it held records of hidden supply caches or bitter rants about the outpost’s chronically failing heating systems. 
When the elevator passed the seventh floor, home to the dust-sheeted empty navigation office, one of the drifting prisms of scattered light landed directly on the cipher dial, catching the tiny etched alignment markings that matched the unique lens grind of the captain’s missing goggles, which she had found tucked in her coat pocket that morning when she reported for duty. 
Her warmly inquisitive instinct won out over the unspoken rule against touching the captain’s personal effects, and she lifted the goggles to hold them over the drifting prism, decoding the lock through scattered light by letting the focused refraction trace the dial’s notches until the mechanism clicked open without a single forced turn. 
The diary’s stiff leather cover fell open when she set it on the elevator rail, and she leaned in to read as the car climbed past the eighth and ninth floors, too curious to wait until she reached the private quarters to unfold the crisp, long-sealed pages the captain had guarded so closely for years. 
She expected terse, bitter logs of storm damage and navigational errors, the same gruff, unyielding voice the crew had come to know, but the entries were soft, crammed with pressed alpine edelweiss, quick sketches of the crew laughing at last winter’s impromptu snow festival, notes about the way the rising sun painted the roiling cloud sea tangerine at dawn, scribbled recipes for spiced cocoa he had planned to make for the crew once the overdue supply ship arrived with extra cocoa powder and cinnamon sticks. 
A folded entry tucked in the diary’s final pages, dated the morning of his fatal crash, explained that he had hidden all his quiet warmth behind a sharp, unapproachable facade after his teenaged daughter died on her first training airship voyage three years prior, convinced that letting himself feel or show unguarded joy would make the weight of his loss ache too sharply to bear through the long, dark winters. 
The elevator pinged softly to signal its arrival at the tenth floor, the captain’s private quarters, and Lira tucked the diary securely under her arm, adjusting the dented goggles to rest on her own knit cap as she stepped out onto the ice-carved landing, already planning to make that spiced cocoa for the crew that evening, to read aloud the captain’s kind, unshared notes, to pull the joy he had buried under his anguish out into the open and share it with the ragged, tight-knit family that made their home on the icebound spire above the clouds.
