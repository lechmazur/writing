After the last echo of laughter fades from the subterranean crystal cave’s faceted amethyst walls, Lira, the charcoal-fingered portrait sketch artist who’d spent a decade drawing stiff, commissioned wedding portraits above ground, presses her palm to the cold, glinting rock at her back. 
Mnemonically gifted from childhood, she recognizes that faint, dying laugh as her own 12-year-old cackle, a sound she had not let herself remember in decades, even as she can still recall every face she has ever drawn, every faint, suppressed flicker of longing her clients tried to hide behind practiced smiles when she forced her hand to render only the polished, marketable versions of them they paid to see. 
She had followed a half-remembered tug down three miles of winding limestone passages to this cave, chasing the quiet, unignorable pull she’d spent years muffling under rent checks and client feedback, and now a dented iron strongbox sat wedged in a crystal crevice before her, its broken combination lock dangling from its hasp like a slack jaw. 
When she pried the lid open, she found no gold, no old letters, only a stretched linen canvas, blank but for a faint charcoal outline of her own 17-year-old hands, smudged the same way she still smudged shadow lines with the side of her pinky. 
Her perfect memory flooded with the version of herself that had first picked up charcoal in this very cave, a teen hiding from adults who told her fine art would never pay, who’d locked her own wild, vivid creative impulse away and told herself the combination was lost forever. 
The cave’s crystals refracted the faint glow of her headlamp into soft rose and honeyed gold, the same auroral light she had spent 15 years too scared to paint, and she pulled a stick of compressed charcoal from her jacket pocket to rectify the long, quiet harm she had done to the part of herself that ached to make unmarketable, unapologetic art. 
She painted for hours, her fingers blackening with charcoal dust until they matched the permanent stain under her nails that had never faded even when she tried to scrub it away for client meetings, as she rendered every face she had ever memorized, not as the polished versions clients requested, but as they truly were: the bride who grieved her dead mother mid-vow, the groom who dreamed of leaving finance to plant orchards, the widower who carried his wife’s favorite pressed violet in his wallet. 
When the last charcoal stroke settled, she leaned back and realized the linen had pulled slightly loose from its frame, revealing a folded scrap of paper tucked behind the painting, its edges softened by decades of cave damp. 
She pulled the scrap free, and her throat tightened when she recognized her own 12-year-old scrawl, the words she had written the day she hid the strongbox here, between bouts of wild, echoing laughter, before she ever convinced herself art was only a job: “You don’t need a combination to come back. It waits for you.” 
The broken lock made sense now; she had never needed to crack a code to access the creativity she had corked tight for so long, because inescapable callings do not stay locked away, no matter how many years you spend pretending you cannot hear their low, constant hum. 
A thin, wobbly tear dropped onto the corner of the canvas, darkening the charcoal to a deep velvet black, and she did not wipe it away, because the soft sorrow of all those wasted years was not a flaw to be erased, but the first warm, rose-gold streak of an auroral dawn she had long thought would never break for her. 
She tucked the folded scrap into her shirt pocket right over her heart, shifted the canvas under her arm, and walked toward the cave mouth as the crystals glowed behind her, their light soft as held breath, already knowing the small sunlit studio she would rent above ground would hold no stiff wedding portraits, only the messy, true, unapologetic work her charcoal-stained fingers had been born to make.
