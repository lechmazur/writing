The hedge maze designer knelt at the crown of the living bridge garden, where woven ficus roots arched over a creek thick with watercress and the faint, honeyed rot of fallen loquats. 
She had come here not to tend the interlacing hedges that spilled over the bridge railings, but to discover the texture of time, a pursuit she had chased across 30 years of drafting twisting paths that warped visitors' sense of minutes and hours. 
Tucked in the breast pocket of her canvas work coat were a stack of fading photographs, each capturing a version of the garden she had built in her mind but never quite managed to plant: hedges that curved impossible angles, bridges that looped back on themselves, light that fell in slanted gold as if caught between decades. 
Three days prior, a storm had knocked a jagged, pearlescent shard from the stone angel perched at the garden's entry, and old local tales named that shard a halo fragment, capable of showing seekers the hidden shapes woven into the world's quiet corners. 
She thought of the way all her best maze designs relied on hyperbolic geometry, planes that stretched and curved so a walker could turn a corner and stumble on a spot they had left ten minutes prior, or never reach a center that sat just out of line of sight. 
When she closed her fingers around the cool shard where it half-buried itself in moss, the world tilted into a quaking reverie, the hedge leaves blurring at the edges as the shard pulled her not forward into some unwritten future, but backward into the stacked layers of every moment the garden had ever held. 
She realized, with a thin thrill that trembled up her wrists, that she was retrocognitively trapped, not stuck in a single past moment, but adrift across all of them, watching children chase fireflies in 1952 where a mature hedge now stood, watching the original garden builders twist ficus roots into the first bridge span in 1927, watching her own 10-year-old self kneel in this same spot to plant a loquat seed she had carried home from a family trip. 
She did not fight the disorienting drift, because she quickly realized the only path through these stacked temporal layers wound through hyperbolic geometry, the same warped, non-Euclidean planes she had spent a career drafting, where every step closer to a memory made that memory stretch wider, brimming with the granular sensory detail she had never been able to pin down in maze blueprints or half-remembered daydreams. 
She pulled the stack of fading photographs from her coat pocket, watching as the edges of each print began to disintegrate the longer she held them in that crosshatched, time-warped space, the stiff paper softening, the captured images of imagined perfect mazes dissolving into fine silver dust that drifted down to settle on the tender loquat sapling her 10-year-old self had just tamped into the soil. 
For a wild, trembling second she thought she might disintegrate too, scattered across every decade the garden had known, but then she felt the texture of time as clearly as she felt moss under her palms: not a straight line, not a circular loop, but a soft, breathing weave of root and memory and light, rough as loquat skin, sweet as overripe fruit fallen to the creek bank. 
The halo fragment in her palm warmed, then dissolved too, leaving only a faint silver smudge on her work gloves as the layers of past and present settled back into place, the fireflies fading, the young builders vanishing, her 10-year-old self giving a small, secret wave before she too slipped back into the loam of memory. 
She sat for a long while on the crown of the living bridge, listening to the creek gurgle under the woven roots, realizing none of her carefully drafted maze paths had ever captured time's texture because she had been trying to force memory into sharp, fixed lines, when time itself grew like the ficus bridges, twisting and stretching and looping through hyperbolic curves to hold every moment at once. 
When she finally stood to walk back down the bridge, she did not mourn the disintegrated photographs or the dissolved halo fragment, because she carried the soft, trembling shape of time in her bones now, ready to plant a maze that did not trap visitors in twists of hedge, but let them step, for a breath, into the same quaking, luminous reverie she had just known.
