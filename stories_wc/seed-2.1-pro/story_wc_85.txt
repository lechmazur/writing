The cobalt terrace jutted thirty feet above a jagged, forgotten shore at dusk, its chipped cerulean flagstones warm under the knees of Elara Voss, the guilt-ridden black market genealogist who had built her career selling softer pasts to people too afraid to face the ones they actually had. 
She was there under the weight of unspoken words: the ones she had swallowed three years prior, when she chose to manufacture false memories for a client named Kael instead of handing over the thin, salt-stained file that held his real family story. 
Back then, she had thought the kindest choice was to give him the warm, tidy origin he craved: a story of a young lighthouse keeper who had given him up for adoption to give him a better life, complete with a dented brass pocket watch missing its minute hand, a prop she had sourced from a flea market to sell the lie. 
She had not known, until a condolence letter arrived at her PO box six months prior, that Kael’s real mother had been Mara, the reclusive driftwood carver who had lived in a shack at the edge of the forgotten shore for forty years, leaving small, intricately carved figures tucked into the tide wrack for the child she had been forced to surrender as a teen. 
The letter, written by a neighbor who had found Elara’s name tucked in Mara’s stack of unsent letters to Kael, explained Mara had embedded a broken brass watch hand in every single carving, saving the matching watch face for the day she could finally hold her son again. 
Elara had climbed down the weathered rope ladder from the terrace to the shore that evening with a single goal, driven by her quiet, burning motivation: to discover the weight of a withheld truth, the exact measure of the damage her easy lies had left in their wake. 
She knew she could not undo the false memories she had sold Kael, not without shattering the peaceful life he had built around the story she gave him, but she could at least quell the sharp, constant thrum of her guilt by sifting through driftwood carvings along a forgotten shore, collecting the tiny markers of the love her lie had erased. 
For an hour, she knelt in the cold, damp sand as the light faded, running her fingers over smoothed otter figures, tiny lighthouses, carved depictions of a small boy with curly hair, each one holding a sliver of brass that caught the last of the light as she turned them over in her palm. 
The world fell away around her as she worked, softening into a sieved hush where the crash of waves, the cry of gulls, the distant hum of the coastal highway all filtered through in thin, muted threads, leaving only the quiet weight of each carving in her hands, each one a word Mara had never gotten to say. 
She did not plan to track Kael down, to force the truth on him when the false memories she had planted had already rooted into a life he loved; tempered regret, not frantic atonement, was the mood of the evening, a quiet recognition that some damages could not be fixed, only carried gently. 
When her pockets grew heavy with carvings, she climbed back up to the cobalt terrace, setting each small figure along the chipped rail where Kael might one day walk, if he ever felt the quiet pull of the shore he did not know was his birthright. 
She pulled the dented brass watch she had kept from that old client file from her coat pocket (the one she had sourced from the flea market, meant to sell Kael on a lie) and fitted one of the tiny brass watch hands from a carving of a curly-haired boy into its empty spindle, where it ticked slow and steady in time with the waves below. 
She did not leave a note, did not carve her name into the rail, did not make a grand show of atonement; she simply stood there as dusk deepened into indigo, feeling the full weight of the truth she had withheld settle soft and unshakable in her chest, no lighter for having found it, but no longer sharp enough to draw blood. 
When the first star pricked through the darkening sky, she turned to walk down the terrace path, leaving the ticking watch propped against the largest of the driftwood carvings, a small, silent offering to the hush, to the words never spoken, to the quiet, unflashy work of carrying regret without letting it curdle into more lies.
