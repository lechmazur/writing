The esoteric realist climbed the final basalt handhold to step into the verdant grotto above the clouds, where dawn spilled a soft rose hue over moss thick enough to swallow boot prints and air so still it held the faint tang of fir resin like a half-spoken secret. 
It was the week of the migration of the last monarch butterfly, a journey lepidopterists had tracked for three years as the species collapsed, its path erased by pesticide drift and suburban development that had paved over the milkweed corridors once tended by Indigenous farmers whose names no local history textbook recorded. 
She was transiently rooted, never staying in one place longer than the bloom cycle of a local wildflower, never planting anything that would outlast her visit, but leaning fully into the weight of every spot she paused long enough to tend. 
She traveled to forgotten ridge tops and buried settlement sites to honor overlooked histories, the stories of people whose contributions had been cut off mid-sentence, never given the neat, ringing resolution that textbook narratives reserved for wealthy founders and decorated soldiers. 
She thought of those cut-off stories as interrupted cadences, the musical phrase that builds toward a satisfying, expected final chord only to swerve into soft, unplaceable quiet, demanding the listener lean in rather than clap and move on. 
Her practice was building temporary structures as signals, small, fragile installations that would blow apart in the first high mountain wind, no trace left behind save the faint impression of stems on moss and the memory of the stories they marked. 
The grotto held a blushing stillness that morning, the rose-tinted light softening every sharp edge of rock, no bird call loud enough to break the hush as she unloaded her pack: dried milkweed stems, spools of yucca fiber, the pressed luna moth wing she had found the previous spring at the site of a former forced boarding school, where a child had once scrawled a picture of a butterfly on a cell wall before dying of untreated fever. 
She sat cross-legged on the moss and began to knot the milkweed stems into a delicate, open-work lattice, weaving in tufts of cloud-grass and tiny sprigs of alpine aster to form a low arch positioned directly in the path the last monarchs were predicted to take as they rode the thermal currents south. 
When the arch stood steady, light enough to shift in the faintest breeze, she tucked the pale, iridescent moth wing at its highest point, so it would catch the sun and glow like a shard of moon even as the light climbed from rose to gold. 
She had waited three hours when the first flash of tangerine wing appeared over the cloud line, a single male monarch beating slowly, tiredly, toward the grotto, its wings tattered at the edges from storms and lack of nectar along its paved-over path. 
She held her breath, expecting it to glide straight through the arch as if passing through a gateway to a safer valley, the satisfying resolution she had half-dared to hope for, but the monarch swerved at the last second to land directly on the moth wing, its tarsi tapping the delicate membrane as if recognizing a fellow traveler cut short before its journey was done. 
She did not move, did not reach for a camera or a notebook to record the moment, knowing the structure would blow apart by sunset, the moth wing would carry away on the wind to the clouds below, and the monarch would resume its journey when it had rested long enough, no marker left to prove the meeting ever happened save the weight of it in her bones. 
That quiet, unscripted pause was the interrupted cadence made flesh, no grand fanfare, no permanent plaque, just a fragile, fleeting recognition between three souls bound by unfinished journeys: the moth that never reached its mate, the child who never saw the ocean, the monarch that might never reach its winter roost. 
She stayed in that blushing stillness for another hour, her boots rooted just deep enough in the soft moss to feel the mountain's slow pulse, knowing that honoring overlooked histories did not require stone monuments or textbook entries, only the willingness to pause long enough to meet the unfinished stories when they landed, light as wings, in front of you.
