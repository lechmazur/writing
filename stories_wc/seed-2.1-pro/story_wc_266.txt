The frayed cowhide leash, worn thin where Lila had white-knuckled it through every panic attack, every unspoken apology, every step she'd been too scared to take alone, snapped clean three feet from the tidal pull-off, sending her skittish rescue hound loping up the slope of the veiled ridgeway, where mist wove through pine boughs like spun silk. 
She did not hesitate to follow, even when the paved access road faded to mossy stone, because she had spent three years shrinking from every unknown, and this time the call of the hound's distant bark pulled her forward instead of locking her in place. 
She had walked barely a quarter mile when two women, their faces crinkled like sun-bleached driftwood, their shawls stitched with shell beads that clicked soft as lapping waves, stepped out from behind a hemlock to meet her. 
They were the wise ferry guides, she realized, the figures old coastal tales spoke of, who did not carry boats across water but escorted travelers across the thin, mist-shrouded thresholds where ordinary days blurred into what might be. 
One held a dented brass ticket punch with a star stamp, its handle worn smooth by decades of pressing into scrap paper, leaf edges, the cuffs of wanderers' jackets, whatever small token a traveler offered to mark their passage. 
They did not block her path, or demand she turn back, but were gently persistent as they explained that the ridge did not give up its secrets to those who rushed in straight lines, that to reach what she sought she must traverse its fern-lined switchbacks through indirect routes, following the sound of her hound's bark instead of cutting straight through the shifting mist. 
Lila nodded, not even needing to say out loud that she had come to find where unused courage waits, the cache of boldness she had left locked away after her mother's death, the part of her that had stopped speaking up, stopped trying, stopped believing she could walk a hard path alone. 
The older of the two guides pressed the star punch to the frayed broken end of her leash, leaving a crisp, indented star where the snap had split the leather, and told her the journey would unfold as a vigil in twilight, no flash of lightning, no grand epiphany, only the soft, steady work of putting one foot in front of the other as the sky turned from tangerine to lavender to deep, velvet indigo. 
She walked for what felt like hours, turning left when she wanted to charge right, pausing to listen to frog song in the boggy hollows, to run her fingers over moss soft as lamb's ear, to catch the faint, briny tang of the bay far below the ridge, where waves hushed the rocks in a constant, lulling rhythm that matched the beat of her steps. 
She found her hound curled on a flat stone outcrop, where a small, still pool collected rainwater, and when she knelt to scratch his ears she saw the pool held no reflection of her fearful, hunched self, but glowed with the light of all the small, brave choices she had never made: the art show she had not submitted to, the conversation she had avoided, the solo trip she had been too scared to book, the quiet "no" she had swallowed to keep others comfortable. 
She did not need to dip her hands in to carry it away, because the slow, winding walk, the quiet vigil as twilight deepened to star-dusted dark, the choice to keep going when every instinct told her to turn back, had already woven that courage back into her bones, soft and steady as the tide that pulled at the shore far below. 
When she turned to walk back, looping the shortened, star-stamped leash around the hound's collar, the two ferry guides stood at the edge of the treeline waving soft as dune grass, and she noticed the indented star on the leather glowed faintly, enough to light the winding path home, no rush, no fanfare, only the quiet promise that courage never waited at the end of a straight, urgent dash, but met travelers halfway when they were willing to take the long, misty route, one slow, lulled step at a time.
