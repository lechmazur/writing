The candle-lit shrine carved into glacier ice held no gilded idols, only dented trinkets pulled from alpine shipwrecks, crashed bush planes, and the half-frozen pockets of hikers who never made it down the high glacial cols. 
Its keeper was a wreck-diver ghost whisperer, her hands crisscrossed with thin scars from razor ice shards and rusted hull edges, her voice softened by years of speaking to those trapped between the frozen dark and the living world. 
The shrine’s glow held the quality of borrowed starlight, soft and cool and a little untethered to the world of rock and flesh below, gilding frost crystals on the ice walls until they looked like scattered flecks of sky fallen too far from home. 
She did not tend the shrine to lay ghosts to rest, not entirely; her core drive was to salvage something pure from the wreckage, the unbroken threads of love or regret that outlasted twisted metal and frozen bone. 
She wove truth carefully, never forcing revelation on the grievers who trekked up the glacier to find her, because she knew the windows of connection between living and lost were as thin and fragile as the ice crust over a glacial crevasse. 
Her work relied not on dramatic seances or summoned spirits, but on tracing those thin links by analyzing cameo appearances in strangers' dreams, the split-second faces that lingered at the edge of sleep with no name, no anchor to the dreamer’s own memory. 
On this particular evening, a young woman sat across from her on a bench carved from packed ice, twisting a cracked, melted snow globe between her chapped fingers. 
The globe had been pulled from the wreckage of a tour bus that had slid off an icy pass three years prior, its glass warped by freeze-thaw cycles until the tiny ceramic village inside had slumped into unrecognizable lumps, its glitter long leaked out into the glacial silt. 
The young woman had traveled for days because for months, every time she closed her eyes, she saw a girl with her sister’s chipped blue nail polish standing at the edge of every dream, holding a snow globe she could never quite reach. 
The whisperer leaned forward, candlelight gilding the warped curve of the melted globe as she wove together threads from a dozen other dream cameos: a truck driver who saw the girl waving frantically for help minutes after the crash, a park ranger who dreamt of her tucking the globe into a crack in the ice where it would not be crushed under the bus’s twisted frame. 
She did not speak of cold or fear or the long wait for rescue that never came; instead, she chose to expose the quiet truth the sister had carried across every window of connection, across every stranger’s dream. 
The globe had been a peace offering, bought at a mountain gift shop an hour before the crash, meant to apologize for the stupid, screaming fight they had that morning, to tell the young woman she was the best thing in her life. 
When the young woman left, tucking the warped snow globe into her coat pocket with a soft, wobbly smile, the whisperer knew she had done what she set out to do. 
She had not raised the dead, not undone the terrible violence of the crash, but she had pulled one perfect, unbroken piece of love from the ice and twisted metal, something that would travel down the glacier with the young woman instead of staying trapped in the frozen dark. 
Outside the shrine, the aurora spilled pale green light over the ice, and for a moment she could see the faint outlines of all the lost souls who lingered there, not as ghosts to fear, but as quiet lights holding their own small truths out to anyone willing to look for the windows between worlds, their glow the same soft, borrowed starlight that lit the shrine walls.
