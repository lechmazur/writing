After the village hedgewitch’s botched truth-reveal magic trick fails, the guarded druid steps to the froth-fringed edge of the cursed lake, her oak-bark pauldron dented where a stray illusion shard nicked it, her posture held in the tight, unflinching poise of warlike serenity. 
For ten years, she has honed the quiet gift that defines her work among the border clans: she finds lost things, from children’s dropped woolen mitts to faded battle standards buried under landslide scree, from mislaid memory charms to the half-forgotten names of the long dead. 
She has chased every fragment of the truth mirrors refuse to show, the polished glass always bouncing back only the sharp, guarded mask she wears for the world, never the split second of cowardice that froze her in place when her little sister slipped through the thin lake ice a decade prior. 
The hedgewitch’s trick was supposed to yank that buried moment to the surface, but instead it cracked the lake’s frozen crust, revealing the swirling orchard vortex at the heart of the cursed lake, where the waters spin gnarled boughs of the sunken apple orchard the two sisters had tended in slow, grinding rotations, half-frozen fruit glinting like shards of bruised amber. 
She tucks the flesh-eating plant seed, plucked from the bog altar where she’d left an offering three moons prior, into the seam of her leather gauntlet, its ridged shell warm against her wrist, a weapon that feeds on unvoiced regret to unfurl its snapping, acidic fronds. 
She wades into the frigid water, moving through expanding circles of churning current, each ring carrying her closer to the vortex’s core, each step requiring her to soften the rigid set of her shoulders, release the tight clench of her jaw, and unbend the iron guard she has wrapped around her grief for ten long years. 
The water bites through her wool tunic, sharp as a wolf’s tooth, but she does not flinch, her warlike serenity holding steady as orchard boughs creak overhead, their tangled branches snagged with the lost trinkets of a decade: her sister’s woven hair ribbon, the chipped clay cup they’d thrown on the village wheel, the wooden doll she’d carved for the girl’s seventh birthday. 
At the very center of the spin, she finds it: the frozen confession, a block of milky lake ice holding the shape of the words she’d never gotten to scream as her sister slipped under, the apology for letting jealousy curdle her tone that morning, the admission that she’d hidden the girl’s new skates out of petty spite, the truth that she had not moved to help because a small, ugly part of her had wanted the girl to suffer for being the faster, brighter, more beloved child. 
As she reaches for the ice block, a tangle of waterlogged root and congealed shadow lurches from the deep, the cursed guardian of the vortex that feeds on buried shame, its thorn-fringed maw gaping to swallow both her and the confession before she can speak the words aloud and break the lake’s curse. 
She does not reach for her iron hunting knife, instead pressing the flesh-eating plant seed to the churning surface of the water, letting the weight of her unspoken regret seep into its ridged shell to trigger its growth, the vine surging upward in a flash of glossy, tooth-fringed green to snap through the shadow guardian’s thorny limbs, dissolving the corrupted magic into thin, pine-scented mist. 
When the last wisp of shadow fades, she lifts the block of frozen confession from the water, and for the first time in ten years, she lets her guard fully fall, speaking each trapped word aloud until the ice melts against her palms, the cold water seeping into her skin to wash away the rigid, frozen mask no mirror had ever been able to penetrate. 
As the vortex slows to stillness, the lost trinkets from the orchard boughs drift gently to the lake shore, and she knows she has found the last lost thing she ever needed to recover: the part of herself she had frozen tight alongside her unspoken confession, the part mirrors could never show because she had refused to look for it herself.
