The copper spill of late October sun gilded the salt-crusted bars of the lighthouse prison perched on the jagged Teeth Reef, where Luka, son of the shepherd boy who cried wolf, locked away three years prior for "inciting panic" when he warned of a storm the village insisted would never come, knelt in the slant of amber light, hands resting on the carved oak lattice pried loose from the lighthouse's long-abandoned confessional. 
The air held the soft, briny stillness of copperlight repose, the kind of quiet that settles when the gulls have gone to roost and the waves slow their pounding to a murmur, the hush he had chased for every long month of his sentence, when the shouts of "liar" from the village docks faded to a distant echo. 
He knew the coast guard cutter was two hours out, chugging across the choppy sound to investigate the lighthouse's dead radio, that they would find the two wardens' bodies wedged in the reef crevices before the sun dipped below the horizon, and he had no intention of being there when they arrived with their own ready-made story of the liar's son who turned violent, who murdered his keepers to escape. 
The old confessional lattice, rubbed smooth by a hundred years of whispered secrets and unspoken guilt, sustains impossibilities, the last keeper had slurred to him one stormy night, long before the waves swept the man over the rail; it could hold a truth so sharp it cut through decades of fable, if only someone was brave enough to thread it through the gaps. 
Luka's goal was not escape, not really, but to thread a labyrinth with his own story: not the fable of the liar's son doomed to repeat his father's mistakes, not the convenient narrative of a violent prisoner, but the quiet truth of a boy who had watched wolves creep down from the hills to take the village sheep the winter before, who had called a warning no one bothered to hear, who had grieved when the storm he predicted crushed three fishing boats to splinters against the very reef that held his prison. 
The only path to that labyrinth, the keeper had said, ran through the scuffs on a narrow door left ajar for decades, the splintered oak entry to the old confessional that had been propped open since the last priest abandoned the lighthouse in 1982, its lower panel crisscrossed with the scuff marks of every prisoner who had ever knelt there, who had ever pressed their forehead to the wood and begged to be seen as more than the worst thing anyone had ever said about them. 
Luka wove frayed twine salvaged from old fishing nets through the lattice's crisscrossed gaps, tying each knot to a memory: the taste of blackberry pie his mother baked before the village cast his family out, the sound of real wolf howls curling over the hills, the cold shock of rain on his face when he helped pull a fisherman from the waves the night of the storm, the soft hush that fell when he stopped shouting over the villagers' jeers and chose to speak only the truth he knew. 
When the last knot pulled tight, the lattice lifted an inch from the salt-sticky floor, and the gilded copperlight caught the twine to cast a thousand tiny, glowing threads that hovered in the air between the lattice and the ajar confessional door, spinning a path that did not lead down to the reef or out to the village, but into the quiet labyrinth of his own making, where no inherited fable could drown out his voice. 
He stepped toward the glow, boots brushing the decades-old scuffs on the doorframe, and let the hush wrap around him, knowing that when the coast guard arrived to find the empty cell and the reef-tangled bodies, they would tell whatever story suited them--but he would be long gone, walking the labyrinth of his own truth, no longer forced to carry the weight of a wolf cry he never uttered.
