The tidal bluff jutted over glass-smooth swells where no wave crested or broke, three years to the day after friction dissolved from the world, its grassy lip gilded by the pale, unrooted glow of an aurora that pooled like spilled milk across the northern sky, the light that locals called borrowed dawn for how it felt lent, not earned, by the dark. 
Few villagers dared climb the bluff, for it was the known roost of the elusive eel herder, a figure wrapped in oiled sealskin who most claimed they only saw out of the corner of their eye, slithering between slick rock outcroppings to tend the silver eel schools that now slid frictionless between tide pools without so much as a ripple to mark their passage. 
The herder was precognitively cursed, gifted and plagued by split-second visions of every small collapse the world would hold: a child slipping on a friction-slick dock, a fishing net unraveling mid-cast, the slow, quiet fade of the promise their grandmother had made the day the friction first left. 
That promise, the one that hummed at the edge of every vision, was to stitch friction back into the fabric of the world by learning the sacred patterns that had held all things in gentle tension for millennia, patterns the old woman had said were written in the slip of eels, the drift of stars, the weight of rock against rock. 
For three years, the herder had worked by the soft, borrowed light of the aurora, following their grandmother's method: placing painted rocks in mathematical sequences across the bluff, each smooth basalt stone marked with red ochre spirals, white shell hash dots, black charcoal lines that mapped Fibonacci spirals, prime number progressions, the orbital drifts of long-dead suns. 
The eels, silver and slick as liquid mercury, wove between the rock lines each high tide, their bodies tracing gaps in the sequences that the herder would spend hours adjusting, always seeking the alignment that would let them catch and anchor the loose stuff of the world that now slid free of every surface. 
On this particular night, the herder's curse prickled at the base of their skull, a vision flaring of fine, glittering dust sifting down from the aurora, the remains of a collapsed star that had burned out a thousand years prior, its remains drifting slow across the solar system until the loss of friction let it slip through the atmosphere without burning up. 
The herder knew, from the old stories their grandmother had told, that this dust was the missing key, the substance heavy enough with the weight of burned-out suns to hold the patterns together if only they could harness its drift before it slid off the bluff and into the frictionless sea, lost forever to the dark. 
They moved fast, adjusting the final rock in the sequence, a small stone painted with a single eel coiled into a golden ratio spiral, placing it exactly where their precognitive vision showed the dust would first touch the bluff, the line of rocks forming a gentle, sloped channel that guided the glittering particles instead of letting them skitter away into the tide. 
When the first grain of collapsed star dust settled into the spiral at the center of the rock sequence, the herder felt the faint, familiar tug of friction return: the rough wool of their sealskin cloak catching on a grass blade, the weight of their boots pressing firm into the soil instead of sliding, the eels below pausing their endless glide to flick their tails against the tide pool walls, sending tiny, long-absent ripples spreading across the water. 
The aurora softened, its borrowed dawn glow gilding the glittering line of star dust that now sat anchored in the rock sequences, and the herder smiled, realizing the curse that had felt like a burden for so long had simply been another thread in the sacred patterns they had spent years chasing, the promise they had carried not a weight to bear but a path to walk, one small rock, one silver eel, one stardust grain at a time.
