On the frosted external catwalk of the remote xenobiology research station, the obliging sky mechanic Elara tucked her frost-fringed hood tighter, breathing slow within the hush of beating wings at rest. 
The thousand palm-sized lepidoptera, their wings dusted with bioluminescent frost, had settled an hour prior, tucking delicate antennae against the station’s alloy hull as the system’s dim sun dipped low enough to paint the crater valleys violet. 
She had volunteered for every external repair shift across three straight roosting cycles, trading off tedious internal lab maintenance for the bite of vacuum-suited cold, because she was chasing a quiet, unlauded goal: to prove that time moves sideways. 
Three months of cross-referencing sensor logs with the xenobiology team’s moth migration data had shown her that the quiet roosting hour aligned perfectly with the flaring of the magnetic nexus at twilight, a tangled knot of planetary field lines that jolted every system on the station for 90 seconds straight as it intersected the hull. 
She had built her test rig in the small, mechanically warm utility nook off the catwalk, lining its casing with waste heat captured from the station’s life support loops so its delicate components would not seize in the subzero dark, and rigged its projection system to operate through holographic principles, projecting layered slices of local time without distorting the moths’ fragile roosting state. 
The lead xenobiologist had laughed when Elara first shared her hypothesis, calling sideways time a poetic trick of frozen sensor static, but she had helped Elara calibrate the rig’s sensors to avoid disrupting the roost, knowing the ever-obliging sky mechanic had fixed her team’s frozen sample incubators mid-blizzard three months prior without a single complaint. 
A faint, thrumming vibration traveled up the catwalk rail as the magnetic nexus bloomed, painting the ice around her boots in shivering arcs of pale magenta, and Elara reached for the heavy, rubber-gripped lever mounted to the rig’s control panel, her gloved fingers closing slow around its cold surface. 
She pulled the lever down in one smooth, steady motion, and the rig hummed to life, throwing holographic slices of light out across the catwalk that did not blare or flash, but glowed with the same soft, icy warmth as the station’s interior viewports on long, dark nights. 
What unfolded was no dramatic, reality-splitting revelation, but a series of subdued awakenings: overlapping frames of the same catwalk, showing last roost’s moths tucking their wings, next roost’s moths just arriving, the xenobiologist leaning against the same rail holding a steaming mug of spiced cocoa for her, her own younger self soldering the rig’s circuit boards in the mechanically warm nook, all occupying the same space without collision, stacked like pressed flower petals. 
None of the roosting moths stirred, their wings still held soft and still against the hull, as the holographic layers drifted slow and sideways instead of marching forward in the rigid line she had been taught to expect as a child, each moment bleeding gently into the next instead of vanishing into a fixed past. 
The cold of the catwalk seeped through her suit seals, but she did not shiver, because the faint glow of the holograms carried the same recycled, mechanically warm air from her nook, wrapping around her like a well-worn blanket as she watched the moments stack. 
She did not whoop, or rush inside to call the research council, or scramble to log irrefutable data points, because the proof was in the soft glow: time did not march straight ahead, carrying moments away forever, but slid sideways, holding every quiet, kind, unremarkable moment close enough to touch if you knew when and where to reach. 
As the 90-second nexus window began to fade, the closest moth twitched a single, frost-dusted antenna, as if it too could see the layered moments hanging soft in the twilight air. 
She flipped the lever back to its resting position, tucking a loose strand of hair back under her hood as the holograms dimmed, and smiled, knowing she would be back for the next roost, and the next, tending to the station’s systems and the quiet truth she had found wrapped in ice and recycled heat.
