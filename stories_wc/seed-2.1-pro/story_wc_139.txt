The prep room of Marquez & Sons Funeral Home hummed with deathly vitality—fluorescent lights bleaching the pale porcelain embalming table, jasmine wreaths leaking sweet scent over the sharp tang of formaldehyde, half-stitched burial linens folded on a shelf as if holding their breath for the next body. 
I was the con artist's honest child, the girl who never lied even when my father's small grifts paid our grocery bills, the girl who had turned down a chance to fake a work reference for a better apartment because the mathematics of a fair trade had always felt like the only solid thing in a life built on quick escapes and half-truths. 
My father had died three days prior, slumped over his kitchen table while filling out the final appeal for his political asylum claim, the one he had spent two years fighting for after fleeing his home country where he had refused to cook election tallies for a violent ruling regime. 
Resting on the metal cart beside his closed casket was his most prized possession, a heavy cut-glass vintage ink pot he had carried in his coat pocket when he crossed the border, its indigo ink mixed from oak galls and wild indigo by his grandmother, who always told him the ink liquefies rigid thinking, softening the hard edges of unexamined rules so people can see the human cost of the lines they draw. 
I was not there to sneak into the casket or steal mementos, nor was I there to confront the asylum officer who had twice denied my father's claim by hiding behind a wall of checkboxes and procedural fine print. 
I had learned, after weeks of lingering quietly in the funeral home's public halls, that the same officer visited the columbarium every Wednesday to leave fresh daisies at his late wife's niche, and that he always cut through the prep room to avoid the crowds of mourners gathered for afternoon visitations. 
My plan was not to yell or plead or stage a dramatic protest, but to breach the sterile, impersonal barrier between him and the man whose life he had dismissed as "insufficiently credible" by waiting actively—sitting still with the ink pot uncorked, my father's stack of math worksheets (the ones he had used to teach me algebra while we waited in shelter lobbies) laid out on the prep room's counter, no tricks, no cons, just the quiet weight of truth. 
I knew the old story about the ink was just a story, the kind of gentle lie my father would tell to make hard days feel softer, but as I sat there listening to the prep room's fridge hum where other bodies waited for their own services, I watched the indigo surface catch the light, and for a second I believed it could soften even the most rigid, rule-bound mind. 
When the door creaked open an hour later, the officer stepped in, his jacket slung over one arm, a crumpled packet of daisies in his hand, and froze when he saw me sitting beside my father's casket. 
I did not stand or shout or tell him who I was right away; I simply dipped a pen in the vintage ink pot, slid a blank worksheet across the counter, and invited him to sit, to work through the equation of what is given and what is owed, the math I had spent years trying to solve. 
For a long moment he stared, his face tight with the guarded, rigid set I recognized from my father's hearing transcripts, then his eyes softened, landing on the ink pot, on the casket, on the scrawled numbers my father had scribbled in the margins of his asylum application the night he died. 
He pulled out the chair across from me, set his daisies on the edge of the embalming table, and reached for the pen, no forms, no checkboxes, no rules to hide behind, just two people in a room thrumming with the raw, alive weight of death and second chances, ready to finally calculate a trade that did not cheat anyone out of what they were owed.
