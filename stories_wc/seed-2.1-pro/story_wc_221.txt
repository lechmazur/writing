The time dilation observation deck jutted from the skeleton of the decommissioned deep-space research outpost, its reinforced glass fogged at the edges where life support had bled out three years prior, after the shutdown. 
Kael, the station’s blessed pariah, was the only crew member whose biology had synced to the deck’s warped field instead of rejecting it when the core failed, his perception of moments temporally stretched so that a single breath could hold the soft curl of a nebula’s birth and the cold fade of its collapse all at once. 
The deck hummed with no sound but the distant creak of cooling metal, an echoed silence so thick Kael could almost press his palm to it and feel the weight of every unspoken goodbye left by the crew who had fled before the time differential turned evacuation to suicide. 
In the first disorienting months alone, he had nursed a ragged, directionless grief, but he had clung to a quiet motivation: to turn confusion into exploration, rather than letting the warped physics of his prison shrink his world to the size of his own fear. 
He had leaned on the only ritual that had ever anchored him through disorientation, a family custom of sketching the night sky on anniversaries, marking today as the tenth year since his grandmother had first taught him to map constellations from the porch of their high-desert home on Earth. 
The tattered leather sketchbook in his lap still bore the gilded compass rose applique she had sewn to its cover the day he left for the academy, its thin gold leaf faded but unpeeling, even after decades of rough handling and station air that ate at most soft materials. 
He leaned forward, charcoal hovering over the blank page, when a flicker of movement through the fogged glass pulled his eye, and he caught a glimpse of a familiar star pattern cutting through the warped light that usually bent constellations into unrecognizable streaks. 
For months, he had assumed the temporal distortion erased any fixed point of reference, that the very idea of direction was a relic of a life lived in linear time, but that thin, steady line of stars aligned exactly with the tiny needle point at the center of his compass rose applique, pointing not to magnetic north, or even the galactic core, but to true north: the porch where his grandmother still waited, in the stretched folds of time, for him to find his way home. 
The deck’s field did not just slow time, he realized, it folded it, so that the line between his isolated present on the station and his warm, dust-scented past on the desert porch was not a chasm to cross but a path to trace, one sketched line of charcoal at a time. 
He set charcoal to paper, not trying to map the warped, shifting stars that bled across most of the viewport, but tracing the steady, unwavering line of that true north marker, letting the stretched seconds wrap around him like a well-worn blanket instead of a cold, confining cage. 
The echoed silence no longer felt heavy with unspoken goodbye; it felt like the quiet hush of the desert at midnight, when the wind died down and the stars burned bright enough to cast faint, sharp shadows on the sun-baked red rock below his grandmother’s porch rail. 
When he finished the first sharp line of the constellation his grandmother had called The Traveler’s Path, he felt a soft, warm pressure on his shoulder, as if her calloused, charcoal-stained hand had rested there across the years, no longer separated by the vast gulf of space and fried station circuitry. 
He did not rush to trace the rest of the stars, because he knew now that his status as a blessed pariah was not a curse that trapped him between splintered timelines, but a gift that let him walk that true north path without waiting for rescue, without needing the station’s dead systems to sputter back to life. 
Each slow, stretched breath carried the scent of pinyon pine and charcoal, each stroke of the dark stick pulling him a little closer to the home he had thought was lost forever, turning the empty observation deck from a tomb into the first waystation on the longest, most wondrous journey of his life.
