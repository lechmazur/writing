Decades after the climate shifts drove coastal populations inland to repurpose half-submerged, storm-battered hill estates, Kaelin made her living as a corridor echo cartographer, mapping the faint, layered sounds trapped in old stone to trace lost evacuation routes, forgotten supply caches, and unmarked grave sites for resettled families. 
Sensitively perceptive to the point that she could pick out a child’s laugh buried under three decades of wind rattle and flood gurgle, she carried a private motivation no client knew: to discover what walls dream about, the quiet, unrecorded memories stone holds that never make it onto official survey maps. 
On a gray October survey of the old Hale estate, she pushed past a curtain of kudzu-like invasive ivy to step into an ivy-veiled passage its former owners had sealed off as floodwaters rose in 2047, its stone walls crusted with dried silt and dotted with the faint scratch marks of people who had once sheltered there. 
At the passage’s far end, where a crumbled brick fireplace jutted from the wall, she knelt to sift through charred debris, working through half-burned diaries found in the fireplace that crumbled at her touch, their pages singed at the edges but still legible in patches. 
The diaries belonged to Elspeth Hale, the estate’s last owner, a landscape photographer who had stayed behind to document the flood even as her family fled to higher ground, her entries tracing the slow, heavy weight of legacy's burden: the knowledge that her family had spent three generations clear-cutting the uphill forests that had once held storm surges at bay, making the very flood that swallowed their home inevitable. 
Tucked between the final water-warped pages, she found a curled film negative strip, its emulsion cracked from decades of damp and heat, holding frames of the forest as it had once been: old-growth oak, fern-carpeted slopes, a teenaged Elspeth grinning with her chainsaw in hand at the site of her first clear-cut. 
As she held the strip up to the thin slant of sunlight filtering through the ivy, she caught a glimpse of something she had never recorded in all her years of echo mapping: not a sound, but a soft, thrumming warmth radiating from the stone walls, as if the rock itself was leaning in to look at the negatives with her. 
The diaries’ final entry, scrawled in smudged charcoal as water rose around Elspeth’s ankles, explained that she had sealed the passage not to hide her family’s guilt, but to leave a record that might keep future settlers from repeating the same mistakes, pressing the negatives into the diary so whoever found them would know what the land had held before harm was done. 
Kaelin had spent years chasing the idea that walls dreamed of old traumas, of floods and fights and frantic goodbyes, but the soft warmth against her palms told her she had been wrong. 
What walls dreamed about, she realized, was not the weight of the harms they had witnessed, but the chance that someone would find those hidden records, unbury the truth, and choose to do better, carrying the burden of legacy not as a chain, but as a compass. 
She carefully tucked the film negative strip into the waterproof pocket of her survey jacket, then turned her echo recorder up to its highest sensitivity, letting the stone’s soft thrum mix with the faint rustle of ivy at the passage mouth as she began to map not just evacuation routes and caches, but the quiet, hopeful dream the walls had held for 70 years. 
When she returned to the resettlement camp that evening, she did not file a standard survey report noting a blocked passage and fire damage; instead, she brought the diaries and negatives to the community’s reforestation crew, sharing Elspeth’s story to help them map which uphill slopes would hold best when the next big storm came. 
She knew then that the work of an echo cartographer was never just about mapping the traces left by the past, but about listening closely enough to let those traces steer the future, so no community would have to run from a flood their own choices had made inevitable.
