The prism sanctuary under starlight caught fractured pinpricks of sky in its angled quartz walls, spilling shards of silver across the stone floor where the broken mask restorer knelt. 
She was a woman who accumulated silence, the quiet of half-told griefs and unspoken apologies seeping into the cuffs of her wool tunic, the pads of her calloused fingers, the hollow between her collarbones, until even her breath made no sound against the still air. 
Her purpose tonight was to ground restless spirits, the dozens of fishermen dragged to the seabed in a 1692 gale whose tangled, sea-tattered net had been hauled to the sanctuary that dawn, each knotted mesh holding the echo of a man cut off mid-sentence, mid-prayer, mid-promise to return home. 
The space around the net hummed with an interlaced hush, threads of unuttered goodbyes, forgotten shanties, and frozen gasps of terror braided so tight around the hemp fibers that no mortal voice could cut through to reach the trapped souls. 
From her leather tool roll, she drew the slender steel tapestry needle her grandmother had forged for her, its eye worn smooth from decades of pulling silk thread through cracked mask lacquer, its tip sharp enough to tease apart even the tightest, salt-crusted knot without fraying the fragile hemp. 
She did not work in silence, as the village elders had instructed; instead, she began to speak, her long-buried secret spilling slow as she worked, the entire ritual unfolding across sharing a secret she had clamped tight behind her ribs for 22 years. 
Her method was deliberate, unrushed: by unraveling knots in a centuries-old fisherman’s net, one twisted, salt-hardened loop at a time, she would exhume not bone, but the half-lives of the trapped men, each loosened thread freeing a memory that could finally be carried to rest rather than tangled in the dark. 
The feeling in the sanctuary was lucid unrest, no soft fog of ritual to dull the sharp edge of her confession, no numbing chant to muffle the way the net seemed to sigh each time a knot came undone, the freed memories brushing her cheeks like cold sea spray. 
As she spoke of the day her brother drowned, of the way her numb fingers slipped from his wrist when the riptide wrenched them apart, the silence she had accumulated over decades of mending masks seeped from her pores, wrapping each freed memory in soft, gentle quiet so the spirits would not be jolted by the sharpness of their own long-lost voices. 
She teased the needle tip under the first crusted knot, pulling slow until the hemp gave, and the memory of a fisherman who had stowed a hand-carved wooden doll for his toddler daughter in his oilskin pocket drifted up, catching the prism light like a firefly before lifting through the quartz ceiling to the stars above. 
The next knot held the raucous shanty the crew had been singing the moment the gale split their sails, the next the quiet, trembling fear of a 14-year-old cabin boy on his first voyage away from home, the interlaced hush loosening thread by thread as the hours stretched on, until there was only one tight, knotted loop left at the net’s frayed center. 
She slid the needle under that final knot as she spoke the last line of her secret, the part she had never dared admit even to herself: that she had spent 22 years thinking her brother’s ghost hated her for letting go, when he had only been waiting for her to stop holding her guilt tight so he could rest too. 
The knot slipped free between her fingers, and the last spirit--her brother, she realized, his presence warm as sunlight on cold, wet skin--lifted with the rest, leaving the net spread soft and unknotted across the cool stone, the sanctuary holding only the quiet of starlight and the slow, even sound of her breathing, unburdened for the first time since she was a girl. 
She set the tapestry needle across the empty net, knowing she had not banished the spirits but grounded them, giving each a path home where their memories could settle soft into the coastal tides rather than thrashing, tangled, in the cold dark between the living world and the stars.
