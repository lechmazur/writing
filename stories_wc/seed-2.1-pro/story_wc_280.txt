The last padlock clicked shut on the small mountain town’s mystic curio shop at 9:02 p.m., long after the last tourist had wandered out of the winding hedge maze that concealed a sun-dappled mystic orchard garden at its core. 
Lila, the 17-year-old shop clerk who was synesthetically blessed, had lingered after closing, slump-shouldered against a gnarled apple bough, because her first ever open mic performance--three years of writing quiet, folk-tinged songs saved up for a 10-minute set--had been canceled that afternoon when the venue’s roof caved in during a sudden summer thunderstorm. 
She was so wrapped up in replaying the crack of the venue manager’s apologetic phone call that she did not notice the soft rustle of taffeta at the hedge line, the faint whiff of lemon pomade that signaled the presence of the orchard’s long-term resident: an encore-seeking stage ghost who had collapsed mid-curtain call in 1927, seconds before he was meant to step back out for the closing ukulele number he had spent six months writing. 
The orchard existed in a fractured timeline, where the ghost’s 1920s footlights tangled with modern firefly glows, where the echo of his ukulele strings hummed under the beat of Lila’s half-mumbled song lyrics, where missed chances did not dissolve but hung in the air like overripe plums waiting to be plucked by whoever was brave enough to reach for them. 
As someone synesthetically blessed, Lila did not merely hear those thrums--she saw them, warm burnt-gold threads weaving between the apple boughs, growing brighter where the ghost drifted closer, a worn velvet suit jacket slung over his translucent arm, a hand-painted tarot coin balanced on his open palm. 
He never spoke, for his work was done by producing echoes--faint, glimmering snatches of sound pulled from the cracks in the timeline: the roar of a 1920s crowd craning for an encore, the shaky first chord Lila had strummed on her beat-up guitar when she was 12, the soft cheer of her best friend when she finished her first original song, the crackle of the canceled open mic’s planned stage speakers warming up before the storm hit. 
Lila watched as the gold threads began to intertwine, braiding the ghost’s unheard 1927 closing number with the lyrics she had scribbled in her notebook on the bus ride to work that week, braiding the old crowd’s hunger for one more song with her own quiet, crushed hope that someone might hear what she had spent years writing. 
The ghost’s purpose that night, as it had been for every heartbroken dreamer who stumbled into the orchard after hours for 96 years, was to lure possibility from the brink of despair, to prove that a missed curtain did not mean the show was over, that stages did not need four walls and a paid ticket stub to hold real, lasting magic. 
He flipped the hand-painted tarot coin into the air, and when it landed softly in Lila’s palm, she felt a jolt of warm, rising nostalgia: the first time she had held a beat-up secondhand guitar, the way her grandma had taught her to pick sun-warmed plum slices straight from the orchard boughs when she was small, the ghost’s own soft memory of standing in the wings, heart thrumming, ready to step out for the song no one in his lifetime ever got to hear. 
She pulled her guitar from where she had slung it over her shoulder earlier, too sad to even carry it home properly, and began to strum, and as she did, the echoes swelled: the ghost’s ukulele twined with her chords, the 1920s crowd’s soft murmurs mixed with the crickets’ evening hum, the gold synesthetic threads wrapping around every fruit-laden bough until the whole orchard glowed like a lit, welcoming stage. 
For a long, soft moment, there was no canceled show, no collapsed roof, no unspoken final song left hanging in the cool 1927 evening air--just two performers separated by decades, sharing a set under the apple trees, the ghost finally getting his long-sought encore as Lila got her first, most attentive audience. 
When she finished the last chord, the ghost tipped his imaginary hat, and the tarot coin warmed in her palm, a small, hand-painted promise that every fractured timeline held a second stage, that every missed chance was just a pre-show wait for a set that would feel like coming home.
