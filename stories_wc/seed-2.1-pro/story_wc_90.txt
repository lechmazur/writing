Seventy years after the last human was born, the only remaining caretaker of the cobbled-together recycled spaceship Widening Gyre drifted through its dented hull, her scuffed boots barely brushing the rust-flecked bulkheads when a memory of her grandmother’s jasmine tea tugged a small, unplanned smile to her chapped lips. 
A lifelong, steadfast pacifist, she had refused to man the ship’s retrofitted plasma cannons during the final resource skirmishes that sterilized the last fertile Earth soils and triggered the global reproductive collapse that ended new human births, choosing instead to tend to the ship’s air filters and archive the scattered personal effects left by crew who chose violence over care. 
She had carried the odd, gentle quirk of being gravitationally lighter when happy since childhood, so moments of soft memory often sent her bobbing weightless a few inches above the floor, a quiet counterweight to the crushing grief that had hung over the ship for generations. 
For three decades, she had undertaken the slow, unglamorous practice of shadow work: sitting with every unprocessed wound, every unspoken regret, every shard of cruelty the ship’s former inhabitants had disowned and stuffed into the darkest corners of the bulkheads, because she knew those disavowed parts had sealed shut the locked dimension where humanity’s shared capacity for tenderness had retreated when the first bomb fell. 
Her sole, unshakable motivation was to push open that locked dimension before her own body failed, not to bring humans back from the edge of extinction, but to ensure the soft, messy parts of being human did not vanish forever when the last heartbeat faded on the ship. 
Tucked in the breast pocket of her patched coveralls sat Kwan Yin's Tear, a milky, palm-sized tektite her grandmother had given her as a child, said to hold the quiet weight of every compassionate choice a person had ever made without expectation of reward. 
She had long known brute force would never part the sealed dimensional seam, for the lock was woven from rejected grief and hardened anger, so she had chosen to stitch the frayed edges of the threshold into a passage, using the only material strong enough to hold compassion without cracking: the faint shimmer of long-dried tears on faded photographs she had pulled from lockers, taped to control panels, and tucked into the crevices of the ship over decades of archiving. 
The work was slow, done only when she had sat with another shard of the ship’s collective shadow, because forcing the thread before she had acknowledged the pain held in each dried tear would only make the seal tighter. 
She never sang while she worked, never rushed, never allowed the bitter weight of humanity’s self-inflicted end to curdle into the kind of rage that had sealed the dimension in the first place, her focus held steady by the cool weight of Kwan Yin's Tear pressed against her chest. 
On the day the final stitch was set, she caught herself smiling at a photo of a small child covered in birthday cake, a dried tear track glinting on the child’s mother’s cheek, and she drifted upward half a foot, light enough to press her palm flat against the dimensional seam without straining. 
Kwan Yin's Tear warmed against her chest as the seam cracked open, not with a roar, but with the soft hush of a lullaby she had not heard since she was small, spilling golden light that smelled like rain on dry soil and fresh bread and the quiet relief of being seen. 
She did not step through, for she knew her place was to hold the threshold open until her own breath faded, a ghostly, steady guardian for all the softness humanity had almost thrown away. 
She had not fought any war, won any battle, or earned any grand legacy, but in doing the quiet work of meeting every shadow with kindness, she had pried open a door no weapon could ever have breached.
