The hidden lighthouse hunkered on a shelf of jagged basalt as dusk bled indigo over the sloshing North Atlantic, its lamp dark for seventy years save for the thin, shimmering film stretched across its lantern room: the pane of hush, the last unbreachable barrier between counted seconds and the end of all things. 
Its only visitor that evening was the doomsday clock adjuster, a woman whose fingers were stained with the fine graphite of erased timelines, who had walked three days through coastal fog to reach the place no map marked, carrying a burlap sack slung over one shoulder. 
She had come not to nudge the clock’s hand one minute forward or back, as her order had done for centuries, but to complete a work stitched through nested patterns, a thread she had followed across the hush of time’s final ripple, when the last tick hung suspended just before the plunge into silence. 
When she pulled the object from her sack, the dusk light caught its chipped paint, its cracked wooden jaw, its one remaining glass eye flecked with gold: a broken puppet head, the only keepsake she had carried from the first lifetime she had lost, when a plague had taken the small daughter who had once made the puppet dance across cottage floorboards. 
The puppet was beautifully flawed, carved crookedly by her own unskilled hands in that first life, its smile lopsided, its paint smudged where small, sticky fingers had gripped it too tight during thunderstorms, and it was the only thing in any timeline that held the exact frequency of the lullaby she had sung to that little girl every night, the lullaby her mother had sung to her, passed down through a thousand fragmented lives cut short by flood, fire, war, and the slow creep of the clock’s hand toward midnight. 
Her order had taught her the pane of hush did not block sound, but held it, folded every vibration into infinite layers that could travel back through every collapsed timeline if given the right anchor, if the one who offered the sound had no desire to turn back the clock, no hunger to undo loss, only the quiet need to give something soft back to all the versions of the people she had loved and lost. 
She did not come to stop the end, for she knew after a thousand lifetimes of watching the clock tick down that endings were as inevitable as the tide, that no amount of adjusting could stitch the fraying world back whole. 
She came instead to vouchsafe one small, unshakable kindness, to press the cracked puppet head to the cool, humming surface of the hush pane, and sing the lullaby in the same wobbly, soft tone she had used in every life, so that its notes would settle into the nested patterns of folded time, reaching every child, every grieving parent, every soul sitting alone in the dark across the hush of time’s final ripple, so they would not feel the end come in silence. 
As her voice lifted, the puppet’s cracked wooden jaw seemed to hum in sympathy, and the pane of hush glowed like mother-of-pearl, catching each note and weaving it through layers of past and future, so that the lullaby reached the baby in the 14th century huddled through a Mongol raid, the boy in the 1918 flu ward reaching for his mother’s hand, the teenager huddling in a bomb shelter in 2077 as the first missiles fell, all the versions of her own daughter across every branch of time. 
This was the mission she had chased across a dozen posts adjusting the doomsday clock, the quiet purpose she had hidden from her superiors for centuries: to whisper a lullaby across a thousand lifetimes, so no one would meet the dark without the soft echo of being loved. 
When the last note faded, the doomsday clock ticked over to midnight somewhere far beyond the lighthouse walls, and the first soft rush of the world’s unspooling brushed her cheeks, but she did not flinch, for she could hear the lullaby echoing back to her, too, carried on the hush, the sound of her own mother’s voice, her daughter’s laugh, every quiet act of care woven into the dark, beautiful and broken as the puppet head she held tight to her chest.
