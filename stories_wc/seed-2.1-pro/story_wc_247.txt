The luminous harbor breathed under gilded moonlight, the hour hanging soft and unmoored exactly when no one remembers you. 
The man perched on the weathered breakwater piling was quietly charismatic, even with salt crusted thick in his frayed coat cuffs and a dented vintage camera with no film slung on a frayed leather strap across his chest. 
He had drifted there three tides prior, pulled by a quiet, unshakable motivation: to translate silence into music. 
For years he had chased quiet spaces: empty concert halls, snow-locked forests, fog-wreathed headlands, convinced the gaps between sounds held notes no composed score had ever captured, but he had never found a way to pin those notes down before memory slipped away from everyone he had ever known. 
A thin, papery voice at his elbow made him jump, the whispering advisor he had not noticed leaning against the adjacent piling, her own edges fuzzy with the soft erasure that clung to every soul in that forgotten stretch of coast. 
She did not speak aloud again, but lifted gnarled hands to shape words in sign language, her movements slow enough for him to follow even as moonlight gilded her knuckles: the others here have no voices left to give you, but the silences between their gestures are exactly what you seek. 
He lifted his vintage camera to his eye then, understanding at last that its lack of film was no flaw, for he would not capture light, but work by mapping silence, framing every small, wordless moment through the cracked viewfinder to mark its weight, its pitch, its resonance. 
Through the glass, he spotted them: dozens of half-faded figures scattered across the docks and sand, shaping sign language to one another, their gestures soft as sea grass: a fisherman describing the size of the cod that got away, a grandmother miming the way she used to tuck her grandchild's hair behind an ear, a teen flicking imaginary spray at a friend long gone. 
For an hour he mapped, tilting the camera to frame the lull between a laugh-shaped hand and a sad, slow shake of the head, the hush between the wave's crash and the gull's distant cry, assigning each silence a note: a warm cello hum for the grandmother's gesture, a bright trill of flute for the teen's playful tease, a rolling bass line for the fisherman's tall tale. 
When he had mapped every last stretch of quiet, he hummed the sequence under his breath, and the sound began to disrupt the heavy, stagnant hush that had hung over the harbor for as long as any forgotten soul could remember, wrapping around each half-faded figure like sunlight on cold skin. 
The whispering advisor smiled, signing again that no one living might ever remember their names, but for a little while, their quiet joys and soft griefs would sing out over the water, woven into a melody no living composer could have written. 
He laughed as he hummed, the taste of salt on his lips holding that perfect balance of joyful sorrow, the bittersweet knowledge that remembrance did not require a name, only a listener willing to sit with the quiet long enough to hear the song hidden in the gaps. 
The film-less camera swung gently from his strap as he leaned back against the piling, watching the figures pause their signing to tilt their faces toward the sound, some smiling, some letting quiet, long-held tears trace paths through the salt on their cheeks, none of them quite so faded anymore. 
Moonlight gilded every ripple in the harbor, turning the wave crests to scattered flecks of silver that danced in time with his melody, as if the water itself had been waiting for someone to stop chasing loud, grand sounds long enough to map the quiet that lived between them. 
He did not know how long the melody would last, or if any living soul walking the nearby cliffs would catch a faint, drifting strain of it on the wind, but for that moonlit moment, it was enough.
