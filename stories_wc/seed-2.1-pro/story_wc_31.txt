The late-shift pier lamplighter tucked his dented brass lighter into his frayed oilskin coat as the first paper sky lanterns lifted over the high tide, their warm gold spilling thin as melted honey across the inky chop. 
For three years he had tended the splintered pilings’ hissing gas jets after dark, driven not by a paycheck alone but by a quiet, unshakable drive to discover the color of sound. 
He hid behind truth when regulars asked why he lingered past the end of his shift, saying he stayed to watch for loose pilings or drifting debris, never naming the faint, thrumming resonance he had followed along the shoreline since he was a teen. 
Tonight, that resonance hummed louder than ever, and he made his way by wandering purposefully past the last lit piling, down a half-submerged stone stair he had never noticed before, slick with bladderwrack and dusted with the faint blue glow of bioluminescent algae. 
The stair opened into the forgotten library inside a sunken ship, its oak hull arched overhead like a bleached ribcage, water lapping soft as breath at the edges of shelves still crammed with leather-bound volumes held fast by decades of salt and silt. 
On the central oak reading desk, propped against a cracked brass ship’s compass, sat a heavy hotel skeleton key tag, its etched surface worn smooth by tides but still legible: Grand Pier Hotel, Suite 7, 1927. 
When his fingers brushed the cool brass, thin threads of pearlescent light unspooled from the tag, weaving in the salt-damp air between the shelves to form the luminous vows a young lighthouse keeper and his hotel maid bride had spoken there, decades before the storm dragged the ship, then serving as a floating event hall, to the bay floor. 
The light pulsed in time with the distant, soft murmur of the crowd on the pier releasing their lanterns, and he realized the vows did not signify a single spoken promise, but a frequency, a held tone stitched through every quiet choice the couple had made across their 62 years of marriage. 
The air held the meteor hush that falls when every person in a crowd tilts their chin up to watch light climb, no shouts, no rushed words, only the soft thrum of cotton lantern fabric, burning wick, and shared breath tangled together above the waves. 
He leaned closer, watching the pearlescent vow-light bleed into the bioluminescent blue of the algae, the honeyed gold of the lanterns filtering through the hull cracks, the soft silver of tiny minnows darting through the shallow water at his feet, and he finally saw it: the color of sound. 
It was not a single fixed hue, but the shifting, opalescent glow of all the soft, unspoken things people carry close: the hush between two people who do not need to fill silence, the crackle of a lantern wick, the lap of tide against weathered wood, the low thrum of a promise held long after the spoken words have faded from memory. 
He did not take the key tag, knowing it belonged anchored there among the salt-stiff books, a permanent marker for the vow-light that hummed steady under the pier even when the tide ran high and winter storms rolled in to rattle the pilings. 
When he climbed back up the mossy stone stair to the pier, the sky was dotted with hundreds of drifting lanterns, their glow soft as stardust against the indigo dark, and the crowd had fallen into that same meteor hush he had felt fathoms below the waves. 
A small girl holding a crumpled, half-lit lantern paused beside him, her cheeks pink from the sharp sea wind, and asked if he had seen the mermaids that local stories said made their homes in the sunken wrecks under the pier. 
He smiled, and for a moment considered telling her about the hidden library, the glowing vows, the opalescent color he had chased for three long years, but he hid behind truth instead, telling her he had seen light under the water, bright as any floating lantern, because that was not a lie. 
He lit the last gas lamp on the pier as the girl ran back to her parents to release her own lantern, and for the first time, he did not need to chase the sound’s shifting color, because he carried it with him, soft and opalescent, in the quiet thrum of every light he tended, every glowing promise that climbed into the dark.
