In the years after violence becomes impossible, when brine seized every gunlock mid-cock and crumbled every cutlass blade to rust before a blow could land, Mara worked the weathered docks where planks jutted directly over the sublittoral fringe, her movements so fluid and unhurried even hoisting 80-pound crates of salted cod that regulars called her the graceful dock worker, never guessing how tightly she had walled off the soft, tide-tugged parts of herself to survive the decades of raiding and bloodshed that preceded the quiet. 
She had long told herself her sharp edges were the only thing keeping her afloat, pushing away every fisherman who offered her warm cider, every child who tried to press a smooth sea glass into her palm, because vulnerability felt like a luxury she could not afford in a world that had once taken everything she loved. 
Three days prior, she had fished a waterlogged pirate’s wherry from the surf, and tucked in the splintered seat locker she found a pirate’s whalebone scrimshaw, its surface carved with the curling outline of an island no local chart marked, edges worn smooth from decades of being clutched in calloused raider hands. 
Old wharf tales said that scrimshaw was no mere trinket, that it unlocked guarded hearts, and that the unmarked island it depicted rose from the waves only for those brave enough to stop hiding who they truly were, vanishing the second doubt crept back to pull them under. 
Mara had scoffed at the stories at first, tucking the scrimshaw into the inner pocket of her oilskin coat like a secret, but she found herself running her thumb over its carved ridges every time she snapped at a well-meaning neighbor, every time she turned away from a kind word to hide the way her throat tightened with longing for connection she had long denied herself. 
The turning point came at low tide, when she knelt in the cold, kelp-draped rock pools of the sublittoral fringe to retrieve a child’s lost toy boat, and a low, thrumming vibration traveled up through the scrimshaw in her pocket, matching the deep, resonant call of a whale from the deep, so loud it vibrated in her molars and made the surface of every rock pool ripple in perfect concentric circles. 
For a heartbeat, she fought the urge to lean into the sound, to let the hard, defensive shell she had built over decades detach from her shoulders like a barnacle-crusted piling torn free by a rising storm, because letting go of the anger that had protected her felt like stepping off the dock into water she could not see the bottom of. 
That was when she looked up and saw it, the island from the scrimshaw rising half a mile out, its slopes draped in wild sea thrift and pine, no fortifications, no watchtowers, no signs of the fighting that had marred every other stretch of coast she had ever known. 
She thought of all the years she had hidden her love of poetry, her habit of feeding stray cats, her quiet grief for the family she had lost, all to play the part of the tough, unflappable dock hand no one could hurt, and she realized her truest motivation all along had been to find freedom in embracing one's true nature, not in carving out a hard, narrow space for herself where nothing could reach her. 
She did not race for a skiff, did not scramble to reach the island before it vanished; instead, she walked back up the dock to the fisherman who had offered her cider the week prior, pressed the scrimshaw into his calloused palm, and invited him to walk the rock pools with her, letting her guarded heart unlock one slow, brave breath at a time as the whale’s call softened to a low, warm thrum in her bones. 
When she glanced over her shoulder a minute later, the island was gone, but she did not grieve its loss, because she understood it had never been a place to sail to at all--it was the quiet, fierce, tender version of herself she had spent decades hiding, no longer locked away behind walls built for a world that no longer existed. 
When the fisherman laughed and teased her for finally letting someone get close, she rolled her eyes and threatened to drop his next shipment of cod in the surf, but her smile was soft, and for the first time in her life, she did not feel the need to hide it.
