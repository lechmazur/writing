After the breach of contract between the valley’s craft guild and the last line of rafter orchard tenders, the quartz-eye mender climbed the splintered oak ladders into the lush orchard spanning the rafters of an abandoned cathedral, her scuffed leather work bag slung heavy over one shoulder. 
She was endearingly feisty, the kind of craftsperson who would argue for an hour with a stubborn bent nail about its right to sit straight rather than toss it for a factory-made replacement, and she had not come to poach the rare honeycrisp-walnut grafts the elevated orchard grew, but to persist in mending the rift that had left the space untended for three generations. 
Stained glass fragments set into the cathedral’s broken roof dappled the apple boughs and mossy rafter walkways in ruby and sapphire light, and the air smelled of fermented fruit, pine resin, and the faint, chalky scent of the old promise stones the first tenders had set at each graft union. 
The guild had framed the original 1872 orchard contract as an eternal, unbreakable bond, demanding fixed harvest yields, unchanging pruning schedules, and a ban on new grafts no matter how the climate shifted, but her grandmother—last of the official tenders—had always taught her about deciduous vows: promises rooted in mutual care that shed their rigid outer forms each season, rather than hardening into chains that choked both the land and the people tied to them. 
Her core motivation for the climb was to bridge generational wisdom, to carry that lesson about flexible promises back to the guild before they voted to bulldoze the cathedral and raze the orchard for a warehouse parking lot. 
She had packed her standard mending kit: beeswax sealant, soft copper wire, fine-grit sandstone to polish her left quartz eye so it caught light clearly when she spoke to crowds, but when she knelt to tend the first cracked graft union, where the apple wood had split away from the walnut root, she found her sealant had leaked through its tin and soaked through the bag’s canvas lining, ruining the wire and leaving her with no obvious supplies to complete the repair. 
Frustrated but unwilling to climb down empty-handed, she upended the bag over a mossy rafter plank to sort through the mess, relying on forgotten bag contents she had not considered when packing that morning: a half-eaten honey stick, a pressed clover her grandmother had given her at 10, a crumpled note about graft care she had tucked away as a teen, and her seven-year-old daughter's hair ribbon, the sun-bleached crimson silk she had grabbed off the kitchen table after rushing the girl to the school bus before her climb. 
She laughed softly at the random pile, then paused as her grandmother’s note spelled out the old tender trick for mending split grafts when no manufactured supplies were available: wrap the wound tightly with soft, strong natural fiber, seal it with sticky honey, and let the tree’s own growing energy knit the split back together, no rigid metal or permanent seal required. 
The accidental wisdom of the moment settled over her as she worked, squeezing the last of the honey from the stick into the split wood, then wrapping the crimson silk ribbon tight around the graft to hold it fast, just as she would wrap a scraped knee for her daughter, just as her grandmother had wrapped her own small cuts when she was a child learning to tend boughs. 
She had not climbed the rafters planning a grand metaphor, but the mended graft, bound with a child’s ribbon instead of rigid wire, was the perfect demonstration of deciduous vows: promises did not need to be etched in stone or sealed with unbreakable contract terms to hold, they only needed to flex with growth, to draw on the small, forgotten treasures of care carried across generations, and to be tended, again and again, as the seasons turned. 
When she climbed down the ladder an hour later, she carried a sprig of new apple growth tucked behind her quartz eye, the pressed clover in her pocket, and a story to tell the guild that did not rely on stiff legal language, but on the soft, strong proof of a mended tree bound with a little girl’s ribbon.
