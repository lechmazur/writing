The attentive mystic, firmly grounded with boots planted flat on crushed quartz pebbles beneath the arching quartz arbor in mist, leaned toward the rusted 1978 station wagon half-tangled in blackberry bramble at the arbor’s heart. 
She had come at the cold edge of dawn, when assumptions shatter as easily as the thin rime of ice coating every twig and stone, driven by a quiet, unshakable motivation: to prove that objects absorb emotions, holding them long after the people who felt them have vanished. 
For decades, local lore had spun a tragic tale of two teen lovers stranded here in the 1996 blizzard, who fought bitterly before stumbling into the whiteout never to be found, a story the county historical society had dismissed as childish gossip, insisting the car had been abandoned by a poacher in the 1980s. 
The mystic did not carry crystals or smudge sticks, only the chipped glass prism she had found half-buried in the loam beneath the car’s driver side door on her first visit three months prior, its edges frosted from decades of mountain winters. 
She did not need ritual tools, because her chosen method to commune with the stored emotion in the wreck was far simpler: through the patterns in frost on a car window, the delicate, fern-like tracings that held the compressed memory of every warm breath, every shouted word, every quiet sigh that had fogged the glass from the inside. 
A breath of wind curled through the quartz pillars, and the constant, soft murmur of pine boughs fell away into a shifted hush, the kind of thick, charged quiet that settles when the world leans in to listen, holding its own breath so as not to smudge a fragile truth. 
She held the prism up to the faint dawn light slanting through the mist, letting it cast tiny, wavering rainbows across the frosted window, and leaned close enough that her own breath fogged a small circle in the ice, tracing the frost patterns with her gaze as she opened her senses to the stored feeling in the metal and glass. 
The stories were all truthful lies, she realized, as the frost patterns resolved into fragmented, flickering memories: there had been two teens, yes, stranded in the blizzard, but they had not fought, and they had not stumbled into the whiteout to die. 
They had been running away, not from each other, but from homes that refused to accept the girl’s pregnancy, that had called their love a sin, and they had packed the station wagon with baby clothes, a hand-carved cradle, the glass prism they’d bought at a county fair to hang over their future child’s crib. 
The blizzard had hit faster than forecast, burying the road in three feet of snow before they could reach the state line, so they had huddled in the back seat for 18 hours, sharing a single thermos of hot cocoa, talking about the names they might pick, until a park ranger had spotted their tire tracks half-buried in drifts and led them out to safety. 
They had never come back for the car, too afraid their families would track them to the arbor and force them apart, too busy building a quiet life two states over, where no one knew their names or judged their choice to love each other young. 
The old tales of tragedy and disappearance held a kernel of truth, the historical society’s dismissal of the story as pure fiction held another, and the prism, warm now in her palm, hummed with the soft, bright weight of all the hope those two teens had left soaked into the seats and glass, not grief, not anger, not fear. 
She slipped the prism into the inner pocket of her wool coat, careful not to brush the frost from the window, knowing she would bring the couple back to the arbor one day soon, when the last of the mist burned off, to show them their old car still held the quiet joy they had thought they’d left buried in the snow. 
She had tracked them down three weeks prior, using a partial name etched into the car’s dashboard and a faded receipt tucked in the glove box, because she was always attentive to the small clues others missed, rooted firmly enough in the tangible world to know that emotion left marks as real as frost on glass, as solid as quartz, as bright as light split through a prism. 
When the wind picked up again, the shifted hush broke, and the pines sighed once more, carrying the faint, warm echo of a laugh first shared 27 years before, held fast in the ice and quartz and glass for anyone attentive enough to look.
