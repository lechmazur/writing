The starlit signal station sat perched on the jagged lip of the eroding coastal bluff, its rusted radar dish frozen at a 47-degree skyward tilt three days after the hydraulics failed that once let it track seasonal bird migrations for inland wildlife offices. 
Its only current occupant was the patchwork navigator, a woman named Elara who wore a coat stitched from old sailcloth fragments, hand-drawn map scraps, and frayed fabric swatches cut from the shirts of every person she had ever grieved. 
She had hiked six miles up the overgrown access road with a single, heavy item slung in her pack: a patina bronze censer foot, the only surviving piece of the incense burner her grandmother had carried when she fled the coast as a 17-year-old, leaving behind the memory of her fisherman father lost to a brutal 1972 storm. 
Elara had carried the censer foot for seven years, ever since her grandmother died, and knew its quiet magnetic pull fluctuated with moon phases, swinging soft as a breath toward half-forgotten places when the moon was a thin silver sliver, yanking sharp as a hooked fish toward unmarked grief sites when the moon hung full and heavy over the surf. 
She had not come to track migratory birds, but to build a rough waystation for the slow, unreported reverse migration of people like her, people who had spent decades running inland from the sites of their most unfaceable losses, who now felt a quiet, unnameable tug to return and lay their ghosts to rest. 
She had first learned of the station’s frozen dish three weeks prior, through cryptic phone calls at midnight from strangers across the state, their voices thick with line static and unshed tears, describing the same unshakable tug she felt, the same quiet urge to walk back to the coast their families had spent generations teaching them to fear. 
None of these callers had reliable maps for the journey, because the geography of loss does not appear on standard road atlases: it shifts with high tides, washes out with storm surges, hides in the cracks between paved highways and overgrown footpaths, so she had come to navigate it for them, using the weighted bronze censer foot as her only compass. 
That first night after settling in, she swept broken tempered glass from the station’s pocked concrete floor, propped the patina bronze censer foot on the western windowsill where it caught faint starlight, and strung weathered fishing line between the idle antennae to hold handwritten notes from every caller who shared a story of a person they had lost to the unforgiving coast. 
On new moon nights, when the censer foot’s pull went soft and almost imperceptible, she left trail markers of crushed white shell and dried sage at overgrown path entrances to guide travelers up the steep bluff; on full moon nights, when its pull tugged hard enough to make her wrist throb, she left small offerings of sea salt and wild honey at the unmarked spots where waves had claimed loved ones decades before. 
Two weeks to the day after she arrived, the first traveler appeared at golden dusk: a man in his late 60s carrying a chipped ceramic coffee mug that had belonged to his son, a 22-year-old deckhand lost in a sudden 2001 squall, his work boots caked in red clay mud from the 40-mile walk from his inland home. 
Elara did not greet him with grand speeches or hollow platitudes about closure; she simply poured him a cup of bitter pine needle tea steeped over a small camp stove, pointed to the wind-bent bluff edge where the censer foot had tugged most fiercely that afternoon, and sat with him in quiet silence as the first bright stars pricked through the darkening indigo sky. 
She knew the rough waystation would never be a permanent structure, that the soft bluff would erode another three feet by the end of the decade, that the rusted radar dish would eventually tumble into the churning surf below, but that transience did not diminish its purpose. 
What mattered was that she was building a path that did not demand people outrun their grief, but invited them to walk straight toward it, a path lit by starlight and guided by dented bronze, carrying the quiet, unshakable solace of people coming home to the pieces of themselves they had left behind decades earlier.
