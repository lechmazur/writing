After the foghorn falls silent, the venerable oath scribe Thalyn climbs the last rusted fire escape rung to the ancient orchard garden atop the city’s tallest skyscraper, his wool robe brushing fronds of moss that have crept over the parapet since the coastal fog lifted three days prior. 
For seventy years, he has transcribed sworn promises and quiet griefs onto vellum treated with apple resin from the orchard’s gnarled trees, speaking three dead languages of promise and covenant to anchor each feeling so it does not dissolve into the city’s endless wind. 
His life’s work, the quiet charge passed down through twelve generations of scribes before him, is to archive emotional patterns that outlast empires, to capture the exact weight of a promise kept or a regret unspoken before memory erodes it entirely. 
Today he has brought the last unbroken wax cylinder from his collection, a recording made in 1927 of a lighthouse keeper swearing to return to his lover before the fog swallowed the shore. 
He sets his battered brass phonograph on a flat stone ledge under the oldest apple tree, its branches heavy with small, tart fruit that glows pink in the strange, fog-clear golden light that has bathed the city since the horn cut out. 
As he lowers the stylus, it catches on a faint crack he never noticed before, skittering across the wax to carve a thin, coiled wax cylinder phonograph shaving that curls onto the moss at his feet, glowing faintly with the imprint of the keeper’s voice. 
He bends to lift the delicate wax curl, and the touch pulls him unmoored, back via a curious third-grade field trip memory, when his class had climbed to this same orchard and his teacher had told him the stone basin at the tree’s roots held water that remembered every voice that had ever spoken over it. 
He crosses to the basin, its surface dappled with fallen apple blossoms, and leans down to immerse the wax shaving in the cool, still water, expecting the trapped fragment of the keeper’s oath to dissolve and release its emotional pattern to be transcribed in his vellum journal. 
The wax melts on contact, sending thin iridescent ripples across the basin, and instead of the lighthouse keeper’s rough, salt-strained tenor, he hears a voice woven from all the dead languages he has spoken his whole life, soft as apple blossom petal, speaking words he never wrote, never intended to speak. 
It tells him that the foghorn fell silent because the fog is gone for good, that the coming years will strip every new, untested promise from the city’s streets, that the seventy years of emotional patterns he archived on apple-resin vellum are not a record of the past, but the only seed bank of human commitment left to rebuild when the last scouring winds die down. 
He stumbles back a step, catching himself on the apple tree’s rough bark, because he had come only to tuck one more small grief into his archive, not to become the vessel for a warning no one had asked him to carry. 
He glances down at the basin, where the last wisp of wax dissolves, and catches a flash of his own eight-year-old face, wide-eyed, holding the crumpled crayon drawing of a lighthouse he had dropped into the water on that long-ago field trip, as if the child had been waiting all those years to pass the message along. 
The phonograph spins on, silent now that the wax is gouged too deep to track, and the air smells like ripe apple and brine, a scent that should feel like home but instead settles over him like velvet lined with thin, cold pins. 
He had spent his whole life believing he was a curator of what had already been, a keeper of old words and faded feelings, never guessing that a misaligned stylus, a half-forgotten childhood trip, and a single curl of wax would turn his archive into the first text of a world he would not live to see. 
He reaches for his vellum journal, his fingers steady despite the cold prickle under his skin, and begins to write, not as a scribe of the past, but as the unplanned witness to what will come.
