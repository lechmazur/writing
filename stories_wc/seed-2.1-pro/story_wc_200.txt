In the hush of a hidden sunrise, where pale tangerine light seeped only through narrow cracks in Echo Gorge’s striated sandstone walls, the lost soul collector knelt to brush crumbly lichen from the weathered stone ledge that held her most trusted tools. 
Modestly fearless, she wore no sweeping shadow cloak or scythe of cold iron, just scuffed canvas boots caked in gorge mud, a frayed wool shawl stitched with wildflower motifs, and a polished crocodile's tooth strung on worn leather around her throat. 
Her work did not involve dragging screaming souls to some distant, fiery judgment, but holding space for spectral confessions, the unspoken weights that pinned restless dead to the gorge’s stone walls for decades or centuries, looping the same painful memory on repeat until their essence frayed to thin wind. 
For generations, collectors before her had treated these confessions as transactions, tallying sins and extracting remorse to earn a soul passage, but she carried a quiet, unshakable motivation: to integrate laughter into healing, because no truth is whole without the bright, messy joy that ran alongside even the sharpest grief. 
That morning, she had come to pursue a soul that had evaded her for three moons: Elspeth, a 17-year-old ferry operator who had slipped on the gorge’s wet docks in 1952, convinced she had knocked her 10-year-old brother into the crocodile-haunted pools below as she fell. 
Elspeth had run rather than speak her guilt, darting off the path of stone and memory into the only escape route the gorge offered to fleeing spirits: through the distorted notes of a misaligned harp, left wedged between two 300-foot spires by a heartbroken prospector in 1898, its wooden pegs warped by decades of mist so every wind-tugged note bent, doubled back, and wove a disorienting labyrinth of sound that swallowed anyone who entered without an anchor. 
That anchor was the crocodile’s tooth at her throat, given to her 20 years prior by Elspeth’s brother, then a gray-haired man of 72 who had hiked the gorge to leave an offering for the sister he had spent 60 years missing, telling her the story of how he had caught himself on a rock ledge 10 feet down, how a curious young crocodile had nipped the toe of his boot before slipping away, how he had climbed back to the docks calling for his sister only to find she had vanished into the current. 
Stepping to the edge of the ledge, she closed her eyes and let the warped harp notes wrap around her, the thrumming sound warbling so sharply it made her teeth ache as she stepped into the sound labyrinth, no weapon, no grand charm, only the steady weight of the tooth and the echo of the brother’s laugh, rough and warm as sunbaked pine, when he had described pulling the loose tooth from the crocodile’s abandoned basking spot a week after the accident as a silly trophy to give his sister when he found her. 
She found Elspeth huddled in a fold of twisted sound, her translucent form shivering as she replayed the split second of the slip on loop, whispering the same confession over and over that she had killed the brother she loved. 
The collector did not interrupt, did not rush her to finish the litany of guilt, just sat beside her in the humming dark of the sound labyrinth and held up the crocodile tooth, letting the memory of the brother’s long life, his college graduation, his messy wedding, the decades he spent telling silly stories of his "crocodile battle" to his own kids and grandkids, the laugh he never lost, unspool slow as sunrise between them. 
When Elspeth’s ghost finally laughed, a wobbly, bright sound that cut through the harp’s distorted warble, her spectral confession settled soft as dandelion fluff, no longer a crushing weight but a glowing bridge, and the collector smiled, knowing healing was never about erasing the sharp edge of grief, but letting laughter curl around it, light enough to carry both of them home into the growing sunrise.
