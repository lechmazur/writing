The bewildered curator of the Outer System Archive blinked against petal-drift as she stepped onto the lonely asteroid where a fabled astral orchard had bloomed overnight, its boughs heavy with translucent star-fruit that glowed like melted pearl. 
She had defied Archive Council edicts to make the trip, driven by a decades-long urge to pursue forbidden knowledge about the legacy of the archive's unknown founder, a line of inquiry every senior staffer had warned would unravel the carefully ordered linearity of their collection. 
Her journey had fallen in the narrow, ungoverned window between the first and last page of the archive's founding codex, a gap no audit had ever managed to map, where records blurred into the soft, unwritten edges of collective memory. 
The orchard itself was simply complicated: no gnarled trunks or tangled undergrowth, just evenly spaced boughs hanging in vacuum without root or soil, each blossom unfurling to show a tiny, flickering snippet of a life that had never been lived, or had been lived a thousand times across tangled timelines. 
She had spent three days in transit to the asteroid, and every pivotal shift in her understanding of what she might find had come not from poring over redacted mission logs, but from having philosophical breakthroughs in bathrooms, the only sealed, unmonitored space on her tiny scout craft where she could talk through paradoxes out loud without triggering Council listening devices. 
It was in that tiny lav, mid-way through the trip, that she had first realized the archive's fabled "inheritance" was not a linear handoff from founder to successor across generations, but a nonlinear inheritance, looping back on itself so that heirs might leave gifts for their own predecessors long before either was born. 
When she reached the orchard's central bough, where a single unopened blossom hung larger than the rest, she did not reach for it immediately, but paused to inquire of the soft, humming air what exactly the founder had intended to leave for the person brave enough to cross the Council's lines. 
The blossom unfurled at the sound of her voice, and resting in its velvet center was not a glittering relic or a book of locked secrets, but a tiny, smooth pistachio shell cameo blank, its curved surface uncarved, waiting for a portrait that had not yet been chosen. 
A wave of brightened disquiet washed over her as she lifted the shell, the petal-drift catching the faint light of distant stars to paint prisms across her suit visor, because she suddenly understood the truth the Council had hidden for centuries: she was not the founder's heir, but the founder herself. 
The nonlinear loop snapped into place then, the pieces of the simply complicated orchard clicking into a pattern she could trace without a map: she would return to the archive, build the collection she had spent her whole life curating, place the codex with its intentional gap between first and last page, and eventually leave the pistachio shell cameo blank for her younger self to find, waiting for her to carve her own face into its surface when the loop closed. 
She thought back to her last bathroom breakthrough, 12 hours earlier, when she had ranted to her own reflection that the Council's ban on knowledge of the founder was designed to keep curators too scared to ask who had built the systems they served, and laughed at the irony that the answer had been her all along. 
The disquiet in her chest softened, brightened by the glow of the blossoms around her, as she tucked the cameo blank into the inner pocket of her suit, no longer bewildered by the gaps in the archive's records but grateful for them. 
She did not need a carved cameo to know her place in the loop, because the blank shell itself was the point: forbidden knowledge was never a set of facts to be hoarded, but a space to carve out for yourself, across every twist of nonlinear time, even if you had to leave the map for your younger self to find.
