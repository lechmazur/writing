The mirror maker stepped into the ruined orchard district lost in centuries of thick fog, her boots crunching thin rime the moment twilight frost spread its first shimmer across gnarled, fruitless boughs. 
Unlike the fog-dazed residents who drifted through half-remembered days chasing snatches of half-formed longing, she was firmly grounded, her calloused hands carrying the weight of every pane she had ever poured, polished, and mounted to hold light steady rather than let it slip. 
Tucked in the inner pocket of her wool coat sat the astronomer’s broken sextant, its brass arc bent, its sight glass spiderwebbed with cracks the old scholar had despaired of fixing the day the fog rolled in 300 years prior, swallowing the stars that had once anchored every traveler’s path. 
She had come to capture a runaway dream: the astronomer’s last vision of a constellation-aligned orchard heavy with frost-glowing pears, the dream that had slipped out of his sleeping mind the moment the fog erased the line between waking and sleeping, between memory and mist, between what was lost and what could be found. 
For centuries, the fog’s sloppy erasing of boundaries had left the district hollow: trees forgot how to set fruit, residents forgot the faces of their loved ones, even the wind forgot which direction it was supposed to blow to carry the scent of ripening fruit down to the river valley. 
She did not come to fight the fog, or to chase the dream through drifting mist like so many before her; she came to recreate the sextant not as a tool of rigid measurement, but as a mirror that could hold the same light the dream carried, so it would have a place to land instead of running forever. 
She knelt on a patch of frost-crusted moss between two gnarled apple trunks, laid the broken sextant on her spread canvas apron, and began to breathe in slow, even cycles, matching her inhales to the faint thrum of the dream she could feel drifting just beyond the thickest bank of fog, her exhales syncing to the slow spread of frost crystals across the sextant’s cracked sight glass. 
The air took on the sharp, luminous clarity of glass mountains as her breathing steadied, no longer separate from the fog, the frost, the dream, or the slumbering memory of the orchard’s old fruit-laden boughs. 
This was the gentle, intentional work of erasing boundaries: not dissolving all things into a shapeless mist, but letting the hard, sharp lines between maker and object, dream and waking, past and future, soften just enough to let light pass through without bending away. 
When her breath matched the dream’s pulse exactly, the cracks in the sextant’s sight glass filled with a thin, flawless layer of mirrored silver, the bent brass arc straightening without heat or hammer, held in place only by the shared rhythm of all the things the fog had spent centuries pulling apart. 
The runaway dream drifted down then, no longer running, because it no longer hit a hard wall between its own glowing light and the solid world of moss and bark and frost; it slid straight into the mirrored surface of the remade sextant, carrying with it the scent of ripe pears, the glint of long-vanished stars, the quiet echo of the astronomer’s laugh when he first mapped the constellations above the orchard as a boy. 
As a mirror maker who had always been firmly grounded, she did not let the dream’s glow sweep her away into misty nostalgia; she lifted the remade sextant to her eye, aligned its sight to the faint glimmer of starlight now cutting through the fog, and watched as the first tiny pear bud pushed through the bark of the nearest gnarled branch, proof that erased boundaries did not have to mean loss. 
The fog thinned just enough to let the frost’s shimmer carry across the whole district, the air holding that same crisp, unclouded resonance of high glass peaks, as the first dream-caught light touched every dormant root waiting under the soil to remember how to grow.
