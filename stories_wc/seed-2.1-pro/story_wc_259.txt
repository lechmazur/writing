Beneath the wind-hollowed sandstone vaults of the lost civilization archive, the saffron-veil historian ran a calloused finger along a shelf where papyrus scrolls had dissolved to fine, tawny silt nearly a millennium prior. 
She had trekked 270 miles across cracked alkali flats and thorn-choked canyons to pursue forbidden knowledge, the unredacted record of the world before the ruling theocracy seized power, across the end of innocence when communities traded shared warmth for suspicion and ordered fear. 
The archive’s inner corridors were steadily unpredictable, shifting stone slabs and rearranging shelf bays every 19 breaths to repel intruders who relied on memorized routes or repetitive, predictable movement. 
She knew from tattered marginalia left by the last archivist that the only way to reach the central vault without triggering a cave-in was by avoiding patterns, never placing her weight on the same floor tile twice, never humming the same note for more than a second, never letting her gaze rest on a single carving long enough to form a familiar habit. 
Three hours of careful, errant movement brought her to the vault’s core just as honeyed gold light slanted through a high, cracked skylight, illuminating the bronze prism pagoda at twilight, its tiered facets catching the fading sun to throw shards of tangerine and indigo across the bare stone walls. 
The pagoda was no mere relic, the marginalia had explained, but a resonant vessel holding the atoms of nostalgia, tiny, indestructible fragments of every shared laugh, lullaby, quiet meal, and gentle goodbye that the theocracy had tried to scrub from collective memory when it burned villages and rewrote history to center obedience and fear. 
For generations, those atoms had been tainted by the theocracy’s psychic wards, twisted to evoke only shame and regret for the pre-authoritarian past, so that even people who stumbled on faint echoes of old joy would recoil, convinced the memories were sinful delusions. 
Her task, the old notes had promised, was to purify those atoms, stripping away the taint of coercion so they could drift out of the archive on desert winds, seeping into the dreams of children and elders alike to rekindle the collective warmth the regime had stolen. 
The wards layered around the pagoda fed on rigid ritual and repetitive intent, so the same commitment to avoiding patterns that had carried her through the corridors would be the only force strong enough to dissolve their hold, no chants, no prescribed gestures, no fixed sequence of thought allowed. 
She set a palm lightly against the pagoda’s coolest bronze facet, letting her mind drift without focus, skipping from the memory of her grandmother’s date bread to the sound of rain on her childhood tent to the sharp, bright thrill of climbing a canyon wall at 16, never lingering long enough on any one thought to give the wards a hook to grab. 
Slowly, the taint burned away, and the atoms of nostalgia began to swirl up through the pagoda’s prisms, carrying snippets of sound and scent that made her chest ache with equal parts grief and glowing, unnameable delight, the sharp duality of anguished joy that comes from recovering something you were sure was gone forever. 
She caught a whiff of jasmine from a garden that had been paved over 800 years prior, heard the trill of a songbird hunted to extinction for its bright feathers, felt the weight of a child’s small hand in hers belonging to a lineage the theocracy had erased entirely, and tears tracked through the dust on her cheeks as she smiled. 
She knew the atoms would drift for months, touching people who had no idea why they suddenly ached for a home they had never seen, why they found themselves humming half-remembered tunes, why they reached for the hand of a stranger as if they had known them all their lives. 
The end of innocence had stretched for a thousand brutal years, but as the last of the sun dipped below the horizon and the first cool evening wind carried the glowing, unseeable atoms out through the skylight, she knew the long, slow work of remembering had finally begun.
