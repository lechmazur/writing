The vineyard hush of the umbral grotto above the clouds wrapped around Elowen, the grotto’s committed caretaker, three days after she had forgiven the unforgivable. 
For twelve years, she had carried a sharp, cold weight of anger for the logger whose careless clearcut on the lower slope had triggered the mudslide that killed her young ward, Lila, and she had long believed letting that anger go would unlock the quiet, steady contentment she had spent decades seeking. 
Kindly decisive by nature, she did not wallow in the restlessness that lingered after the logger, frail and gray, had stumbled up the mountain with his apology, choosing instead to lean on the old ritual that had always cleared her crowded thoughts: climbing trees to think better. 
Before she began her climb up the gnarled trunk of the grotto’s oldest cloud cedar, she paused at the terraced moss beds that fed the grotto’s sacred pool, leaning down with a smooth river stone to scallop the neat, curved edges of each bed, tucking stray strands of silver-flecked memory moss back into the dark peat and plucking invasive grasses that would siphon nutrients from the plants anchoring the grotto’s work. 
She pulled the antique hairpin the logger had returned from her pocket then, running a thumb over its carved jet surface and inlaid mother-of-pearl ivy, the very piece Lila had worn every summer day to pin her unruly auburn curls back while she hunted for wild cloudberries along the grotto’s ledges. 
The man had dug the pin from the thick mud of the slide twelve years prior, carrying it in the inner pocket of his flannel shirt through every logging season, every quiet, guilt-ridden night, until he could summon the courage to climb the mountain and face the harm he had caused. 
She twisted the thick silver braid coiled at her nape, securing it with the hairpin before reaching for the first low, sturdy cedar branch, her bare feet finding the familiar worn handholds she had used since she was a girl of seven, first taken in by the grotto’s previous caretaker. 
As she climbed, the thin, pine-scented wind carried the soft, resonant hum of the grotto’s central pool, where the cycle of rebirth turned slow and unhurried, catching the essence of every living thing that passed on the mountain, filtering it through moss and root and stone, and sending it trickling down to the valleys below to emerge as new fern fronds, spotted fawns, and laughing children with berry-stained fingers. 
She had tended that cycle for forty-two years, watching swallows return to the same nests, trilliums push through the same patches of loam, children grow into elders who were carried back to the mountain on pine-strewn biers, and she had always assumed contentment was a distant spring she would one day stumble across, once she checked every last box of virtue: forgiving fully, tending perfectly, letting go of every last scrap of grief. 
When she reached the highest bough, thick enough to hold her weight and cushioned with decades of soft, shed pine needles, she sat with her legs dangling over the edge, watching the sun gild the rolling sea of clouds far below the grotto’s ledges, the hairpin cool against her scalp where it held her braid fast. 
It was there, breathing in the sharp sweetness of pine and distant ripening blackberries, that she realized she had been searching for the source of contentment as if it were a thing to be found at the end of a hard climb, rather than the sum of the small, gentle choices that made up every ordinary day. 
It was the slow turn of the rebirth cycle below her, never rushing to punish or reward, just turning; it was the neatly scalloped edges of the moss beds she had tended that morning, the worn handholds in the cedar bark, the weight of the old hairpin tying her to the girl she had loved and lost, the quiet choice to let a tired old man walk down the mountain free of the weight he had carried for half his life. 
A soft breeze stirred the cedar boughs, carrying the faint, honeyed scent of clover from the valleys far below, and she smiled, no longer searching, because she had finally found what she had spent decades looking for, right in the quiet hush of the life she had already been tending all along.
