The budding rune scholar Lira climbed the last weathered iron rung to the nocturnal observatory on a floating isle three weeks after spoons bend reality, the chaotic mage-botched ritual that melted flatware, warped doorframes, and unspooled half the known laws of light and shadow across the sky archipelago. 
Quietly deliberate in every movement, she brushed wind-tousled thistledown and flecks of crystallized starlight from her wool robes before unlatching the observatory’s oak door, careful not to jostle the dented brass case slung across her back. 
Her core motivation, the question that had pulled her up three thousand feet of frayed rope and wind-buffed stone, was simple: to find where shadows go at noon. 
In the weeks after the ritual, every midday had stretched bright and flat, no dark edge clinging to fence posts, no dappled shade under oak boughs, no silhouette cast when a person held a hand up to the sun. 
Senior scholars had written the phenomenon off as trivial reality bleed, but Lira knew the missing shadows were a sign of sealed awakenings: slumbering entities locked out of the world by old rune wards, now tugging loose the threads of perception to slip back in. 
She set her brass case on the observatory’s stone workbench, flipped the latches, and lifted out the only tool that could mend the fracture: a clockwork automaton key, its teeth etched with the same looping runes she had spent two years memorizing from crumbling archive manuscripts. 
The key did not turn locks, but wound back misaligned moments, the small tears in reality left when cause and effect slipped out of sequence. 
Lira had come to the observatory to relearn the old ward logic her professors had dismissed as outdated, the rune patterns that once held the boundary between waking world and slumbering shadow realms perfectly taut. 
She knew from marginalia in the oldest manuscripts that mending reality after a warp could not be done with grand spells or shouted incantations, but only by closing loops: tying every loose, unmoored effect back to its original cause, one tiny, deliberate adjustment at a time. 
For the next six hours, as constellations wheeled slow over the observatory’s domed glass roof, she worked by the light of a single guttering tallow candle, matching every disjointed memory she had collected from village residents to the tiny, whirring gears set into the observatory’s orrery, which had spun backward since the ritual. 
She closed the loop of the farmer who remembered his wheat ripening before he planted it, touching the key to a gear etched with a stalk of grain until the orrery’s arm for the harvest season clicked into its proper forward rotation. 
She closed the loop of the weaver who found finished tapestries on her loom before she had dyed her first spool of thread, adjusting a gear marked with spindle and thread until the orrery’s slow spin steadied, no longer stuttering between past and future. 
Each small click of the key tightened the frayed boundary between worlds, pushing back against the sealed awakenings that had siphoned shadows away to carve cracks for their entry. 
When the first pale edge of dawn pinked the horizon, Lira found the final loose loop: her own childhood memory of sitting on this very observatory’s steps, watching her grandmother trace runes of protection into the stone, a lesson she had forgotten in the rush of formal classes that prioritized flashy magic over quiet, steady warding. 
She pressed the clockwork key to the final gear, the one etched with the silhouette of a girl and her grandmother, and felt the last thread of misaligned reality snap back into place. 
When the sun climbed high enough to hang directly overhead, she leaned over the observatory’s stone parapet and looked down, smiling as her own sharp, dark shadow stretched long across the floating isle’s thistle-covered slopes, proof the shadows had returned from the narrow gap between worlds where they had been trapped. 
She tucked the key back into its brass case, already drafting notes for the archive that would frame sealed awakenings not as threats to be fought, but as misaligned loops to be gently, patiently closed, one careful choice at a time.
