Lira, the reckless idealist who tended the catacomb orchard where tree roots snake between bones, moved only within the glow of half-lit corridors, her boots pressing soft on loam mixed with fragmented rib and weathered knuckle. 
She was fiercely gentle, calloused fingers brushing gnarled root crowns as she checked each fruit for the faint, thrumming pulse of unclaimed fate left behind by travelers who had bartered destinies for cheap victories, quick wealth, or hollow revenge in the market above. 
Her sole motivation was to patch holes in the soul, the ragged gaps left when people traded away the soft, unglamorous parts of their own life paths for shiny rewards that curdled within a year, taking with them the capacity for quiet joy, ability to grieve fully, and instinct to trust. 
She practiced her craft by inlaying meaning everywhere, tucking pressed clover into root crevices to mark small moments of grace, carving tiny runes for belonging into fruit skins, pressing lost buttons and faded letters into the orchard loam so those discarded memories could feed the roots that wove between the long-buried dead. 
Most folk above thought her work reckless, a fool's errand that risked unleashing tangled fates that could trammel entire villages if a root slipped and spilled its hoard of unclaimed longing, but Lira knew the greater danger lay in letting those hollow soul holes yawn open wide enough to swallow whole lives. 
That evening, a girl no older than sixteen stumbled down the corridor steps, her sleeves smudged with candle soot, her chest gaping with a soul hole so wide Lira could see the faint glint of the fate market's silver chains tangled in the gap, left after she traded her own future as a weaver of story tapestries to pay for her little brother's fever medicine. 
Lira guided her to sit on a flat stone half-buried in loam, and pulled from her leather pouch the burned tarot card she had carried for seven years, its edges singed black, the faint outline of The World still visible beneath the char, the last fragment of her own sister's destiny, lost when she traded it for a chance to pull Lira from a frozen river as a child. 
The card hummed, warm against her palm, for it held not just loss, but the quiet weight of the love that had driven that trade, a meaning Lira had inlaid into every fiber of the charred paper through years of tucking it next to pressed wildflowers, whispering her sister's favorite lullabies to it, holding it while she listened to every traveler's tale of regret. 
She pressed the edge of the card to the girl's chest, letting that hum seep into the ragged soul hole, not to give back the traded future, but to stitch a new edge to the gap so it would not fester, so the girl could weave new stories from the pieces she had left, free of the hollow hunger that made so many trade again and again until nothing remained. 
The dusky resolve that had carried her through seven years of cold corridors and skeptical village elders settled soft over her shoulders as she watched the girl's breath even out, the gap in her chest closing to a thin, silvery scar that glowed faintly in the guttering torchlight. 
When the girl had climbed back up the stone steps to the world above, Lira tucked the tarot card back into her pouch, and knelt to tuck a fallen apple seed into the loam right where the girl had sat, pressing it down next to a shard of polished bone so a new root would grow there, fed by the quiet courage of the choice that had brought the girl down to the orchard. 
She knew she could never undo the tangled web of bartered destinies that stretched from the market above down through the bones and roots, but every small stitch, every fragment of inlaid meaning, every soul patched whole was a quiet rebellion against the idea that fates were fixed, even when the corridors dimmed and the gnarled roots shifted soft around her boots.
