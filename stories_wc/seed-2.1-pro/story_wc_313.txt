Twilight pooled soft and gunmetal-gray over the midnight ridgeway, the last tremor of a cracked copper wind chime hung from a gnarled white pine falling silent ten full seconds before the vigilant levee warden shifted her weight from the scuffed steel toe of her waterproof work boot. 
The air held a mirrored hush, the drawn-down surface of the valley reservoir so still it reflected the first pricks of emerging starlight without a single ripple, framing the cracked asphalt and crumbled foundation edges of the long-drowned town that had spent 66 years under 40 feet of cold mountain water. 
The warden was known across the valley as precisely humane: she logged every millimeter of levee seepage in waterproof ink, calibrated her slope measurements to within a hundredth of a degree, and turned a deliberate blind eye to the small groups of former residents and their descendants who slipped past the temporary chain-link to walk the exposed streets, leaving crumpled wildflowers on old porch steps where their families once lived. 
She had not taken the post to enforce arbitrary bureaucratic rules, but to spin stories into shared meaning, pushing back against the 1957 state engineering report that had tried to expunge every trace of the town’s tight-knit cultural life to justify flooding the valley, framing displaced families as transient, unrooted laborers with no lasting ties to the land. 
On this particular twilight walk, her boot scuffed the edge of a curled, silt-caked photograph half-buried in the old main street’s cracked macadam, its faded surface showing a teen girl in a gingham dress holding a jar of wild blueberries, grinning at the camera. 
Ten feet further on, she found a second photo, then a third, each placed as if left deliberately: a shot of two boys perched on the general store’s porch rail holding stringers of glistening trout, a blurry snapshot of a square dance in the town hall, a polaroid of rows of glass bottles lined up on a wooden shelf, glowing blue in sunset light. 
The trail ended at the crumbled concrete foundation of the old general store, where a shard of hand-blown glass jutted from the silt, a perfectly preserved sapphire-hued liqueur label stuck fast to its curved surface, its gold lettering reading "Haven's Rest Blueberry Cordial, Batch 1956." 
For a long, quiet beat, the warden stared at the label, a trail of photographs leading to a forgotten memory she had not accessed since she was seven years old, sitting on her grandmother’s porch listening to stories of the town the water had swallowed. 
The memory unfolded soft and clear: her grandmother, 16 at the time the flood gates closed, had hidden a full bottle of that very cordial under the general store’s floorboards, planning to dig it up the following spring to share at her older sister’s wedding, never guessing the water would rise so fast, or that officials would work so hard to erase every sign the town had ever existed. 
She knelt slowly, running a gloved finger along the edge of the label, careful not to smudge the faded gold ink, and thought of the community potluck she had helped organize for the coming weekend, where descendants of the town’s residents would bring dishes pulled from handed-down recipes, share old letters and keepsakes, and walk the resurfaced streets together without fear of being turned away. 
She did not take the label or the photographs with her when she stood to continue her levee inspection, instead marking each spot with a small, smooth river stone so visitors would know to pause there, letting the fragments of the lost town speak for themselves rather than locking them away in a climate-controlled archive far from the people who cared about them. 
Somewhere far above, a faint gust of wind stirred the pine boughs, but the chime stayed quiet, the reservoir holding its mirrored hush as the stars came out, as if the town itself was leaning in, ready to finally have its stories told out loud, together.
