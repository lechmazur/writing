Clutching her scuffed cello case to her chest, the part-time orchestra pit musician picked her way across warped lobby floorboards three days after the storm that had swelled the river and breached the old opera house's foundation. 
She had strung battery-powered flickering lanterns from sagging box seat railings the night before, sick of waiting for insurance adjusters who treated the gilded, 120-year-old space like a write-off instead of the place where she had first learned to listen for the quiet magic tucked between notes. 
Amid the hush of unstruck chords, with no conductor's baton raised and no audience murmuring behind velvet curtains, the cold, calf-deep floodwater lapped at the cuffs of her waterproof boots as she waded toward the sunken pit, where her music stand still jutted crookedly above the surface. 
Lately inspired by the old stagehands' tales of a 1920s composer who had vanished mid-premiere, leaving no final score and only whispers of a hidden message for the first musician sharp enough to find it, she had begun sneaking into the condemned building every dawn, chasing a hunch that felt less like fancy and more like an invitation. 
Her goal was simple: to unravel the code left by a ghost, a composer named Elias Voss who had written his final opera about the liminal, in between spaces where sound lingers long after the last note fades, where grief and joy twist into something too soft to name. 
She had nearly given up the search that morning when her boot knocked against a waterlogged leather binder half-buried in silt beneath the pit, its cover embossed with the opera house's gilded lyre logo. 
When she fished it out and pried it open, she found it held a single antique opera program, its pages bloated and warped from three days of sitting in cold floodwater. 
At first she thought the pages were blanked out by mud, but when she tilted one toward the nearest lantern, she spotted water damage creating images across the thick cotton stock, faint sepia shapes bleeding together like half-developed photographs. 
The first image was a tiny sketch of the orchestra pit, with a tiny X marked on the exact floorboard where she stood, cello case slung over one shoulder. 
The next pages held staves of music, not printed ink but mineral deposits and faded ink leached from the program's original advertisements, arranged in the exact key of the lost final aria Voss had never gotten to stage. 
She did not gasp or call out, knowing the hush demanded a wordless conversation, one musician to another across a hundred years of silences, no spoken thanks needed because he had left the score exactly where someone who listened for the gaps between notes would find it. 
She was so focused on tracing the notes with a water-wrinkled finger that she almost missed the low creak of a sagging ceiling panel overhead, which dislodged a shower of crumbled old plaster that dropped straight into the floodwater to disrupt the glassy, silent surface and send concentric ripples lapping at the program's frayed edges. 
She lifted the program clear of the lapping ripples, her eyes lifting to sweep the flooded opera house lit by flickering lanterns, where shadowed box seats looked like rows of quiet guests waiting for the first note to ring out. 
She did not startle, because she knew the in between spaces did not give up their secrets without a small, playful test, a reminder that nothing precious was ever handed over without a little risk. 
She held the program tight to her chest, wading slowly back toward the lobby stairs where she had left her waterproof notebook, already mentally mapping the rich cello line that would anchor the lost aria when the opera house reopened, when the water was pumped out and the velvet curtains were rehung, when the chords finally struck for an audience that had waited a hundred years to listen. 
A soft, cool breeze curled through the broken lobby window, carrying the faint scent of river mud and jasmine from the vines climbing the outer wall, and she smiled, knowing the ghost's code had not been a puzzle to solve for glory, but an invitation to meet him in the quiet gap between silence and song.
