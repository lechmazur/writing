High above the tattered quilt of low-hanging storm clouds, the opal library curved like a frozen soap bubble, its iridescent walls catching the permanent gold of stratospheric sunset. 
Adjoined to the library’s curved outer wall stretched the museum of extinct feelings, across which the glass-orchid caretaker had tended her post for seventy unmeasured years. 
She was the sole steward of the deserted marvels stacked on floating obsidian shelves: pressed four-leaf clovers from first crushes no living person recalled, handwritten letters of a devotion so fierce it had burned out of collective memory, shards of laughter from summer evenings that had dissolved into the unremembered past before any living witness could carry them forward. 
Her routine was gently systematic: each shift, she walked the length of the museum’s wings, ran a soft silk cloth over the fragile, translucent glass orchids that anchored each sound archive, checked the vacuum seals on frosted glass memory jars, and cataloged the faint, fraying sounds that drifted through the wispy cloud vapors before they dissolved entirely into the upper atmosphere. 
She had taken the post as a sharp-eyed, soft-hearted girl of nineteen, driven by a quiet, unshakable motivation to preserve the sound of forgotten names: names of mountain villages washed away by glacial floods, names of children who had died before they could speak their own truths to anyone but their mothers, names of wild birds whose species had vanished so thoroughly no sketch or story remained to mark they had ever sung. 
Tucked on the highest, most wind-tousled shelf in the library’s sound archive, wrapped in a scrap of faded indigo cotton she had brought from her childhood home, was her most fragile charge: a wind chime bamboo tube cracked along its length, its smooth surface etched with tiny, faded characters that no modern linguistic archive could decode. 
For three slow months, she had watched the last faint, resonant hum seep out of the fine crack, knowing that if she did not intervene soon, the hundreds of names held inside the tube would drift up into the dark vacuum beyond the atmosphere and be lost forever, with no record left to prove they had ever been spoken. 
She had pored over the oldest opal-bound codex in the library’s restricted collection until her eyes stung, learning that a cracked sound vessel could never be repaired with glue or cord, and could only be recharged by the echo of a long-forgotten song, the kind that hummed in the hollows of the bones even when the conscious mind could not place its origin or its words. 
On the morning the last thread of hum thinned so much she could barely feel it against her palm when she held the tube, she carried it to the open arch of the library, where thin, cold cloud wind tangled in her silver braids, and knelt to light the small moss wick that sat in the chime’s hand-carved cedar cradle, a tool designed to catch faint echoes and weave them tight enough to seal cracked sound vessels for another century. 
As the wick glowed a soft, mossy green, she closed her eyes and let the song rise in her throat, half-memory, half-dream: a lullaby her grandmother had sung to her when she had fever as a small child, about fireflies dancing above rice paddies, about stone paths leading home, about the way bamboo chimes sang when the first monsoon wind rolled over the hills where her village once stood. 
For a long, breath-held moment, nothing happened, and she felt the familiar foxglove regret curl soft in her chest, that muted, purple-tinged sorrow that clung to every exhibit she passed on her daily rounds, the quiet grief of things half-remembered, almost lost, the sharp, soft knowledge that even the most careful stewardship could not stop every name from fading. 
Then the echo of the song curled around the cracked bamboo tube, seeping into the fine split in its surface like rain into parched earth, and slowly, a sound began to spill out, not the sharp, broken rattle she had feared, but a soft, clear chorus of names, each spoken as if by a familiar voice long thought gone. 
Among the names, she heard her grandmother’s, and the name of her village, which had been washed away in a flood when she was seven, and she smiled through quiet tears, knowing the chime would hold these names safe for another hundred years, carried on the high cloud winds, tended with the same soft, steady care she gave every glass orchid and half-forgotten memory in her keep.
