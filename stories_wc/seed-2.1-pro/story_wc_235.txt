The lucid dream chronicler kicked crusted pine snow off their scuffed patrol boots as they shouldered into the drafty mountain ranger cabin, 20 minutes out from the backcountry plot where they’d just stared down at their own moss-furred grave marker, chipped at the edges to show a death date 30 years prior. 
They were chronologically confused, a side effect of decades of crossing waking and dream thresholds so often the line between memory, premonition, and vivid imagination frayed thin enough to see starlight through. 
Tucked in the inner pocket of their frayed canvas coat was the birch bark scroll they’d carried for as long as they could hold a pen, its surface crammed with smudged ink fragments of dream sequences, half-remembered conversations, and snippets of sky seen from impossible heights. 
Their life’s work, the reason they chased half-formed dream images across state lines and mountain ranges, was to distill chaos into narrative, to stitch the disjointed flashes of past, future, and parallel lives into something a person could hold without flinching. 
Lately, though, the work had curdled into a quiet, sharp habit of getting jealous of past selves: the 19-year-old ranger who’d first claimed this cabin as a summer post, who’d never stumbled on his own grave, who’d thought dream-chasing was just a fancy word for stargazing from the roof. 
That feral nostalgia, the kind that gnaws at the edges of your ribs when you smell pine resin and wood smoke and realize you can never go back to the version of you that got to smell those things for the first time, had started bleeding into their dreams, too, turning controlled lucid sequences into messy, hungry tumults of memory. 
They pulled the scroll free, running a calloused thumb over the faint scratch marks the 19-year-old them had carved into its top edge, when a gust of wind slammed the cabin door shut and the world tilted, soft as a breath, into the lucid space they’d spent a lifetime learning to navigate. 
For a second, they expected the usual disjointed flashes: half-formed monsters, unfinished conversations, the quiet dread of finding their own grave again, but instead they felt their boots lift off the splintered cabin floor, light as dandelion fluff, and they began to soar, straight up through the cabin’s rotting shingle roof into the bright, cold mountain air above the treeline. 
Below them, they could see every version of themselves at once: the teen ranger laughing as he hauled a split log up the cabin steps, the exhausted 40-year-old chronicler collapsing on the same steps after a 12-mile hike, the weathered 72-year-old version of them they’d never get to be, kneeling to place a pinecone on their own grave. 
The old jealousy pricked at their throat for half a heartbeat, sharp and familiar, before it softened, because they could see now that none of those versions were gone, no matter how jumbled the timeline felt, no matter how many graves they stumbled across on backcountry trails. 
They fumbled for the charcoal stick they kept tucked behind their ear, holding the birch bark scroll steady against the wind as they began to write, not forcing the chaos of overlapping timelines into a neat, linear story, but letting each memory and premonition land on the page exactly as it was: the sting of cold teenage cheeks, the ache of middle-aged knees, the rough texture of pinecone against elderly palms, the thrill of soaring high above the pines where no grave could hold them. 
For years, they’d thought distilling chaos into narrative meant sanding down the rough edges, erasing the chronological confusion, tamping down the feral, gnawing nostalgia that made them jealous of every younger self that got to experience the mountain for the first time. 
They drifted slowly back down through the cabin roof, landing light-footed on the splintered floor as the last line of ink dried on the scroll, and felt the tight, weary knot in their chest loosen, just a little. 
They were still chronologically confused, still half-convinced the grave they’d seen was waiting for them just down the trail, still a little jealous of the kid who got to call this cabin his without knowing all the loss and wonder the decades would bring, but for the first time in years, that didn’t feel like a failure. 
The birch bark scroll, now heavy with the messy, overlapping truth of every version of themselves, was proof that narrative didn’t need to be neat to hold weight, that even the most feral, unruly nostalgia could be a thing worth carrying, as long as you let yourself soar high enough to see all the pieces at once.
