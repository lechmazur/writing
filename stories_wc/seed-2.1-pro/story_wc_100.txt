The weary undertaker, Elias Voss, had just stripped off his mud-caked wool coat after three straight days of reburying storm-tossed caskets when the mysterious package arrived, thudding soft as a held breath on his cottage porch. 
He pried the canvas wrapping open to find a jagged shard of the broken lighthouse lens, the same lens that had shattered 70 years prior when the head lighthouse keeper vanished mid-storm, leaving ships to founder on the rocks and the sunken, forgotten underwater city library half a mile offshore to claim their crews. 
Elias was inadvertently stubborn, a trait he mistook for simple dutifulness; for years he had chased stillness for the unquiet dead, only to watch high tides and gale winds tear their resting places loose, leaving lost souls to wail in the surf like cracked buoys. 
His deepest, unspoken motivation was to give rest a permanent address, a place no storm could reach, no tide could erode, where every lost sailor and coastal villager could stay untroubled for good. 
When the lens shard warmed in his palm and thrummed, pointing straight out to the patch of ocean where the sunken library lay, he did not hesitate to lash his small dinghy to the dock and row out, the shard glowing brighter as he neared the coordinates fishermen whispered held impossible spaces, pockets of air and preserved stone that defied water pressure, time, and all known laws of the deep. 
He slipped over the dinghy’s gunwale expecting icy, lung-crushing pressure, but the shard pulled him through a curtain of glowing plankton straight into the library’s vaulted main hall, where oak shelves still held leather-bound books, dust motes danced in slanted golden light, and the air smelled of old paper and dried sea lavender, no water in sight. 
For decades, the trapped souls of drowned sailors and washed-out villagers had sent faint, unheard distress signals, their pleas tangled in the current because no one had thought to look for a resting place outside the bounds of the shifting, unforgiving shore. 
The shard tugged him to the library’s central rotunda, where an empty iron frame waited, the original mount for the broken lighthouse lens, its curved prisms designed to cast light for miles in every direction. 
Elias realized he did not need to rebuild the whole lens from scratch; the impossible spaces the library occupied already held every fragment, scattered across shelves and tucked between book spines, pulled there over decades by the same quiet pull that had delivered the first shard to his porch. 
Over the course of three slow, quiet days, he gathered each shard, fitting them into the iron frame until the broken lighthouse lens was whole again, its surface glowing with the same soft blue light that had wrapped the package, no electricity or flame to feed its glow. 
When he slotted the final shard into place, the lens cast warm, steady light through every hall and stack of the forgotten library, and he felt the distress signals soften, no longer sharp with panic, as the space hummed to life to revitalize the long-held promise of shelter for anyone the land had refused to keep. 
He did not need to mark graves or carve headstones; the library’s shelves held space for every name, every memory, every half-told story the dead had carried with them when the tides took them, turning the impossible sunken space into the permanent address he had spent his whole career chasing. 
The chased stillness he had sought for so long settled over him too, heavy and soft as a wool shroud, as he realized he would not be returning to the cliffside cottage; there was no more need to rebury caskets or chase lost souls across eroding shorelines, when he could tend the library’s light and keep welcome for anyone who needed a place to stay. 
From the rotunda, the repaired lens cast its glow not just through the library halls, but up through the waves to the surface, a quiet beacon that no longer warned of jagged rocks, but promised that no lost soul, no matter how far it drifted, would ever be without a permanent, unshakable home again.
