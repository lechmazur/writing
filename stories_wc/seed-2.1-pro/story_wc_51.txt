After the first snowmelt, thin, cold rivulets of meltwater curled down the pocked, verdigris-crusted walls of the bronze grotto under starlight, finding the fractal weaver where she knelt on a mat woven from dried moss and shed caribou hair. 
The air hung in a shingled hush, each thin, overlapping layer of quiet laid flat as the slats on a coastal cottage roof, holding the thick, slow warmth of whalebone reverie that softened the sharp metal edge of every memory that drifted down through the grotto’s open mouth. 
She had traveled three hundred miles over thawing tundra to reach this spot, driven by the quiet, unshakable motivation to collect the weights of different burdens left by travelers, lovers, and grieving folk who could no longer carry the sharp, unyielding pull of unprocessed sorrow on their own. 
Resting at her knee, dented from decades of being tucked into her pack, sat the kerosene lamp burner whose steady, gold flame heated the small iron crucible she used to melt down fractured bits of guilt, regret, and unspoken apology into threads fine enough to weave into the fractal tapestries she strung across the grotto’s back wall. 
Most of these burdens arrived boundary-lessly trapped, drifting like dandelion fluff across the tundra after snowmelt, no clear start or end to their weight, sticking to coat cuffs and boot soles and the edges of dreams until they threatened to pull unsuspecting carriers off the narrow, thawing trails into boggy hollows. 
She knew from years of practice that she could not simply yank these drifting weights out of the air to render them harmless; by orienting properly, turning each shard of sorrow until its light-catching facet aligned with the slant of starlight coming through the grotto’s vent, she could separate the sharp, cutting edge of pain from the soft, learned lesson that lived at its core. 
The sharp, unneeded edges, the parts that made the burdens stick and fester, would calcify when held in the kerosene burner’s gentle heat, hardening into tiny, smooth, pearl-like beads light enough to string into the fractal patterns of her weavings, where they caught starlight and cast tiny, swirling rainbows across the bronze walls. 
She worked slowly that first night after melt, her fingers calloused from decades of pulling thread and holding sharp shards, pausing every few minutes to adjust the lamp burner’s wick so the flame stayed steady, never hot enough to scorch the fragile threads she pulled from the crucible. 
The first burden she processed that night belonged to a young trapper who had left his grandmother’s favorite knife embedded in an ice floe three winters prior, his guilt so formless it had drifted for months, weighing down every step he took on his trapline. 
She turned the wisp of guilt slowly in the starlight, orienting it until its soft, silver edge caught the light, then held it over the lamp flame until the sharp, self-castigating edges calcified into a single opalescent bead, leaving a thin, silken thread that held only the trapper’s warm memory of his grandmother teaching him to sharpen that very knife when he was seven. 
She strung that bead onto her growing tapestry, tucking the silken memory thread into a branching fractal pattern that mirrored the way meltwater trickled down rock faces, the way pine boughs split into smaller and smaller needles, the way stars clustered in tight, swirling groups across the dark spring sky. 
By midnight, she had collected a dozen small weights: a teacher’s regret for speaking too harshly to a quiet student, a traveler’s guilt for leaving home without saying goodbye, a child’s sorrow over a lost fox kit, each separated from its sharp, festering edge by the same slow, careful orientation to the starlight. 
The shingled hush around her deepened as the night wore on, each processed burden adding another thin, soft layer of quiet to the grotto, until even the plink of meltwater on stone seemed to slow, matching the unhurried rhythm of her breath and the steady, warm glow of the kerosene flame. 
She did not keep the light, warm memory threads for herself, instead letting them drift back out of the grotto on faint eddies of cold spring air, light enough now to follow the meltwater trickles down to the people who had once carried them, unburdened, soft, ready to be held without pain. 
Wrapped in that slow, whalebone reverie, she leaned back against the cool bronze wall, watching starlight catch the calcified beads on her tapestry, and waited for the next boundary-less wisp of burden to drift down from the thawing tundra above, already knowing exactly how to turn it toward the light.
