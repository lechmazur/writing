The weathered clapboard farmhouse hunkered in the constant, thrumming shadow of three giant wind turbines, their slow-turning blades chopping sunlight into strobing slants across the unkempt yard two years after the federal happiness mandate took effect. 
Its sole resident, a blank-faced performer who had left the state's mandatory cheer tour six months prior, did not register the strobing light as he sat on the splintered front porch step, a block of soft pine balanced on his denim-clad knee. 
He had returned to the farmhouse after his younger sister's fatal fall from a turbine service ladder, carrying nothing but the tattered suitcase of tour props and the dried flower bouquet she had left pressed between the pages of her high school poetry collection, tied with frayed jute from the farm's old hay baler. 
He carried a quiet, unpayable debt that made his presence morally ambiguous: during his final three months on the tour, he had reported three nearby farming families for unapproved displays of sorrow to reduce the lien the state had placed on the farm after his sister's turbine accident left her unable to work for six months. 
His sole, quiet aim in returning was to cultivate lightness in grief, not the state-mandated performative joy that earned tax credits, but a weightless, unjudged softness that did not require him to pretend his sister was merely gone on a long trip. 
He pulled a sharp, pocket-sized whittling knife from his jacket and began to whittle thin, curling shavings from the pine block, leaning into the stage-honed skill he had used for years on the cheer tour: dramatically narrating mundane tasks as if they were epic, world-shaping events to draw audiences out of their quiet despair. 
His voice, flat and stripped of the overbright cheer he had projected for tour audiences, did not lift or waver as he narrated, the constant low thrum of the turbines swallowing most of the sound before it could carry past the porch rail. 
He described each drag of the knife as a heroic act of clearing: first carving a shallow notch to cradle the dried flower bouquet, then smoothing the wood's edges until no splinter remained to catch on the brittle, faded petals of clover and black-eyed Susan his sister had picked the week before her accident. 
No tears pricked his eyes, no tightness pulled at his throat; the emotional void he had honed over years of performing forced joy for state inspectors settled over him like a well-worn blanket, soft and unyielding. 
This was the remade hush he had come seeking: not the total silence of an empty field before the turbines were built, when he and his sister would lie in the clover and watch clouds drift for hours, but a small, bounded quiet carved out of the constant mandated noise, held steady by the slow drag of his knife and the low, rhythmic cadence of his own voice. 
He knew the local enforcement officer would drive by at dusk, would slow to check that he displayed the required window decal marking "active joy practice," and would not stop to ask about the shavings at his feet or the dried flowers resting on the step beside him. 
When he finished carving the small wooden stand, he lifted the dried flower bouquet gently, tucking its brittle stems into the notch he had cut, and set the arrangement on the porch rail where the strobing sunlight would catch the faded petal edges between each pass of the turbine blades. 
He did not ask forgiveness for the reports he had filed, did not seek to make amends through grand gestures, because he knew the remade hush could not hold the weight of judgment any more than it could hold the sharp, burning grief the mandate had banned. 
He sat back on the step, closed his eyes, and let the thrum of the turbines wrap around him, the faint, dust-dry scent of the dried flowers mixing with the pine resin on his hands to form a small, unregulated lightness that required no performance, no inspection, no proof of anything beyond its own quiet existence.
