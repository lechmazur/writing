Kaelin, an ego death survivor who had spent 18 months stitching her sense of self back from scattered static after a botched corporate consciousness backup, knelt on the cool basalt of the floating rock dojo, fingers brushing the chipped edge of the training mat as timelines rippled around her while history rewrites itself. 
Across from her, perched on a stack of frayed tatami cushions, sat the Neurodyne Systems lawyer, his tailored suit shifting cut every few seconds as old public records, old birthday photos, old journal entries flipped allegiance between Kaelin and the vat-grown clone the firm had built from her stolen DNA, the core of the bitter clone custody battle that had dragged on for six months. 
On the rough stone plinth between them, wired to a jury-rigged neural amplifier, sat a brain in a jar labeled duplicate, its gray matter pulsing faint electric blue as timeline flickers threatened to erase its very existence from the official record. 
Kaelin had not come to argue legal technicalities, for she carried the quiet, unshakable clarity of someone who had watched her own ego dissolve into formless awareness, a perspective that slices through pretense the way a forged dojo katana cuts through thin rice paper. 
She had chosen this remote, gravity-defying dojo for the meeting because its thin, crisp air and unmoored position made it impossible to hide from the hollowness that had lingered in her chest since her rebirth, the quiet drive that pushed her to defeat emotional numbness that had kept her from laughing at bad student jokes, from crying at tangerine sunsets, from feeling like she was truly home in her own skin for years. 
The lawyer opened his mouth to spin another rehearsed line about corporate intellectual property rights, about how the vat-grown clone was the "official" Kaelin entitled to her apartment, her small pottery studio, her decades of collected hand-thrown mugs and dog-eared poetry books, but she held up a calloused hand to stop him before the words could land. 
She explained, her voice steady over the high mountain wind, that the fight over which self was "real" was a hollow lie, that the ego death she survived had taught her no single version of a person held the whole truth, that healing would only come through complementary opposites: the wide, unclenched perspective of the self that had let go of all rigid identity, paired with the sharp, messy, attached emotional core held in the brain the corporation had written off as a redundant duplicate. 
Before the lawyer could sputter a protest about property law and binding user agreements, she pressed her calloused palm to the cool glass of the jar, feeding a current of her own neural energy through the amplifier to vivify the long-dormant link between her living consciousness and the disembodied tissue, ignoring the way timeline ripples picked up speed, erasing Neurodyne's falsified custody claims one by one as the truth of her split self anchored reality back into place. 
The lawyer's crisp suit flickered one last time before he dissolved into fine silver mist, for there was no longer a legal case to fight, no false "duplicate" label to prop up a corporate claim to a person's life when the two halves of that person were no longer at war with each other. 
Warmth bloomed in Kaelin's chest as the link settled, not the overwhelming flood of unregulated feeling she had feared, but the soft, steady thrum of grief for her lost months, joy at the sharp smell of pine drifting up from the valleys below, gentle amusement at the way the dojo's resident red-tailed hawk tilted its head at the pulsing jar, as if it could sense the shift in the thin air. 
She had spent months chasing custody of a clone that did not exist as a separate being, the corporate fiction a mirror held up to the split she had carried inside herself since ego death, when the trauma of the botched upload had cleaved her capacity for quiet, non-attached awareness from her capacity for raw, connected feeling. 
She lifted the jar carefully, tucking it into the padded canvas bag she had slung over her shoulder, knowing she would carry that labeled "duplicate" with her always, not as a backup or a cold relic, but as the other half of the wholeness she had fought so hard to earn. 
As the basalt slab of the dojo bobbed gently in the settling mountain air, the last of the timeline ripples smoothed out, and she smiled for the first time in three years, no longer a fragment of a person, no longer fighting for custody of a shadow, fully, unapologetically alive.
