The lavender bluff under aurora hummed with the low thrum of forest elections, the mycorrhizal webs beneath the purple blooms pulsing bioluminescent blue as pine, fir, and wild lavender colonies cast distributed votes on whether to shift their ranges three miles north to outrun the coming decade’s heat domes. 
Elara, the luminous skeptic, leaned against a weathered basalt outcrop, the bioluminescent freckles across her cheekbones flaring soft gold when she scoffed at the old trail tales her grandmother had told her about wanderers who carried personal weather: spitting rain when they grieved, warming the air around them when they loved, leaving frost on doorknobs when they hid from connection. 
She had climbed the bluff that election night to learn why some people carry weather with them, driven by the quiet gap in her grandmother’s stories that no amount of archival climate data or neural mapping had ever been able to fill. 
Twisted around her left index finger was her grandmother’s tin compass ring, its face pitted with decades of rain and pine resin, its needle frozen for as long as Elara could remember, pointing nowhere no matter how she tilted her hand. 
The bluff held a terrestrially cosmic weight, the kind of place where the aurora’s swirling green and violet ribbons tangled so closely with the pine-scented air that visitors swore they could touch the edge of space without ever leaving the soil, and Elara had learned as a child to read the world by sensing through taste changes, a quirk of her gene-modified senses that let her pick up chemical shifts in air and mycelium as readily as most people picked up sound. 
That night, she tasted first the sharp tang of approaching snow, then the honeyed warmth of summer clover, then the briny bite of coastal storm surge, each flavor drifting up the bluff from the trails below as weather-carriers began to converge, drawn by the same unspoken pull that had led her from her glass-and-steel city apartment three days prior. 
She twisted the tin compass ring on her finger, startled when the long-frozen needle twitched, not pointing north, not pointing to any fixed landmark on her crumpled topo map, but swiveling to track each arriving weather-carrier as they stepped onto the bluff, a loose constellation of people wrapped in frayed wool and rain-streaked canvas, their personal atmospheres lapping gently at each other like high-tide waves. 
This was the emergent compass she had spent years dismissing as backcountry fairy tale: not a tool to chart fixed geography, but a living network of connection, calibrated not to magnetic poles but to the quiet, unmarked pull of people who carried pieces of the world’s weather inside them, tied through the same mycorrhizal threads that were even then counting election votes beneath her scuffed hiking boots. 
She tasted wild lavender honey thick on her tongue when the oldest weather-carrier, a woman with silver braids tangled with pine needles and dried moss, stepped close, and Elara realized the weather people carried was not a random quirk or genetic curse, but an ancient covenant: they held fragments of the forest’s collective memory, each small storm or warm front a piece of the land’s own voice, carried in human bodies so the world could feel itself even when cities paved over its living soils. 
The tin compass ring on her finger warmed against her skin, its needle spinning faster now as the forest’s election results rippled up through the bluff’s stone and root systems: a unanimous vote to welcome the weather-carriers as traveling stewards, to weave their personal atmospheres into the mycorrhizal network so no storm or warm spell would ever be lost to the accelerating churn of the changing climate. 
Elara, who had spent a decade doubting every story that could not be measured on a lab sensor readout, laughed softly when a fine, warm mist curled around her wrists, realizing her own long-dormant weather was waking too, the tin ring now pointing steadily inward, not to a fixed coordinate on a map, but to the quiet, terrestrially cosmic truth that people and forests and sky had never been separate at all, even when concrete screens and cold data tried to draw hard, uncrossable lines between them.
