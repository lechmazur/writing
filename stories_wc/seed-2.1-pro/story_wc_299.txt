The dented quarry office trailer perched 30 feet above Echo Valley’s limestone lip held the smell of old motor oil and rain-soaked pine when Jase, ex-band roadie turned antiques appraiser, shouldered through its screen door 48 hours after his very public on-air appraisal flop. 
He had driven up the rutted access road without a plan, fleeing the comment sections that called him a fraud, the colleagues who sent overly sympathetic texts, the hollow routine of insulating emotionally with facts that had carried him through 12 years of grief after his band’s lead singer died in a late-night tour van crash. 
For years, he had hidden behind manufacture dates, original finish percentages, and verified provenance, refusing to let the soft, messy weight of memory seep into his work, convinced hard data could keep the sharp edge of longing at bay. 
The old site manager had left a cardboard box marked "BAND JUNK 2011" on the trailer’s chipped linoleum counter, the last remnants of the raucous quarry-edge sets the group had played every summer before their first record deal, long before the crash, long before the applause faded for good. 
He flipped through water-stained set lists, a cracked guitar pick stamped with the band’s hand-drawn logo, a crumpled receipt for 12 cases of cheap beer, until his fingers brushed a frayed, dun-colored speaker grille cloth scrap caught under a rusted tape measure. 
He knew the scrap instantly, could recite its factual profile without pausing: it was from the original 1959 Silvertone amp they had hauled to every early gig, the one Lila had kicked mid-set during their raucous cover of a 90s grunge track, blowing the speaker so hard the grille had torn clean down the middle. 
A sharp gust curling up the quarry face caught the scrap before he could tuck it into his jacket pocket, sending it skittering across the counter toward the open window, and he lunged to wrest it back before it could tumble 100 feet to the jagged rock below. 
His fingers closed around the frayed edge half an inch from the windowsill, and he collapsed back against the counter, breath coming hard, as a faint, distorted echo of old applause drifted up from the quarry’s curved stone walls, a trick of wind and memory he had not let himself hear in more than a decade. 
He realized in that quiet, ringing moment that his public failure had not been a misread of serial numbers or wiring schematics, but a refusal to see the stories clinging to every object he appraised, the way the grille scrap held not just textile composition and manufacturing origin, but the sound of Lila laughing mid-kick, the roar of a crowd that had showed up for no cover charge and no record label, the sticky sweat of summer nights that felt like they would never end. 
He had spent years chasing certainty, building a career on definitive answers that could not be hurt by loss, but the scrap in his palm felt like a quiet invitation to stop hiding from the soft, unprovable parts of the work that had first drawn him to road life, to old gear, to the messy magic of live sound. 
He did not have a fully formed plan for what came next--no script for the apology video he owed his audience, no blueprint for how to rework his appraisal practice to center story as much as specification, no clear map for how to carry Lila’s memory forward without walling himself off from the world. 
Yet he felt confidently uncertain for the first time since the crash, grounded by the rough texture of the cloth scrap against his palm, the echo of old cheers curling through the trailer window, the quiet truth that purpose after loss does not arrive as a verified provenance or a perfect appraisal, but as a small, frayed thing you wrest from the edge of the void, long after the last round of applause has faded into the valley stone.
