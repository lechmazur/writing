After the final warning from her council of star-readers, who pressed cold iron talismans into her palms and swore the high ridge held magic that would unravel every thread of her reign, the disenchanted queen slipped from her keep at golden hour, no guard, no retinue, only a tattered map scrawled from the fragments of her own recurring dreams. 
She had spent three years choking on courtly platitudes, signing edicts that bled her poorest villagers dry, smiling through suitors’ hollow flattery, and she was determined to climb the jagged rock face to the ridge to unravel the mystery of recurring dreams that showed her a light softer than any castle torch, a place where the crown did not grind into her temples. 
The pine boughs snagged her velvet gown like gnarled fingers, loose shale skittered down into the valley where her castle’s spires glinted like shards of broken glass, and when she pulled herself over the last ledge, she found the ember grotto on the ridge, its mouth ringed with smoldering moss that glowed like banked hearth fire, wreathed in thin, lace-like veils of mist that curled around her wrists with a faint, prickling warning. 
She stepped past the mossy threshold, and the air shifted from sharp pine resin to cool, mineral-rich stillness, revealing the cobalt grotto above clouds, its walls shot through with veins of glowing blue stone that jutted over a sea of roiling white cumulus, as if the mountain held a shard of the night sky itself trapped in its heart. 
Thin streams of bleeding starlight seeped from fine cracks in the grotto’s ceiling, dripping in slow, luminous drops that pooled on the stone floor and sent tiny, glowing ripples outward when they landed, and for a second she thought she saw a figure standing at the far end of the cave, her own face, younger, unburdened by the weight of the crown, watching her. 
The figure did not move, but her voice carried across the grotto, softly commanding, as if she had been waiting for years for the queen to stop performing royalty long enough to find her, and she spoke of how the starlight was the collected magic of every choice the queen had abandoned to meet others’ expectations, bleeding out of her slowly until she felt nothing but hollow disenchantment. 
The queen stepped closer, the crown slipping from her head to clatter on the stone and send a ripple through the pooled starlight, and she stood shoulder to shoulder with the girl she had been, both of them looking out over the cloud bank as the last of the sunset faded to indigo. 
They let out a shared exhale upon spotting the first fireflies of summer, tiny pinpricks of gold rising up through wispy gaps in the clouds, drifting to mingle with the dripping starlight until it was impossible to tell which light was born of the mountain and which was born of the valley below, the lacy menace of the grotto’s mist softening into something like welcome. 
The star-readers had warned her that seeking the grotto would unravel her reign, and they had not lied, for when she turned to pick up her crown, she found its gold had turned to dandelion fluff, light as air, dissolving at the slightest touch, because the reign they had begged her to protect was built on the parts of herself she had buried to wear it. 
The younger version of herself smiled and faded into the drifting starlight and fireflies, leaving only the faint scent of clover, the same scent that had drifted through her nursery window when she was small, long before anyone had told her she had to be sharp and unyielding to rule. 
She leaned against the grotto’s cool cobalt wall, watching fireflies and starlight twist together in delicate, web-like strands that looked for all the world like fine lace, each thread sharp enough to cut the lies she had lived by for a decade, and for the first time since her coronation, she did not feel hollow. 
She did not know what waited for her in the valley when she climbed down at dawn, whether rebellion or welcome or the ruin the star-readers had promised, but she knew the dreams that had pulled her up the ridge were not a curse; they were the first piece of herself she had found in years.
