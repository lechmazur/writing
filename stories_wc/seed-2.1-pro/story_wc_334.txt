The chronically curious hands-on folklorist had clung to one unshakable habit for 38 years: turning her back on the briny stretch of coast where her grandmother once tended heritage apple trees, never stepping close enough to smell salt on rotting fruit or hear the gurgle of tidal water through cracked greenhouse glass. 
That habit broke on a crisp October evening when she found a tattered water-stained page of her grandmother’s constellation journal tucked in the spine of a donated folk tale collection, the edges smudged with the same cinnamon cider stain she remembered dabbing on the page as a wiggly seven-year-old. 
Decades later than she ever thought she would return, she pulled on scuffed hip waders at low tide and sloshed out toward the sunken orchard greenhouse ringed by shipwrecks, the angular ribs of broken masts poking through the surf like the spines of half-buried storybooks. 
She had carried the quiet motivation of that tattered journal page for three weeks before making the trip: to recover stolen memories, the soft, sun-dappled fragments of her last summer with her grandmother that had vanished the day the storm swept the orchard out to sea, leaving only a blank, hollow space where stories of apple pressing and firefly jars should have lived. 
The half-submerged greenhouse frame came into view as the tide pulled further out, its triangular panes of surviving glass tilted at odd angles so they caught the first pricks of evening starlight and scattered them across silt-caked raised beds where gnarled apple tree branches still curled up through the shallow water, heavy with small, tart, brine-kissed fruit. 
As a hands-on field researcher who had spent her career tracing folk memory ties to coastal foraging traditions, she did not rush, instead kneeling in the cool silt to run her fingers over half-buried ceramic plant markers, rusted cider press parts, and the soft, tan-capped clusters of fairy ring mushrooms growing in a perfect circle around the greenhouse’s old central stone hearth. 
She remembered, in a faint, prickling flash, her grandmother telling her that fairy rings in tidal ground did not lead to fairy revels, but held the pieces of things the sea tried to steal, and that you could find what you had lost if you knew how to look past the distortions the water cast across the sky. 
For a long minute she sat cross-legged in the center of the mushroom ring, tilting her head to detect the faint, familiar pattern she had been half-searching for her whole adult life, not by digging through silt or prying open rotting crates, but by studying broken constellations. 
The surviving shards of greenhouse glass jutted at crooked angles, and the thin layer of seawater pooled across the silt rippled with the soft pull of the outgoing tide, shattering the familiar star patterns of Ursa Major, Cassiopeia, and the little dipper her grandmother had taught her to name into scattered, disjointed pinpricks that did not match any sky chart she had ever studied. 
She leaned forward, adjusting her position inch by inch until the shifted refractions lined up, and the broken constellations resolved not into the night sky above the coast, but into the freckled map of her seven-year-old memory: her grandmother laughing as she dropped a too-tart apple into the cider press, the two of them making up their own constellations out of the starlight slanting through the greenhouse glass, naming one for the stray cat that napped on the hearth, one for the fairy ring mushrooms that sprouted every fall after the first high tide. 
The memories did not flood back all at once, but settled soft as salt spray, sharp as the crisp October air: she could smell the cinnamon her grandmother stirred into mugs of hot cider, feel the rough cap of a fairy ring mushroom under her seven-year-old finger as her grandmother warned her never to pick the rings, for they held more stories than any book on her shelf. 
When the tide began to creep back in an hour later, she tucked a small, dried mushroom cap into the pocket of her field jacket, left a crumble of the cinnamon oat bar she had packed for the trip on the old hearth, and turned to walk back to shore, her chronically curious mind already turning over the ways these sunken, shipwreck-ringed places held more folk truth than any archived interview could ever capture.
