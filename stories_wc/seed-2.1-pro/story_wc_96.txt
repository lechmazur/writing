Beneath a perforated steel roof crisscrossed with rust veins that let pinpricks of starlight sift onto cracked linoleum, the vagrant penny poet hunkered on a splintered oak stool, counting out copper coins she’d earned reciting hand-scrawled verses to passersby that afternoon. 
As the moon clears the skyline, its silver glow slants through the library’s shattered west window to gild the dented coffee tin with a secret false bottom that sits at the poet’s elbow, the only possession she has carried through three straight months of shifting selves. 
She had not meant to stay in the city when she molted her ragged street-preacher personality back in late spring, when her verses had curdled into fire-and-brimstone rants that made pedestrians cross the street to avoid her, but a court order for unpaid loitering fines had landed her three weeks of community service sorting moldering paperbacks at the maximum-security prison on the city’s industrial edge. 
It was there, tucking tattered mystery novels back onto sagging wire shelves, that a friendship sparked while sorting books at the prison library: a quiet, steady bond with Maren, a former textile worker serving time for slashing the tires of the private police fleet that had bulldozed the city’s last community garden to make way for a luxury parking garage. 
Maren had pressed the dented coffee tin into her hands the day before the poet’s service ended, tapping its side twice to signal the hidden compartment that held crumpled slips of paper, each scribbled with a line of verse, a seed packet for native milkweed, a scrawled note naming a neighbor who still held out against the developer’s predatory buyouts. 
Most months, she molts personalities monthly, shedding old preferences, grudges, and small joys like a sun-scorched cicada shell left on a fence post; she discards old trinkets and half-finished thoughts in bus stops or alleyways, leaving the weight of a self that no longer fits to make space for whatever version of herself will walk the streets next, but she could never bring herself to leave the tin behind. 
The city around her had frayed into ragged, ungoverned anarchy that summer, as unhoused encampments were repeatedly cleared by private security, neighborhood libraries were padlocked for lack of public funding, and neighbors who once waved from front porches now hurried past each other with heads down, too worn down by constant precarity to reach out across their fear. 
The rusted library under stars was the first branch the city had abandoned, its exterior streaked with orange corrosion, its locks rusted through, its shelves half-looted, its children’s nook dotted with broken crayons and water-damaged picture books that no city worker had bothered to cart away when the doors were chained shut three years prior. 
She had not come here to hide from the chaos, exactly, but to rebuild, starting with small, uncelebrated acts, because the blooming resistance Maren had written of in her crumpled notes did not need thunderous marches or fiery speeches to take root; it needed people to show up, even when their own sense of self felt unmoored, even when the world around them felt cracked beyond repair. 
Her core motivation, the quiet golden thread that had run through every shifting personality that cycled through her that year - street preacher, foraged fruit seller, migratory bird watcher, soft-spoken penny poet - was the stubborn, unshakable urge to forge beauty from anarchy, to stitch small, glowing connections back into a world that kept being ripped apart at the seams by greed and neglect. 
As the moon climbed higher, clearing the last jagged edge of the downtown skyline to flood the library’s cracked floors with silver, she unscrewed the coffee tin’s false bottom, scattered a handful of milkweed seeds into the crumbled mortar of the library’s broken front step, and tucked a slip of Maren’s verse into the spine of every salvageable book left on the shelves, knowing that when she molted into her next self a week from then, the seeds would still sprout, the verses would still wait for a curious reader, and the fragile, luminous fracture of what they were building would outlast any single version of herself.
