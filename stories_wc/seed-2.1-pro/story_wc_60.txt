From the pause of migrating geese, when the high V hung frozen above the rusted spires as if the wind itself had held its breath, Lira stepped into the city of forgotten fountains. 
The cobblestones stuck to the thin soles of her boots, crusted with decades of dried mineral deposits left when the water mains failed, and the faint silver glow seeping from her cuffs marked her as the luminous skeptic, a figure the city's remaining residents whispered had no business chasing tales they had long abandoned. 
She was realistically imaginative, too, to buy into the soft, urgent stories her grandmother had told on her deathbed--no grand prophecies, no world-ending curses, just a quiet wrong that no one had bothered to fix for three generations. 
Her motivation was simple, unburdened by heroics: to liberate trapped memories, the snippets of first kisses, lullabies, shared bread, and quiet grief that the city's failing magic had siphoned into a hidden vault when the fountains ran dry. 
She navigated the cracked plazas and weed-choked basins by following breadcrumbs, not the stale crumbs of fairy tale, but tiny, flecked shards of colored glass her grandmother had tucked into curb cracks and fountain rims across the decades, each one placed with the slow, unrushed care that defined sidereal patience, the kind of waiting that measured time in star orbits rather than election cycles or rent deadlines. 
The city's residents watched from curtained windows, their faces blank with the amnesia that came from letting memories rot in a vault, and Lira moved in a defiant hush, refusing to call attention to her quest even as she deliberately ignored the hand-painted signs declaring the old magic dead and buried. 
The last glass shard sat in the lip of the central fountain, the largest basin, which had once shot water 30 feet into the air to catch the sunset, and when she brushed away the moss covering the stone at its base, her fingers found the edge of a stained glass box, its panels soldered together with silver that glowed in time with the light from her cuffs. 
The box was wedged tight, sealed by decades of disuse and the quiet, unexamined resignation of the city's people, and Lira did not pry at it or shout or cast a flashy spell, because she knew the work required of her was not brute force, but the same slow attention her grandmother had used to place the glass shards. 
She pressed her palm flat to the box's crimson panel, which held a tiny shard of the first sunset her grandmother had watched with her grandfather, and she breathed in time with the distant geese, who still waited far above, held by the same old magic that had locked the memories away. 
It took 17 slow, even breaths before she felt the seal give, the faint pop of magic releasing as she began to unstick the lid not with force, but with the quiet recognition that those memories were worth waiting for, worth walking across cracked cobblestones for, worth defying a city's collective amnesia for. 
When the lid lifted, the first memory slipped out: a child's laugh, bright as the geese's first call, and it drifted up to the paused flock, as if giving them permission to move again. 
More memories followed, soft and warm, seeping out of the box to drift down the empty streets, slipping through window cracks to land on the tongues of residents who had long forgotten what it felt like to remember being loved, to remember being young, to remember hoping for something better than slow decay. 
Far above, the geese resumed their southward journey, their V shifting and settling as the first trickle of water bubbled up in the central fountain, clear and cold, catching the last of the afternoon light. 
Lira did not stay to be celebrated, because skeptics do not wait for praise for doing the work no one else had the patience to start; she tucked the empty stained glass box into her satchel, turned on her heel, and walked toward the city limits, the faint silver glow from her cuffs softening as the first curtain pulled back, and a resident stepped out onto their stoop, smiling at the sound of running water. 
She knew the fountains would take time to run full again, that memories would take time to settle soft and easy into the bones of the city, but that was the point of sidereal patience: the work did not need to be finished in a single day, only started, one glass shard, one quiet breath, one unstuck lid at a time.
