Lila, the bunker builder’s claustrophobic child, had spent 12 years squeezing between reinforced concrete walls her father insisted would outlast every apocalypse save one: the hourly gravity inversion that lifted loose, unmoored things above the cloud line to the xenial trellis strung between glinting storm spires. 
She snuck out on the cusp of the inversion for one reason: to collect the flavors of different silences, the quiets that did not press against her ribs like cold cinder block. 
She knew the rules of the hour-long flip by heart, from her father’s panicked safety briefings: by limiting exposure to the open upward pull, a person could avoid being stranded permanently among the high, wispy drifts. 
When the first tingle of weightlessness lifted the scuffed toes of her work boots, she let herself rise slow, fingers brushing clover tops until the trellis’s twisted, jasmine-woven slats caught her, warm and welcoming in the way her father’s bunkers never were. 
The air here held a gilded hush, thick with honeyed sunlight that softened every sharp edge, every contracted tightness in her chest that had lived there since she was small enough to get locked in the bunker’s supply closet for three terrified hours. 
That hush was intangibly tangible, settling on her tongue like jasmine nectar when she breathed in, a silence that held space instead of squeezing it out of her. 
She knelt on the slats, tucking a scrap of cloud into the small glass vial she’d tucked in her overalls pocket to hold that first flavor, when she spotted it half-buried in a drift of star moss: a tiny porcelain doll, its painted cheeks chipped, its frayed linen dress caught on a trellis slat as if it had been waiting for her. 
She recognized it instantly as the doll she’d lost the day of the supply closet lock-in, the one her father had told her must have fallen into a construction crack, lost forever under tons of concrete. 
When she lifted it, a fleck of gold from the gilded trellis light caught in the moss at its feet, and she watched, wide-eyed, as tiny, pale green leaves began to sprout from the doll’s chipped ceramic palms, each unfurling leaf holding a new, distinct quiet. 
One leaf held the soft hush of fireflies blinking on at dusk, sweet as blackberry, another held the quiet of snow falling on pine, crisp and cold without the sharp bite of bunker concrete, and a third held the quiet of her father’s voice when he thought she was asleep, murmuring apologies for working too late, for building walls so thick he forgot how to let light in. 
She felt the familiar, contracted clench of claustrophobia in her ribs start to stretch, softened by the flavors of silence she was collecting, no longer a tight fist around her lungs but a warm, open hand. 
She glanced at the faint, glowing timer she’d drawn on her wrist in charcoal, noting she had 10 minutes left before gravity snapped back down to normal, enough time to tuck the doll into her overalls, pluck the three sprouted leaves to tuck into her vials, and breathe in one last lungful of the gilded, open hush. 
When the pull downward started, slow at first then sharp, she let herself fall easy, no panic tightening her throat, because she carried pieces of the trellis’s welcome quiet with her, and the doll whose sprouted leaves hummed soft against her chest. 
She landed soft in the clover field at the edge of her father’s bunker complex, right as he came sprinting out, his hard hat askew, his face tight with the same fear he tried to wall out with concrete. 
She held out the doll to him, watching his face soften as he spotted the sprouted leaves glinting with gilded trellis light, and told him she had brought back a silence that didn’t need thick walls to hold it, a quiet that felt like home. 
Later, when they sat together on the bunker’s concrete steps as the sun dipped low, she let him taste the silence from the first leaf, sweet as jasmine, and watched the permanent tight line between his shoulders relax, the same contracted tension she’d carried in her chest for years stretching soft between them. 
For the first time in years, neither of them felt the need to hide behind walls, because the best silence, they learned, was the kind you shared instead of hoarded behind reinforced concrete.
