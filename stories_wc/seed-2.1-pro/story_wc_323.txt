Three weeks after confirmed contact with intelligent extraterrestrial life, the benthic city of Veyra clung to the abyssal basalt 3,000 meters below the Northeast Pacific, its floodlit domes bleaching drifting marine snow pale silver. 
Maren, the city’s lead navigation instructor, was balancedly curious about the alien signal, neither succumbing to the doomsday panic that locked down the upper docking bays nor buying the euphoric claims of first-contact diplomats who promised instant interstellar utopia. 
For 120 years, Veyra’s residents had navigated entirely by sonar pings, seabed contour maps, and the faint chemical signatures of hydrothermal vent currents, so when the alien transmission included a note that their craft would meet any surface delegation at a coordinate 200 kilometers above the old Oregon coast, Maren fixed on a quiet, unshakable motivation: to learn to navigate by starlight, the same way the traveling aliens did, rather than relying on instruments that only worked under kilometers of crushing dark. 
She kept a single pitted boat anchor chain link on the workbench in her navigation lab, salvaged from a 19th-century sailing wreck that had slid down the continental slope a decade prior, its iron surface worn smooth by decades of abrasion from sediment and slow, cold current. 
When she first began drafting lessons for a new generation of navigators who might travel beyond the seabed to meet the visitors, she realized the chain link held the key to understanding how star positions worked, through the quiet, precise logic of edge geometry. 
Each angled face of the link caught the lab’s LED light at a slightly different angle, just as the curved edge of the planetary horizon caught distant starlight at a predictable tilt, letting a navigator calculate their position without sonar, without pressure sensors, without anything but a clear line of sight to the dark above. 
For months, she taught small groups of teenaged trainees to use the chain link as a practice sighting tool, helping them expand their sense of what navigation could be, untying their mental maps from the fixed basalt slopes and vent fields that had bounded every Veyran resident’s whole life. 
The day the first training sub ascended past the sunlit zone, Maren held the chain link up to the porthole, watching as the inky dark above resolved into pricks of unwavering light, the unfolded horizons of interstellar space no longer a distant rumor but a tangible presence stretching in every direction. 
She did not feel the giddy triumph she had expected when she lined the link’s beveled edge up with the bright pinprick of Polaris, matching the angle she had calculated from the alien transmission’s star charts, only a quiet, weighted awe, the kind that comes from realizing every map you ever trusted was only a single page of a book too large to hold in one mind. 
Edge geometry, she explained to the wide-eyed trainees clustered around the porthole, did not care if the edge was the worn lip of an old anchor link, the curve of a planet’s atmosphere, or the light-bent boundary of a distant solar system; it only required that you paid close enough attention to the angle between where you stood and the light that found you. 
One trainee asked if they would be lost out there, among the stars and the aliens and the vast unmarked dark, and Maren turned the chain link over in her palm, feeling the worn indent where 150 years of tide and time had worn away its sharpest, harshest corner. 
She thought of the generations of Veyrans who had lived and died without ever seeing a star, who had built a warm, tight-knit home in the crushing dark because they had no other choice, and felt no sharp grief for that long, careful seclusion, only a soft, steady wonder at the way the same tools that once tied people to safety could guide them outward. 
The sub drifted higher, and the stars spread out so thickly they looked like scattered diamond dust across the unbroken black, and for a long moment no one spoke, each of them holding the quiet knowledge that they would never again see the world as small enough to fit between the reinforced walls of their domed seabed home.
