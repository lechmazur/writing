The wandering fireworks designer shouldered her scuffed canvas pack as she stepped through the cracked iron gates of the blooming fortress at dusk, when the last honeyed light of sunset drained fast to leave the hush of a night with no stars. 
She chanted while walking, soft, wordless syllables stitched half from childhood lullabies and half from the precise ratios of charcoal, sulfur, and strontium she memorized across a decade of chasing light for towns that forgot how to look up. 
Tucked in the outer pocket of her pack, bumping her hip with every step, was the fire spinner’s kerosene rag knot, a frayed, soot-stained memento from the friend who had first taught her that flame did not have to burn to destroy, that it could dance if she gave it space to move. 
She had traveled 300 miles to reach this fortress, because word had reached her that the people trapped behind its vine-choked walls had stopped believing light could return, and she had built her life’s work around a single stubborn goal: to defy the gravity of hopelessness, which pulled even the sturdiest communities down into quiet, unresisting dark. 
She did not rush to unload her mortar tubes or prep her chemical mixes when she reached the fortress’s central courtyard, because she had learned long ago to filter the mood of a place not through questions or shouted announcements, but through ambient awareness, letting the small, unspoken signs seep into her bones before she struck a single spark. 
Beneath her boots, half-buried under drifts of fallen jasmine and clover, was a note in mosaic, its cracked tile pieces faded by decades of rain and neglect, assembled by the last generation of fortress residents who had chosen joy even when invading armies had camped at their gates. 
Kneeling, she brushed the debris from the tiles to read the words spelled out in shards of cobalt, terracotta, and milk glass: "Light does not fall from the sky. It is spun, knot by knot, by hands that refuse to stop moving." 
She smiled, recognizing the turn of phrase, because the fire spinner who had given her the kerosene rag knot had spoken those exact words to her the night a flood had washed out her first workshop, when she had sat in the mud ready to abandon her craft forever. 
Slowly, faces began to peek from shuttered windows and arched doorways, quiet as moths, as she pulled jars of colored composition from her pack, tucking a pinch of bioluminescent moss, plucked from the fortress walls, into every shell she planned to launch. 
She did not use a standard lighter to touch off her fuses, but instead lit the frayed end of the fire spinner’s kerosene rag knot, holding the spinning, dripping orb of flame like a maypole ribbon as she danced between the rows of tubes, her old chant rising loud enough to carry to every window ledge. 
The first shell burst low over the courtyard in a shower of soft gold, not the sharp, thunderous fire of royal celebrations, but gentle, bobbing points of light that drifted slow as dandelion fluff, as if refusing to bow to the same gravity that dragged heavy hearts down. 
She launched bloom after bloom of colored light: clusters of jasmine that matched the flowers drifting over the mosaic, tiny spinning fire spinners that twirled mid-air, silly grinning cat faces that pulled quiet, rusty laughs from the residents who had begun to step out into the courtyard, barefoot, to watch. 
She had not planned these specific shapes, of course; they had risen unbidden as she filtered the quiet wants of the fortress through ambient awareness, picking up flashes of memory from the hush around her: missed festivals, childhood pets, the smell of jasmine wine at summer gatherings. 
She knew the light would fade, that the starless dark would creep back when the last ember fizzled out, that hopelessness did not vanish after one night of fireworks, but that was never the point of defying its gravity. 
Defiance, she thought as a small child reached out to touch the still-smoldering fire spinner’s kerosene rag knot, laughing as the warm, soot-stained fabric tickled her palm, was the quiet, playful work of building small lights, one after another, even when the sky offered none of its own. 
As the last firework burst overhead in the shape of a tiny, spinning knot of flame, a breeze stirred the jasmine vines crawling up the fortress walls, carrying the sound of shared laughter through the hush, proof that even the darkest starless night could not outlast a courtyard full of people who remembered how to play with light.
