Three months after the waterlogged, crudely carved substitute body was pulled from the silted ditch at the hedge maze's entrance, Elara--our small town's resident forgiving skeptic, who dismissed decades of changeling gossip about her own childhood but never held the gossip against the neighbors who spread it--ducked through a narrow, thorn-fringed gap she had not seen since she was seven to step into the secret garden hidden behind the hedge maze. 
She carried her grandmother's dented bone-setting kit in her canvas tote, and moved with that shrewdly innocent air she had honed over years of listening to old wives' tales without swallowing the fear tangled in them, stopping only to pluck a stray blackberry from a bramble arch as she followed the overgrown stone path to the garden's sun-dappled center. 
For years, she had watched the village let the old changeling myth curdle into quiet cruelty--neighbors leaving iron nails on her porch as a teen, whispering that her quiet love of stargazing meant she was not fully human--and she yearned to reframe those old, hurtful mistakes into something soft and luminous, living out the quiet promise she had made to herself long ago to turn mistakes into constellations. 
She had come to the garden certain the carved wooden figure was nothing but a decades-old prank, a cruel joke some teen had carved to stoke the changeling rumors, but the moment she knelt to brush moss from the cracked stone bench at the path's end, a tiny, pearlescent object rolled from a crevice to rest against her knee, opening a sharp, warm cleft in certainty she had not thought possible for someone as grounded as she was. 
It was a changeling's baby tooth, its surface etched with tiny, swirling star maps no human child's tooth could bear, and she recognized it instantly from the stories her grandmother had told her, not as a mark of a monster swapped into a human cradle, but as a token left by a child who had gotten lost crossing between worlds, too scared to stay, too kind to leave no trace of their passage. 
She pulled the cracked wooden substitute from her tote--she had asked the village council for it the day it was found, ignoring their warnings that it was cursed--and laid the pieces across the stone bench, using the same gentle, precise bone setting techniques her grandmother had taught her to pin its splintered wrist, reset its chipped jaw, and smooth the rough edges where decades of silt had worn away its carved smile. 
The work felt like the heart of that nostalgic innovation she had always chased, honoring the old tales her grandmother had shared while stripping away the fear that had turned them into weapons, no grand magic required, just the same care used to set a broken collarbone or plant a forgotten flower bed. 
When the figure was fully mended, she set the changeling's baby tooth into the small, empty carved hollow at its chest, where a heart would sit, and watched as sunlight caught the etched star maps on the tooth's surface, casting tiny, pinprick constellations across the garden's mossy stones and tangled rose vines. 
She did not suddenly abandon her lifelong skepticism to declare fairies real, or that she herself had been swapped as a baby; the cleft in her certainty only meant she no longer needed to prove every old story false to find value in it, no longer needed to hold the village's past cruelty against neighbors who had only been taught to fear what they did not understand. 
As a bumblebee drifted past her ear to land on a pale pink rose blooming out of season, she smiled, knowing the mended figure would not erase decades of awkward glances or silly gossip, but it would offer a new story to grow into, one rooted in forgiveness rather than fear. 
She would bring the mended figure back to the village square at harvest time, she decided, and tell the story not of a terrifying changeling swap, but of a lost child who left a star map, a village that let curdled fear turn neighbor against neighbor, and a choice to stitch those messy, human mistakes into something people could look up to, the same way stargazers turn random pinpricks of light into constellations that guide travelers home.
