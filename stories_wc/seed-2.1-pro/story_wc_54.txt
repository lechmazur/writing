Three days after the carnival packed its frayed striped tents and rumbled out of the high desert basin, curious civil engineer intern Maren shouldered her dented survey bag through the cracked blast-glass doors of the abandoned spaceport passenger terminal. 
She was not there to tag structural decay for the county demolition order, though that was the line item on her timesheet; she had come to puzzle out new equations for hope, the kind that did not rely on grant funding or crumbling political promises to hold weight. 
The terminal’s concourse had not been stripped bare, as the county reports claimed; carnival workers had dragged their equipment through its arched corridors mid-storm two weeks prior, leaving a tangled trail of tent stakes, cotton candy stick wands, and mismatched directional signs that coiled into a maze of intentions, every turn marked by a half-formed plan someone had abandoned before it could bear fruit. 
Maren was functionally creative by training and temperament, so she did not fight the maze’s odd logic; she followed the slant of faded floor decals and the faint glint of metal half-buried under drifted dust, letting the space lead her instead of forcing a straight line to the old observation deck. 
Halfway down a service corridor blocked by a stack of old carnival funhouse mirrors, she found it: a tarnished explorer's sextant piece, the curved arc of its graduated scale cracked clean through, wedged between a rusted drainage grate and a crumpled poster advertising the carnival’s zero-gravity thrill ride. 
She recognized the fragment from old spaceport archival photos: it had belonged to the port’s first lead navigator, who had plotted the first commercial flights to the outer system before budget cuts grounded the entire fleet in 2097. 
For three hours, she traced the terminal’s hidden water and power lines, cross-referencing their slopes with the sextant’s etched angle marks, realizing the maze of half-abandoned plans around her mapped exactly to the flows the port’s original designers had built to sustain long-distance travel, flows later diverted to flood county fairgrounds and feed carnival generator banks when the port was shuttered. 
Maren did not believe in magic, but she believed in hydraulics, in circuit load math, in the way carefully calibrated angles could pull forgotten potential back to the surface. 
Using the sextant shard as a straightedge to mark new junction points, she pried open rusted valve covers and reset circuit breakers, working through redirected flows of rainwater trapped in the terminal’s foundation and residual power stored in old carnival battery packs left behind in the concourse. 
She did not set out to resurrect a ghost, but when the last valve clicked into place, the terminal’s old arrival board flickered to life, its split-flap display clattering to show departure times for settlements the world had written off as unreachable a generation prior. 
The rainwater she had routed trickled up through the cracked planter beds by the ticket counters, feeding the dormant desert wildflower seeds the carnival had trampled two weeks prior, sending tiny green shoots pushing up through dust and crumpled ticket stubs. 
Maren leaned against a cold concrete pillar, the sextant piece tucked into the breast pocket of her work vest, and scribbled notes in her field book, adjusting the standard civil engineering flow equations to account for the weight of abandoned hope, the friction of unmet longing, the lift that comes when scattered intentions are aligned instead of left to tangle. 
She did not whoop or celebrate; stoic wonder settled over her like the thin, clear desert light slanting through the terminal’s skylights, as the split-flap board clattered out a new route, one no old planner or carnival barker had ever thought to map, built not from grand budgets or flashy thrills but from the quiet, persistent work of noticing what others had thrown away. 
When she finally shouldered her bag to leave as the sun dipped low over the basin, she left the valves open and the breakers flipped, letting the new equations work, one small redirected flow at a time.
