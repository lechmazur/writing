The hush of the university radio station’s basement studio clung to Elara Voss, the department’s only full-time celestial signal interpreter, as she traced frayed edges of decades-old astronomical charts spread across the mixing board. 
Up the concrete stairs, the campus quad had split into warring camps over the past three weeks: one group insisted the repeating, honey-toned signal pulsing through every local speaker was a first-contact greeting, while the other decried it as a foreign psyop designed to erode local trust ahead of the municipal watershed vote. 
Elara knew neither faction was right, because three nights of cross-referencing the signal’s frequency shifts against her charts had revealed the signature of the Lyran tricksters, amorphous cosmic entities that fed on the friction of societal rifts, stoking petty power plays until communities fractured into easy, fearful prey. 
She was cautiously hopeful she could stop them, though she knew the risk of intervening: the last interpreter who’d attempted to disrupt a Lyran feed had been written off as a conspiracy theorist, her career dismantled by the very paranoia the entities had seeded. 
The station’s 50-year-old transmitter, rarely cranked to full power these days due to budget cuts, was capable of broadcasting across the entire valley, and she had spent the previous night calibrating a counter-signal tuned to cut through the tricksters’ manipulations through subsonic frequencies tuned to trigger quiet empathy rather than defensive rage. 
To interfere would mean violating the station manager’s explicit order to loop the tricksters’ signal between indie rock sets as a ratings gimmick, a choice that would almost certainly get her fired if the counter-signal failed to work as intended. 
Her motivation was not professional glory, but the quiet, hard-won drive to outwit cosmic tricksters that had pulled her through seven years of graduate school, after she watched a nearly identical Lyran signal turn her tight-knit rural hometown against itself over a fabricated school board scandal when she was 12. 
She flipped the switch to override the scheduled broadcast, the transmitter’s low thrum vibrating up through her scuffed work boots as the air took on the faint, honeyed weight of star stuff tangled with static, the particular texture of cross-talk between Earth and the edges of the known galaxy. 
Through the studio’s smudged ground-level window, she watched the shouting on the quad falter, the two groups of sign-waving protesters pausing mid-chant as the subsonic tone curled through phone speakers, car radios, and portable bluetooth sets, softening the tight, angry set of their shoulders. 
The charts tucked under her elbow marked the tricksters’ expected retreat window, three minutes of broadcast time before their signal would burn out and drift back to the empty stretch of interstellar space they’d come from, but she saw the dials spike sharply 90 seconds in, a sign the entities were fighting to hold their grip on the crowd’s fraying emotions. 
She leaned into the mixing board, twisting the subsonic gain dial up two notches even as the frequency made her teeth rattle and her vision blur at the edges, refusing to cede ground when she was so close to prying the community free of the entities’ influence. 
The spiking dials settled to a soft, steady hum after 10 long, breath-held seconds, and through the window she saw a student holding a "First Contact" sign offer a half-crushed granola bar to a man holding a "Stop the Psyop" sign, the two of them laughing shakily as they realized how ridiculous their mutual fury had felt moments before. 
The studio door slammed open as the station manager stormed down the stairs, but he paused mid-rant when he caught sight of the crowd outside, his own tight anger softening as the subsonic tone wrapped around him too, the same quiet empathy seeping into the rigid set of his jaw. 
Elara leaned back against the mixing board, fingers brushing the worn edges of her astronomical charts as she watched the crowd merge into loose, chatting clusters, knowing the cosmic tricksters would return to stoke new power plays in other divided towns, that the work of guarding small, fragile communities would never be glamorous or easy. 
For now, though, as the radio transmitter hummed under her hands and the first crisp notes of a local folk track drifted out over the valley, she let herself hold that small, hard-won win, the grit of basement concrete under her boots and the faint echo of distant stars in her ears enough to keep her going until the next signal arrived.
