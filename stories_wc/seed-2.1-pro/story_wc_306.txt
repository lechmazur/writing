The campfire’s last spark crumbled to ash on the jagged shore rock the moment Kael pushed himself to his feet, turning toward the breezy lighthouse at dawn as salt wind nipped at the cuffs of his wool coat. 
Kael worked as a conscience for hire, a trade for people who had walled off their own internal moral compasses behind walls of rigid, unyielding certainty, too afraid of misstep to listen to the quiet, messy voice that once guided them. 
His current client was Mara, the lighthouse keeper who had run the beacon for 42 years, ever since a sudden, unforecast squall had drowned her younger sister Lila while the two were out checking fishing buoys at 17. 
In the decades after, Mara had wrapped herself in what she thought was armor: perfectly timed fog horn blasts, mathematically calibrated lamp angles, zero deviations from written procedure, no room for guesswork, softness, or regret. 
Kael knew that stiff, unyielding armor for what it truly was: the cloak of doubt, woven tight from every unadmitted fear, every suppressed what-if, every quiet grief she had refused to let breathe for fear it would crack her resolve entirely. 
His usual opening gambit was sincerely insincere, a casual, almost silly sales pitch that disarmed clients long before they realized he was there to dig at the soft, protected parts they had buried for years. 
He climbed the winding stone steps of the lighthouse to the lantern room, where Mara was already polishing the giant Fresnel lens, and found the line of wind chimes Lila had hung from the gallery rail when they were teens, strung with sea glass, cracked buoy bells, and shards of old pottery. 
Kael’s work never relied on grand speeches or moral lectures, because rigid certainty deflected those like rain off a lighthouse roof; instead, his primary method was deciphering wind chime selections, listening for the specific sequence of notes that carried the quiet truths clients had stopped letting themselves hear. 
He leaned against the rail for three quiet minutes, letting the wind sift through the chimes, catching the bright, trilling sequence that sounded like Lila’s laugh, the slow, heavy toll that sounded like Mara’s unwept tears, the soft, wavering note that said there was no single perfect way to keep people safe. 
When Mara finally turned to acknowledge him, her jaw set to turn away any stranger selling trinkets or dredging up old pain, Kael pulled the hollow mirror shard from his satchel, its surface fogged and thin, designed not to reflect the hard, certain face she presented to the world, but the hollow spaces where she had walled off her grief, her softness, her capacity to adapt. 
He delivered his usual opening line about selling custom good luck charms for lighthouse keepers, all casual charm and deliberate half-smiles, the sincerely insincere patter he had honed for years, and held the shard out so she could see the way it caught the dawn light, not as a harsh beam, but as scattered, soft flecks that painted the walls in wobbly rainbows. 
For a long minute, Mara stared at the shard, then at the wind chimes, which picked up a gust off the water and rang out the exact three-note sequence Lila used to whistle when she was calling Mara down to the shore to skip stones, and her shoulders softened, the stiff line of her spine losing the rigid edge it had held for decades. 
Kael did not lecture her about perfection, or grief, or the cost of certainty; he simply stepped close enough to reach the heavy, frayed edge of the doubt cloak slung tight around her shoulders, and waited for her to nod before he moved to unhook the brass clasp that held it fastened at her throat. 
The cloak fell away in a swirl of cold, damp air, and Mara gasped, not from pain, but from the lightness of being able to breathe without the weight of impossible perfection pressing down on her chest, finally able to welcome nuance into certainty, to accept that she could follow procedures and still listen to the wind, still grieve her sister, still make small, kind adjustments to the beacon when the chimes warned of shifting currents no timetable could predict. 
Kael slipped the hollow mirror shard into her palm before he left, letting the wind carry him back down the lighthouse steps, knowing she would keep it close to remind her that vulnerability did not erase her strength, and that the most reliable beacons were never rigid, unyielding things, but soft, shifting lights that made space for both certainty and doubt, for memory and for change.
