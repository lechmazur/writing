The knock on Clara’s office door landed soft as a moth wing ten minutes after the blood work came back, confirming her quiet hunch that her longest hospice patient, Eleanor Hale, had mere hours left before she crossed to the radiant haven above the clouds she’d described in glowing, fragmented stories for three straight years. 
Clara, the serene hospice music therapist, was known across the ward as decisively kind, the sort of caregiver who never dithered over small, meaningful gestures when a patient needed comfort, who traded formal care checklists for presence the second someone crossed into their final hours. 
Today, her sole motivation was to execute a flawless goodbye for Eleanor, who had grieved her husband Tom for seven years, ever since a winter car crash had snapped their favorite wedding hymn record in two as he’d reached for it on the dashboard mid-skid. 
Eleanor had kept one jagged piece of a broken vinyl record tucked under her pillow for all those years, saying she could not bear to play half a song without the person who’d taught her to dance to it. 
The matching shard had been pulled from the pocket of Tom’s frayed work jacket at the crash site, passed to Clara by the hospital chaplain six months prior with a note that Eleanor would know when it was time to hold both pieces again. 
When Clara pushed open Eleanor’s door, the room hummed with pulsing stillness, the quiet, thrumming hush that settles when the line between the living world and whatever comes next grows thin enough to see through. 
Eleanor’s eyes were half-lidded, but she smiled when Clara pulled a chair close to the bed, not reaching for her guitar or a printed hymnal as she usually did, but leaning in to begin hymning the mundane, the small, uncelebrated moments that had made up Eleanor and Tom’s life together. 
She sang soft, wordless lines that mimicked the gurgle of their morning coffee pot, the squeak of their front porch swing, the scratch of a record needle catching vinyl grooves before a favorite song starts, the rattle of Tom’s old pickup truck pulling into the drive after a long day at the lumber yard. 
As she sang, a soft, warm scent curled through the room, equal parts pine resin from the Christmas trees they’d cut every December and lemon polish from the kitchen table where they’d spread tax forms and birthday cards and crayon drawings from their grandkids. 
This was the scent of hope, Eleanor had always said, the exact smell of that radiant haven where no records broke and no goodbyes lasted forever. 
Clara slipped the second vinyl shard from her pocket, pulled the jagged piece from under Eleanor’s pillow, and pressed the two edges together to reunify the broken record in Eleanor’s outstretched palm, fitting the ragged cracks so perfectly no gap remained between them. 
Eleanor’s fingers curled tight around the mended plastic, her breathing slow and even as Clara kept singing, adding the pop of summer lemonade fizz, the crunch of fall leaves under their hiking boots, the soft hush of snow falling on the roof the night they’d brought their first daughter home from the hospital. 
For a long moment, the only sounds in the room were Clara’s soft voice and the faint, steady beep of the heart monitor, and the pine-and-lemon scent hung thick enough to taste, sweet and bright and unshakable even when the monitor’s tone flattened into a single, steady note. 
Clara sat for three full minutes in that thrumming quiet, not rushing to call the nurse, not rushing to tidy the linens or tuck the record shards into a labeled envelope for the family, because she knew the goodbye had been flawless, no unspoken words, no missed gestures, no awkward platitudes to dull the soft edge of the moment. 
She could almost picture Eleanor stepping through gilded cloud banks into the warm glow of that haven, the mended record pressed to her chest, already lifting a hand to wave at Tom, who waited with their old record player set up on a sun-warmed patch of grass, ready to let the needle drop on their song.
