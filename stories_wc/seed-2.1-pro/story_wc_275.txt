After the spire of the old Sky Patrol tower crumpled three months prior, dust still gilded the curved panes of Lila’s glass-domed desert rose farm, the only intact structure for forty miles across the cracked alkali flats. 
Lila, the bounty hunter’s pacifist daughter, had never picked up one of her father’s plasma carbines, though she had inherited his senselessly perceptive eye, the knack for picking out the faintest shift in light or weight that most folk missed entirely. 
Her driving motivation, in the unmoored months after the tower fell, was to weave continuity through chaos, to tend something steady when raider bands and scavenger crews picked through the wreckage of the old world just beyond her dome walls. 
She did this not with weapons or stockpiled ammo, but by inculcating plants with values, speaking soft, consistent lessons to each desert rose as she amended the sandy soil, adjusted the misters, plucked dead leaves from their succulent branches. 
The scorched afternoon light slanted through the dome in honeyed streaks, holding the tranquil blaze of a world that refused to stop burning even as new growth pushed up through ash, when her perceptive eye caught a glint of tattered canvas half-buried in the dust drifted against the dome’s outer seal. 
She slipped out through the airlock, cloth wrapped over her nose and mouth against the fine alkali dust, and pulled the fragment free: a frayed airship ballast sandbag scrap, stiff with old sand and singed along one edge from the tower’s explosive collapse. 
When she carried it inside, brushing dust from its weave, she found faint, hand-stamped letters pressed into the canvas, a cargo tag marking it as property of the very supply airship her father had been chasing the day the tower fell, the last bounty he had never collected. 
For three years, she had kept his short, sharp hunting dagger tucked on a shelf above her planting table, never touching its edge, never letting its cold steel break the soft hush of the dome, where the only sounds were the drip of misters and the faint rustle of succulent leaves. 
That hush was the engraved quiet she had tended so carefully, a stillness not of emptiness or fear, but of deliberate, carved-out peace, etched one gentle word to a rosebush at a time, no sharper than the thorn on a young stem. 
She took the stiff canvas scrap and folded it into a thick, soft liner, tucking it snugly inside the worn leather scabbard that had sat empty beside the dagger for all those years, because a weapon does not need to be destroyed to be made harmless, only given a gentler purpose. 
She lifted the dagger slowly, holding it up to the golden slant of light so its polished edge caught the pink and crimson blooms of the roses around her, and did not flinch when a distant rifle crack echoed from the flats beyond the dome, a reminder that the chaos outside had not abated. 
With steady hands, she began to sheathe the dagger, guiding it into the scabbard lined with the airship scrap, feeling the rough canvas catch gently on the blade’s sharp edge, holding it fast so it could never again cut anything but the soft, pressed petals she would tuck into the scabbard’s opening each spring. 
She thought of the lessons she whispered to her roses, of patience and rootedness and refusal to meet harm with harm, and knew the inculcated values did not end at the succulent stems, that they wove through every object she mended, every fragment of the broken world she repurposed into something that held life instead of taking it. 
Outside, the sun dipped lower, painting the alkali flats in burnt orange, and the tranquil blaze of the light through the glass gilded the hilt of the sheathed dagger where it sat on the planting table, a new marker of the continuity she was building, one small, gentle choice at a time. 
A young desert rose bud, swollen and blushed deep pink, leaned toward the dagger as if listening to the quiet etched into every part of the dome, proof that even the sharpest remnants of the old world could become part of the soft, unshakable peace she grew, one rooted, steady day after the fall.
