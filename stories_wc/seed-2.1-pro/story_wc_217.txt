The cobalt lagoon breathes slow after rain, its surface stippled with the last fat drips that slide from mangrove fronds to plink against half-buried shell shards. 
The quiet shoreline poet kneels at the high-tide line, her knees damp through frayed linen trousers, her pack slung over a gnarled driftwood root half-sheathed in moss. 
A high, thin crack cuts the soft air as a kite string snaps, sending a scrap of sun-bleached cotton shaped like a heron cartwheeling over the dunes to snag in a stand of sea oats. 
Warmly attentive to every small disruption of the post-storm calm, she lifts her chin to track the kite’s fall, her fingers still curled around the frayed edge of the tapestry she has spent three seasons stitching. 
She calls the work Woven Dawns, each thread plucked from discarded fishing line, shed bird plumage, dried sea grass, and scraps of sailcloth washed loose from passing skiffs, each strand holding a fragment of a morning that has felt too broken to hold on its own. 
Her motivation has never been to craft something perfect, but to discover beauty in the incomplete, to stitch together the bits of the world others step over or toss aside when they fail to fit a neat, expected shape. 
When she pushes through the sea oats to reach the tangled kite, she finds a juvenile mallard drake trapped in the cotton twists, one iridescent emerald tail feather half-loose, one wing twisted at an awkward angle where the string has cinched tight during its fall. 
She moves slow, soft words humming in her throat as she kneels to tend the small, trembling bird, her fingers working loose the knotted string without pulling at its caught feathers or jostling its sore wing. 
She does not rush to force its wing straight or strip loose the wobbly half-fallen feather, for she has long practiced mending not by forcing broken bits to match an unbroken ideal, but by harmonizing discord, finding the quiet resonance between hurt and healing, loss and new purpose. 
When the last loop of string slips free, the drake shakes itself, and that loosened tail feather drifts down to rest on the damp sand at her knee, its emerald edge shot through with a thread of cobalt that matches the lagoon’s post-storm glow. 
The burnished hush of the hour settles over them, gold-edged storm clouds bleeding soft pink into the sky as the first sliver of sun crests the dune line, gilding the drake’s iridescent head as it takes a wobbly step toward the lagoon. 
She does not reach for the feather until the drake has slipped into the shallow water, paddling slow toward the mangroves, its wing held slightly crooked but strong enough to carry it home. 
When she lifts the feather, she sees that its vane is tattered at the tip, chewed thin by burrs, marked with a faint smudge of kite string ink, and she smiles, knowing it is the perfect thread to tuck into the bare, gaping spot at the center of her Woven Dawns tapestry. 
That gap has been there for six weeks, left when a sharp gust ripped half-finished stitches free during a storm, and she has refused to patch it with uniform, unblemished thread, waiting instead for a fragment that carries the same messy, living weight as all the rest. 
She walks back to her driftwood perch, the feather held light between her fingers, and weaves its quill into the frayed edges of the gap, letting its tattered vane catch the growing dawn light so the cobalt streak glows like a small piece of the lagoon caught in cloth. 
The tattered kite, too, she weaves in, cutting small squares from its sun-bleached cotton to tuck beside the feather, so the sharp, unexpected snap of the string does not become a moment of loss, but a thread that ties the bird, the sky, the lagoon, and her own quiet work into one unbroken, if uneven, whole. 
Somewhere out in the mangroves, the drake gives a soft, trilling call, and the lagoon laps gentle at the shore, as if leaning in to admire the way broken, mismatched things can hold more light than any perfect, unmarked surface ever can.
