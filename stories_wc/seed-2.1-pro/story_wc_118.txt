The reclaimed silo library rose eleven stories above carpets of white clover, its pitted iron hull burnished to a soft bronze sheen by decades of wind and sun, in the long hush after the last human dream was dreamt. 
The planetary axis had slipped a hair’s breadth in that final moment of dream-fade, a displaced axis that did not crack continents or flood coasts but unspooled linear time just enough to leave snatches of sound, memory, and half-formed tale stuck fast in every wall, mortar line, and wooden beam like dust in a sunbeam. 
Its sole curator was an odd historian named Thorne, who had never learned to turn a deaf ear to the whispers of old places and was so sensitively perceptive he could trace the weight of a hundred-year-old laugh in a crack in plaster or the echo of a child’s sob caught in a window frame. 
His life’s work, his quiet unshakable motivation, was to collect the stories walls have heard, transcribing each faint fragment before the slow drag of the misaligned axis wore them away to silence forever. 
He did not use fancy recording devices or archival pens for this work, relying instead on a method passed down by the last generation of silo dwellers: through half-burned diaries found in a fireplace on the silo’s ground floor, charred volumes left by families who had sheltered there through the long, chaotic decades before the dreams stopped. 
The carbon on the charred page edges acted as a natural conductor for the stuck echoes, so he would tear a thin strip from a diary margin, press it to a seam in the wall, and probe for the thrum of a stored story the way a dowser probes for underground water, waiting for the faint tingle up his wrist that meant a memory had caught. 
On a gray afternoon when the bronze light slanted low through the silo’s top-floor windows, he worked his way along a section of interior wall built from stones salvaged from a ruined abbey thirty miles west, his pack slung over one shoulder holding half-transcribed tales of weavers, stable hands, and knights who had never made it into official history books. 
He pressed a charred diary strip to a pitted mortar gap, and the tingle came faster and sharper than it ever had before, pulling a memory so vivid he could smell wool dye and wood smoke as the echo resolved into the image of a young weaver’s apprentice tucking a fragment of a medieval tapestry showing dragons into the lining of her traveling cloak, the day she fled the abbey as raiders approached. 
The echo carried him along the apprentice’s whole journey: her weeks walking overgrown roads, her arrival at the half-buried silo that would become a refuge for dozens of wandering survivors, the day she pulled the tapestry fragment from her cloak and tucked it into the gap between two abbey stones as a talisman for the people who would shelter there long after she was gone. 
When the echo faded, Thorne ran his finger along the mortar gap, and his nail caught on the frayed edge of wool, pulling free the same tapestry fragment, its dragon threads still bright with indigo and cinnabar after eight hundred years tucked against the stone. 
It held none of the dust or rot of centuries, as if the displaced axis had tucked the small piece of story out of time, waiting for someone who would listen rather than catalog it as a disconnected artifact. 
He did not lock the fragment away in a climate-controlled case as more conventional archivists might have, but laid it flat on the scarred oak reading table by the top-floor window, where the warm bronze evening light could gild the curved, stitched spines of the dragons mid-flight. 
He pulled his leather-bound transcription journal from his pack, dipped his iron-gall pen in ink, and wrote down the weaver apprentice’s tale in slow, neat script, careful not to miss the small, bright details: the way she had fed a stray cat a crust of bread the morning she fled, the song she hummed as she walked, the quiet hope she had tucked into the wall alongside the tapestry fragment. 
In the hush of the silo, with no more dreams to spin new tales into the world, the work felt like holding a small, steady flame, each collected story a light against the long, soft quiet of the years to come.
