After time became visible, threading the void between rocky outposts like spun, honey-thick amber, the baroque asteroid Veyra drifted in its slow, tilted ellipse, its gilded spires pocked with micrometeorite scars and its vaulted plazas half-buried in pearlescent regolith. 
Its only permanent resident was Kael, a defunct revolutionary who had laid down his plasma welder a decade prior after his movement to free outer-system settlements collapsed under Concord bombardment, his poetically inclined temperament softening the jagged edge of his unprocessed loss into something slow, warm, and unshakably determined. 
The off-world Concord archaeological team that visited every three solar cycles peddled a single, unchallenged narrative: Veyra had been a decadent, tyrannical outpost whose population had welcomed orbital bombardment as liberation, a claim anchored in curated artifacts pulled exclusively from the governor’s bombed-out palace, and this carefully massaged falsehood formed the core of a bitter, years-long archaeological dispute Kael had no formal credentials to fight. 
Kael’s motivation, quiet and unshakable through every long, cold void-night, was to reimagine an entire civilization stripped of Concord propaganda, to recover the voices of the weavers, potters, poets, and rebels who had lived and loved on Veyra before the skies burned. 
He knew the risks of digging too deep, for when the periodic solar radiation storms swept across the asteroid belt, time tangled thick enough to seep through every polished, baroque mirror set into Veyra’s walls, projecting false, Concord-seeded memories of a cruel populace to any onlooker who met their glass. 
To protect his work and his own memories from this tampering, he followed the old Veyran folk practice of covering mirrors during storms, draping them in faded tapestry scraps he pulled from collapsed residential domes so no distorted, weaponized past could seep into his thoughts. 
It was on a dig in a small, tile-floored cottage at the edge of the plaza, three days after the last storm had passed, that his trowel brushed against a sharp, glinting object half-buried in fine dust: a crystal vase shard, etched with a tiny, looping scene of two people passing a loaf of bread between them under a flowering vine, its edge smoothed by decades of wind-blown regolith. 
The shard did not match the grand, militaristic crystal vessels the Concord team pulled from the palace, shards they claimed proved Veyra’s elite hoarded resources while commoners starved, and Kael held it up to the faint, amber glow of visible time drifting past the dome’s cracked viewport, his chest tight with a burnished sorrow that felt less like grief and more like recognition. 
In that moment, he began to devise a new kind of archaeological record, one built not from grand palace artifacts or official state edicts, but from the small, intimate traces of ordinary life: chipped teacups, children’s chalk drawings on stone walls, tapestry scraps stitched with wildflower patterns, and this single, perfect crystal shard that told a story of shared bread and quiet joy. 
As a poetically inclined observer who had long measured history in the weight of small, held things rather than the bluster of state power, he did not need peer-reviewed journals or institutional backing to make his record real; he wrote the stories of Veyra’s people on cured hide scrolls, tucking them into crevices across the asteroid where future travelers might find them, untainted by Concord lies. 
He did not delude himself that his work would undo the bombardment, or bring back the neighbors he had known as a child when his own family had fled Veyra on the last evacuation shuttle, but he knew that a civilization remembered through its small, gentle acts was a civilization that could never be fully erased. 
When the next storm rolled in three weeks later, he draped every mirror carefully, then set the crystal vase shard on a small stone shelf above his writing desk, where it caught the amber drift of visible time like a tiny, glowing lens. 
Outside, the solar wind howled through the gilded spires, carrying the faint, distorted whispers of Concord’s fake past, but inside, surrounded by scrolls and teacup shards and the quiet weight of memory, Kael wrote of the two people who had once owned the vase, of the bread they had shared, of the vine that had grown outside their cottage door. 
The burnished sorrow in his chest softened into something like hope, for he knew that even the most carefully constructed lie could not outlast a single, true story, held tight and passed on, like a shard of light cutting through the dark.
