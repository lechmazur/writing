Elara woke curled on the threadbare wool rug of the quiet ridge tower at dawn, the throbbing ache in her left palm sharpening the moment the last of the local anesthesia from her scar tissue biopsy wore off. 
She was the kind of closure seeker who opened wounds rather than letting them scab over, having spent three years picking at the ragged silver scar on that palm, replaying the night a searing meteor blazed over the ridge and her younger sister Lila vanished mid-laugh, unable to stop searching even when friends begged her to let the grief soften. 
On the scarred oak table before her sat the frosted hourglass Lila had left propped against their scuffed stargazing log the night of the strike, its fine gold sand suspended mid-flow, as if the meteor’s shockwave had frozen the moment between one shared laugh and the next. 
When she shifted to push herself upright, her bandaged palm brushed the thick emerald moss creeping up the tower’s sandstone base, and a soft, rustling voice flooded her head, no louder than crushed clover, a strange, unlisted side effect of the biopsy’s anesthetic cocktail leaving her accidentally telepathic with plants. 
The moss murmured of cold spring seeps and dappled summer sun, of paths pressed into leaf litter by small, hurried feet, and when she turned her gaze back to the table, she noticed the faint, layered brown coffee rings left by the chipped ceramic mug she had carried up the ridge before sunrise, and leaned in to begin examining coffee rings the way she had long studied the faint, branching lines of her palm scar. 
She had climbed the tower that gray, soft morning driven by a quiet, unshakable motivation: to learn to read the stories in scars, not just the sharp, jagged narrative of loss she had carried for three years, but the softer, hidden stories of connection that outlast the initial blaze of pain. 
As she traced the widest coffee ring with the tip of her uninjured finger, the English ivy coiled thick around the tower’s long-bricked hearth pulled away of its own accord, its tendrils moving slow as breath, to reveal a narrow, root-walled tunnel sloping down into the dark, its earthen walls glowing with the faint cool luminescence of the moss she had touched minutes before. 
The moss’s voice softened as she knelt at the tunnel mouth, explaining the displaced miracle that had stolen Lila three years prior: the two girls had made a whispered wish that night, as the meteor blazed overhead, to never lose the ability to talk to each other, no matter how far apart they grew, but the meteor’s wild, uncalibrated magic had snagged the wish mid-cast. 
Instead of weaving a permanent telepathic bond between the sisters as intended, the magic had yanked Lila into the thin, root-crossed space between world-threads, trapping her just out of ordinary reach, leaving only the frozen hourglass and the scar on Elara’s palm as a frayed, glowing thread between them. 
The whole world hung in that familiar meteor hush, no wind sifting through the ridge pines, no wren call cutting the gray dawn light, no distant hum of the town in the valley below, as Elara lifted the frosted hourglass from the table and tucked it into the breast pocket of her flannel shirt. 
She felt a faint tingle against her chest as she did, and glanced down to see the long-frozen gold sand beginning to trickle again, measuring out the first steady seconds of connection instead of loss. 
The moss whispered that the tunnel’s root walls hummed with the same frequency as her scar, that if she followed the soft glow, she would not find a ghost to bury, but a sister waiting to talk, the bond they had wished for finally settling into place after years of being knocked loose from its intended path. 
She lowered herself carefully into the tunnel mouth, the throb in her palm softening instead of sharpening as the roots curled gently around her wrists to guide her down, and for the first time in three years, she did not feel like she was picking open a wound to chase a hollow memory, but like she was reading the first clear, warm line of the story her scar had held all along.
