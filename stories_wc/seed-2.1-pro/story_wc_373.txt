Beneath the splintered, rot-soft slats of Brineport Pier, the pirate market hummed so low it barely rumbled the barnacle-crusted stalls, bound for three generations by a truce of hush that forbade loud quarrels, snitching to lawmen, and any grudge carried past the first pink edge of sunrise. 
The air smelled like spiced rum, fried conch, and the briny, mildewed tang of waterlogged timber, a familiar warmth tangled tight with the sharp, unshakable prickle of danger that clung to every shadow, a mood regulars called comforting unease. 
Lila, the moonshine runner’s daughter, had snuck past the torch-toting sentries with a frayed wool shawl pulled tight over her shoulders, her boots squelching in cold tidal muck as she hunted the turncoat who had framed her father for smuggling, landing him a two-year sentence in the state penitentiary. 
Curiously steadfast for a girl who had spent her teens hauling mason jars of white lightning up rutted back roads at midnight, she carried no knife, no pistol, only the dented antique watch fob her father had given her for her sixteenth birthday, fitted with a tiny wax cylinder recorder built to capture sounds too quiet for most ears to hold. 
Her goal was simple: to capture the sound of trust breaking, the exact split second the turncoat, a former market regular named Rook, struck his deal with the customs agents waiting on the pier above, selling out every vendor and smuggler under the boards for full immunity and a fistful of cash. 
She knew she had only until sunrise, when the truce of hush dissolved and Rook planned to lead the agents down the splintered access ladder to make their arrests, before the market packed up its wares and vanished into the coastal mist. 
Her efforts to sneak close enough to record him unfolded through false starts: she tripped over a crate of stolen pocket watches and drew the eye of a tattooed rum seller, she fumbled the fob and sent it clattering into a puddle of fish guts, she mistook a pair of whispering oyster shuckers for Rook and his contact and wasted ten minutes huddled behind a pile of fishing net while they debated the price of fresh clams. 
Each misstep made her chest tight, but she did not rush, pausing to brush muck off the fob, to nod politely at the vendor selling carved bone dice, to breathe through the prickle of paranoia that told her every shadow hid an informant ready to turn her over to Rook before she could get her proof. 
When she finally spotted Rook huddled on an overturned crate at the far end of the market, speaking into a crackling walkie-talkie held close to his mouth, she crept within arm’s reach, pressing the record stud on the fob just as he laughed and told the agents on the line he would leave the access ladder unlatched in five minutes, no one in the market would suspect a thing. 
The fob whirred so softly only she could hear it, capturing the thin, reedy edge of his voice, the distant lap of tide against the pier pilings, the faint, sharp crack of the truce of hush fracturing the second he put profit over the community that had hidden him from the law for ten years. 
She stepped out from behind the piling before he could click off the walkie-talkie, holding the fob aloft so he could see the tiny recording cylinder spinning, and called out loud enough for every vendor within thirty feet to hear, laying bare his betrayal for the whole market to act on. 
In that moment, she did not just thwart Rook’s scheme to hand the market over to the agents; she gave the ragtag group of smugglers, fishermen, and outcasts enough warning to scatter their wares, latch the access ladder, and melt into the tidal caves before the first sunbeam peeked over the horizon, the recorded sound of his betrayal playing over and over from the fob’s tiny speaker to confirm no one else had been part of the plot. 
She slipped the fob back into her shawl pocket as the market vanished, knowing the recording would not only clear her father’s name, but stitch the frayed edges of the truce back together long after the tide washed the last traces of the market from the mud under the rotting pier.
