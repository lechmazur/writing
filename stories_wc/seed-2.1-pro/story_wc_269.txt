Forty-two years after the global fossil fuel ban took effect, Elara tended the rust-flecked sea level monitoring station moored three miles off the remains of the Great Barrier Reef's northern fringe, a structure that felt both rooted to the seabed and perpetually drifting on the slow, breathing swells of the Coral Sea. 
Now 78, she was one of the last living coral bleaching witnesses, present for the 2040s mass bleaching event that had turned 90 percent of the reef bone white before the ban's cascading ecological repairs had begun to stitch scattered patches of coral back to vivid life. 
She had stayed at the station for 17 years, driven by a quiet, unshakable motivation to discover why some paths disappear--why the winding mangrove channels she had kayaked as a teen had erased themselves under rising tides, why the coral cays where she had camped as a researcher had dissolved into sand and surf even as emissions fell and recovery took root elsewhere. 
She kept time with her searches through a practice of tapping rhythms on surfaces: the hollow rail of the station's deck, the frosted face of the tide gauge, the glass jar that held the water samples she pulled twice daily, tapping the same three-count pattern her grandmother had taught her to signal safe passage across reef flats. 
Tucked in the breast pocket of her oilskin coat, she carried a tattered atlas page torn out of a 1998 maritime map of the reef, its edges soft from decades of salt spray, its ink bleeding where a 2047 storm surge had soaked through while she clung to the station's antenna during the worst of the bleaching-era king tides. 
The reef itself felt periodically eternal: it died back in swathes, regrew in bursts, vanished under heat waves only to send up new polyps a decade later, as if time moved in loops rather than straight lines for the living structures that built the seabed over millennia. 
On the deck outside her small cabin, she had begun to assemble a mosaic of found objects tied to the lost paths she tracked: shards of ceramic from old research station mugs, smooth sea glass from the bottles she had tossed overboard as a grad student, fragments of staghorn coral that had broken off during storms, each placed to match the coordinates marked on her tattered atlas page. 
One gray afternoon, as rain spotted the deck and the tide gauge ticked up a millimeter higher than the week prior, she leaned against the rail and tapped her three-count rhythm, her gaze drifting over the mosaic she had built piece by piece over 12 years. 
The revelation did not hit her in a sudden crash, but unspooled slow as a tide, the delayed payoff for decades of quiet watching: the paths she thought had disappeared had not been erased, only moved, reshaped by the same slow, cyclical growth and retreat that made the reef periodically eternal. 
She pulled the torn atlas page from her pocket, laying it flat against the mosaic to compare its inked lines to the arrangement of shards and coral, and saw that the new channels cutting through the recovering reef sat exactly parallel to the old paths, shifted just enough that a human eye fixed on loss would miss the echo. 
For decades, she had grieved the vanished cays and channels as permanent deaths, unable to see that the reef was not a static map to be memorized, but a living thing that remade its roads the same way the tides remade the shore, one slow shift at a time. 
She tapped her three-count rhythm on the atlas page, the sound soft against the crinkled paper, and felt the familiar drifting moored feeling that came with living half in the bleached past of her youth and half in the vivid, growing present of the recovering sea: she was anchored to the station, to her memories, to the reef, but unmoored from the old belief that lost things stayed gone forever. 
A school of neon parrotfish darted past the station's pilings, following one of the new reef channels, and she smiled, knowing she would spend the rest of her days tracing both the lost and found paths that wove through the periodically eternal waters around her.
