The zen-minded potter, who had walked three weeks across crusted alkali flats navigating by following sun patterns, ducked under the splintered wooden arch marking the abandoned beekeeping oasis in the scorched wasteland just as the last wisp of her high-desert camp’s chimney smoke flattened into a hazy, copper-toned sheet across the brassy horizon. 
Her quiet, unshakable motivation for the grueling trek was straightforward, unclouded by desire for treasure or old-world salvage: to understand why some places resist change, even when fractured timelines warped the rest of the wasteland into jagged, overlapping shards of past and future that erased roads, homesteads, and whole stands of sage in the span of a single breath. 
She had expected dust-choked hive boxes, the dry rattle of tumbleweeds tangled in split fence posts, and the sharp, metallic tang of baked topsoil, but instead she caught the faint, honeyed scent of white clover hanging in the still air, and spotted a single pane of faded lantern glass propped against the rough stone base of an intact cedar hive stand, its surface frosted where decades of sun and grit had scoured away its original clear factory coat. 
She knelt slowly, careful not to crush the tiny clover sprigs pushing up through cracked clay at her knees, not reaching for the glass immediately but bowing her head in quiet respect for the place that had outlasted so much, and felt the warm air around the lantern glass thrum, soft as the collective wingbeat of a hundred foraging bees, as if the oasis was testing the weight of her intent. 
The potter knew, from years of walking the warped wasteland, that the latent magic stitched into these holdfast places was phototropic to kindness: it bent toward soft, generous intent the way desert sunflowers bent toward midday light, and turned cold, opaque, and unyielding to anyone who arrived ready to take, to strip, to carve up a place for profit or trivial curiosity. 
When she lifted the glass carefully, holding it up to the flat, copper-tinged light, she did not see her own reflection, but the overlapping, fractured timelines the oasis had anchored across a hundred and twenty years, each stitched to a small, unrecorded act of care. 
She saw the original beekeeper leaving pans of sugar water out for his hives during the decade-long drought that baked every other homestead to dust, sharing his last jar of honey with a feverish refugee child, prying loose a cactus spine from a coyote’s paw even when he had barely enough food for himself, his hands chapped and bleeding from the unforgiving wind. 
She understood then, watching the timelines weave tight around those small, uncelebrated moments, that places did not resist change because of strong walls or old spells, but because the weight of consistent, unplanned kindness acted as a keel, holding the ground steady even when the world around it split and slid between possible worlds. 
As a potter, she knew this truth in her hands: clay did not hold its form because it was fired to unbreakable hardness, but because every slow, intentional press of the palm anchored it to the shape it was meant to hold, a parallel to the small, kind presses that had held this oasis fast through every timeline fracture. 
She did not take the faded lantern glass with her when she left as dusk settled, knowing it belonged as the oasis’s quiet eye; instead she left a small, hand-thrown clay bowl of clear water at the base of the hive stand, a small act of care to add to the weave, so the place would hold fast for the next traveler lost in the shifting wasteland. 
She did not fool herself into thinking one small bowl would stop the timeline fractures forever, or that the wasteland would soften its scorched, unforgiving edge for anyone, but she knew every small, kind act was a thread strong enough to hold a little patch of ground steady for someone who needed it most. 
When she glanced back over her shoulder as she followed the sun’s last slanted rays toward her next camp, she saw the faded lantern glass catch the light, its thrum so soft it almost blended with the first tentative buzz of a handful of bees returning to their hives, as if the oasis was saying thank you.
