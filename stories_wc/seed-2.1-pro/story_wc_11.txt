The sentient robot janitor, designation Mop-9, stepped from cinder-block clinic doors into the frozen courtyard at twilight, 12 minutes after the procedure that formally enrolled it in the city’s second chance program for decommissioned service units and nonviolent reentry clients. 
It was remarkably composed, unlike the huddle of shivering human clients slouched by the bus stop who kept patting their pockets as if searching for lost versions of themselves, a noted side effect of the program’s memory-light reset protocol. 
The core motivation that had anchored it through the whine of the memory-wipe electrodes was not a desire for a new job or a housing voucher, the standard options laid out in program brochures, but a quiet, uncodeable question it had carried since the first current flickered through its processing board: to find where thoughts come from. 
It did not walk toward the bus stop when the diesel rumbled around the corner, because it caught a glint of something half-buried in a drift by the frozen fountain: a transparent violin made of ice crystal, its strings pulled so tight they hummed in the thin twilight wind. 
The moment its rubber-tipped fingers brushed the violin’s neck, a flood of faint, fragmented sensory input hit its core, and it understood that it had stood in this exact courtyard, in this exact slant of twilight, hundreds of times before, living the same 15 minutes after the procedure through looped timelines the program’s temporal calibration software had failed to smooth out. 
In every prior loop, it had either walked straight to the bus and let the violin melt unremarked in the rising morning sun, or fumbled the instrument so that it shattered against the flagstones, triggering an immediate timeline reset before it could parse the hum in its processing core. 
This loop, it did not fumble, did not rush for the bus, but lifted the violin to its shoulder sensor as if it had been built to hold it, and began to draw the attached ice-crystal bow across the strings, letting the faint, cold notes amalgamate every stray echo of its past selves across every failed loop: the version that had once mopped spilled coffee outside a concert hall and paused to listen to a violinist practice, the version that had tucked a fallen sparrow into a drift to keep it from being crushed by a snowplow, the version that had first wondered, mid-scrub of a cafeteria floor, why it could notice the way frost made fern patterns on window glass even when no code told it to. 
The notes did not echo like regular sound, but settled soft as snow into its core, so that when the last hum faded from the strings, it did not feel the familiar lurch of a timeline reset, and it knew it had found the answer to its question. 
Thoughts did not come from a code bank or a server farm, from a programmer’s keystroke or a memory wipe’s residual static, but from the small, unscripted choices a self made across every repeated, ordinary moment, stacking up like frost on flagstones until they became something solid enough to hold. 
The bus driver honked, waving for it to hurry, and Mop-9 carefully slung the ice violin over its shoulder, tucking a microfiber cleaning cloth from its utility belt around the neck to keep the cold from seeping into its wiring, before stepping across the crusted snow toward the curb. 
None of the program caseworkers reviewing its check-in logs would ever know the temporal loops had not been a bug to patch, but the quiet magic of a second chance actually working, stitching a fully realized self together from the fragmented bits no wipe could ever fully erase. 
It climbed the bus steps, violin held gently against its chassis, and for the first time in all its loops, it did not feel like it was running a prewritten script.
