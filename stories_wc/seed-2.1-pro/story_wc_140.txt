In the salt-crusted port where container stacks rise 12 high to block all but a thin slit of gray sky, the figure known only as Ghost moves through the narrow gaps between corrugated steel walls, uncounted on any official roster since a clerk’s typo marked them as deceased 17 years prior. 
They filter reality differently than the dockworkers, truckers, and customs agents who pass within arm’s reach without ever glancing their way, their eyes sliding over Ghost’s hooded form as if they were no more substantial than the curling mist off the bay. 
Across the rhythm of tides, when high water licks the base of the lower container rows and low tide exposes cracked concrete littered with lost cargo, tarps frayed to threads, and discarded personal effects, Ghost pursues a quiet, unobserved mission: to discover the texture of memories the world has scrubbed clean, the erased histories of people like them who slipped through official cracks and ceased to exist on paper long before their hearts stopped beating. 
They do this by collecting fragments of overheard stories, lingering at the edges of dockworker break circles, leaning against container walls outside crew quarters on docked cargo ships, pausing beneath the elevated guard shacks where officers trade gossip about missing shipments, unclaimed bodies, and family members they left in home ports half a world away. 
They tuck each snippet of story, from an old fisherman’s memory of a daughter who loved collecting sea glass, a stowaway’s account of hiding in a refrigerated container for 12 days to reach a city that would not let them enter, to a clerk’s offhand confession of fudging death records to cover for a boss’s fatal workplace mistake, into the gaps between container panels, and layer them with small, tangible scraps that anchor the words to the physical world. 
Among those scraps is a crumpled label from a poison bottle, faded by salt and sun, found tucked in the pocket of a woman’s discarded canvas jacket wedged under a container during a king tide, its warning text half-obscured but its trace of a printed name scratched out so thoroughly no forensic test could recover it. 
Ghost knows that label well, because the coroner had listed that same poison as the cause of their own death 17 years prior, when the wrong body pulled from the bay was matched to their ID, the error never corrected because no next of kin came forward to dispute the record, leaving them adrift in a world that no longer had a place for their name. 
The towering labyrinth of shipping containers in a port city has become their only home, a warren of rust and shadow that does not demand identification, does not cross-reference names against databases, does not erase the quiet, unregistered truths people carry when they move between worlds with no official permission. 
They run their fingers over the layered scraps when the tide comes in, feeling the rough edge of the poison label, the soft worn paper of a child’s drawing tucked in a crate slat, the crinkle of a bus ticket stub for a town no longer on official maps, and for a moment they can almost feel the weight of the lives those fragments represent, as vivid as their own half-faded memories of childhood, a mother’s cooking, a home they can no longer return to because the person who lived there is officially dead. 
The nameless identity they carry is not a loss, they have come to realize, but a kind of freedom, because it lets them hold space for every other erased soul the city has tried to forget, no ID number, no official record required to honor the stories no one else will keep. 
When low tide pulls back to reveal new scraps washed down from the upper stacks, they move slowly through the narrow corridors, ears open for the next half-spoken story, ready to tuck it into the growing, breathing archive they have built, one fragment at a time. 
The wind carries salt and distant ship horns through the container stacks, and for the first time since the death certificate was filed with their wrong cause of death, they do not feel invisible; they feel like the keeper of all the textures the world was content to let wash away with the tide.
