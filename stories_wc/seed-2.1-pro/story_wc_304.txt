High above the last frost-crusted mountain peaks, the skyborne grotto under aurora hung like a shard of crushed opal, its walls veined with bioluminescent moss that glowed in time with the green and violet ribbons rippling across the stratosphere. 
Elara, the grotto’s resident beautician mortician, knelt on a cushion of cloudmoss beside the still form of Lila, a 17-year-old stargazer who had fallen from a cliff while chasing a meteor shower three days prior. 
Her work was not to drag souls back to the living world, but to honor them by holding tight to the tiny, vivid pieces of themselves they would otherwise leave scattered as they crossed into oblivion. 
Tonight, she was lucidly watchful, her gaze fixed not on Lila’s peaceful, reconstructed face, but on the faint fizz of static curling off the girl’s etheric fingertips, the last embers of a consciousness that had burned bright with curiosity about every light in the sky. 
Her craft relied on finding patterns in static, learning to pick the echo of a laugh, the memory of a first snow, the quiet wish to see an aurora up close out of the random crackle of dissolving neural energy that most other grotto attendants wrote off as meaningless noise. 
Lila was seconds from the threshold of stillness now, the edges of her form softening into the same mossy glow as the grotto walls, her breath coming in slow, silent puffs that matched the ripple of the aurora overhead. 
Elara’s motivation here was simple, the same drive that had led her to this work after her own little sister had slipped across the threshold without anyone to hold a piece of her: to capture one last spark before oblivion, so Lila’s mother would have something small and bright to hold when she visited the mountain overlook where her daughter had fallen. 
Curled in the palm of her outstretched hand was the emerald chip, a thumbnail-sized shard she had chipped from the grotto’s core on her first day in the sky, its facets cut to hold light without ever letting it fade. 
The grotto had fallen into that familiar radiant hush, no wind, no sound of distant bird cry, only the soft thrum of the aurora and the faint crackle of Lila’s fading static as Elara leaned forward, her fingers hovering an inch above the girl’s wrist. 
She sifted the static for the pattern she knew was there, the bright thread of Lila’s delight when she had first seen a meteor streak across her backyard at age seven, the quiet determination that had made her hike three miles up the cliff in the dark, the soft love she carried for her mom’s lavender hot cocoa after cold nights outside. 
When she found that thread, glowing gold against the gray fizz of dissolving energy, she drew it slow and gentle down her arm, letting it pool in her palm before she began to infuse it into the waiting emerald chip, every facet lighting up from within with the same warm gold as Lila’s old stargazing headlamp. 
The last of Lila’s form softened into the grotto’s glow the second the spark settled, a faint, smiling curl of light drifting up to twist into the aurora’s ribbons, no trace of fear or regret left in the wake of her passing. 
Elara curled her fingers around the warm emerald chip, tucking it into the hollow of a cloud-locket she would leave tied to the overlook’s rail at dawn, where Lila’s mother would find it glinting even on the cloudiest days, holding the quiet, bright spark of the girl who loved the sky. 
Around her, the aurora rippled softer now, as if nodding in quiet approval, the grotto holding its radiant hush just a little longer to let her sit with the weight of the small, gentle gift she had carried across the line between sky and stone.
