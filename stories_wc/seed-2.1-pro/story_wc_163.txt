The landscape astrologer picked her way across the silt-crusted catwalk of the forgotten underwater city research facility, her rubber boots squelching over faded 1970s survey tags half-buried in marsh muck that had seeped through the cracked pressure seals decades prior. 
She had traveled three coastal bus routes and hiked two miles of tidal trail for one reason: to learn the rhythm of belonging, a pulse she had never managed to trace in the neat suburban birth charts or rigid almanac forecasts that paid her rent. 
The facility, half-submerged in the brackish backchannel where the coastal marsh sloped into the sunken remains of a pre-colonial trade settlement, had been abandoned after a 1982 storm flooded the observation bays, leaving angled glass walls that filtered twilight into wavering, indigo streaks across concrete walls papered with water-warped star charts and fish population surveys. 
On the rusted central observation table, she found a burnt feather, its charred black tip frayed to soft down, its pale gold vane still catching the faint, slanted light as if it held the last embers of a signal fire lit for travelers long gone. 
She was not alone: an elderly former research tech, who had stayed to tend the marsh after the facility closed, sat on a cinder block by the cracked bay window, whittling a reed flute, and offered her a chipped mug of foraged blackberry tea without preamble. 
The astrologer, whose practice relied on reading the way land and sky stitched light together rather than parsing distant, cold star positions alone, carried a softly ingenious set of tools: a frosted glass astrolabe calibrated to marsh tides, a journal bound in cattail fiber, and a small tin of beeswax to seal pressed plant specimens between its pages. 
She explained her search for the rhythm of belonging as the old tech poured a second mug of tea, describing how she had chased solstice light across mountain ridges and meteor showers over prairie grasslands without ever feeling like she was in the right place at the right time to hold that rhythm close. 
The old tech nodded, tapping the burnt feather where it lay on the table, and explained it had fallen from a marsh hawk that had nested in the facility’s broken rafter for 12 years, a creature that belonged neither fully to the sunken city below the water nor the open sky above, but to the thin, shimmering seam where the two met. 
Dusk deepened to indigo as they sat in quiet, the distant gurgle of tidal water seeping through the walls mixing with the trill of marsh frogs, and they decided to stay until sunrise, watching the light shift across the sunken ruins instead of rushing back to paved roads and scheduled lives. 
When the last faint glow of twilight bled out of the sky, they leaned toward the cracked window together, a shared exhale spotting the first fireflies of summer rising from the marsh grass, their tiny gold lights bobbing above the water where the sunken city’s stone foundations jutted through the silt. 
The sight did more than delight the astrologer: it managed to captivate the part of her that had spent years chasing belonging across vast, dramatic landscapes, the part that had assumed connection required a grand, blazing sign rather than small, slow, shared light. 
For hours, they sat in that luminous respite, watching the fireflies drift over the ruins, the old tech playing soft notes on his reed flute while the astrologer tucked the burnt feather into her journal, mapping the pattern of the firefly glows against the faint starlight visible through the cracked ceiling to mark the rhythm she had spent so long searching for. 
She realized the rhythm of belonging was not a fixed point to be found in a distant star or a perfect unbroken landscape, but a pulse stitched from the seams between worlds: between water and sky, ruin and regrowth, stranger and friend, between the last dark of night and the first pink glow of dawn creeping over the marsh as sunrise neared. 
When the first sunbeam slanted through the observation bay to gild the edge of the burnt feather peeking out of her journal, she knew she would not need to chase that rhythm any longer, for she had finally learned to breathe in time with it.
