Long after the last glow-fruit dimmed to a soft, pearlescent smolder and the cavern orchard grew silent beneath the moon, the secretive conjurer stepped off the worn stone path to trace the line of trees nourished by constant, cold, mineral-rich drips from jagged stalactites overhead. 
She had come to this tucked-away grove for three years running, driven by a quiet, unshakable motivation: to understand the mathematics of emotion, the uncountable variables that had made her sister walk into the orchard one twilight and never return, leaving no body, no note, only the faint, unresolved shape of ambiguous loss that clung to every drip and curled leaf. 
From the frayed wool pouch at her belt, she withdrew the taxidermist's glass eye pair her sister, who ran the valley’s tiny taxidermy shop, had left on their shared workbench the day she vanished, one hazel flecked with gold, one soft gray, polished smooth from three years of being clutched in the conjurer’s palm during every secret midnight trek. 
Her craft was gently inventive, reliant not on thunderous incantations or smoldering sigils but on the quiet alignment of forgotten, loved things, so she knelt before the cracked mosaic stepping stone half-buried in moss at the orchard’s heart, tracing its chipped patterns that mapped constellations the two of them had invented as children. 
She cast her quiet spell via the chipped patterns on the mosaic stepping stone, matching each fleck of missing tile to a half-remembered moment: the time they’d stolen honey from the orchard hives, the way her sister had laughed so hard she snort-laughed when a bee landed on her nose, the quiet promise they’d made to tend the orchard together for as long as the stalactites dripped. 
She set one glass eye in the chip where the mosaic showed their shared favorite star, and the other in the narrow crack that marked the orchard’s oldest apple tree, letting the slow, cool drip of stalactite water pool around each lens until the edges of memory and present began to dovetail, no harsh lines separating what was from what might be. 
For years she had chased a precise, solvable equation for grief, convinced that if she could map every variable of her sister’s absence--every word left unsaid, every chore left undone, every fruit left unpicked--she could solve for the answer of where her sister had gone, erasing the ambiguous loss that had settled like fine silt over every corner of her life. 
The glass eyes did not spark, or roar, or summon her sister back in a flash of light, as the loud, flashy conjuring manuals she’d studied in secret had promised they might; instead, they caught the faint silver of moonlight filtering through the cavern’s high mouth, and for a second she saw it: the mathematics of emotion was not a problem to solve, but a joint to fit, a shape that held space for both presence and absence at once. 
A stalactite dripped directly onto the hazel eye, and the ripple that spread across the shallow pool cast an image on the moss at her knees: her sister, older now, tending a grove just like this one somewhere far away, holding a half-eaten glow-fruit, smiling like she knew someone was thinking of her. 
She did not have the clean answers she had come seeking three years prior; she did not know if her sister would ever come back, if she was even alive, if the image in the pool was a trick of refracted moonlight or a true thread stretched between two distant, stalactite-fed groves. 
But she left the glass eyes set in the mosaic that night, tucked just under a layer of soft sphagnum moss where no casual forager or wandering explorer would find them, and as she turned to walk back toward the cavern mouth, she noticed a single young glow-fruit sapling pushing up through cracked stone directly beside the stepping stone, fed by the same slow, mineral-rich drips that had nourished the orchard for centuries. 
The ambiguous loss would never resolve into a neat, final answer, but she would return every moonlit night to tend the new sapling, holding space for the quiet, unproven hope that somewhere, across any uncrossable distance, her sister was doing the same.
