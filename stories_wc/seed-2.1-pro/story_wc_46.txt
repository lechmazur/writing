The serpentine grotto in mist curled along the jagged Pacific coast, its smooth, water-worn walls winding in tight, looping curves that held fog like crumpled silk. 
It was here the earthbound star watcher made her workshop, three years after a misstep on the aurora bridge sent her tumbling from the sky to the wet, salt-kissed shore, her ability to call down crackling starfire snuffed out by the shock of mortal air. 
Her core motivation had not dimmed in all that time: to share light without using fire, for she had seen too many coastal villages scorched by runaway campfires, too many seabird nests smoldering from sparks blown off careless torches. 
As memories resurfaced--of spinning star threads into constellations, of the soft glow that lit sky paths without heat or flame--she leaned over her stone workbench, fingers brushing the collection of sea glass she had gathered from the tide line over three years of walking the shore. 
Her craft was gem polishing, learned from an old lighthouse keeper who had taken her in her first storm-battered winter, using smooth river stone grit and beeswax to buff dull, frosted surfaces into something that could hold light like a vessel. 
She would weigh each shard in her palm first, judging its thickness, the curve of its edges, how its frosted surface caught the faint, mist-diffused light that seeped through the grotto's mouth. 
On this particular afternoon, her fingers closed around a shard of pale aqua sea glass, its edges worn smooth by a hundred years of tides, a fragment of an old ship's deck light lost in an 1870s gale. 
As she worked, moving the shard in slow, circular motions across her grit stone, the fog shifted outside, sending thin zephyrs curling through the grotto that carried the scent of coastal blackberry bloom, soft enough not to disturb the loose grit on her workbench. 
Memories drifted sharper now: the quiet hush of the sky between stars, the way light did not need to burn to travel, the way a single held glow could calm a panicking traveler, soothe a crying child, guide a lost fishing boat home through fog without singeing a single blade of grass. 
When she wiped the last of the beeswax from the shard with a scrap of old sailcloth, she held it up to the grotto's faint light and gasped, for the surface was reflectively vibrant, catching not just the muted daylight but the faint, stored glow of a hundred years of sunlit tides, of starlight that had slanted through wave crests, of the moon's soft silver kiss on midnight water. 
At the very center of the shard, nestled where decades of tide friction had worn a tiny, perfect indentation, sat a pearl of stillness: a concentrated point of quiet light that did not flicker, did not heat, did not consume fuel, but held steady like the first breath of dawn over a calm sea. 
A soft zephyr curled through the grotto then, carrying the first bloom of late spring wild roses from the headlands, and the light from the sea glass shard spilled across the basalt walls, gilding the tiny moss fronds and casting soft, warm circles that did not scorch, did not smoke, simply was. 
She knew then that her years of gathering, of weighing shards, of polishing worn glass had not been wasted, for she had not needed to climb back to the sky to fulfill her purpose. 
She would carry this first shard down to the village at dusk, leave it on the windowsill of the fisherman who had lost his way last winter, and return to the grotto at low tide to polish more, until every home along the coast held a small, cool glow to light their evenings without risk of flame. 
As the mist curled soft around her wrists and the zephyr carried the faint, sweet scent of bloom further into the grotto's winding curves, she smiled, running a thumb over the smooth sea glass, and let the pearl of stillness glow bright enough to gild the edge of every memory she carried, light without burn, warmth without fire, home right where she stood.
