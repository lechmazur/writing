The nostalgia ban made the tides illegal first. [8] Water remembers; that was the whole of the crime. [17] So the Ministry of Present Hours poured the harbors into pipes, and the tide dancers, who had spent nine generations translating the moon's pull into steps that any grieving village could follow, were offered ledgers and quiet jobs. [55] Ceyla, the most skilled tide dancer of her cohort, declined. [65] She chose exile, which the law permitted, because a woman walking away is easier to file than a woman dancing. [85] Her elective exile brought her here: a twilight garden under stars, twelve miles past the last tide-clock, where black basalt paths cooled underfoot and the jasmine opened only after the sun had gone. [118] She danced there nightly, alone, with what her old teacher had called obsidian grace: no ornament, no mercy, every line of her a dark blade turned edge-on to the wind. [148]

There was no sea to answer her, so she learned to read the sky instead, and she learned it by observing gaps. [170] Not the stars themselves - the censors had renamed those - but the black intervals between them, the pauses the Ministry could not legislate. [192] In those gaps, measured across four hundred nights, she found a rhythm that scanned like language, and she copied it into her feet until the whole of it lived in her calves and her breath. [227] A cosmic verse, forty-one syllables long, and useless in the air, because air forgets. [241] It had to be written on mortal skin, where it would itch and ache and be carried into rooms the Ministry had swept clean. [265]

For ink she had one thing: a moonstone cabochon, her mother's, polished blue-white and utterly illegal to own. [283] Ground against basalt, it yielded a dust speck no larger than a seed of pollen, and that speck, suspended in lamp oil and her own blood, was enough for forty-one syllables and not one more. [318] She had no one's skin but her own, and her own would be found. [332]

The boy arrived in the ninth month of her exile, walking badly. [344] His name was Idris and he had been an apprentice tide dancer for eleven days before the ban, which meant he knew the first form and nothing else. [372] He was also spontaneously anchored. [377] It happened to perhaps one dancer in three hundred: mid-phrase, without warning, the body decides on a spot of ground and will not leave it, and the dancer stands rooted for an hour, a day, a week, until the anchor releases. [418] The Ministry had no file for it; there was no crime in standing still. [432] That was precisely what Ceyla needed. [438]

"They inspect the ones who move," she told him, in the garden, under stars that had been given numbers instead of names. [460] "A boy who stops in a doorway for two days is a nuisance, not a suspect." [476] He asked what she wanted to put on him, and she said: a verse, and the instructions for reading it, which are the same thing. [501] He asked whether it was nostalgia. [507] She said no, and meant it: nostalgia looks backward, and this looked outward, at the gaps, which are older than any harbor and will outlast the pipes. [534]

It took three nights. [538] She worked the needle while he stood anchored in the jasmine bed, unmoving as a post, which spared him the shame of flinching. [561] Blue-white syllables rose along his spine and out across both shoulder blades, and when the last one closed the line, the pattern resembled, unmistakably, a pair of folded wings. [590] "You will fledge," she said, and it was not a metaphor she had planned. [604] The dust speck was gone; the cabochon was a dull pit of nothing; her mother's stone had become a boy's back. [625]

At dawn the anchor released him. [631] He took one step, then another, learning that his own weight was his again. [645] Ceyla taught him the second form and the third, enough to move like a man with somewhere ordinary to be, and sent him toward the cities with forty-one syllables under his shirt. [677] She stayed in the twilight garden, which was the arrangement she had chosen and would not now unchoose. [695] Exile was the price of the ink; someone had to be the empty room the verse came from. [713]

Years later there were rumors of bathhouse attendants in six cities who could recite a poem read off a stranger's shoulders, and of children in the pipe districts who danced a step no one had taught them. [750] Ceyla heard none of it. [754] She danced her gaps nightly, alone under the numbered stars, and never once looked back at the sea. [772]
