Mireya called herself an esoteric realist, which meant she believed in omens and measured them with a ruler. [18] The grotto sat above the clouds, verdant as a held breath, ferns dripping everywhere, moss blushing coral where the first sun touched it. [41] She had climbed here in September, as her great-grandmother had, to wait for the monarchs. [56] This year there was only one. [62] The radio in the ranger hut said so, in the flat voice reserved for weather and endings. [79] One female, tagged 4471, moving south along a corridor her ancestors had used for ten thousand years. [96] Mireya's work was to build her a landmark. [104]

She built temporary structures as signals: arches of bent willow, lattices of reed, small roofless chapels of stacked slate, each one knotted with the same cord. [130] The knot was a doubled fisherman's bend her great-grandmother Aurelia had used to tie tags to wings, a knot no museum ever credited to her, because Aurelia was a laundress in Michoacan and the men who published the maps called her a local woman. [174] Mireya's whole enterprise, she admitted, was an attempt to honor overlooked histories with objects too small to be stolen. [193] Her structures were transiently rooted: pegged into loose moss, staked into scree, designed to stand nine days and then let go. [214] A permanent monument, she thought, lies about time. [222] A temporary one keeps its promise and then keeps moving. [232]

In the crown of every structure she set a moth wing. [243] There had been a tin of them in Aurelia's sewing box: nine wings from a single silver moth that her great-grandmother had caught, described, and drawn, and that a visiting entomologist had entered in the ledger as a butterfly, misfiled, misnamed, forgotten for a century. [288] Mireya could not correct the ledger, so she corrected the sky. [299]

While she worked she hummed the hymn Aurelia had sung over washtubs, and she always stopped one note early. [318] An interrupted cadence: the chord that leans toward home and is not allowed to arrive. [333] It was, she had decided at nineteen, the only honest music for an unfinished debt. [348]

On the ninth day the wind stopped. [354] The grotto went so still that Mireya heard the seep of water into moss, and the whole hollow flushed pink, a blushing stillness that felt less like weather than like being seen. [386] Then the butterfly came over the lip of the cloud deck. [397] She was ragged, one hindwing scalloped by a bird, and she flew the interrupted cadence too: three beats, a stall, a glide, as if her body kept forgetting the resolution. [427] The monarch circled the willow arch twice and settled on the moth wing at its crown. [443] Mireya did not reach for her notebook, which surprised her. [453] A realist records. [456] But the esoteric half of her understood that some measurements are made by holding still, and she held still for forty minutes while the last monarch drank the last warmth out of the last morning of her line. [494]

When the butterfly lifted, the moth wing lifted with her, caught in her legs for a moment, then spinning loose down through the cloud. [518] Two names aloft together, one for the first time. [527]

Mireya untied the knots that evening. [533] The willow went back to being willow; the slate chapels became scree again; the reeds she laid in the spring to rot. [555] She kept the cord, coiled, with its creases where the knots had been, because a crease is a record and records were the whole point. [580]

Down in the valley the ranger asked what she had built up there, and whether it would last. [598] 'Nothing lasts up there,' Mireya said. [604] 'That is why it works as a signal.' [612]

She wrote her report that night: coordinates, materials, the knot's name, Aurelia's name, the wing's true species. [629] She sent it to three archives and one newspaper and expected nothing, which is different from hoping nothing. [647] Then she hummed the hymn and stopped, as always, one note early. [659] Not superstition, she told the dark: bookkeeping. [666] The note was still owed, and someone would have to come up the mountain next September to sing it. [685]
