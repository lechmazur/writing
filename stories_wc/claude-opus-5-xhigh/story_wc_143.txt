The crater inspector climbed the last switchback at midnight, when the summit road turned to silver. [16] Vera Solnit had walked it for thirty-one years, and she still stopped where the pines gave out, because from there the moonlit summit looked like a plate of exposed silver waiting to be read. [50] Tonight was the observatory's last night. [56] The Survey had cabled in March: the space age had outgrown mountains, and photographs of the Moon would henceforth be made from close range, by machines that did not need to sleep or eat or grieve. [92] Vera did not resent the machines. [98] She resented only their schedule. [103] At 6:14 that morning, an impactor named Hermes Nine would strike the floor of a small, unremarkable crater in Mare Nubium, and the crater's ninety-million-year-old rim would become, forever after, a place where humanity had left a dent. [141] The crater was named Nightwatch. [146] Vera had named it herself, in 1949, on the form where inspectors were permitted one proposal a decade. [164] Tomas Rey had been the observatory nightwatch then, a small, purposefully curious man who asked questions the way other people breathed, and who timed his rounds with an hourglass because he distrusted anything with a spring in it. [202] He had a better trick than the hourglass, though. [211] Tomas wrote her messages in the darkroom using chemical reactions for timed messages: a phrase brushed in clear silver salts on a blank sheet, another wash of developer laid over it, and the words would bloom out of nothing an hour later, or three hours later, exactly when he had calculated she would need them. [266] COFFEE IS FRESH, a sheet would say at two in the morning. [278] THE SEEING WILL STEADY AT FOUR, said another, and it did. [289] He had loved the hour before sunrise best, the dawning hush, when the wind lay down over the ridge and the air went so still that the stars stopped trembling and a lens could see almost anything. [326] He had died in 1958, on the road, in his truck, at dawn. [339] The hourglass broke when the truck rolled; Vera had swept the gravel of it into an envelope and kept one grain, the nightwatch's hourglass sand grain, in a lens vial in her coat pocket ever since. [375] Now she opened the dome and swung the old refractor east, and set out her plates in the cold. [394] She had come to photograph the last of something precious: crater Nightwatch as it had been for ninety million years, unhurt, its terminator shadow lying long and clean across the floor. [425] The Survey did not require the plate. [432] No one would ask for it. [438] But an inspector's whole discipline was attention paid to things that could not thank you, and she intended to outlast this one night at her post, however her hands ached. [468] At three she was too cold to feel the shutter cable. [479] At four the wind lay down, exactly as Tomas had always promised it would, and the seeing opened like a door. [500] Vera made eleven exposures, counting the seconds under her breath, and for the longest one, the four-minute plate, she took the vial out of her pocket and set it on the desk where she could see it. [537] One grain of sand is not a clock. [545] It is only a promise that time can be held in the hand. [558] She developed the plates at five-forty in the red light of the darkroom, and crater Nightwatch came up out of the emulsion whole and sharp, its rim a thin bright crescent, its floor unmarked. [592] Then, at the back of the drying rack, she found the sheet she had left there in 1958 and had never been able to throw away: blank, curled, faintly stained, the last one he had prepared for her. [630] It had never come up for her in an hour or in three. [643] It had been coming up for seven years, so slowly that she had stopped looking, and now, in the red light, it said in faint gray letters: STAY UNTIL IT IS LIGHT. [675] So she carried her plates outside and stayed. [683] At 6:14 the Moon was low and pale in the west, and nothing about it changed that any eye on the summit could see; somewhere on that small floor a machine had ended, and a new crater a few meters wide had begun. [726] Vera Solnit stood in the dawnlit cold with the vial in her fist and thought: someone will inspect that new crater too, someday, and they will need to know what the ground looked like before. [761] The hush held a moment longer, and then the first bird went off down the ridge, and the mountain gave up its last night without complaint. [787] She went in to label the plates. [794]
