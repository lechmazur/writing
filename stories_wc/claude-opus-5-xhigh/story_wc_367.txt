The last print newspaper came off the presses on a Tuesday, and Ines kept a copy in the lamp room because paper was the only thing that would hold the herald's words. [32] Screens forgot; newsprint remembered. [36] Three years on, its margins were crowded with her handwriting, and every line had come true. [52]

The lighthouse stood on a spit of green basalt, and the glass of its tower had been cast with copper salts long ago, so the whole shaft glowed emerald when the stars came out. [86] Sailors called it the Green Candle. [91] Ines called it work. [95]

Her routine never varied, and it was not entirely of this world. [107] At the ninth hour she climbed one hundred and forty steps, wiped the salt film from the lens, and lifted the glass lantern wick out of its velvet case. [136] It was not a wick that burned. [143] It was a hair-thin rod of drawn glass, older than the tower, and it carried light the way a vein carries blood: through total internal reflection, every ray bouncing inside itself, losing nothing, escaping only at the tip. [181] Set into the lens housing, the wick did not brighten the beam. [193] It threaded shadows into it. [198]

The shadows were ancient, and they were not hers. [207] Nine slabs from the drowned harbor lay stacked in the base of the tower, carved with figures whose names no living mouth could pronounce, and the wick could sip their darkness and pour it into the light. [244] Ines had learned to steer those shapes across the clouds like a hand steering a tiller. [260] Fishermen read them as weather. [265] She read them as a rehearsal. [271]

The herald came with the first low pressure of autumn, as he always did, walking up out of the surf with his coat full of wind. [297] He was laughing before she opened the door, and he laughed the whole way up the stairs, a sound like hail on a tin roof, delighted and unkind and impossible to resent. [329] Storm heralds cannot prophesy on purpose. [335] They only babble, and their babble is accidentally correct. [344] "Ines, Ines," he wheezed, dripping green light, "the sun will come up twice and neither one will be the sun!" [364] Then he howled at his own joke and fell over a bucket. [376] She wrote it in the newspaper's margin, beside the shipping tables, in the last column of the last edition anyone would ever fold. [399]

The coast had been under storm for eleven weeks. [408] No harvest, no ferries, no dawn worth the name -- only a grey smear where morning should be, because something in the upper air had come loose and would not settle. [438] Ines meant to fix that. [443] She meant to shape a new dawn out of the oldest shadows she had, to cast the nine carved figures so far and so wide across the sky that the air would remember its own order and lie down again. [483] It would look like sunrise. [488] It would not be sunrise. [493] "Twice," she said, and understood. [498] The herald, upside down, applauded. [503]

At the ninth hour she lit the lamp, seated the glass lantern wick, and laid the first slab against the housing. [524] Darkness ran up the rod, folding into itself, and came out of the tip as a shape with nine arms. [544] She put her palms on the drive gears and steered. [553] The emerald beam swung, and the figure walked the clouds from the headland to the horizon, and behind it the storm opened like a mouth deciding not to bite. [582] Slab after slab, hour after hour, under stars she had not seen since June. [596] By the eighth slab her hands were bleeding and she was laughing too, which frightened her more than the storm ever had. [618] At the ninth, false dawn broke: a low copper glow along the water, ancient and borrowed, warm as any morning. [638] Boats came out. [641] Nets went down. [644] Forty minutes later the true sun rose behind it, thin and astonished, and for a while the coast had two mornings and no wind at all. [670]

The herald left at slack tide, still chuckling, promising nothing and predicting everything. [683] Ines cleaned the wick, returned it to its velvet, swept the lamp room, and wrote the date in the margin. [703] The ninth slab had cracked along its carved spine; eight mornings remained, then none. [717] She looked at the two suns, one true and one stolen, and felt the thing the herald felt, the thing that made him laugh in the dark: elation with a shadow inside it, the joy of holding a whole coast steady with borrowed light. [761] At the ninth hour, she would climb the stairs again. [771]
