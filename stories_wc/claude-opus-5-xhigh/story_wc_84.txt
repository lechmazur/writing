The pilgrim arrived at the ancient observatory an hour before dusk, and only then did Ilse allow herself to unpack the chip. [22] She was a drone pilot, sharp-eyed even in bad light, which was the only kind of light the mountain offered anymore. [43] Below the cracked dome, the valley towns kept their lamps low, a brilliant dimness that let the sky do the talking. [64] The chip itself was a type foundry matrix chip, brass on one face, silicon on the other: a mold for casting letters that some long-dead engineer had taught to hold data. [95] It was missing eleven glyphs. [100]

"Missing pieces," the pilgrim said, setting down his pack. [109] His eyebrows sat high on his forehead, perpetually surprised, as if the world kept handing him gifts he had not ordered. [130] "You cannot read a sentence with eleven letters gone." [139] "I can read enough to know it is a sentence," Ilse said. [151]

The frequency had been broadcasting for nine years from somewhere past the ridges, a slow pulse that people in the valley called the belonging signal because it made strangers weep and stay. [183] Ilse wanted to decode the frequency of belonging, not because she believed in it, but because she had never once felt it, and she distrusted any door she could not open. [214] The matrix chip was the key: each cast glyph produced one tone, and the eleven absent glyphs were the eleven silences that made the message gibberish. [240] The old foundry had scattered its type across eleven hill shrines two centuries ago, hiding the alphabet from an empire that burned alphabets. [263] No map survived. [266]

"Then how do you find them?" the pilgrim asked, and his surprise looked, for once, earned. [282] Ilse pointed at the dome's slit, where the first stars were smearing into arcs on her long-exposure feed. [300] The shrine builders had aligned every foundation to a rising star, so the way to find them was by following star trails: let the camera drink an hour of sky, watch the arcs curve, and fly the drone down the throat of each curve until stone answered. [347] Ten shrines had already given up their letters. [355] She had flown those ten alone, sharp-eyed and sleepless, reading arcs the way other people read scripture. [372] The eleventh arc ended in the observatory itself. [380]

She launched the drone into the violet air, and it climbed the way a thought climbs, quick and small and unafraid. [401] Its light found the north pier of the dome, where a brass plug sat flush in the mortar, unremarkable for two hundred years. [424] It took both of them to pull it free, the pilgrim's hands over hers on the strap, the mortar shrieking, the plug coming out with a sound like a held breath released. [456] Inside lay the last glyph, and it was not a letter. [467] It was a space. [471]

Ilse seated all eleven into the matrix chip and let the reader cast them into tone. [487] The belonging signal resolved, and it was not a hymn or a summons. [500] It was a roll call: names, thousands of them, the ones the empire had unmade, read out in order, with a pause between each name where a listener could say their own. [532] The eleventh glyph was that pause. [538]

The pilgrim wept the way the valley folk wept, and Ilse understood, finally, that the signal did not confer belonging; it only left room for it. [564] The missing pieces had never been in the mortar. [573] She keyed the transmitter, waited for the gap, and put her name into it, and the mountain, dim and brilliant, took it without surprise, as if it had been holding the space open all along. [608]

Ilse handed the pilgrim the matrix chip, still warm, eleven pieces complete. [620] His eyebrows stayed high, but his mouth had changed. [629] "Say it again," he said, "and leave me the pause." [639] Above them the star trails wheeled on, patient as type in a case, and Ilse said the names again, leaving room. [660]
