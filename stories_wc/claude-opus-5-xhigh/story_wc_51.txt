After the first snowmelt, the creek gave back everything it had been keeping, and I walked down to the shore to see what it had brought me. [27] It brought a kerosene lamp burner, brass gone green, with my brother's initials filed into the collar. [44] Tobias had drowned in October, and the lake had held his lamp all winter, as if deciding. [61] The old women in our village say that grief you cannot carry should be taken to the bronze grotto, where the fractal weaver waits under starlight to collect the weights of different burdens. [94] They say it, then they laugh, the way people laugh at things they half believe. [109] I went anyway, because the burner in my coat pocket was heavier than brass has any right to be. [128] The grotto opens on the north headland, a bronze hollow where copper-veined rock has been polished by the sea into false metal, and its roof is stacked with whale ribs the storms wedged there generations ago. [164] The ribs overlap like roof shingles, and under them the sound of the world stops in layers. [181] Fishermen call it the shingled hush. [187] Standing in it is like standing inside a whalebone reverie: you hear your own blood, and beneath that, something older dreaming slowly. [209] The weaver was in the back of the grotto, and I understood at once why nobody describes it the same way twice. [231] It was a pattern more than a creature, a spiral of thread and salt that repeated itself smaller inside every joint, and smaller again inside that, forever. [258] "You brought light," it said, and its voice arrived in the same nested way, a whisper inside a whisper. [277] I set the burner on a shelf of flat stone. [287] The weaver told me the burner had no wick and no oil left, but that it did not need them; it needed only to face the right way, slot toward the mouth of the cave, where the starlight came in. [327] By orienting it properly, a quarter inch and then a quarter inch more, I made the brass catch the sky. [347] Then light unspooled out of the burner's little slots and fanned across the wet walls, and every ray split into rays, and those into more, until the grotto was a lit tree of branching brightness. [382] "That is my whole trouble," the weaver said. [390] "I am boundary-lessly trapped." [394] It explained, patiently, the way a tide explains a shoreline. [404] There were no walls holding it, no chain, no door; it simply could not reach an edge, because every part of it contained another whole of it, and so it went inward without end and outward without arrival. [442] Only weight could stop it. [447] "Nothing anchors a pattern like a burden," it said. [456] "So I collect them, and weigh them, and learn what each one truly weighs, and the weighing holds me still a while." [478] It showed me the others it had taken in. [487] Along the grotto floor sat pale lumps, some fist-sized, some like sleeping dogs, each one a sorrow that had been set down and left to calcify into a smooth white stone. [518] A drowned son, a bad debt, a marriage that had gone silent in its own shingled way. [535] The weaver asked for mine. [540] I thought about it longer than I expected to. [549] Handing over the burner would not be handing over brass; it would be handing over the October night I rowed out too late, and the sound of my own voice calling a name across flat water. [585] "If I leave it, does it stop hurting?" I asked. [595] "It stops moving," the weaver said. [601] "That is not the same thing." [607] So we compromised, the pattern and I. [614] I left the burner where it was, throwing its branching light forever at the whale ribs; and I kept the night itself, the rowing and the calling, because it was mine and because a burden you carry stays soft. [653] The weaver weighed the brass, and said it was the heaviest small thing it had ever held, and went briefly, gratefully still. [675] In that stillness the hush thickened, layer over layer, and I heard, or dreamed I heard, my brother's laugh come up out of the bone roof, unhurried, the way sound moves in deep water. [709] I climbed back up the headland while the snowmelt ran loud in every gully. [723] Now, when someone in the village goes quiet in that particular way, I tell them about the grotto, and I laugh a little when I say it, so they can half believe me. [756] Then I walk them down, and show them how to turn the light toward the stars. [772]
