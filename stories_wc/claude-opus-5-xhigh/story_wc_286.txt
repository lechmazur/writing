Sana found the oasis the way she found everything worth finding: by following sun patterns. [15] For eleven days she had walked the scorched flats, marking where shadows of dead pylons fell short, where noon arrived a half hour early, where the light lay down wrong. [45] Wrong light meant a seam, and a seam meant a place where time had cracked and grown a callus. [64] She wanted a callus. [68] She wanted to understand why some places resist change while others, like her village, burn in an afternoon and stay burned. [89]

The oasis announced itself as a thrum. [95] Bees. [96] Impossible bees, in a wasteland where nothing flowered, working thirty white hive boxes under date palms that had no business being alive. [118] The beekeepers were long gone; their smokers lay rusted in the sand, their veils shredded to lace. [135] But the hives thrummed, and the sound had a doubled edge, like two choirs a breath out of step. [154]

Sana knelt, unwrapped her cloth bundle, and took out the faded lantern glass. [167] It had come from her grandmother's kiln shed, the only thing the fire left whole: a pane gone milky with decades of heat, useless for lighting anything. [194] Useful, though, for looking. [198] Through it, the oasis split. [203] She saw two afternoons stacked like wet clay slabs: one where a woman in a white veil walked the rows humming, one where the sand had swallowed the last box. [233] Fractured timelines, her teacher had called them, the way a bowl fired too fast keeps every version of its breaking inside the glaze. [256] Most seams healed shut. [260] This one had not. [264]

She stayed. [266] A potter learns to want what the clay wants, and Sana had spent twenty years unlearning argument with materials. [285] The keeper's cottage still had a stone chimney, a cistern of sweet water, and a grindstone that, with patience, became a potter's wheel. [308] Sana had patience the way other people had debts. [317] She dug clay from the spring's throat, wedged it, threw bowls, and fired them in a kiln she built from hive bricks and cottage stone. [342] Every evening, as chimney smoke flattened into a gray shelf over the palms, she carried a shallow dish of sugar water to the hives. [366] She did it because thirsty things should drink. [374]

She noticed the vine on the third week. [381] It grew along the cottage wall, thin as wire, and it did not lean toward the sun. [398] It leaned toward her. [402] Not toward her hands when they carried water, which would have been sensible, but toward her hands when they were gentle. [423] Phototropic to kindness, she wrote in her notebook, then crossed it out for being pretty, then wrote it again because it was accurate. [446] When she cursed a cracked bowl, the vine curled away. [456] When she set a chipped rim aside instead of smashing it, the vine reached. [470] Through the lantern glass, the vine existed in both afternoons at once, and it was the only thing that did. [490]

That was the answer, and it arrived without ceremony, the way answers do while your hands are busy. [508] Places do not resist change by being strong. [516] They resist by being tended on a rhythm the world can set its teeth into. [531] Some keeper, in some afternoon, had walked these rows at the same hour every day, following sun patterns, offering water, humming to bees. [554] The kindness had been so regular it became a groove, and the timelines caught in it like a wheel-head catching clay. [575] Her village had never been tended on a rhythm; it had only been lived in, and so it went all at once. [597]

The knowledge was not comfort. [602] It had an edge to it, and she let it cut where it needed to. [617] Then she went back to work, because grief and glaze both need something to hold them. [633]

By the second autumn she had a hundred bowls, forty jars, and a habit. [647] She set the faded lantern glass into the cottage window, where the low sun struck it each evening and threw one soft square onto the floor. [673] She stopped needing to look through it. [679] The two afternoons had begun to overlap the way a hand overlaps its own shadow, and the thrum of the hives had gone single and clean. [705]

Once, at dusk, as the chimney smoke flattened, she saw the woman in the white veil at the far end of the rows. [728] Neither of them hurried. [732] Sana raised a hand. [736] The keeper raised hers, and the vine on the wall turned toward the space between them, leaning hard into that small light. [758] Then Sana carried the water to the hives, on time, as she would tomorrow. [772]
