The Bone Canyon does not forgive walking. [6] Sela learned that at nine, when her mother stepped off a ledge that the echoes had promised was solid, and the dust took her the way it had taken everything else, patiently, in layers. [40] So she became a harmonic stone skimmer, like her grandmother, and like the four hundred skimmers whose names are notched into the guild post at the canyon mouth. [68] The work is simple to describe and impossible to do well: you appraise a stone by weight and grain and the way it sweats in your palm, you skim it low across the dust-flats, and you listen to what comes back. [109] The towers of bone answer differently than rock does. [118] They are hollow, those old spires, ribbed and porous, and a good skim sets them humming in chords that tell you where the floor is firm, where the sinkholes wait, and how far the next tower stands. [155] Sela was historically reverent about all of it. [163] She kept the guild's ledgers of echo-times going back through centuries of layered dust, and she could recite which strata belonged to which generation of skimmers the way other people recited prayers. [195] That reverence was not sentiment; it was method. [203] The canyon changed slowly, and the old measurements still held, if you knew how to correct for the dust. [222]

Her ambition, the one the younger skimmers laughed at, was to learn to navigate without moving. [238] Every step in the Bone Canyon costs something; every step is a guess. [251] She wanted to sit on one ledge until she died and know the whole canyon anyway, mapped entirely by sound, so that no one would have to walk it blind again. [282]

The problem was the pulse left wandering. [289] Three hundred years ago a skimmer named Ovan threw a stone that no one ever heard return. [306] The ledgers record his last entry in a shaking hand: the pulse still goes. [320] Sela believed him. [323] In a canyon of endless echoes, a sound could find a path that fed it back into itself, circling forever, and such a sound would be a thread laid through every corridor of the place - a complete map, if you could ever catch it. [367]

She could not chase it. [372] Chasing was walking, and walking was death. [379] So she watched the birds. [384] Dust-swifts nested in the tower sockets, and they flew by hearing, not sight; the canyon was too dim for eyes. [404] For eleven years Sela sat and read the patterns in a flock of birds in flight, and she noticed what her grandmother had noticed and never understood: twice a day, at no fixed hour, the flock buckled. [441] The birds swerved around something that was not there. [450] They were dodging Ovan's pulse. [455] She began writing down the buckles - where, when, which way the flock folded - and over eleven years the wandering pulse drew itself for her, corridor by corridor, a slow signature across the dark. [488] By the end she had the map. [495] One thing remained: proof, and a voice loud enough to leave behind. [507]

Her grandmother had left her a stone arrowhead, black and fluted, older than the guild, older than the bone towers - knapped by hunters who walked this canyon when it still had a river and animals worth shooting. [544] Sela had appraised it a thousand times without ever daring to throw it: the flattest, densest, truest skimming stone she would ever hold, and a relic she had been raised to honor. [576] Honoring it, she decided at last, meant using it. [585] She waited for the flock to buckle at the third socket, which told her the pulse was rounding the east gallery and would pass her ledge in ninety heartbeats. [614] Old hands, weathered resolve; she did not hurry. [622] She skimmed the arrowhead low across the dust-flats at the count of ninety, and the towers of bone took it up, and Ovan's three-hundred-year-old pulse met her fresh one and doubled. [653] The chord that came back was the canyon entire. [662] Every gallery, every sinkhole, every tower, arriving in order of distance, a single sustained answer that lasted long enough for her to write all of it down. [689]

She never left the ledge. [694] Skimmers still come to sit where she sat, and they read her ledger, and they do not walk blind. [713] The arrowhead lies somewhere out in the flats, under new dust, and the doubled pulse is still going. [731] On still evenings the swifts swerve twice, politely, and the skimmers stand and bow. [745]
