The birds had left the trunk an hour before the crews arrived, and Ilse took that as permission. [18] She climbed the spiral stair inside the great hollowed oak, past shelves the scholars' guild had carved directly into living wood, and she counted her steps because she could not count faces. [50] Three hundred and eleven steps, the same as always. [59] Outside, the foreman's whistle waited in his mouth, unblown. [68] The trunk was condemned: rot at the taproot, a lean over the almshouses, a verdict signed by men whose faces she had never held. [92] Ilse was a portrait artist who had never once recognized a sitter twice. [105] She knew her patrons by voice, by the drag of a bad hip, by the smell of tallow or lavender in their sleeves. [128] Faces, to her, were weather: present, enormous, unrepeatable. [136] So she had spent nine years painting something she could actually keep, and the guild had paid her to do it: a ceiling in the reading vault meant to mirror the sky inside walls. [170] Above the lectern she had laid clouds that branched into smaller clouds, and those into smaller still, fractally precise, so that a scholar looking up at midnight could believe the roof had been lifted off. [205] Today it would become splinters. [210] The hush in the vault was not silence but attention, the whole hollow leaning in. [225] Dust hung without falling. [229] Ilse went to the third shelf and pulled down the fat, false volume the guild called Aristotle on Rain. [248] It was a secret book safe, its pages fused and hollowed, its lock a wooden tumbler that opened to a sequence she had memorized as a rhythm rather than a number. [279] Inside lay one object: a centuries-old coin, thin as a fingernail, worn faceless. [292] The guild's founding charter mentioned it once, calling it the Warden's pattern, and no scholar in three generations had been able to say what it depicted. [318] They had looked at it, all of them, under lamps and lenses. [330] Ilse closed her eyes and read the way she always read the world, through faint indentations on the centuries-old coin, her thumb moving in slow arcs. [356] There: a spiral of dimples, and inside each dimple a smaller spiral, and inside those, tooling so fine it registered only as a change in warmth. [382] It was her own ceiling, four hundred years early. [391] Not a copy, but a proof. [397] The Warden had been blind to faces too. [405] The charter said she carved by touch and never by likeness, and now Ilse understood why: the coin was a self-portrait made of sky, the only face its maker could hold. [436] Boots on the stair. [440] Ilse did not turn, because turning would tell her nothing. [450] The gait was quick and right-footed and apologetic: Bex, her apprentice, who had asked eleven times whether the ceiling could be saved. [472] "They're waiting on you," Bex said. [478] "One minute more." [481] Ilse tilted her head back for the last time and looked at nine years of work, every fork of every cloud, and felt the thing she had been avoiding since the surveyors came. [514] A painted sky ends. [518] It ends at the plaster, at the taproot, at the edge of what one pair of hands can hold, and pretending otherwise had been the only vanity she had left. [548] The Warden had known that, which was why she had put the pattern on something small enough to be carried out of a falling building. [573] Limits were not the enemy of the work; they were its shape. [585] She pressed the coin into Bex's palm. [592] "Learn it with your hands, not your eyes," she said. [602] "Then we craft it again, somewhere with a taproot that holds." [613] They went down three hundred and eleven steps into a morning holding its breath. [627] The crews stood so still that Ilse could not tell one man from another, and for once it did not matter. [648] The whistle blew. [651] The oak came down along its own grain, and the vault opened, and for one instant the painted clouds met the real ones and could not be told apart. [680] Then dust, and the coin warm in Bex's fist, fractal and patient, waiting for walls. [695]
