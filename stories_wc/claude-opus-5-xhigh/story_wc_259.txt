The bronze prism pagoda existed only at twilight, and Vashti had learned to arrive early and wait. [17] It assembled itself out of the last slanted light, facet by facet, nine tiers of hammered metal that were nowhere at noon and nowhere at full dark. [44] Beneath it lay the archive of the Sorrel, a civilization that had erased itself so thoroughly that even the word for their country was a guess. [70] Vashti wore the saffron veil of her order, which meant she was permitted to study the dead and forbidden to become them. [92] She went down the stairs without counting them. [100] That was the first rule of purification: avoid patterns. [109] The air in the archive was thick with atoms of nostalgia, fine as flour, shed by every object that had ever been loved. [132] They were not dangerous in themselves. [137] They were dangerous because they were drawn to rhythm: a repeated footfall, a hummed refrain, the tidy metronome of ordinary breathing. [158] Give them a pattern and they would settle into it, thicken, and build you a past so warm you would sit down inside it and never stand up again. [187] Three historians of her order sat in the lower galleries still, smiling, dust-furred, breathing in perfect time. [204] So Vashti moved the way the pagoda's light moved, steadily unpredictable, four steps then one, then seven, pausing to cough, to whistle a broken note, to walk backward for no reason at all. [237] The atoms could not find a handhold on her, and where she passed the air went clear, purified for a few clean seconds. [260] She had come for the sealed vault at the archive's heart, the shelf her order called the Forbidden Ledger, which recorded the Sorrel across the end of innocence -- the eleven years in which they discovered what they were and decided to stop. [302] Her order forbade it because reading it had killed everyone who tried. [314] She wanted it because she was tired of writing histories with the last chapter missing. [329] The vault door opened to an irregular knock she invented as she made it. [343] Inside, the nostalgia lay knee-deep and silver. [350] The Ledger was not a book but a drum of copper wire, and as she unwound it the archive read itself aloud into her. [374] The Sorrel had built the pagoda to purify their cities of longing, and it had worked. [390] Then a scholar-child asked why longing needed purifying, and the answer, once spoken, ended their innocence: they had been harvesting the atoms, breathing the sweetness of their own grandmothers, farming their dead for comfort. [424] For eleven years they argued, and then, rather than live as a people who ate their own memory, they walked into the pagoda's light in a long, patient, perfectly rhythmic line. [455] The wire ran out. [459] And the atoms, which had been waiting for a listener to hold still, found her. [474] She had stopped moving to read. [480] Her mother's kitchen assembled around her out of silver dust: the smell of scorched barley, the crooked stool, her mother's hands, ordinary as weather. [504] Vashti laughed out loud, and the laugh tore something in her throat, because she understood at last that the Sorrel had not been monsters; they had only been homesick, and so was she, and the joy of that kitchen was the exact shape of the thing that would kill her. [554] She stayed for one breath more. [560] Then she began to ruin it. [566] She sang the wrong words to a song she loved. [576] She spoke her mother's name and then a number, then a nonsense syllable, then nothing. [591] She danced badly, without measure, weeping and grinning, and the kitchen came apart into ordinary dark. [607] She climbed out through the clear air she made by refusing every rhythm her body wanted. [623] Above, the pagoda was already coming apart, twilight thinning off its bronze facets tier by tier. [639] Vashti pulled the saffron veil from her face and let the wind take the dust out of her hair. [658] She would write the last chapter tonight, and every historian who read it would lose their innocence too, and that, she thought, was a kindness -- the only honest one left. [688] She walked home by a route she had never taken, and never would again. [702]
