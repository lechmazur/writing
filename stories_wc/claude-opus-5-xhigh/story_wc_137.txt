The town council sent Ines to measure the stargazer's open tower for demolition, because Ines was the only surveyor in Bellhaven who did not believe it sang. [27] She had loved her aunt Odile without ever once agreeing with her. [39] That was the shape of her grief: a tenderness that kept refusing to be superstition. [54] The tower stood on the headland with no roof at all, only a rim of basalt and a floor of one hundred and forty-four clay tiles, each stamped with a four-by-four grid of numbers. [88] Odile had called them magic squares and claimed they were a score, not a decoration. [103] Ines had called them a hobby for a lonely woman, and had been wrong in a way she could no longer apologize for. [126] She arrived in the third week of the whale migration, when the bay filled with grey backs moving south and the water carried a pressure you felt in your teeth. [156] One tile near the tower's center was loose, and warm, though the sun had not touched it since dawn. [175] Ines pried it up to note the subfloor, and the moment her palm covered the numbers she remembered something that had never happened to her. [200] She remembered being twenty-two and pregnant and standing in this tower in a storm, deciding not to jump. [218] It was Odile's memory, whole and warm, and it came with a hundred others tangled into it like nets hauled up together: a fisherman's first name, a stillborn lamb, a hymn nobody had finished writing. [253] The skeptic in her tried three explanations, fumes and fatigue and grief, then discarded them all with the same honesty she had once used against her aunt. [280] Under the loose tile lay Odile's notebook, wrapped in oilcloth, and in it the word she had underlined twice: entangled. [300] The whales, Odile had written, sing below human hearing; the tower was built to make that unheard symphony audible, not by amplifying it but by giving it a shape to return from. [332] Her intention, stated plainly on the first page, was to sculpt space for reflection: to arrange the tiles so the headland's stone threw the song back at itself until it thickened into something ears could hold. [368] The arrangement was not hers, either. [373] She had copied it from the old harbor almshouse, from a mosaic three centuries dead, working through forgotten patterns that masons had once used to lay floors for the dying, so the dying would have something to count. [411] Odile had died with eleven tiles still out of order. [421] Ines sat on the basalt rim for a long hour while the bay breathed whales. [436] She did not believe in magic; she believed in acoustics, in her aunt's arithmetic, and in the fact that she had just remembered a storm she had never stood in. [466] Then she began to transcribe. [471] Each tile's magic square gave a constant, thirty-four, and the path of its numbers from one to sixteen gave a direction; she wrote the constants and the paths into her survey ledger, column after column, until the ledger stopped being a demolition report and became a score. [518] It took four days, and on the fourth she moved the last eleven tiles with her hands raw and her knees bleeding through her trousers. [543] The migration was thinning; two nights remained at most. [552] When she set the warm tile last into its place, the tower filled. [565] Not with sound exactly, but with something arriving from every direction at once, low and enormous and patient, and inside it the tangled memories of everyone who had ever stood on that floor: the fisherman's name, the lamb, the unfinished hymn, her aunt at twenty-two choosing to stay. [613] The honest thing, the hard thing, was to walk down the headland and knock on doors. [629] Ines was not brave about ridicule; she had spent her whole life on the safe side of belief. [647] She went anyway, house by house, and said: come up tonight, bring whoever you have lost, I cannot explain it and I am not asking you to agree with me. [677] Thirty-one people climbed the headland, which the harbormaster later called the most courageously kind foolishness he had witnessed in forty years at sea. [700] They stood on the numbers under an open sky while the last whales went south, and nobody spoke, and everybody wept, and no two of them heard the same thing. [730] The council voted to keep the tower. [737] Ines filed her ledger as the official survey, every constant summing to thirty-four, and she still tells anyone who asks that she does not believe in magic squares. [765] She only believes in the floor, and in whoever comes next, and in keeping the eleven tiles she moved exactly where her aunt would have put them. [792]
