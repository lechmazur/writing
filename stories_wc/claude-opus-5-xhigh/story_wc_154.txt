The ceasefire talks had stalled over a single clause, and Mele Faletau, translator for the Commission, had run out of ways to say the same word twice. [27] The atrium of the old mission hospital filled with mist at twilight, the way it had for a hundred years, ferns dripping, the mountain breathing down through the broken skylight. [57] On one side of the folding table sat the Landowners' delegation; on the other, the men from the mine and the government that leased them the river. [84] Both sides said security; neither of them meant the same thing by it, and Mele's job was to carry the word across without letting it break. [110] At nine o'clock she gave up and went down the corridor to the children's ward, where Sina was waiting to be put under for the second time. [137] Sina was eight, and a shell fragment in the taro garden had done what shell fragments do to an arm. [157] For six weeks she had worn a plaster cast, and because she could not be still, and because everyone at every checkpoint gave her something to carry, the cast had become a ledger. [190] She wrote on it with a fragment of brain coral she had picked off the dead reef at Tanu, a stub of stone whose grooves fit her thumb like a face she knew. [223] Coral on plaster leaves a chalky, smudging line. [231] Mele had asked her once what the marks meant, and Sina had looked up with that shrewdly innocent face children learn in occupied places and said, 'Errands.' [258] Now the anesthetic kicked in, and the girl's fingers loosened around the coral, and Mele, who had spent the day failing to translate between two kinds of fear, began to read the arm. [291] The cast was a cipher of necessity: three languages, mission-school abbreviations, a private shorthand of dots for who had said a thing and dashes for whether it had been whispered. [321] Decrypting it took the whole surgery. [326] Sina had been recording the village's messages, yes, but also, in the gaps, the things the men muttered while she waited: the sentry above the tailings dam, the mine surveyor at the Landcruiser, the old fighter at the roadblock with his dead brother's name on his rifle strap. [374] She had written them all down without judging any of them, the way a child collects shells. [391] And what they said was the same. [398] No fish at Tanu; children coughing red water; nobody will believe we did not want this. [414] Twenty-one entries, and only three that could not have been spoken by either side. [428] Mele copied it out in the corridor light, and her hands shook, because for nine months she had been paid to render one side's words into the other's and had never once been permitted to say the obvious thing: that they were afraid of the same water. [475] She went back to the atrium with the coral fragment in her pocket. [488] The mist had come down to knee height; the delegates were putting on their jackets. [503] Mele asked for four minutes and read the list aloud, twice, once in each language, attributing nothing. [520] She watched men on both sides of the table go still as they recognized their own sentences in the other's mouth. [541] Then she set the brain coral fragment on the clause they had been fighting over for eleven days. [559] 'Security is written as guns and hectares,' she said, out of turn, past the edge of her job. [577] 'Write it as the reef, then. [583] A nursery at Tanu, staffed by the sons of both sides, grafting living fragments onto the dead coral heads until the scar becomes the frame that holds the new growth.' [613] They argued until the mist turned to rain, and something began to unknot: not the war, only the clause, only one strand of it. [637] Strand by strand is the only way rope has ever given up a knot. [651] In the morning Sina woke with her arm in fresh plaster, blank and white, and cried about it in a way that had nothing to do with pain. [679] Mele gave her back the coral and read the old list to her from her notebook, and the girl copied it onto the new cast in her own hand, correcting two of Mele's mistakes. [713] Outside, the mountain exhaled its evening mist, and the atrium filled again, and Mele sat in it with the ache of a lotus opening in muddy water: sorrow used, at last, for something. [746] She was only a translator; she had decided, that week, what the word meant. [760]
