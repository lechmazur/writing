The Combine paid Vesna Ilic for eleven years to explain that nothing was wrong. [14] She was sharply observant, and the sharply observant make the finest conspiracy debunkers, because they can name the exact hour a rumor was born. [38] Her masterwork was the diamond. [43] Shaft Nine had produced one stone in forty years, a flawed thing with a black inclusion at its heart, and after eleven miners died the district decided the stone was cursed. [74] Vesna wrote the pamphlet that killed the curse: no hexes, only bad luck, bad timing, bad hearts. [91] She believed she had served the truth until, after the liquidation, she was handed a key to the records annex and three hours to clear it. [117] The auditors had taken the ledgers; nobody had bothered with the shredder bins. [130] She spent that winter at her kitchen table reassembling shredded letters, strip by strip, forty-one of them, all in the same square handwriting. [153] They were from a woman named Marta Zhu, widow of a Shaft Nine driller, and they asked for filters, grafting wax, and an answer. [177] The Combine had answered none of them, which was itself an answer. [189] What held Vesna, though, was the weather in the margins. [199] Marta noted wind directions the way other people note prayers: dust from the east at dawn, from the west by evening, and at the center of her days, nothing at all. [230] Vesna had spent a career proving that no one survives the radioactive dust storms of the Kolvar flats, and here was a woman describing calm air in the middle of them. [261] From the letters she began to intuit a shape: the storms did not cover the flats, they circled something. [280] A hidden stillpoint, sheltered by the very violence that hid it. [291] She walked in March, when the storms run low and slow, with a dosimeter, nine liters of water, and the reassembled letters taped between two panes of glass. [319] For two days the dust chewed at her goggles and the needle climbed. [332] On the third morning the wind simply stopped, the way a shouted argument stops when someone finally listens. [350] Ahead of her stood an orchard. [356] Sixty-odd trees in trenched rows, apricot and quince, their leaves gray with settled powder but their fruit swelling, fed by a spring that the encircling wall of dust kept sweet. [386] The dosimeter's needle sank to almost nothing. [393] Marta Zhu was seventy-one and unsurprised. [399] "You're the pamphlet woman," she said, and poured tea. [408] She kept the diamond in a tin with her husband's union card. [420] The Combine had given it to her in place of the survivors' pension, calling it a gesture, and had let the curse story do the rest. [446] That was the arithmetic Vesna had never done. [454] A cursed stone explained eleven dead men; her pamphlet then explained away the curse and left the eleven explained by nothing, which meant nobody had to explain the unfiltered air in Shaft Nine, or the ventilation invoices, or the sealed survey that showed groundwater still running clean under the flats. [504] Two comfortable lies, stacked, and she had built the upper one. [515] "They said the land was dead so no one would come," Marta said. [528] "I let them think it, because I wanted the quiet." [538] "Then you want it more than you want the pension," Vesna said, "and I have come to excavate truth from comfortable lies, including my own." [563] Marta held out the diamond, flaw upward, black seed in clear ice. [575] "Take it to your inquiry, then." [581] Vesna closed the old woman's fingers around it. [589] "A stone proves nothing, it only makes people feel something," she said. [601] "Your letters prove it, forty-one of them, dated and ignored, and the fruit proves it, and this quiet proves it." [621] The inquiry took nine months. [626] The letters, photographed pane by pane, went into the record beside the ventilation invoices, and the district learned the dull, useful truth: the flats had been written off because a dead land owes nobody anything. [661] Marta got her pension and kept her quiet, since the storms guard the orchard better than any fence. [679] Vesna goes out each March with grafting wax and glass jars, and returns with apricots that no one in the district will eat. [702] She eats them herself, one at a time, at noon, in the hour when the wind forgets to blow. [721] Every good thing she knows of has a flaw at the center, and grows anyway. [736]
