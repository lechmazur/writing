The nebula garden bloomed once every four hundred years, and Ilsandre had been born into the waiting. [17] She tended it from a glass gondola that drifted between the petals, each one a sheet of frozen hydrogen wider than a city. [40] Her mother had called her a sentimentalist, which in their family was a diagnosis. [54] Ilsandre kept the accusation the way she kept everything: in a pocket, beside the gear. [69] The gear was brass, the size of her palm, the last surviving tooth-wheel of her great-grandmother's calculating machine. [87] That machine had been built to solve one problem, and it had failed, and the failure had become inheritance. [106] The problem was the transit. [111] Every four centuries the sleeper-ark Meridian passed through the garden's outer petals, carrying ninety thousand dreamers who had never seen anything but dark. [134] Ilsandre's family had appointed themselves, generations ago, the ones who would show them light. [148] Her great-grandmother had used fire. [153] Hydrogen petals and an ignition flare: the garden burned for eleven years, and the Meridian passed through smoke and saw nothing. [174] Her grandmother, correcting the error, used a smaller fire, and burned a smaller quadrant, and was seen by no one. [194] Her mother had refused to burn anything and had spent sixty years feeding numbers into the calculating machine, searching for a method that did not consume what it illuminated. [223] The machine gave her an answer that approximates infinity: a series that climbed and climbed and never closed. [241] She died still adding terms. [246] Ilsandre understood the pattern by the time she was twelve. [256] Each generation inherited the same hunger, tried the opposite of what its mother had tried, and failed in a new direction. [277] The pattern was the true heirloom; the gear was only its souvenir. [289] So she did not calculate, and she did not burn. [299] She listened. [301] The garden's petals were dense enough to carry sound, and Ilsandre learned to speak to them by sonar pinging from the gondola's hull -- short pulses, patient, like a knuckle on a door. [333] Struck at the right frequency, the frozen hydrogen fluoresced: a cold, blue, borrowed glow that took nothing from the petal and gave it back a moment later. [360] Light without fire. [363] It had been in front of them for four hundred years, and none of them had heard it, because all of them had been busy making things brighter. [391] The trouble was scale. [395] One gondola could ping perhaps forty petals in a night. [405] The Meridian's transit would last across the quiet sigh of falling petals -- the garden's shedding season, ninety days -- and then the ark would be gone into the dark for another four centuries. [437] She would need ten thousand gondolas, or ten thousand years, and she had one, and ninety days. [454] So Ilsandre did the sentimental thing, which was also, she suspected, the only correct one. [469] She took the brass gear from her pocket and welded it to the hull as a resonator plate. [487] Its teeth split each pulse into forty-one harmonics, and each harmonic found a petal, and each petal woke its neighbor. [507] The glow spread the way gossip spreads, or grief, or family: outward, unevenly, without permission. [522] By the fortieth day the garden was a lantern ninety million kilometers wide, and the Meridian slid through the center of it, and Ilsandre sat in her gondola with nothing left to do. [555] The ark did not signal. [560] Sleeper-arks never did; the dreamers would not wake for another eleven centuries, and whatever their sealed eyes had registered would surface only in dreams they would never be able to source. [591] This was the troubling part, and Ilsandre made herself sit inside it: she had solved the problem and would never be told so. [614] The serenity came anyway, which was worse and better. [623] Petals fell around the gondola in their slow blue sigh, lighting themselves as they went. [638] She thought about her daughter, asleep below deck, who would inherit a gear welded to a hull and a garden that already glowed. [661] The girl would want to do the opposite of her mother; that was the pattern, and patterns did not care what you had proved. [685] Perhaps she would light a fire. [691] Ilsandre found she could hold that thought without flinching, here, in the long shedding, with the ark going quietly through her one good answer. [715] She pinged once more, for no reason, and forty-one harmonics went out across the dark, and something very far away turned briefly, beautifully white. [739]
