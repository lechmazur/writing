Marek Sova whistled while he weeded the Halvard plot, which the villagers thought unseemly, though the dead had never complained. [20] He was a blithe cemetery gardener in a trade that rewarded gloom, and his cheer sat on the graveyard like velveteen: soft, dark, warm to the touch. [47] For eleven years he had played chess with old Ivo Halvard on the bench beneath the lindens, the brass chess clock ticking between them like a patient heart. [75] Ivo died on a Tuesday with his rook lifted, and Marek let the game stand. [90] After the chess clock ran down, its flag falling with a small red click, he finally packed the pieces away. [110]

The will was read that same afternoon. [117] To his daughter Ilse, who had not written in nine years, Ivo left the farm's debts and a sealed condition; to Marek, a tapestry with a frayed border and no explanation at all. [150] Ilse wanted the antique sovereign case. [156] Everyone in the village knew Ivo had one, walnut and mother-of-pearl, and that he had stopped carrying it the winter she left. [178] She came to the shed where Marek kept his seeds tenderly organized in labeled tins, alphabetical, aster to zinnia. [197] "He hid it to spite me," she said. [205] Marek did not think so, but he could not prove kindness any more than she could prove malice, and that was the whole difficulty: he wanted to discover the shape of intentions, the way you learn a bulb by the flower it becomes. [248]

So he hung the tapestry in his shed and studied it by lamplight. [261] The picture was ordinary - reapers, a dog, a blue tower - but the border was a lattice of knotted threads, and Ivo had been a man who hid his meaning in margins. [292] It took nine nights to untangle them. [299] The frayed strands hung in eight columns and eight ranks; read as a chessboard, each knot marked a square, and the squares in order replayed their unfinished last game, ending where Ivo's rook had hovered. [334] That final square, laid over the woven picture, fell exactly on the blue tower - and the blue tower, Marek realized, was no tower. [357]

It was the cobalt silo on the Halvard land, painted that impossible blue the summer Ilse was born. [375] He walked out at midnight, and the silo stood under stars like a chess piece nobody had moved in years. [395] Inside, the dark was plush, and every sound came back strange. [406] Marek said his own name and the silo returned it three times, each version older, until the last echo sounded like Ivo saying it. [430] These were the uncharted echoes the old man had told him about once, laughing: acoustics no surveyor had mapped, a curve in the wall that made confession possible because it answered you. [462]

Marek found the niche where the echo went thin, and in it the antique sovereign case, walnut, mother-of-pearl, cold as a stone in a stream. [487] It held one gold sovereign and a folded letter, and the letter was addressed to Ilse in a hand that shook. [508] He read it in the starlight at the silo's mouth, because he had come all this way for intentions, not for gold. [530] Ivo had written that he was a coward who could speak only to walls; that the debts were his and the land hers, free, the moment she claimed it; that he had kept one sovereign back so that whoever found the case would have to bring it to her and say the rest aloud. [584] The frayed border, he understood then, had been Ivo's slowest way of asking a favor. [599] Marek laughed, and the silo laughed back in a voice that was not entirely his. [614]

In the morning he brought Ilse the case, the letter, and a tin of asters. [629] She read standing up, twice, and then sat down on the shed floor among the alphabetized tins. [646] "He hid it so you would have to speak to me," she said, and Marek nodded, because that was the shape of it. [669] That autumn she planted the asters on her father's grave, and Marek whistled while she worked, which she found unseemly, and then did not. [693] He wound the brass clock again and set it running for two. [705]
