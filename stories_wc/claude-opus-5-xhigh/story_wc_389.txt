The Grieve Observatory sat on a spike of rock above the clouds, and Ilse Vantry had lived there alone for nine years. [22] Her only door was a cobalt glass portico, hinged in three panels, built by men who believed blue kept lightning polite. [43] Lightning was not polite. [47] It came nightly, cracking the sky white, and the portico threw blue lozenges across her floor like a cathedral that had gone feral. [70] She kept a goat named Pontiff for milk and argument. [80] In the vault beneath the dome lay the reason she stayed: four hundred smoked-glass plates from the Hollow Dynasty, each one a shadow caught during an eclipse and fixed with resin. [111] Ancient shadows, the catalog called them. [117] Read in sequence, they were a calendar; read as verse, they were something better. [131] The world below had rotted into checkpoints and coal barons, and Ilse had decided, in her thirty-first year, that it needed a new dawn more than it needed accurate tide tables. [162] So she built a transmitter out of the old star-clock and spoke. [174] She could not send a whole sermon; the barons jammed anything strong enough to matter. [189] So she sent it in pieces, through fragmented radio frequencies, a line here at 44 kilocycles, a line there at 812, timed to the storm's own static so the jammers heard only weather. [222] The words came from the plates, translated into her own tongue and made lyrically prophetic, because plain instructions get argued with and songs get memorized. [247] "When the ash-hen counts her seven eggs, the mines will unfold like a hand," she told the dark, and the dark carried it down. [271] The trouble with fragments is that nobody receives all of them. [282] In the salt towns they caught the verses about tides and founded a creed of patient waiting. [299] In the coal valleys they caught only the verses about hands and unfolding, and they burned a baron's counting-house. [318] Ilse listened to the results on a receiver held together with solder and hope, and learned a hard word for what she had made: variant allegiance. [344] One voice, nine gospels, each faction certain the others were deaf. [355] Then a boy in the coal valleys began stitching the fragments together. [367] His name was Corb Ellery, and he owned a radio set, a bicycle, and an unreasonable memory. [384] He rode between towns collecting verses the way other boys collected bottle caps, and after four years he could recite the whole thing. [407] They called him the Prophet by the fifth year, and by the seventh the salt towns and the coal valleys marched under one flag with an ash-hen on it. [436] Ilse watched the cloud deck glow orange in nine places, and milked her goat, and did not feel triumphant. [455] She felt the specific loneliness of a woman who has invented a friend for everyone but herself. [472] In the ninth year, someone knocked on the cobalt glass. [482] Corb was twenty-six and thin and had climbed nine thousand feet in a borrowed coat. [497] He looked at her the way people look at weather they have survived. [510] "I came to ask what the last verse is," he said. [521] "The one about the dawn, and nobody ever caught the end." [532] There was no last verse. [537] The Hollow Dynasty's final plate was cracked, and she had been improvising for nine years off a broken calendar and her own stubborn hope. [561] She could tell him that, and the flag with the ash-hen would come apart into nine flags again. [579] Or she could lie, and be a god, and be lonelier. [590] Instead she took him down to the vault and showed him the cracked plate. [604] "The end isn't written," she said. [610] "That's the good news." [614] "You will have to write it yourself, and you will get it wrong, and someone will argue with you." [633] Corb laughed until Pontiff head-butted him, which she took as the plates' final blessing. [647] They spent the night at the transmitter, sending the truth out on all nine frequencies at once, unencrypted, unlyrical, and dull as bread: there is no prophet, there are only fragments, assemble them together. [681] The barons jammed it and nobody heard, which was, Ilse admitted, extremely funny. [694] At six in the morning the storm broke and the cloud deck turned the color of a struck match. [713] Ilse pushed the three panels of the portico open, and the cobalt glass unfolded like a hand. [730] Blue light lay over both of them, and over the goat, and over nine thousand feet of weather. [748] "Say something prophetic," Corb said. [753] Ilse considered the sunrise, which was ancient and secondhand and arriving anyway. [765] "Mind the step," she said. [770] They went down together, into the cloud, to argue with the morning. [782]
