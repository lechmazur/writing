Nobody in Coldhollow remembered how to sing the orchard awake, and the trees had begun to notice. [17] The apples came small and bitter; the grapes shriveled on the arbor's old ribs; the bees went elsewhere. [35] Nine years of thin harvests had convinced the village that soil could be argued with, and it could not. [54] So they went, at last, to Odile Brannoch, who lived in the mill-house full of clocks that told the wrong time on purpose. [77] Odile was a tinkerer of the whimsical sort, prone to building umbrellas that predicted arguments and kettles that whistled in harmony with the wind. [101] She was also the last person alive who had heard old Cantor Hesp sing the harvest rites, forty years ago, when she was small enough to hide under the pressing table. [132] "I remember their shapes, not their words," she said, "and shapes are enough scaffolding for a machine." [149]

What she built, over three months, was a spindle-organ: brass pipes, a treadle, a cylinder of beeswax, and a bow strung not with gut but with a living vine tendril cut from the arbor's oldest stock. [185] Dead string holds a note, she explained; green string holds a memory, because the vine had stood in the arbor every autumn since the songs were still sung there. [214] The tendril curled around the peg like a finger remembering a hand. [226]

Her motive was not the apples. [232] Odile wanted the sacred practices restored: the dawn walk, the naming of the rows, the cup poured out at the root, because a village that forgets how to thank a place will one day forget the place, and then it will sell it. [275] The remedy had to arrive through forgotten songs, she insisted; nothing else had ever fed that ground. [292] And it could only be tried when everything aligned: the harvest moon full and low, the equinox sun coming up opposite it, the arbor in leaf, the whole village awake and standing still. [325] That happened, by her wrong-on-purpose clocks, once every nineteen years. [335]

They came up the hill in the dark, ninety-one of them, carrying lanterns they did not light. [352] The arbor at dawn was verdant beyond reason for so late in the year, leaves still deep green, the grapevine ropes overhead braided into a low roof. [379] Westward the harvest moon sat huge and rust-colored on the hills; eastward the sky was going the color of cider. [399] A gathering hush moved through the crowd, the kind that is not silence but a hundred people deciding at once to listen. [421]

Odile set the spindle-organ on the flagstone where the Cantors had always stood, and worked the treadle. [438] The bow drew across the pipes. [444] What came out was thin at first, a scraping, and someone's grandmother laughed nervously. [458] Then the vine tendril tightened on its own, tuning itself, and the machine began to spin golden stories out of the wax. [480] That is the only honest way to say it. [489] Threads of sound came off the cylinder like fiber off a distaff, and they carried pictures: a man in the row with a cracked cup, a girl counting rows by their old names, Marrow and Widow and Little Bell. [528]

The tunes were not new to anyone. [535] That was the astonishment of it. [541] They had been living in the village all along, hidden in lullabies and counting rhymes and the thing the millers hummed while waiting for the sluice. [567] The machine had only sorted them back into their order. [577] Old Tobin Hesp, ninety and unmusical, sang the second verse before he knew he was singing. [593] Then the rest of them, badly, all in different keys, walking the rows as the moon went down and the sun came up and for one narrow minute both hung in the leaves at once. [628] Odile stopped treadling and let them go on without her, which had been the point of the whole contraption. [647]

The soil did not heal overnight, of course; remedies that work through songs work at the speed of the thing being remembered. [669] But three autumns later the arbor gave eleven barrels, and the children knew the names of the rows, and nobody needed the machine. [692] Odile took the tendril off the peg and planted it, and it grew. [705] She kept the spindle-organ in the mill-house anyway, dusted, strung with plain gut now, in case the village ever forgot again. [726] It had nineteen years to wait, and clocks that were wrong on purpose, and all the time in the world. [746]
