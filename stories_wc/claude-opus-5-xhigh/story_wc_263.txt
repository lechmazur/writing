Ilva was called moonstruck, and she never argued, because the word tasted true. [13] She dreamed with her eyes open, and she heard the words people did not say. [28] In Sarn that was a wound, not a gift. [37] Because Sarn had been emptied of making. [44] Forty winters ago, when the burning fleets came up the coast, Ilva's grandmother Deneb had gathered every song, pattern, and half-finished carving in the town and sealed them under the old aurora-lit archway at the cliff's edge, where the northern light poured through the stones like green water. [92] She meant to keep them safe. [98] She died before she could tell anyone how to open the arch again. [111] So the town survived and stopped inventing. [118] Weavers copied their mothers' patterns exactly; the fiddlers played six tunes; nobody wrote anything down, and the almanac the printer set out each spring stayed blank, an unwritten calendar of days nobody expected to be different. [154] Ilva heard what that cost them. [160] All day the unspoken words pressed at her: a stanza the baker swallowed with his bread, a lullaby the harbormaster refused to hum, ten thousand sentences dying behind teeth. [189] It was like living beside a river dammed at the throat. [200] She wanted, more than rest, more than bread, to free the trapped creativity of Sarn before it forgot it had ever existed. [222] Deneb had left one thing: an astrolabe of meteor iron, black and pitted, its rete cut into a spiral of falling sparks. [244] When Ilva held it under open sky, she heard it too - not words exactly, but a low unspoken ache, the way a thing remembers being something else. [271] Remembered stardust, she decided; the iron had been a star, and it missed the fall. [286] And the arch was cut from the same black stone. [296] That was the whole of the key, and it took her a year to believe it: the arch would answer to its own history, if someone would tell it back. [326] So she began by tracing the path of falling stars. [336] Every clear night she lay on the cliff grass with the astrolabe and the printer's blank almanac, and when a meteor tore its bright seam across the dark she drew the line, hour and altitude and azimuth, onto the empty page for that date. [380] Line by line, the unwritten calendar filled with the falling of stars. [392] It took years. [395] Sarn watched her go strange - the moonstruck girl on the cliff, the dreamer who would not rest, thinning through summers, coughing through winters, refusing to marry, refusing to explain. [424] She could hear them pitying her, which was worse than hearing them speak. [437] But the work was rapture, shadowed rapture, joy with a grave dug under it: every night she gave away sleep and got back one more line of a story the sky had been telling since before there was anyone to hear it. [479] On the last night of the eleventh year the almanac had no empty dates left. [494] Ilva carried it to the archway, where the aurora hung in long green curtains and lit the black stones from within. [515] She read the book aloud from the first page - every meteor, every angle, eleven years of falling - and she felt the arch listening the way a person listens when someone finally says the thing they could never say. [553] The stone remembered. [556] It remembered being stardust, remembered the long bright fall, and in remembering it let go. [571] What came out of the archway was not light exactly. [581] It was forty years of unfinished things, and it went down into the town like weather. [597] The baker woke saying his stanza aloud. [604] The harbormaster hummed. [607] A weaver cut a pattern nobody's mother had ever made, and by morning the printer was setting type for a calendar with things written in it. [633] Ilva sat down on the cliff grass with the astrolabe cool and quiet in her lap, and for the first time since she was a girl she heard nothing at all - no swallowed songs, no dammed river, no ache of iron missing the sky. [677] Everything was being said. [681] So she let herself rest, there under the green curtains, and slept until the town came up the path singing to find her. [704]
