The net-mender kept his stool at the harbor's elbow, where the tide left its arguments, and he answered questions only after he had finished a knot. [26] The fishermen called him a philosopher, partly as a joke, because he charged for mending and gave away his conclusions free. [47] Mira came to him during firefly season, holding a bamboo tea whisk wrapped in her grandmother's sleeve. [64] "I want to find the man who made this," she said, with the demure audacity of a girl who had rehearsed the sentence for a month. [90] Ozu turned the whisk in the lamplight. [97] Eighty splines, thin as insect legs, curled inward like a held breath. [109] "Why?" he asked. [112] "Grandmother whisked tea with it for forty years and never learned his name, and I would like to thank a hand that split bamboo eighty times without slipping once." [141] The net-mender set down his shuttle, which he had not done for any of the merchants that week. [159] "Then you are asking about hidden patterns," he said, "which is the only subject I know." [175] He explained that nothing made by hand is anonymous. [184] A net carries the mender's hesitations in the spacing of its knots; a whisk carries the maker's wrist in the lean of its tines. [208] This one leaned left, and its knot-thread was tied in the double loop used only by the craft houses of Sarai, three coves north. [232] "Go tonight," he said, "by following moon paths, the way fishermen went before there were lamps." [248] Mira did not understand, so he took her to the sea stairs and pointed. [262] Rain had come and gone at dusk, and the moon lay across the wet flagstones in a broken silver road that bent where the stones were worn. [289] "Walk where the light is," Ozu said; "the light finds the paths that feet have polished for two hundred years." [309] So she went, and the road of moonlight took her out of the harbor, up through terraces where fireflies rose off the flooded paddies in slow, cold sparks. [337] The season made the whole hillside scintillatingly somber, a thousand small lights insisting on themselves above black water, each one lasting a week and knowing it. [363] Where the moonlight stopped, she found a wall. [371] It was the old sea-defense of Sarai, and after rain the parapet whispered. [384] Water drained through flutes cut into its cap-stones, and the flutes were cut at differing depths, so the draining made a low, uneven speech that rose and fell for an hour after every storm. [418] Mira stood with her palm on the whispering parapet and understood that the depths were not random. [435] Somebody had tuned a wall. [440] Below in the village, one house still had a lamp, and inside it an old man sat splitting bamboo with a blade no wider than a fingernail. [467] His left wrist leaned as he worked. [474] Mira, who had never in her life spoken first to a stranger, put her grandmother's whisk on his mat and asked whether he remembered making it. [500] The old man, whose name was Tomi, laughed and said he had made eleven thousand of them and remembered none. [520] Then he looked closer at the worn tines and stopped laughing. [531] "This one drank forty years of tea," he said, "and nobody ever came back to tell me whether the splits held." [552] They made tea on his brazier at midnight, and she whisked it herself, badly, and he corrected her grip twice. [572] Before she left, she asked about the wall. [580] Tomi said his grandfather had cut the flutes, and had cut them so the drainage would whistle a warning pitch when the sea got high enough to mean danger. [609] Nobody thanked him either. [613] Mira walked home on the same silver road, carrying the whisk, understanding at last why the net-mender answered questions only after finishing a knot. [637] He was not being rude; he was showing her that the work is the part nobody watches. [654] That autumn she began keeping a ledger of makers - wall-cutters, rope-layers, the woman who glazed the harbor lamps - and she read it aloud at the tea house on rainy nights, when the parapet was talking and everyone could hear that a thing built well keeps speaking long after the builder is gone. [706] Ozu listened from the doorway, mending, saying nothing. [714] When she finished, he whistled once, low and approving, the pitch of the wall. [728]
