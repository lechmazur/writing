They called Ilva the vigilant horizon mapper, because she had spent eleven winters drawing the exact place where the sky met the ridges. [23] Her charts hung in the basalt sanctuary by the frozen lake, black stone on black water, and the novices thought them useless. [45] Kesh thought them worse than useless. [51] He was seventeen, and he wanted the valley freed before the first snow. [64]

Below the sanctuary the magnetic viaduct ran nine miles across the marsh, a spine of lodestone piers that the Concord had built to haul ore out of the valley. [93] Dry, it was dead weight. [98] The magnetic viaduct after rain was another creature entirely: water woke the current in the stone, and the sledges floated, and the yoked teams of the valley walked them out humming. [129] The teams were people: debtors of the Concord, yoked in pairs at the shoulder, walking ore to the coast for the price of their bread. [154] Kesh's mother was one of them. [160]

"Burn the piers," he said, the night he came up the scree with a satchel of pitch. [177] Ilva took the pitch, set it in the corner, and gave him a pencil. [191] She was softly purposeful in the way of people who have already decided everything and are only waiting for the world to catch up. [215] "We will unyoke them," she said. [221] "Not the piers, not the sledges, not one hair of the haulers." [233]

"When?" [234]

"After the lake freezes over," she said. [241]

Then she showed him why, and this was how she began to sculpt patience from impatience: with arithmetic. [259] The overseers slept in the tower at Vesk Gate, and the guard on the marsh road changed at moonrise. [278] Twice a year, and only twice, the moon rose in the notch above Corrin Ridge and laid a white road straight down the viaduct's length, blinding anyone who looked east along it. [310] Ilva knew the date by tracking moon paths for eleven years across her charts, marking each rise against the horizon she had measured stone by stone. [336] The winter notch came eleven days after the lake normally froze. [347] Ice meant the haulers' families could cross from the far shore on foot, four hundred of them, instead of walking eight miles around into the guns at Vesk Gate. [376] And they needed rain, because a wet viaduct carried its own load; the sledges would drift free of the haulers' harness, and the Concord's ore would roll to the coast without a single yoked shoulder. [411] The Concord had always sworn the stone needed shoulders behind it; Ilva had measured the current after every rain for eleven years and knew it was a lie. [439]

"Ours is a patient revolution," Ilva said. [446] "Proof, in front of four hundred witnesses, that the stone can pull the load and the people never had to." [466]

Kesh waited badly. [469] He waited through a summer of copying her horizon lines, through the autumn rains that came too early, through the week his mother's shoulder went septic under the yoke. [498] Twice he took the pitch out of the corner, and twice he put it back, and Ilva said nothing either time, which was the whole of her teaching. [526] By the end he could stand at the sanctuary door with his fists open, which is a kind of wildness held on a short rein. [551]

The lake froze on the ninth of Frostmonth. [559] Rain came on the nineteenth, warm and wrong and perfect, and the piers began to hum through the wet dusk. [579] At moonrise the notch above Corrin Ridge filled with white light, exactly where Ilva's eleven years of charts said it would, and the guards on the marsh road turned their faces from the glare. [613] Four hundred people came over the ice without a lantern among them. [625] Kesh walked the length of the viaduct with a knife and cut harness, and the haulers stepped out of the leather, and the sledges kept going, floating on the woken stone, unyoked and unattended, nine miles to the coast. [664] Nobody fought, because there was nothing left to fight about. [674] The Concord's own ore, arriving on time, argued the case. [684]

By spring the yokes were firewood in the basalt sanctuary, and Ilva was on the roof again with her rule and her lamp, mapping the horizon east of the thawing lake. [715] "What now?" Kesh asked. [719] "The coast towns are still yoked," she said, and drew another line. [731] "Nine years, I think." [735] He sat down beside her, and took up the pencil, and began to wait. [749]
