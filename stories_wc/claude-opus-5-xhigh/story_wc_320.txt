Mira Okonkwo was the last celestial signal interpreter left in Halloway Reach, which meant she was also its plumber, its cook, and its keeper of pigeons. [26] The city had been built as an antenna farm: forty steel towers bolted into the cliffs, laced together with catwalks, suspended over a gorge so deep the river sounded like a rumor. [58] When the broadcasts stopped, the engineers walked down the switchback road and did not come back. [74] Mira stayed, because unfinished work is only a question left standing out in the rain. [89]

Her trade, stated plainly, was to listen to the sky and write down what it meant. [105] Her tools were plain as well: a copper loop she had wound by hand, a chalkboard, a notebook, and the hollywood star fragment. [128] The fragment was a wedge of pink terrazzo and tarnished brass, three broken letters, ETH, pried out of a boulevard that no longer existed and traded up the mountain for salt and batteries. [161] Inside the brass, sealed like a seed, was a shard of hush. [173] Silence that old and that complete is rare and useful, because to tune anything you first need a true zero, a place where nothing is pretending to be a sound. [203] Mira held the fragment against her ear each morning the way other people wound a watch. [219]

For eleven years she had chased one signal. [226] It arrived only during lunar perigee, when the moon swung close enough to press on the towers, and the whole city went serenely electric: guy-wires humming a soft chord, her hair lifting, the catwalk railings warm under her palms. [265] Every time, she caught the same eleven seconds of structured noise, and every time she failed to read it. [284] Tonight the moon would come nearer than it had in a lifetime, and the diesel in Tower Nine had four hours of fuel left, which made the arithmetic simple. [313] Last chance, and then the sky would be somebody else's problem. [324]

So she did the practical thing, which her old instructors had called the hardest thing: she began by discarding assumptions. [344] On the chalkboard she wrote them in a column and rubbed each out as she let it go. [362] That the signal came from far away. [369] That it was a message meant for someone. [377] That it was in a language. [383] That it was new. [387] The last one took her until moonrise, and it cost her something, because if it was not new then eleven years of hoping had been eleven years of hearing an old thing badly. [420]

She wiped the board clean, set the star fragment in the copper loop, and let the hush give her a floor to stand on. [444] Then she began to tune: not outward, not up, but slow and close, the way you find a heartbeat instead of a drum. [467] The eleven seconds arrived at 11:42, riding the perigee moon like a stone skipped off a pond. [484] Without her assumptions, it unfolded easily, almost kindly. [492]

It was Halloway Reach's own final broadcast. [499] Forty towers, one last surge of power, aimed high on the night the engineers left, and the moon had caught it, held it, and been handing it back ever since, a little quieter with each pass. [535] A woman's voice, tired and warm, said: "That's the end of the day from us up here. Thanks for keeping us company." [557] Mira sat down on the cold plate steel and laughed until her eyes stung. [571]

An interpreter's real work, she had been taught, is to find the beginning in every ending, and here was an ending that had been circling the sky for eleven years, waiting for someone to hear it plainly enough to answer. [611] She had three hours of diesel, so she used them practically. [622] She patched the transmitter with wire from the pigeon loft, swung the array at the moon, and pressed the key. [642] Her voice went up bright and shaking: "Halloway Reach, back on the air. Good morning." [657] Two and a half seconds later the gorge below her filled with the sound of herself returning, and she wrote in the notebook, under the erased column: signal received, origin local, reply sent. [690] Below in the valley, some farmer's radio would pick it up, and someone would climb the switchback road to see who was still alive up in the towers. [718]

Mira put the hollywood star fragment back in her pocket, where the shard of hush sat cool against her hip, patient, ready to give her another zero when the next silence needed reading. [751] Then she went to see about the diesel. [759]
