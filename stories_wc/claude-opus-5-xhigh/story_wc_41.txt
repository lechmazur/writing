The junkyard prophet kept his prophecies in chalk because chalk forgets, and Ozro needed a medium that could keep up with him. [22] He was precognitively forgetful: he could remember Thursday clearly on Monday, and by Wednesday night the whole day had drained out of him like water through a rusted pan. [51] So he wrote the future down on hubcaps and refrigerator doors, in a hand that the coastal rain took back within a week. [74] I found him at the low end of the yard, where the scrap gave way to the fog sculpture garden the town had built over the drowned mill. [102] Sixty nozzles breathed white shapes into the air on the hour, and the shapes stood there like people you almost knew before the wind unmade them. [128] On a bent car door he had chalked: SHE COMES AT LOW TIDE. BRING THE RECORDER. DON'T LIE TO HER. [148] "You're early," he said, though he plainly no longer knew why he had written it. [163] I told him what I wanted: to locate the last videotape my mother made, the one nobody in our family ever played. [185] For thirty years she filmed the same kitchen on the same June morning, and the crate sat in the hush that old videotapes keep when they are left unwatched, holding a voice that dropped a half-step every summer. [223] I was trying to capture the sound of growing older, and I had come to believe her voice was the only honest instrument for it. [248] After she died, my brother sold the crate to the junkyard with everything else in the house. [265] Ozro nodded like a man remembering a chore, which for him was the same as planning. [281] "I already looked for it," he said. [288] "Tomorrow, I think." [291] He led me down to the tideline, where he had laid driftwood in long arrows across the sand, each one pointing inland at a different angle. [317] The sea rearranged them nightly; that was the point. [326] He arranged the markers on the days he still remembered what his future self would need, so that the man he became each morning could follow them without understanding them. [356] We walked the arrows into the fog garden, from the drowned mill wheel to a hedge of steam, and the white bodies parted and closed behind us. [383] The last arrow ended at a mound of gravel behind the compressor shed, and under it, wrapped in a feed sack, was my mother's crate. [408] Thirty tapes, labeled in her small handwriting, salt-swollen and furred with mildew. [445] The last tape, June of her seventy-ninth year, went in with a sound like a door closing far away. [464] There was no picture. [468] The oxide had shed itself into the damp; her kitchen, her hands, her half-step-lower voice, all of it had gone back to being dust on plastic. [494] What came out of the speaker was hiss: a long grey tide of it, rising and falling, exactly the sound of the water behind us. [519] I sat in the fog with my headphones on and understood that I had found it. [535] The sound of growing older is not a voice dropping; it is the slow, patient hiss of the thing that carried the voice giving itself back. [561] I recorded eleven minutes of it. [601] "Tell tomorrow that I heard something worth hearing." [609] So I wrote it, in letters big enough for a man to read from the gate, knowing the rain would have it by Friday.
