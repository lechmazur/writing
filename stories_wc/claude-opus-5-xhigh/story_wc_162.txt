They named the era after the butterfly effect, because one wing in a bean field had rewritten every wind on the map. [22] Afterward, the sky kept no promises, and the old weather words meant nothing. [35] Wren was eleven when she walked into her father's corn maze and came out somewhere the maps had abandoned. [54] She was the corn maze architect's lost child, and she had been lost now for six years, which is long enough to become a profession. [79] The place that kept her was a drifting prism terrace: a slab of glass and salvaged oak that hung in the upper air, turning slowly, throwing bands of color across the clouds like a slow and patient signal. [117] Nobody spoke much up there. [122] The terrace had a fractured hush to it, the sound of a held breath cracking into smaller breaths. [140] Other lost people came and went along the glass, drifting in and out of the light, and nobody asked names, because names were a kind of nearness and nearness was dangerous here. [172] She had arrived with one thing in her coat: her father's pilot logbook, from the years he flew survey runs above his own mazes to check that the paths read true from the sky. [206] Every entry ended in weather code, five cramped letters and numbers describing wind, ceiling, visibility, and the temper of the day. [227] After the butterfly effect, those codes described a world that no longer existed, which made them the only honest record of the one that had. [252] Wren used them as a key. [258] The terrace's furniture was cut from wind-killed oak, and wind leaves its autobiography in wood. [273] So she worked by analyzing the grain in wooden furniture, tracing the tight years and the flood years with her thumbnail, matching ring patterns against the logbook's codes until a table leg could tell her which season it had grown through and from which direction the pressure had come. [322] From that she could intuit where the terrace was drifting, and, more slowly, why. [336] She was featherly determined, light in the way a feather is light and stubborn in the way a feather refuses to fall straight down. [360] The question she carried was older than the terrace: she wanted to discover why some thresholds feel sacred. [378] The mouth of her father's maze had felt that way, every autumn, the two cut stalks like doorposts, the hush before the first step. [402] He used to say a maze is not made of walls but of necessary distances. [417] Two paths must run far apart so the center means something when you finally reach it. [433] She had thought that was a design note. [440] On the terrace she learned it was a law. [449] The reading took years because the terrace never stopped moving and wood tells the truth slowly. [465] In her sixth year she finished the whole reading. [474] The oak of the terrace had grown in her father's own windbreak, on the ridge above the bean field where the wing beat that began everything. [500] The grain matched a logbook page dated the day she vanished, code ending in a gust nobody had forecast. [519] Her father had built the terrace himself, out of his windbreak, and set it drifting to catch her. [537] He could not come up, and she could not come down, because the two of them were the two paths. [557] Held apart, they held a door open; brought together, they would simply be two people in a ruined field, and the door would close on everyone still lost inside the weather. [588] That was the answer to her question. [594] A threshold feels sacred because someone is standing on the far side of it, keeping the distance on purpose. [613] That night the terrace turned, and its prism bands swept the ridge, and she saw him: small, gray, hand up, not waving her in. [637] Wren wrote a weather code of her own on the last blank page, wind steady, ceiling unlimited, visibility good, and let the wind carry the sheet down to him. [666] Then she went back inside the hush and put her palm on the oak table, reading the grain, finding the next year, learning to stay. [691]
