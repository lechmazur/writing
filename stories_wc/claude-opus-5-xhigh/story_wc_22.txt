The reef died in the third week of that hot spring, and the sea above it went quiet in a way no chart could explain. [25] Ostrel heard about it from the birds before any courier reached Gloom Hill. [38] He had been a coachman on that hill for forty years, and the terns that nested in its dead thorn told him things the roads never did. [65] Their cries had changed pitch: not the two-note complaint of a good season, but the flat, falling syllable they used over water gone bitter. [89] He harnessed the mare and went down to the coast to see what the Concord had become. [106] Forty years earlier, as a young driver, he had carried the delegates of both shores down this same road to sign their peace. [129] They had sunk the treaty rather than store it: divers pressed the words into living coral over Hollow Shoal, where two fleets had burned each other to cinders, so that the reef itself would grow around the promise and hold it. [170] The Submerged Concord, they called it, and every seventh year a reader was rowed out to recite it aloud in Vashken, the drowned tongue both shores had once shared. [199] Ostrel had been that reader for the last twenty years, because a coachman who idles at borders learns dead languages the way other men learn weather. [225] Now the coral was chalk, and the words were sand, and by noon of his arrival both shores had sent officers to stand on the old jetty and argue over whose shoal it was. [259]

Ostrel did not argue. [263] He unloaded, from beneath the coach seat, a collection of dried seed pods he had gathered on every route he ever drove: sea holly, tamarisk, samphire, bladder campion, the rattling black husks of coast lupine. [298] Forty years of pockets. [302] He had never known what they were for until the terns told him the reef was dying and something would have to hold the promise instead. [328]

The jetty was a narrow thing, three planks wide, running two hundred yards into an evening so wide it made his chest ache. [351] That was the whole of his life, he thought: a strip of board, and all that sky. [368] He walked out along it with the pods in his hat and began pressing them into the silt-choked cracks, into the barnacled crevices, into the mud banks the dead reef had let the tide pile up on either side. [407] To fertilize them he used what the shoal itself offered: ash and bone-grit dredged up from the burned fleets, an old war rendered down to grey meal. [434]

The officers watched him from the landward end, contemptuous, then curious. [445] "What are you planting, old man?" one called. [453] "A garden," Ostrel said. [457] "You cannot muster on a garden." [463]

They laughed, but they did not leave, and he kept working while the light went copper and the curlews came in. [484] He listened to them the way he had listened all his life, and he learned things no surveyor could have given him. [506] Where the curlews probed and stayed, freshwater seeped up through the ruined coral; where they lifted at once, the ground was salt and would take only samphire. [533] The oystercatchers' alarm marked the channel edge, too deep to plant. [544]

By full twilight he had sown the whole length of it, and the jetty was already blooming where last year's windfall seed had beaten him to the work: pink thrift in the plank joints, sea rocket white as spilled salt, a haze of it going violet in the dusk. [593] Then he stood at the jetty's end above the pale bones of the reef and recited the Concord in Vashken, all of it, from memory, to the water that no longer held it. [626] The officers had come halfway out along the planks to hear him, careful of the thrift. [642] One of them, from the northern shore, mouthed a phrase along with him; his grandmother had sung it. [660]

"It is only a treaty if someone can read it," Ostrel said, "and only a border if someone can stand on it. [682] In three summers you will not find the shoal under the flowers. [694] In ten, the roots will hold what the coral let go." [705]

He gave the northern officer a pod of coast lupine and told him what the curlews said about the freshwater seep, and how to listen for it himself. [733] Then he drove back up Gloom Hill in the dark, the mare finding the road by habit, and behind him the narrow blooming line lay out across the vast dim water like a sentence someone had finally learned to say. [773]
