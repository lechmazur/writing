Vashti Oren had run the celestial mapping observatory at Kestrel Ridge for thirty-one winters, and in all that time she had never once slept through a clear night. [28] Her plates hung in the drying room like pale windows, each one a patch of sky she had measured, weighed, and named. [50] The work she could not finish alone was the temple. [60] Three valleys south, in a ruin the maps called Anshet, a basalt lintel carried eleven lines of inscription that no philologist had cracked in a century. [86] Vashti had come to believe the inscriptions were not language at all, but light: a record kept through variable stars, whose brightness swelled and faded on fixed and patient schedules. [116] The glyphs, she suspected, were periods; the strokes were magnitudes. [126] But suspicion is a lonely instrument, and she had been alone with it since her brother died. [143]

Above her desk, framed under glass, hung a daylily-pressed telegram: Elias's last message, sent from the dig at Anshet, with a flattened orange blossom he had folded into the paper because daylilies live one day and he wanted her to have that day. [186] The text read: THE LINES ARE NOT WORDS THEY ARE WAITING BRING SOMEONE TO WITNESS. [201] For eight years she had misread that instruction as sentiment. [211]

Idris Kalm arrived in October with a satchel of notebooks and a profession she had to ask him to repeat. [231] He was a lucid dream chronicler: he entered sleep on purpose, kept his attention inside it, and wrote down what he found there before the shapes went soft. [259] Elias had hired him, by letter, the week before the scaffolding fell. [271] The letter had taken eight years and four postal districts to find him. [284]

Vashti expected a mystic and got a man who kept better records than she did. [299] Idris logged sleep onsets to the minute, tabulated recurring images, and cross-checked them against the previous month like a diligent clerk of the interior. [323] He also, Vashti learned, healed with words. [330] Not miraculously; he simply spoke to people at the hour when their grief was loudest, and said the true thing in the plain order, and something in them unclenched. [359] The night watch at Kestrel Ridge stopped taking their sedatives. [369] He had a way of narrating the dark that made it hospitable, a midnight solace with a steady voice and no false comfort in it. [394]

They fell into a division of labor neither had planned. [404] Vashti gave him the photographic plates of Anshet's lintel, and the light curves of forty-one variable stars, and her sleepless arithmetic. [425] Idris carried them into his dreams, because that was the only room large enough to hold eleven lines and forty-one stars at once, and there he watched the glyphs pulse. [455] He could not calculate; she could not see. [463] So they traded: he described the rhythm, she named the star. [474] Shared burdens, he told her once, are not halved; they are simply carriable. [487]

For eleven weeks the pairings failed. [493] Then, on the last night of December, Idris came down from the dome at four in the morning with ink on his knuckles. [516] He had dreamed the lintel again, but this time he had turned it over. [530] The eleven lines were not to be read left to right, he said, but stacked, like a chord. [548] Vashti laid the plates one atop another on the light table and slid them until the strokes agreed. [566] Eleven periods, superposed, resolved into one interval of five days and nine hours: the pulse of the star the temple faced, and the calendar of a rite performed only while its light was rising. [600]

The inscriptions were an observing log. [606] Three thousand years of somebody sitting up all night, keeping faith with a star that dimmed and returned, writing it down so that the next watcher would not think herself mad. [637]

Understanding dawned exactly when the dawn did. [644] Grey came up over the eastern ridge and Vashti found she was crying, which she had not done at the funeral, or since. [667] Idris did not comfort her. [672] He said, in that plain order of his, 'He was right, and you finished it, and he knew you would need a second pair of eyes, which is why he sent for me instead of for help.' [709]

She looked at the telegram under its glass, the daylily gone the color of weak tea, and understood the last word finally as a job description. [735] Witness. [736] Not to mourn a thing alone, but to sit up with it, in company, and record what the light did. [756] That evening she opened a new ledger and wrote both their names at the top of the page. [774]
