Between regimes, the palace at Babylon held its breath. [9] The old king lay embalmed in the cedar hall; the new one had not yet ridden in from Opis. [28] So the astronomers kept the calendar alone, which suited Nidintu-Anu perfectly. [39] She was frugal to the point of legend. [47] She scraped old tablets smooth rather than requisition new clay, burned lamp oil at a miser's wick, and had worn the same brown cloak for nine years, mending it each spring with thread pulled from its own hem. [85]

The palace itself had been reimagined twice in her lifetime. [95] The last king, hungry for omens, had torn out the audience court and rebuilt it as an instrument: bitumen floors inlaid with a grid of glazed tiles, walls of stepped mirrors, and a roof shutter that could be drawn back so the throne sat under open sky. [142] At dusk the mirrors passed the light down in soft descending steps, and the room hummed with a dusky harmony that no music could improve. [167] Nidintu worked there because it was free. [174]

For six years her draughtsman had been Sin-leqe, a Susian who inked her sky-charts. [188] Her vows to Nabu forbade the plain word love, and the palace forbade fondness for foreigners, so they had courted through schematic representations, which no censor thought to read. [217] He would hand her a diagram of Venus at greatest brilliance, and the little ticks marking her altitude would fall, if you counted them as syllables, into a promise. [246] She would answer with a floor plan of the reimagined court, its tiles numbered, and the numbers she chose were never arbitrary. [268] This was encrypted devotion, and it cost nothing but attention, which was the only currency she spent freely. [286]

When the king died, the guard swept the palace of every Susian hand. [299] Sin-leqe left her one object: a magic square tile, kiln-fired, four rows by four, its glaze the blue of the hour they both loved. [323] Every line summed to thirty-four; so did the columns, the diagonals, the four corners, the middle four. [340] Thirty-four numbered the cracked tile in the floor grid where he had always stood. [354] It took her eleven nights to see the rest. [363] Read as positions on her own numbered plan, the square traced a path across the floor; walked in order, the path spelled a word in the old wedge-script, one stride per stroke. [395] The word was wait. [399]

She found this empirically ironic, and said so aloud to the empty hall: a woman who had measured the return of every wandering star could not calculate the return of one man. [431] Her tables could give her Saturn's place in eighteen years. [441] They could not give her his. [447] So she did what her discipline allowed: she made an observation, and then a bargain. [462]

On the last night between regimes, with the new king's dust visible on the northern road, she climbed to the roof and hauled the shutter open to uncloak the court to the sky. [495] Then she came down, unpinned her mended cloak, and laid it aside as well; frugality, she had decided, was not the same as withholding. [519] She prised the broken piece from position thirty-four and set the magic square tile into the gap. [536] It fit. [538] Above her the mirrors carried the last violet light down step by step, and the hum of the room settled into that dusky harmony, sums balancing in every direction. [567]

She walked the path of the square, stride by stride, saying the strokes. [580] She did not pray to Nabu, or to Marduk, or to the king coming down the road. [597] She spoke to what she had spent her life staring at and never once named: the dark between the fixed lights, the thing her tables approached and never touched. [626] "I will keep the record honestly, whether or not he comes," she said. [639] "You will keep whatever you keep. [645] Let the tile be the seal." [651] It was, she knew, a covenant with the unknown, sealed by a thing worth less than a jar of oil, and therefore the most expensive object in Babylon. [679]

The new regime came, crowned itself, and demanded fresh omens by morning. [691] Nidintu gave them accurate ones, cheaply, for eleven more years. [701] In the ninth of those years a Susian draughtsman was permitted back through the Ishtar Gate, and found her at position thirty-four, scraping a tablet smooth. [727] He said nothing. [730] He drew: a square, four by four, and in its center cell, two figures standing on one tile. [748] She checked the sums out of habit, and they balanced, and she took his ink-stained hand. [764]
