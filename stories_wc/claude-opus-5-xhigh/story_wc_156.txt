The question arrived at 4:11 in the afternoon, while Ilse Bezan was mixing a green she had mixed nine hundred times. [21] Her apprentice, Odile, stood at the base of the scaffold with paint on her chin and asked, "Do you know what the missing part of the mural shows?" [49] Ilse did know. [52] That was the trouble. [56] Thirty years of restoring made her a machine of certainty: she could read a fragment of ochre and name the hand, the century, the price of the plaster. [84] The mural itself wrapped the inner curve of the Sound Wave Garden, a municipal oddity of parabolic concrete shells set among reed beds, built in 1931 so that a whisper at one dish arrived, warm and strange, at a dish sixty feet away. [127] Ilse had painted its vivid figures back to life, panel by panel, methodically playful about it: she hid a wren in every restored cornice, a private joke with the future. [157] Only the last panel resisted, three square feet of loss above the eastern dish. [171] So: the question, and then the pause before her reply, which is where all of this happened. [188] In her pocket lay a hobo nickel she had found in the mortar behind the eastern dish, a 1913 buffalo coin some drifter had carved into a woman's face with a nail: high cheeks, one eye winking. [225] She had spun it a hundred times on the concrete lip, liking the sound. [239] Now, waiting to answer, she spun it again, and the ring of nickel on concrete went out across the reeds, struck the far dish, and came back curved. [267] Reflected sound is not the same sound; the garden gave it back to her as a voice. [284] "Guess," it said, and bewitched her. [290] She woke on the scaffold. [295] That was the first awakening, and it felt ordinary: brushes in the tray, Odile's face tilted up, the smell of lime. [316] Except the gap in the plaster was now filled, and what filled it was a woman with high cheeks, one eye winking, holding out a coin. [342] Ilse reached to touch the paint and woke again. [350] The second awakening put her inside the garden of 1931, unrestored and raw, the shells pale as new bone, and a drifter sat on the eastern dish scratching at a nickel with a nail. [384] "I'm making her up," he said, without looking at Ilse. [394] "That's the whole pleasure." [398] "If I knew her face I'd have nothing to do." [408] He flicked the coin at the concrete, and the echo took her. [420] The third awakening was smaller and stranger: she was the wren she had painted in the cornice, and from inside her own brushwork she could see how much of the mural she had invented, panel after panel, confident forgeries of a hand that had never been sure of anything. [469] It was not a shameful sight. [475] It was a funny one, and the wren laughed, and the laugh went out across the reeds and came back as her own voice saying, "Guess." [501] Then she woke, finally, into the fourth thing, which was simply the afternoon: 4:11, green on the palette, Odile waiting, the coin still ringing itself to a stop. [529] Nothing had moved except her. [534] Certainty, Ilse understood, was a thing you could hand back, like a tool you had outgrown; you could trade it, deliberately, for the better currency of not yet knowing. [563] She had spent thirty years being right and had not been surprised since she was nineteen. [579] "No," she said to Odile. [584] "I don't know what it shows." [590] Odile's eyebrows went up, because Ilse Bezan always knew. [599] "Then what do we paint?" [604] "Something," Ilse said, climbing down with the nickel warm in her fist. [616] "We'll make her up." [620] They worked until the light left the reeds: a woman with high cheeks, one eye winking, a coin held out toward whoever came next. [644] Ilse's colors were, as always, vivid past the point of politeness, greens that shouted, a red that made the concrete look shy. [666] She hid a wren in the woman's hair. [674] And at the base of the panel, where the plaster met the parabola, she wrote no date and no attribution, only a small line for the next restorer to find in some other century: Spin a coin here and wait for the answer. [717] Then she buried the hobo nickel back in the mortar, where a curious hand would eventually be bewitched, and went home not knowing, gloriously, what tomorrow's green would be for. [747]
