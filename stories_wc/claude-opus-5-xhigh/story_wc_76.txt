The tide went out like a long, slow exhale, and Wren Halloway walked into the space it left behind. [19] She was the only clockmaker in Grennock who built her movements to run counterclockwise, and the village had never forgiven her for it. [42] Her shop was neatly inventive: brass gears sorted by tooth-count in oyster shells, escapements labeled in her small square hand, every tool hung on its own nail. [69] The causeway to the island appeared twice a day, black and glistening, and today it uncovered the hunger stone. [88] Fishermen said the stone only showed itself when the sea was about to take something, and its carved line had frightened Grennock for four hundred years: WHEN YOU SEE ME, THE LEDGER IS ALREADY WRITTEN. [123] Wren crouched, touched the letters, and felt the old anger warm her ribs. [136] Her mother had died the year the stone last rose, calm as a woman keeping an appointment, saying it was written. [157] Wren had spent twenty years since then trying to build a mechanism that could step beyond fate's design. [175]

The lighthouse waited at the end of the causeway, a chalk-white finger on a heap of rock, unlit since the last keeper walked out of it and into the water. [205] No flame had ever burned in its lamp room; the lens itself was said to hold a latent radiance, sleeping light, waiting for someone able to initiate it. [233] In her satchel she carried the reason she believed she could. [244]

The yoyo had come up in a crab pot two winters back: two discs of dark ivory on a bronze axle, older than the village, older maybe than the stone. [274] It was a puzzle, and Wren had solved it the only way she knew: by taking it apart. [292] Inside the axle sat a sleeve, a spring, and three tiny pawls, and reverse-engineering them had taught her something no clock had: the mechanism was designed to guarantee return. [321] Throw it however you liked; the string wound back, the discs came home, the future arrived on schedule. [339] That was fate, she thought, drawn small enough to hold: a clutch that never slipped. [354] But the ancient maker had left a flaw, or perhaps a mercy: a fourth pawl-seat, empty, cut counterclockwise. [372] Fill it, and the spool could refuse to rewind. [381] She had spent two years filing that pawl. [389]

The lighthouse door was swollen shut and gave with a groan like an animal waking. [404] Inside, the stair spiraled the wrong way, and Wren laughed out loud, because of course it did. [421] In the lamp room the lens hung in a cradle of gimbals, and at its heart, where a burner should have been, was a bronze axle with an empty spool. [451] The marks matched the yoyo's, thread for thread. [459] The keepers had not been chosen by fate; they had simply failed the puzzle, and drowned themselves believing the failure was destiny. [481]

Wren worked with the tide going out beneath her, her tools laid in their row, her hands steady. [499] She seated her filed pawl in the lighthouse spool, wound the cord counterclockwise, and set the escapement she had carried from Grennock in her coat. [524] Then she threw. [527]

The spool ran out to the end of its cord and did not come back. [542] Light woke in the glass, not kindled but uncovered, as if it had been waiting there all along beneath a lid. [563] It swung out across the flats, and the hunger stone shone white in it like a struck bell. [581]

Wren saw the tide already turning, silver threads filling the causeway channel, and understood the price with a clarity that felt almost like joy. [605] The mechanism would not rewind, which meant she could not either. [616] There was no road home tonight, or any night; the light needed tending, and no one else in four hundred years had solved the axle. [641] She had stepped off the ledger and onto a rock two hundred yards wide. [655]

Below, Grennock's windows opened one by one, faces turning toward a light no living person had seen. [672] They would say the sea had taken her, because the stone had shown, because it was written. [689] Wren hung the yoyo on its own nail beside the lens, wiped her tools, and set them in their row. [709] Then she stood in the turning glare with the water closing behind her, alone, unwritten, and grinning at the black exhilaration of it. [732] Under the rising water the carved letters went dark, and stayed dark, and she let them. [748]
