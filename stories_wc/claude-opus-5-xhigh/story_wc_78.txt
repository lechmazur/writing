The orchard rode a circle of driftwood in the open seas, nine quince trees rooted in ballast soil, and Mira had never known any other country. [26] Her teacher, the leeward tide prophet, sat on the rim where the current ran out from under them, because that was where the future arrived last and could still be argued with. [58] She interpreted cloud movements the way other people read faces: the slow anvils meant a week of hunger, the raked mares' tails meant the wind would swing and shove the orchard south. [90] In her belt she carried a xylophone mallet, its rosewood head worn to an egg, the last object saved from the drowned conservatory where she had been born. [118] "Everything that blocks us," she liked to say, "is only an instrument that has not been struck yet." [136]

Mira had believed this the way children believe weather until the salt reached the roots of the eldest quince and killed it in a single winter. [162] A dead tree on a raft is an obstacle: it drinks nothing, feeds no one, and holds the wind like a bad sail. [185] So the prophet handed Mira a stick of charcoal and told her to make a sketched resonance of it. [204] That was the old conservatory method: strike a thing, listen, and draw the shape the sound wants, the branching lines of its overtones, until the drawing tells you where to cut. [235]

Mira struck the dead quince with the mallet for eleven days and drew what she heard. [251] The drawing came out looking exactly like the tree. [260] "Of course," the prophet said, delighted. [266] "A trunk splits into limbs, a limb into twigs, and a note splits the same way into its own smaller notes." [287] "Copy the tree's own arithmetic and you get an instrument that plays itself out to the edge of hearing." [306]

They built it by fractal generating: every bar Mira cut, she cut two shorter bars in the same proportion, and two shorter again, and again, hanging them from the dead limbs on gut cord until the whole crown of the tree was a xylophone the size of a chapel. [355] Struck once at the root, it answered in a restless euphony that would not settle, a chord that kept dividing and rebuilding itself for a full minute over the water. [385]

They needed it. [388] The orchards of the drift fleet had been scattered by three seasons of bad wind, and no sail is visible past nine miles, but sound crosses calm water for twenty. [418] The prophet had always meant to build a beacon that could be heard, so that the hungry rafts could find the orchard that still had fruit. [444]

They played at the blue hour, always, when the clouds went the color of ink in milk and the wind lay down and the sea turned to a drumhead. [473] The prophet read the sky each evening and chose the key by it: high bars when the cirrus ran east, the low bars when the sky stacked up gray and heavy, so that any listener out there could learn the weather from the music alone. [518]

For two months nothing answered but gulls. [525] Then, on the ninetieth evening, while Mira was still counting her way down the smallest bars, a sound came back across the flat water, out of the dark east: four notes, wrong-tuned, stubborn, repeating. [559] Someone had hammered on their own obstacle -- a cracked hull plate, a mast stub, something -- and made it sing back. [579]

The prophet stood up on her old legs and laughed until she coughed. [592] Then she put the rosewood mallet into Mira's hand and closed the girl's fingers over it. [608] "Answer them," she said. [612] "They will steer by you now." [618] Then, in the last of the blue, she dubbed her successor with the mallet -- one tap on each shoulder, the way her own dead teachers had done it in a hall now full of fish -- and named her the new leeward tide prophet. [661]

Mira climbed into the branches and struck the root bar. [671] The chord went out from the dead tree in its dividing, unresting thousands, and far off in the dark the four stubborn notes kept time. [696] Above them the clouds were combing themselves out into long east-running threads, which meant fair wind before morning, which meant that by noon there would be two orchards where there had been one. [729] Mira played the sky she had read, and did not stop until the stars came. [744]
