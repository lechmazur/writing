The first time I met him he was called Absalom, and he sold me four lines of verse for a penny I did not have. [25] We were sorting books in the prison library, a windowless room where the state stored what it could not burn. [45] He had come in for vagrancy during the food riots; I had come in for burning a census office, which I still consider tidy work. [70] Absalom shelved by rhythm rather than alphabet, so I spent a week undoing him and a month understanding him. [89] In February he was Absalom, grave and biblical. [97] In March he introduced himself as Kit, a laughing thief of a man who rhymed only in threes. [115] He molted personalities monthly, the way other men shed coats, and he told me it was the only door a cell cannot lock. [138] Each new self carried the same possessions: a stub of pencil, a bootlace, and a dented coffee tin. [156] The tin had a false bottom, pressed from a ration lid, and beneath it he kept seeds. [173] Poppy, hollyhock, bindweed, sunflower, gathered from the cracks of every town that had moved him along. [189] Guards shook the tin, heard coffee grounds, and handed it back. [200] "Anarchy is only raw material," he said, sorting Ovid into the wrong decade. [213] "The trick is to forge beauty out of it before someone forges a rifle." [227] They released him in August, when he was a woman named Junia who spoke in questions, and me in September, still myself. [249] The city outside had finished collapsing while we argued about catalogues. [260] Militias held the bridges, water came by bucket, and the central library on Ferris Street had lost its roof to fire two winters back. [284] I found him there in October, when he was Absalom again, standing among rusted shelves under open stars. [302] Rain had welded the iron stacks into a red forest; ash lay in drifts where the poetry had been. [321] "We rebuild," he said, and did not mean the roof. [331] We hauled the burned pages out and filled the shelves with soil instead, level by level, a hanging garden in a skeleton. [353] He unscrewed the coffee tin's false bottom and let the seeds fall into my palms like small brown punctuation. [372] We planted at night, because daylight belonged to men with rifles who considered gardens a kind of argument. [390] They were right. [393] By spring the rusted library was blooming, and people came to see it: poppies pouring out of the reference stacks, sunflowers standing sentry in the vestibule. [419] He stood at the door in whatever self the month had issued him and sold poems for a penny each, or for a nail, or for nothing. [446] Every poem told the reader where to find seed and how deep to bury it. [461] That was the resistance: no manifesto, no ambush, only a slow insistence spreading through the ruins in courtyard after courtyard. [481] In June the militia came with kerosene and burned the vestibule, and the iron shelves buckled and held. [499] They broke three of his fingers and took the tin, and found nothing in it but grounds, because it was empty by then, because the city was the tin now. [529] He came back in July as someone quiet whose name he never told me, and he wrote with his left hand. [550] I asked him once whether he ever missed being one person all the way through. [565] He said a man who breaks in one place breaks everywhere, but a man made of a dozen selves only ever cracks, and light gets in through cracks. [593] We are sitting in the shell of the reading room tonight, sorting salvaged books by rhythm, his method, since I lost the argument years ago. [618] As the moon clears the skyline, the light comes down through the missing roof and the whole rusted place turns silver and green. [641] He hands me a penny and asks for a poem, and this time I have one. [657] It is four lines long, and it names no one, and it tells you where the seed is buried. [676]
