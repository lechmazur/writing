The gallery opened at dusk, because that is when the illusions behaved. [12] Ilse Varga unlocked the east wing and let the mirrors decide how tall she was tonight. [28] She was a fever dream architect, licensed by nobody, paid in favors and stranger currencies. [43] Her discipline was rational enchantment: no wishes, no chanting, only arithmetic pressed against attention until attention buckled. [60] Feverish people build corridors in their sleep; Ilse made certain those corridors had load-bearing walls. [75] The optical illusion gallery was her drafting table. [83] Tourists came for the staircase that climbed into itself and the checkerboard floor that leaned without moving. [100] They left convinced they had been fooled, which was the useful part; a fooled eye is a loosened hinge. [119] Ilse worked after hours, kneeling on the tiles with a spool of sinew thread. [133] The thread came from a butcher on Ferry Street, waxed and dried, and it held tension the way memory does: reluctantly, then absolutely. [156] She built her structures by weaving patterns across the floor grid, one anchor nail per illusion, angles measured to the minute. [177] Her brother Tobias lay in Ward Six with encephalitis, burning at forty degrees, and his fever had already opened three rooms she recognized from childhood. [202] The nurses said he was calm. [208] He was calm the way a held breath is calm. [218] Ilse had come to the gallery to build him a room that would keep. [232] Not to save him; she was honest about her limits. [242] She wanted something smaller and greedier: to discover the texture of connection, to put her hands on the thing everyone claimed to feel. [265] She had never felt it. [270] She had loved Tobias for forty-one years by inference, the way an astronomer loves a planet by its wobble. [289] The room she designed was realistically imaginary: it obeyed gravity, held dust, echoed at the correct delay, and existed nowhere but in a sick man's head. [315] That was the whole point of rational enchantment; a dream that cheats collapses, but a dream that budgets can be lived in. [337] She threaded the sinew between nails, and the pattern took the shape of their grandmother's kitchen: the tin table, the window over the yard, the smell of scorched milk. [366] She worked as memories alter, which is the only window a fever gives you. [380] By midnight the yard in Tobias's version had a dog they never owned. [393] By two, the tin table was oak. [400] Ilse revised her angles to match, because arguing with a fever is how architects lose buildings. [416] The gallery helped. [419] Its mirrors multiplied her lattice until the pattern was large enough to hold a grown man's history. [436] At 3:40 she tied the final knot, and the floor erupted. [447] It erupted quietly, the way bread rises: tiles lifting into a doorframe, sinew going white with strain, the checkerboard leaning at last into a real slope. [473] There was no wind, no music, nothing to be afraid of, and Ilse was afraid. [488] She stepped through and stood in the kitchen with her brother, who was nine and also fifty. [505] He was calm. [508] "You built this," he said, not surprised, because dreamers are never surprised. [520] She asked him to hold the thread. [526] One end in his fist, one in hers, the sinew running through the doorway between waking and not. [544] Then she felt it, the texture she had come for, and it was not warmth and not light. [562] It was rough and slightly damp, like wet rope, and it pulled from both ends at once. [579] It hurt in a manner she could only describe as structural. [590] At 4:12 the fever broke. [595] Tobias woke in Ward Six and could not remember his sister's name, only a kitchen with an oak table and a dog, and the certainty that someone had measured it for him. [627] In the gallery the tiles had settled flat again, and the sinew thread lay slack across the floor in a pattern the morning guides would sweep away, calling it art. [657] Ilse coiled what she could and wrote the measurements in her ledger, because the room still stood in him and would need maintenance. [680] She kept her hands open on the tram home, palms up, learning the shape of what had been there. [699] Connection, she noted, has a grain; you can only feel it by pulling against it. [714] She was already drafting the next room, in case he needed a hallway, in case he forgot the door. [733]
