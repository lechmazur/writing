Meret oiled the labyrinth's hinges every Thursday, because miracles, like gates, seize up if nobody tends them. [17] The maze was hornbeam and yew, nine hundred turns, and it grew around the stormlit tower the way a scar grows around a splinter. [41] Lightning stood over the tower most nights, patient as a heron, and the moon came through the storm anyway, which was the first ordinary impossibility she had learned to stop remarking on. [73] Her job, per the ledger, was upkeep: trim the hedges, sweep the gravel, log the sorrow. [89] Sorrow here came in units, and the units were footsteps. [99] A widow who walked eleven hundred paces to the center had eleven hundred paces of grief in her; the hedge counted, and Meret wrote the number down in a column, and by morning the number had dropped, and that was the whole miracle, dull as bookkeeping. [145]

The astronomer had come in spring with a sextant and a question. [157] He wanted to know what trees witness. [163] Not metaphorically: he believed the hornbeams had been standing at the tower's foot for four centuries, sighting the same stars over the same weeping strangers, and that a tree was therefore the most honest instrument ever built. [200] He meant to interview them. [204] He got as far as the ninth turn before the lightning took the tower's weathervane and the sextant leapt out of his hands onto flint. [229] The index mirror cracked in three places; the astronomer, seventy-one, cracked in one, and went home to die of something unglamorous in a city with no hedges. [256] He left the sextant in the tool shed with a note: it heals in moonlight, but slowly, and only if someone is watching. [279] So Meret watched. [282] She had no grief in the ledger herself, which was its own kind of loneliness; watching was the only thing she was qualified to do. [307]

Three years, on clear-enough nights, she carried the instrument up to the tower's top landing and set it where the moon could get at it, and the cracks closed a hair's width each season, like a fingernail growing back. [346] Meanwhile she did the other chores. [352] Pilgrims slept in the labyrinth's alcoves, and their dreams did not stay put; they came loose and drifted along the corridors, snagging in the yew like wool on wire. [381] Part of upkeep was collecting them, and the only way to collect a dream was by mapping it: chalk the corridor, note the hour, sketch where the image hung. [410] By the third year Meret had four hundred maps of scattered dreams, and she noticed they overlapped. [427] Laid on each other, the maps drew a shape, and the shape was the hornbeams' root system. [444] The trees had been drinking the dreams for four hundred years, and the labyrinth's tally of grief was simply the trees' appetite, measured in footsteps. [469] That was the astronomer's answer, and she had gotten it with chalk and a pencil stub instead of glass and stars. [490]

The night the last crack closed, she took the sextant down into the maze to sight the trees the way he would have. [513] The storm hung overhead without speaking; the moon was full; nine hundred turns of hedge held their breath. [531] In that silence a pin fell in the corridor behind her, the sextant's index pin, tapping once on flint, and at that sound the whole instrument came apart in her hands. [562] She understood then that the moon had not been mending it. [573] The moon had been teaching it to deconstruct itself, patiently, joint by joint, the way she uncoupled a rusted gate to save the good iron. [598] She laid the pieces out on the gravel and saw what they were: not an instrument anymore, but a set of small useful parts, a mirror, a scale, a pin, the beginnings of something a groundskeeper could actually use. [637]

She used the mirror to catch moonlight into the alcoves, so the sleepers healed faster. [652] She used the scale to rule her dream-maps into a proper atlas, and gave the pin to the chalk-box hinge, which had needed one for years. [678] The column in the ledger kept dropping. [685] Widows still arrived at eleven hundred paces and left at four hundred, and Meret still wrote the numbers down, and the hornbeams still stood in the lightning with their roots full of other people's nights. [720] She mailed the atlas to the astronomer's daughter with a note of her own, three lines, no flourish: the trees witness everything, they keep it underground, and it can be counted. [751] Then it was Thursday again, and the hinges needed oil. [761]
