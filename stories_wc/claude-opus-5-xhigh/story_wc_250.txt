The rain stopped at the eleventh bell, and the dock turned cobalt, as if the city had been dipped in ink and lifted out to dry. [26] Sennet was already there, sitting on the bollard with her lantern between her boots, the way she sat every night since the lamps were forbidden. [51] She was the canal guardian, and she was silent, and the city had long ago stopped wondering which came first. [71] The girl came down the water stairs at a run and stopped short, breathing hard, holding her ribs. [89] "They're going to," she said, and then the sentence died in her mouth. [102] Sennet did not lean forward or lift a hand to hurry her. [114] She had learned that a half-spoken secret has an edge, and that people stand on it the way they stand on a wet gunwale: any pressure at all and they go under. [146] So she stayed spiralingly calm, the calm turning slow circles outward from her chest like oil on the canal, until the girl's shoulders came down half an inch. [174] That was all the answer either of them needed for now. [185]

Sennet opened her satchel and took out the dental mirror. [195] It had belonged to her father, who pulled teeth on Fisher Row and who used to say a good mirror shows you the tooth you cannot see. [222] She set it against the lantern's throat, angled so the flame's light bent north up the canal instead of spilling uselessly at her feet. [246] Then she began creating patterns with the shutters. [253] Two long, one short, a pause the length of a held breath. [265] Far off, past the Stonecutters' Bridge, a window answered: two long, one short. [278] The girl watched the light go, and Sennet watched the girl. [289]

Since the Prefect had ordered the canal lamps dark - to save oil, he said, though everyone knew it was to keep people from seeing who was taken off the docks at night - the water had become a long black throat. [329] Sennet could not shout a warning; she had never had a voice to lose. [343] So she had taught the docks to weave instead. [352] Shutter by shutter, window by window, the signals crossed and crossed again, until the dark hours held a fabric of small lights, each one a message: they are coming, go inside, the boat is safe, the child is found. [391] Her mother's word for it had been embroidery; Sennet's word, if she had used words, would have been stars. [410] You could not argue with a Prefect, but you could teach the darkness itself the value of stars, one shutter at a time, until it could not remember how to be empty. [442]

The girl tried again. [446] "They're going to search the almshouse at midnight," she said, "and my brother is - " and again she stopped, and this time her eyes went to the black water as though it might be listening. [480] Sennet nodded once, slowly, and did not ask what the brother was. [492] Hidden, wanted, hurt, or dead - it changed nothing about the next four minutes. [505] She turned the mirror a hair to the east and began again. [517] Three short, one long: the almshouse pattern. [524] Then the sign for midnight, which was six quick flickers, like a man counting his own pulse. [541]

The tension on the dock was the stillwater kind, the kind that does not ripple and does not break, that simply holds while everything underneath moves. [567] Sennet gave the lantern to the girl and put her hands over the girl's hands on the shutters. [585] Together they sent it again, and the girl's grip steadied inside hers, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying: this was the whole of it, one person feeling exactly what another felt and answering in light. [627]

Upriver a shutter answered, then another, then nine more, then the whole far side of the dock coming alight in ragged sequence, the message running ahead of any boot on any cobblestone. [659] By the time the Prefect's men reached the almshouse, its beds were warm and its rooms were empty. [677]

The girl came back the next night, and the night after, and by winter she could weave the patterns alone. [697] She never finished the sentence about her brother, and Sennet never needed her to. [711] They sat on the cobalt boards after the rain with the little mirror catching the flame, and taught the darkness, patiently, what a sky is for. [737]
