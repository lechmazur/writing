The atrium had been icebound for eleven winters, and Marit Okonjo still swept it every night under starlight. [18] Frost had sealed the glass dome into a lens, so the stars came through enlarged and trembling, laying their light across the dead planting beds. [43] She was the last heirloom seed saver of her line, and her inventory had shrunk to one shelf of jars and one empty oak acorn cap. [69] The cap had once held the acorn of the Solstice oak, the tree her great-grandmother planted at the atrium's center; fire had taken the tree, and time had taken the acorn, grain by grain, until only the little wooden beret remained. [110]

What her family had instead of money was the vortex inheritance. [121] It passed from mother to daughter: a slow spiral of air that could run the world backward in a small place, tidying heat back into cold, smoke back into wood, ruin back into order by decreasing entropy. [158] It could be spent once, and it woke only after an egg hatched, because order borrowed for one thing must be repaid by something newly alive. [184] Marit had carried a wren's egg against her collarbone for nineteen days. [196]

Her grandmother had spent the family spiral on herself and bought nine extra years, and she had described them exactly once, near the end, calling them furiously tranquil, a stillness with a storm's engine inside it. [232] Marit had wanted, ever since, to discover the texture of borrowed time: whether it felt like silk or sand or like nothing at all. [256]

The egg hatched at midnight. [260] The sound was smaller than a fingernail tap, and then the atrium changed. [273] Above the frozen fountain, the air began to turn. [282] It gathered the loose frost into a column, and the column revolved, unhurried and enormous, throwing off a wild serenity that made Marit's hands shake while her heartbeat slowed. [311] The wren chick, blind and wet in her palm, breathed like a bellows the size of a thumbnail. [329]

She could step into the spiral herself. [335] She was sixty-eight, her lungs were bad from the fire, and nine years would be nine more seasons of saving what nobody else bothered to save. [361] Or she could set the acorn cap in the vortex's throat and ask it to reopen what the fire had closed. [382] A seed reopens; that is all germination is, a locked thing consenting to open twice. [397] But the Solstice acorn had not merely closed; it had scattered, its starch and oil dispersed into eleven winters of dust, and only a decrease in entropy could call it back. [428]

Marit knelt on the ice and thought about her great-grandmother, who had planted a tree she would never sit under. [448] Every seed in her jars was a letter to a stranger. [458] She set the wren chick in a nest of wool inside her coat, walked into the column's edge, and held the acorn cap up on her flat palm. [486] The spiral bent toward it like a horse toward an apple. [497]

Then the atrium ran backward. [502] Dust rose off the beds and sorted itself; motes that had drifted apart for a decade came home in filaments, threading into the cap, and Marit watched an acorn assemble itself the way a word assembles out of stammering. [541] It was brown and cool and slightly ridged, and it filled its cap exactly. [555]

And passing through her hands, on its way from nowhere to now, borrowed time had a texture after all. [574] It was grain, fine and warm, like sanded oak under a thumb, and it moved against the skin in one direction only. [596] She understood then why her grandmother had used two words that fought each other. [610]

The vortex thinned, sighed, and was gone, spent for good. [620] Marit chipped a hole in the ice over the old bed with her trowel, pressed the acorn in, and left the cap on the soil as a marker. [648] In her coat the wren chick complained, hungry, alive, the receipt for everything that had just been borrowed. [666]

Before dawn she cracked the atrium's frozen vents and let the cold out, so the beds could thaw by degrees. [686] Starlight went thin and blue above the dome, and Marit sat on an upturned crate, breathing badly, entirely calm. [705] She had spent her inheritance on a tree she would never sit under, and she found she was not sorry; she had learned what she wanted to know, and the knowing was enough to plant a hundred more. [743] In April, the acorn would reopen underground, quietly, in the dark, all by itself. [757]
