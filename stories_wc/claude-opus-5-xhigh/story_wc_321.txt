The commuter ferry Perpetua vanished every night at 11:47, and Ilo Vasquez was the only crewman who considered this a schedule rather than a tragedy. [25] He was a deckhand, ardent in the way that only a man with no shore left to return to can be ardent. [47] He coiled lines, chipped rust, counted heads, and told every passenger the same thing: we will get somewhere. [65] The passengers, ninety-one of them, had stopped believing him around the four hundredth crossing. [79] Ilo had not stopped. [83] Here is what happened, as near as he could reconstruct it: three years ago the 11:20 run out of Harrow Point crossed a channel where clocks ran sideways, and the Perpetua slid off the ordinary line of hours like a boot off wet planking. [127] Now the vessel repeated one hour forever, mooring nowhere, arriving never, a permanent transient on a permanent transit. [145] Every loop ended in the grotto. [151] It was a spectral grotto, a cathedral of black basalt open at its crown, and through that opening came starlight that fell in two overlapping patterns, as though the sky above were being printed twice and slightly out of register. [191] Two skies, two channels, two ferries. [197] In the other one, Ilo was sure, the Perpetua had never slipped, and ninety-one commuters had gone home to their suppers. [218] He knew because of the radio. [224] Down in the grotto, where the two starlit patterns crossed, the wheelhouse set picked up a woman named Mara Quintero, a dispatcher at Harrow Point who insisted, calmly and daily, that no ferry had ever been lost. [261] They had argued about it for two hundred nights before she believed him. [274] She believed him when he described the hourglass. [282] It sat bolted to the chart table, brass-collared, entirely empty -- no sand in the upper bulb, none in the lower, the glass swept clean as a held breath. [310] In Mara's world the same hourglass sat in the dispatch office, full, and it drained normally, one hour at a time. [331] The sand had to be somewhere; nothing that measured time could simply be robbed of its measure. [348] That was Ilo's whole theory, and his whole hope: the two timelines were not strangers but a single fabric folded, and folds can be pressed flat. [374] To merge them, they worked via interconnected patterns. [382] Mara sent him tide tables, and he matched them against the grotto's double starlight. [396] He sent her the ninety-one passengers' habits -- who bought coffee, who slept, who always sat portside -- and she found the same ninety-one boarding her 11:20, doing the same small things in the same order. [430] Habit was a lattice. [434] Repetition was a seam. [438] Where the patterns aligned -- wake against wake, footstep against footstep, one man's cough at 11:43 in both worlds -- the fold thinned. [459] It took eleven months of loops to build the lattice dense enough to walk on. [474] The crew called Ilo delusional; he called himself employed. [483] He was resiliently positive in a manner that irritated everyone, hauling the same lines, polishing the same brass hourglass, saying the same sentence at the same minute until the sentence itself became part of the pattern. [519] We will get somewhere. [524] On the last loop, the grotto's twin starfields slid into one. [535] Ilo keyed the radio and read out the final alignment: heading, minute, the cough, the coffee, the portside seat. [554] Mara answered from a place with weather and Tuesdays: "Bring her in." [566] Sand began to fall in the empty hourglass. [574] It fell sideways at first, then found down. [582] The Perpetua came out of the basalt mouth into ordinary harbor light, ninety-one commuters blinking, clocks all pointing the same tedious direction, three years arriving at once like a delivered letter. [613] Mara was on the pier with the dispatch log open and her own hourglass under her arm, empty now, spent. [633] Only one merged world remained, which meant only one Ilo, and he stood at the rail understanding at last what he had spent. [656] His name was not in the log. [662] In the timeline that had never lost its ferry, no deckhand named Vasquez had ever signed on; he was the seam, and seams do not appear in the cloth once pressed. [693] Mara offered him the pen anyway. [699] He looked at the shore -- weather, Tuesdays, suppers -- and did not take it. [712] "Somebody has to hold the crossing open," he said, "in case it folds again." [726] He turned the hourglass over, and the sand began its ordinary hour. [738] Then he coiled the lines, chipped the rust, and called out to the pier, cheerful as a man who has never once arrived: the 11:20 boards now, all aboard, we will get somewhere. [771]
