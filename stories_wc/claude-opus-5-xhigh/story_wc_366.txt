Ansel Kerr had skippered the supply run to Fennick Rock for eleven years, and in that time he had said perhaps four hundred words to the keeper. [27] Miren counted them all. [31] She was warmly observant in the way of people who live alone with a light: she noticed his split knuckles, the extra sugar he took in winter, the way he never looked directly at the tower. [67] "You don't like the lantern room," she said once, unloading oil drums. [79] "It's haunted," he answered, which was three words more than he meant to spend. [93] She did not laugh. [97] The keeper before her had gone up in a February gale and never come down, and everyone on that coast knew the glass up there held a shape that shouldn't be in it. [130] Instead she taught him her stones. [136] Three flats and a round one on the gallery rail meant all well; two flats leaning east meant a squall inside the week; a single stone on its narrow end meant come up, I need you. [172] The herring boats learned to read her cairns from a mile out, and she learned to stack them for whoever was passing, so that nobody had to ask for anything aloud. [203] Ansel understood that immediately. [207] The letters he understood later. [212] They began arriving in his mailbag around the third winter: unsigned pages, copied in strange hands, each one carrying instructions to copy it again and pass it to someone who might be sitting up late. [247] The chain went up and down the coast for years, village to village, dory to dory. [263] No name on any of them, which was the point; a letter with no sender cannot be pitied, and a reader with no name cannot be shamed for needing it. [293] Ansel carried hundreds of them and never copied one. [302] He was not a writer. [307] He was a man who brought oil to a lamp so that other men could find the harbor mouth, and he had decided long ago that this was his whole share of protecting anyone. [341] In October the Board sent word that Fennick Rock would be automated, and Miren, who was sixty-eight and had no other address, went ashore to her sister's in Cardigan Bight. [371] Ansel ran her trunks across on a flat grey sea and said, "Well." [384] She put something in his hand: a piece of sea glass, green as a bottle bottom, worn soft. [402] "The last night," she said, "go up." [409] He did not promise. [413] The lamp flickered out at 4:12 on the morning of the twenty-ninth, and after it did, the dark over the rock was not empty but crowded, the way a room is crowded a moment after someone stops speaking. [451] Ansel moored, climbed the hundred and nine steps, and stood in the haunted lantern room for the first time in eleven years. [473] The lens hung cold above him, a thousand prisms holding the last of the light like water in cupped hands, and in the seaward pane there was indeed a shape - a long hand-sized smear that would not polish out. [512] It ached to look at, luminous and faint, the ghost of a palm pressed against forty years of weather. [531] On the sill below it, Miren had stacked three flats and a round one. [545] All well. [547] Beside the cairn stood a clear bottle, corked, with a folded page inside and nothing else. [563] The page was the first letter of the chain, in her hand, unsigned. [576] I have spent my life trying to bottle the feeling of safety, it said, and I have never managed more than one night at a time, so I am sending you one of mine; when you have read it, send someone else a night of your own. [623] Ansel sat down on the iron floor with his back to the pedestal, the way a man sits when his legs have stopped agreeing with him. [649] He took the sea glass out of his pocket and turned it, and the faint shine of the dead lens went through it green, and for a moment the whole room was underwater and calm. [684] He had been wrong about the ghost, he thought. [693] The handprint was not a warning; it was a man reaching out to hold the light steady for somebody in a boat. [715] Ansel put the sea glass in the bottle with the letter, so the next reader would have something to look through. [736] Then he did the two things he had never done: he copied the letter out in pencil on the back of a manifest, and at the bottom, breaking the rule, he wrote his name. [770] It was addressed to Cardigan Bight. [776] Going down, he restacked her stones on the sill: one stone, standing on its narrow end, for whoever came next. [796]
