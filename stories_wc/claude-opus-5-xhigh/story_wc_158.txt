The shell was so gargantuan, and had been beached so long, that villagers called it a hill, and the hill had a door. [23] Inside, ensconced among coils of mother-of-pearl that held the cold like a jug holds water, lived Ilit: sixth keeper of the shell, its resident poet, and the worst sleeper on the coast. [55] She had not slept a full night since she was eleven, and insisted that insomnia was the only qualification the job required. [77] Above the shell, the headland orchards; below it, the reef. [87] The orchard folk pruned, the reef folk dove, and for three hundred years they had killed each other over the freshwater seeps that surfaced in the shallows, where roots and coral both drank. [120] Every spring, when the sea-plums blossomed, the fighting stopped for nine days. [132] They called it the blossom armistice, and nobody could explain why it held, except that the keeper of the shell sang. [153]

Ilit's method was inherited and ridiculous: half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind, the shell's throat bending her voice until it came out of the spiral flat and strange, a sound that made grown fighters put down their gaffs and remember being small. [196] She hated being heard. [200] She sang anyway, shyly unstoppable, the way water is. [209]

The shell's inner wall was papered with the work of six keepers: elegies for drowned divers, curses set to nursery rhythms, inventories of grief. [233] At the center hung the only object none of them had written: a galaxy postcard, foxed at the corners, showing a spiral of dust and light. [259] The first keeper had received it from a lover who left to go measure the sky and never came back; the message side stayed blank. [284] Across centuries of longing it had been dusted, wept on, argued with, and left blank on purpose, because a blank space is a place where something might still be said. [314]

That spring the blossoms came three weeks early and the seeps ran thin, and both sides read the omen the same way: fight now, before the other is ready. [344] Ilit climbed to the shell's lip at dusk and found the wind wrong, blowing seaward, which would carry her lullabies out to empty water where nothing needed comforting. [373] So she did the thing keepers were forbidden to do, and left the shell. [387] She walked the tideline with blossom in her hair, sick with shyness, humming to whoever was nearest. [404] She sang to a reef boy sharpening a spear, and to an orchard woman filling jars, and to the two war-captains, who did not know what to do with a small hoarse person singing at them off-key in the dark. [444]

By the third night the orchard woman was singing with her, badly. [456] By the sixth, the reef boy had traded his spear-shaft for a length of pipe and learned the tune, and the wind, still wrong, no longer mattered, because the song had found feet. [489] On the ninth day, when the armistice should have expired, the captains came to the shell instead of the shallows. [509] They wanted terms, and there was no paper on that coast fit for terms. [523] Ilit took down the galaxy postcard and turned it over. [533] They wrote the sharing of the seeps in nine lines, because nine was the number the blossoms had taught them, and both captains signed beneath the spiral, their names small under all that light. [567]

Ilit had been taught that a keeper's work was to photosynthesize pain into beauty: to stand in the dark green light and turn what hurt into something that fed people. [597] She had always thought it a poet's boast. [605] That night, hearing two hundred people murder her lullaby around the seep-pools, she felt it happen in her chest, a reef-shadow joy, dappled and moving, gladness with cold water under it. [636] She went back inside and, for the first time in twenty-nine years, slept until the tide turned. [653]

The treaty hangs where the postcard hung, spiral outward, so that visitors see the galaxy first and the nine lines second. [674] Ilit still wakes at three most nights; the shell still hums seaward. [686] But now, when she cannot sleep, she does not lie there composing elegies for the future. [702] She lies there listening for someone else's bad singing on the wind, and it always comes. [718]
