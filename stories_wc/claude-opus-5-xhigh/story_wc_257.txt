The last thing Ilse Vandeleur sold was her workbench, and after that there was nothing left to sell but the habit of breathing. [23] For thirty years she had been a mourning jewelry maker, weaving the hair of the dead into braids fine as violin strings, setting them under glass in lockets of jet. [53] Then her daughter Mira died at twenty-six, and Ilse discovered she could not braid grief that belonged to her. [72] She hit rock bottom in a rented room above a fishmonger's, three months behind, hands shaking too badly to thread a needle. [94]

The first origami figure arrived that same week: a paper wren on the windowsill, weighted with a pebble. [112] Inside, in Mira's cramped hand, were two numbers - latitude and longitude - and nothing else. [126] Ilse walked four miles to the coordinates and found a bench where Mira had once drawn her portrait. [144] The second came a month later, a folded fox, then a crab, then a beetle with hinged wings. [162] The fishmonger's boy finally confessed that Mira had paid him a year in advance, one figure a month, and sworn him to silence. [185] She had been dying for a long time, and she had used the time to build a trail. [203] Ilse followed it because following was easier than deciding, and because each set of coordinates gave her one more month of being expected somewhere. [227]

The twelfth figure was a crane, and it sent her up the chalk road to the old observatory on Wick Down. [248] Nothing in that building had kept time since the war. [258] The pendulum clocks stood stopped at different hours, the logbooks ended mid-sentence, and dust lay over the brass in a way that suggested not decades but a single very long afternoon. [289] Old Halloran, who had taught Mira to read the sky, was still there, feeding a stove with broken chair legs. [309]

"She said you would come in autumn," he said, and pointed at the heavy black drapes ringing the dome. [328] "Curtains of intention, Mira called them." [334] "You don't open them idly, because whatever you point the glass at, you have decided to see." [351]

On the observing floor, pinned beneath a paperweight of sea glass, lay a tattoo stencil on vellum. [368] Mira had been a tattooist, and this was her last design: a rose in full collapse, petals curling, drawn with hundreds of needle pricks instead of ink lines. [396] Ilse's throat closed; the woman had spent her final strength perforating a dead flower. [410]

Halloran hauled on the rope, and the curtains of intention parted. [421] Moonlight came down the slot and struck the vellum, and the pricked rose threw its shadow across the floorboards - not withered now, but immense, each pinhole a star, the whole collapse re-read as a constellation opening. [457] The pinholes did not merely mark the petals; they matched a real field of sky, and Halloran named it for her. [478] Mira had taken the shape of her own dying and mapped it onto something that had been burning for ten thousand years. [500] That was the instruction, and Ilse understood it the way one understands a stone dropped into water: teach withering to bloom again. [522]

She stayed the winter in the observatory, and Halloran let her use the optics bench as a workbench. [540] Her old trade had used jet, vulcanite, glass beads, spun sugar-fine wire - materials that never pretended to be alive. [559] Mourning work was genuinely artificial and always had been, and Ilse had loved it for that honesty: nothing in a jet rose lied about being a rose. [586] Now she drilled the petals through. [592] She cut Mira's collapsing rose from black glass, following the stencil, and set each pinprick with a chip of quartz, so that the brooch, held up to a lamp, would illuminate the wearer's throat with a scatter of small hard lights. [633]

Grieving people came up the chalk road for them, one at a time, the way pilgrims come. [650] She did not promise comfort; she was too tired for that, and comfort was not the point. [667] She told them what the pinholes were, and then she drew the curtains and let them look at the field of sky their dead had been folded into. [695] Most wept, quietly; a few laughed. [701]

Ilse kept the twelve paper figures in a drawer beside the stencil, and folded a thirteenth herself, a wren, with coordinates inside for the observatory. [726] She left it on a windowsill in town whenever she heard of a house gone dark, which was often, and which was, she found, a kind of work she could do with shaking hands. [760] The clocks never started again, and she stopped minding. [769] Some nights, alone under the open slot, she pressed the brooch to her mouth like a person keeping a vow. [789]
