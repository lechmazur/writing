The arboretum kept its promises in slow motion, the way trees do. [12] Nasrin had inherited it after the last lighthouse keeper died, along with his ledgers, his bad knee's weather predictions, and a cliff that lost a hand's width of itself to the sea each year. [46] The light below ran itself now, automated, blinking without anyone to love it. [59] She loved it anyway, out of habit, the way she loved things that had outlived their keepers. [76]

Ilse arrived on Thursdays, sixteen and furious at nothing she could name, sent by a clinic that had run out of ideas. [98] Nasrin put pruning shears in her hands and said very little for three weeks. [112] The girl turned out to be quietly talented at grafting, patient with the whip-and-tongue cuts, steady in the wrist. [131] That was the arboretum's real work: taking one living thing that had been cut and persuading it to join another. [151]

On the shed wall, above the seed trays, hung a crumpled photograph in a frame too good for it. [170] Nineteen people at a wedding table, a crease running diagonally through them like a fault line, because Nasrin had held it in her fist for two days in a hospital corridor in Sana'a. [203] Eleven of the nineteen had not survived the thing that came out of a clear sky at the end of the toasts. [225] Ilse asked about it once. [230] "A drone," Nasrin said, and named the date, and went back to potting up seedlings, because facts were the only comfort she trusted. [253]

What she had learned in the years after, from a therapist who worked out of a borrowed church basement, she taught Ilse through somatic markers: the tightening under the jaw, the cold band across the shoulders, the hands going far away. [294] "Your body files the warning before your mind does," she said. [305] "Learn its handwriting." [308] "Then you can choose to freeze instead of being frozen." [318] She called the center of the practice a pearl of stillness: not calm, not peace, just one small round hard place inside the noise that you could hold with your attention while everything else roared. [353] A pearl, she explained, is a wound the oyster kept working on. [365]

In September the county sent a survey drone to measure the erosion. [377] Nasrin had signed the permission form herself, in her own tidy hand, weeks earlier. [391] Knowing a thing is coming is not the same as your shoulders knowing. [404] The sound arrived first, that flat insect hum over the monkey puzzle trees, and she went down onto the gravel path with her arms over her head, and she could not hear Ilse calling her. [439] What she heard was nineteen people laughing. [446] Then a hand on her sternum, not pushing, just resting there, and a voice reciting her own words back at her badly: jaw, shoulders, hands. [471] Ilse had the seed catalogue rolled in her fist and was tapping it against the ground in a slow rhythm, the way Nasrin tapped it when Ilse's own panic came. [501] Nasrin found the pearl somewhere behind her collarbone and held it until the hum went out over the water. [520]

They sat on the path a long while. [527] "It will happen again," Nasrin said, when she could. [536] "I'm not going to tell you it won't." [544] That was the whole of her teaching, the thing she had come to this cliff to do: to anchor optimism in realism, to plant things where the ground was provably going. [575]

The survey report came in October and said what the keeper's ledgers had said: the eastern terrace had perhaps forty years, the seed shed less. [600] Ilse read it twice and asked what was the point of grafting a pear tree onto a cliff that was leaving. [621] Nasrin took the crumpled photograph off the shed wall and put it in the girl's hands. [637] "Eleven of them died," she said. [643] "Eight of us did not, and I am the one who kept the photograph, so I get to say what it is for." [666]

Then she unrolled the keeper's old survey map on the potting bench and showed Ilse the field she had bought in August, two miles inland, with money the lighthouse trust had meant for a gift shop. [702] Cuttings travel. [704] Grafted wood carries the whole line of a tree in a stick the length of your finger, and a quietly talented girl with steady wrists could move an entire arboretum inland, one scion at a time, before the sea took the terrace. [746] The cliff was not negotiable. [751] The collection was. [754] They worked until the automated light came on below them, and Nasrin, kneeling in the cold, kept one hand flat on the ground and felt for the small hard stillness that was hers. [787]
