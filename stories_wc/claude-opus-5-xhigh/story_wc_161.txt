The courier pigeon trainer came to my bookshop in April, when the wisteria on the alley wall was blooming so hard it looked like it hurt. [26] She wanted the attic. [30] Not the shop below, with its vintage stock of unsold hymnals, not the ledgers - only the attic, with its single skylight and its smell of dry glue. [57] Her name, she said, was Ilse Marek, and she had trained couriers for a postal service in the mountains. [76] In June she told me she had grown up in a lighthouse and learned birds from her grandmother. [94] In August the grandmother became a deserter uncle, the lighthouse a barge. [106] I began keeping a private ledger of her contradictions, because I am a man who believes in paper trails, and because I had been lied to before by someone I loved. [137] The birds, at least, were honest. [143] Fourteen of them, in a loft she built against the chimney, and every morning she climbed my stairs with grain and a notebook. [166] She would stand under the skylight and do nothing for twenty minutes, watching. [179] She was mapping the swirl of dust motes under that single skylight - drawing arrows in the notebook, tracing how the air turned when the shop door opened below, where it stalled, where it slid cold along the north beam. [218] Damp follows the slow air, she said. [225] Paper dies where the motes hang still. [232] That was how I learned what she had really come for. [243] Her crates held four hundred song sheets from the Vrsna valley, copied in pencil from the singing of old women, most of them now dead, the valley itself under a reservoir since 1974. [276] No recordings survived; a fire in an archive had taken the wax cylinders. [289] What she had was pencil, and paper, and pigeons. [298] She would not trust one archive again, she said, so she was scattering copies: a verse at a time, to seventeen addresses across three countries, carried by bird because birds leave no seizable record and because she liked the thought of a melody crossing a border in a quill-sized tube. [348] To preserve the echo of lost songs, she said, you must make so many paper trails that no fire can find them all. [371] Each capsule had to balance. [376] Too heavy on one side and the bird flew crooked, tired early, went down in a field. [393] So she weighed each capsule on a jeweler's scale against one counterweight: a chip of Mars meteorite, dull red, no bigger than a fingernail. [417] It had been her father's, bought at a rock fair; it was the only object in her possession she never told two stories about. [441] Everything else she reinvented. [445] I confronted her in October, ledger of contradictions in hand, the wisteria outside gone brown and clacking. [462] She read my list without flinching. [468] Then she said that a woman who carries other people's songs across borders learns to leave no history a censor can pull on, and that she had reinvented herself so many times that the versions had begun to feel like sisters. [509] Ask me about the songs, she said, and I will never lie. [521] So I asked, across that winter and the next spring, and she never did. [535] That is how trust came back to me: not all at once, but the way she taught the young birds - short flights first, then longer, always the same loft to return to. [567] I began copying too, in ink, at the shop counter, and I moved the fragile crates to the corner where her arrows showed the air running quick and dry. [596] She died in her sleep in the third August, sixty-eight or seventy-four depending on which of her stories you believed. [616] The pigeons circled the skylight all morning. [622] In her notebook, on the last page, she had written a real name, a real village, a real sister's address - one true paper trail, left for me, weighted down by the meteorite chip. [655] I still fly them. [659] Two verses a week, seventeen addresses, each capsule balanced against a stone that fell out of another sky. [677] The wisteria blooms and withers; the old women are gone; the songs go out in pencil and come back, sometimes, in letters from strangers who have learned to sing them. [707] Under the skylight the dust still turns in the shape she drew. [719] I keep my old ledger of her contradictions in the still corner, where the motes hang and the damp gets in, and I let it go soft. [746] Some histories are meant to wither. [752] The songs are not. [756]
