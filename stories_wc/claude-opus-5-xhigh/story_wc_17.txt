After the climate shifts, the old estates stopped being houses and became weather. [13] Vines drank the brick, monsoons rewrote the floorplans, and sound went strange inside them. [27] That strangeness was Ines Varga's inheritance, and her trade. [36] She was a corridor echo cartographer, the last one certified by a guild that had drowned with its coastal archive. [56] Her work was simple to describe and impossible to explain: she clapped, sang, dropped ball bearings, and listened to how a building answered. [79] From the answers she drew maps of rooms no surveyor could enter: collapsed wings, sealed stairs, cavities where plaster kept its own history. [102] Her mother had taught her the craft and left her the burden of it, along with the Halloway commission, unfinished for eleven years. [125]

The Halloway house sat in a valley that used to be dry, and Ines arrived by flat-bottomed boat. [143] She was sensitively perceptive in the way that made ordinary life difficult and this job possible; she could hear a room's grief in the way it swallowed a footstep. [172] The client, a preservation trust, wanted the floorplan of the east wing. [184] Ines wanted something her mother had written about in the last year of her life: she wanted to discover what walls dream about. [207]

In the parlor she found the fireplace stuffed with half-burned diaries, a whole shelf's worth shoved in and lit and then, apparently, abandoned. [230] The rain had come down the chimney and saved half of every page. [243] They were her mother's. [247] Ines read them on the wet floor for two days, reconstructing the method from scorched fragments: that a house rehearses its loudest moments forever, that echo is memory with the meaning burned off, and that the meaning can be restored if you know where to stand. [293]

Between two pages lay a film negative strip, four frames, brittle as a dragonfly wing. [308] Held to the grey window, it showed the ivy-veiled passage that ran along the estate's north face, and a figure standing in it with her arms raised, mouth open, mid-shout. [338] Her mother, calibrating. [341] The diaries called that passage the throat. [348]

Ines went at dusk, when the air was still and the frogs, new to this latitude, had not yet begun. [368] The passage was a stone gallery grown shut, ivy hanging in curtains so thick the space inside was green and dim as a lung. [392] Sound behaved wrongly there; her footsteps came back late, and warm, and slightly wrong in pitch, like a voice remembering a song rather than singing it. [418] She stood where the negative said to stand, counted the ribs of the vault, and shouted the way her mother's arms had taught her. [442]

The passage held the sound, folded it, and gave it back layered. [454] Underneath her own voice were others. [460] Not words, exactly, but the shape of every loud thing the stone had ever been made to hold: a wedding, an eviction, a century of servants running, the long low groan of the drought years, a boy's laugh that repeated four times because the house had liked it. [508] That was the glimpse she had come for, and it lasted perhaps three seconds. [522] Walls do not dream of people, she understood; they dream of pressure, of being struck and struck and asked to stay standing. [544]

Then, last and clearest, her mother's own shout came back, eleven years old and still traveling. [560] It was not a calibration tone. [566] It was a word: Ines. [571]

She sat down in the ivy and understood the fireplace at last. [583] Her mother had burned the diaries not to hide the method but to spare her daughter the standing and the shouting and the listening, the whole inherited weight of hearing what buildings cannot stop repeating. [618] The rain, indifferent and new, had refused the mercy. [627]

In the morning Ines filed the east wing map with the trust, accurate to the inch. [643] She kept the negative strip and the diaries, and she added one page of her own, addressed to whoever came after her. [665] It gave the standing place, the count of ribs, the pitch. [676] It did not tell them to come. [683] It only said that the passage was still there under the ivy, still holding everything it had ever been given, and that no one is obliged to listen. [711] But if they did, someone should be there to answer. [721]
