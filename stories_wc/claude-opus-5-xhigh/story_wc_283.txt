Merren turned the hourglasses beneath the cliffs, in a warehouse whose walls were amber, so that even the dust drifted like slow honey. [23] She was the twelfth hourglass turner, and her duties were three: keep the sand falling, keep the count true, never open a jar. [46] The jars stood on the high shelves, nine hundred of them, wax-stoppered, each holding one breath of canyon echo. [65] Ninety years ago the oracle had shouted her prophecy into the gorge, and the Order's runners had sprinted the canyon's length with jars uncorked, catching the echo wherever it struck stone. [96] They caught all of it, and none of it in order. [107] So each solstice the Elders unstoppered a single jar, and the warehouse filled with that unearthed silence Merren had known since childhood, the hush of a sound ninety years buried, before the oracle's cracked voice let go of two syllables and died. [149] Ash, one solstice; drown, the next; the children, the next. [159] From ninety years of fragments the Order had assembled a doom, and taught the valley to live small beneath it, planting nothing that took more than a season to fruit. [189] Merren did not want to break the prophecy; she wanted to unscramble it, to spin it back into raw possibility, the way a doom becomes a question again once you know where its sentences begin. [224] The jars themselves were mute about it; their labels had been written by nine hundred different runners in nine hundred different hands, and the amber floor had swallowed the old ledger long before her time. [259] What the floor had not swallowed were buttons. [267] Runners had come in gasping, tearing their coats open in the heat, and for ninety years the resin-seamed cracks between the flagstones had held every button that popped loose. [296] Merren began cataloging lost buttons. [301] Bone, horn, hammered tin, one carved with a heron for the river-station coats, one stamped with the eleventh mile-mark; she mapped where each lay, and each told her which runner had stood where, setting down which jar, at what distance from the oracle's mouth. [345] Distance was time; the farther down the canyon a jar had been filled, the later its syllable belonged. [363] It took her four years, and as the truth unraveled it unraveled quietly, one button and one shelf at a time, with nobody in the warehouse but her and the falling sand. [395] The Elders had been reading the prophecy backward. [403] Set in true order, the oracle had not promised drowning; she had described it, and then refused it. [421] On the last night Merren opened the final jar herself, the one a runner had filled at the canyon's mouth, where the echo had grown so thin it was almost breath. [452] The silence before it was the deepest she had ever stood inside, an unearthed hush with the weight of the cliffs in it. [475] Then the oracle said: no one mouth. [482] Strung to its rightful neighbors, the whole of it ran: 'Ash and drown are only what I saw alone; ask the children what the water wants, ask the diggers, ask the ones who come after; no one mouth holds the whole of it.' [525] Ninety years of obedience, and the prophecy had been asking for company all along. [539] Merren sat down among her trays of buttons, quietly inspired, and understood that she had not solved anything; she had only made room for other people to. [566] In the morning she did not wait for the Elders. [576] She copied the true order into a fresh ledger, wrote the button-map beneath it so that anyone could check her work, and carried both up the cliff stair to the valley, where the growers were sowing another one-season crop. [615] She read it aloud, and then she gave them the ledger, because shared wisdom was the prophecy, not its subject. [635] They argued all afternoon; a girl asked what the water wanted; an old digger said the gorge had shifted twice since the oracle's day and could be made to shift again. [666] Someone laughed, which no one had done about the prophecy before. [677] Merren went back down to the amber warehouse at dusk, past the shelves of spent jars, and turned the great hourglass over. [699] The sand began again, and for the first time in her life it did not sound like something running out. [719]
