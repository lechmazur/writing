The aliens had been confirmed for eleven months, and still nobody in Thessaly Deep could see the sky. [18] Mira Odeyale, navigation instructor of the Third Rim, taught four kilometers beneath it. [31] Her classroom was a pressure-blister on the eastern rim of the benthic city, where the lamps of the kelp-farm barges swayed like slow constellations, and her students, all of them born in the dark, had never needed a star in their lives. [73] They navigated by magnetics, by thermocline, by the grumbling of vents. [84] Then the signal came from beyond Neptune, patient and geometric, and the Assembly voted to expand the city's charter upward. [104] Someone had to meet the visitors at the surface, and whoever went would have to steer by starlight. [122] Mira had never done it either. [128] She had only the stories of her grandmother, who piloted the last legal surface trawler out of Valparaiso before the storm-decades closed the water to keels. [154] What survived of that boat was a single anchor chain link, thick as a wrist, its iron scabbed orange, and Mira kept it on the lectern where a globe should be. [185]

On the first day of the starlight seminar she set nine students at the table and passed the link around. [205] "This is the only horizon any of you have ever touched," she said. [218] "A closed oval." [221] "Every map we draw down here is a closed oval, because the dark ends where our lamps end." [239] "Above, the horizon unfolds, and you must learn to unfold with it." [251]

She taught them by edge geometry, the old dome-builders' craft: cut a sphere into panels, measure only the angles where the panels meet, and the whole curved world can be rebuilt from its creases. [284] Every night the class folded paper skies, creased them along the ecliptic, and read declinations off the edges the way a diver reads a seam of basalt. [311] Her best student was Kel Aduma, a vent-mechanic who was balancedly curious, eager enough to ask why the visitors had chosen an ocean world, careful enough to check the answer twice before believing it. [345] It was Kel who noticed that their signal was itself an unfolding, a polyhedron described edge by edge, angle by angle, a shape that assumed the receiver would know how to close it. [379] "They navigate the way you teach," he said, and something settled in Mira's chest like ballast, awe with grief stirred into it. [401]

The seminar's cruelty was arithmetic: a body could be carried from four kilometers to the surface across ninety days of staged decompression, but no one had ever been safely pushed back down. [435] Whoever surfaced to meet the visitors would trade the city for the sky, permanently. [449] Mira taught them anyway, and taught them well, because that was the only honest thing left to do. [468]

In the ninth week she gave them the link. [477] "Fold this," she said, and let them laugh, and then let them stop. [490] "It will not fold and it will not open, and yet it held a boat against a current for sixty years," Mira said. [513] "My grandmother steered by Canopus with this iron under her feet." [524] "Whoever ascends carries it, so the first thing the visitors touch is something that has been in both worlds." [543]

Kel volunteered in the tenth week, and four others after him, and the Assembly chose Kel because he had checked his own answers twice. [567] On the last night of the seminar Mira walked him to the ascent lock and put the chain link in his hands. [589] "Ninety days," she said. [593] "Then you will see what I have only ever folded out of paper." [605] He asked what she wanted him to send back down. [615] "Angles," she said. [618] "Just the edges; I can rebuild the rest from here." [628]

Ninety-one days later the first transmission came down the cable: three angles, a time stamp, and one line of description. [648] "The horizon keeps unfolding." [652] "It does not close anywhere, and the visitors say it never will." [664] Mira read it to a new class of nineteen, then thirty, then a hundred. [678] They folded paper skies in the blue dark of the benthic city, beneath a sky none of them would ever stand under, and learned the angles by heart. [708] On the lectern where the anchor link had rested, Mira left the ring of rust it had printed into the steel, a closed oval, and beside it she chalked a single line, open at both ends. [746]
