The terrace had been old before the war began, and the war was three hundred years old. [17] Midsummer heat had cracked its flagstones into a map of nowhere, and grapevines had come up through the cracks to hold the stone together. [41] Ambassador Ilsa Verain considered that the best any of them could hope for: a broken thing braced by whatever had grown in the breaks. [65] She had until the moons touched to muster ninety-one witnesses, and eighty-four had come. [79] The rite required one kinsman from every surviving clan of the Hemmel valley, because the dead had to be named by people who had loved them, or the naming meant nothing. [110] Ilsa worked the terrace with her ebony clipboard held against her forearm, being gentle at each of them in turn. [130] Her gentleness was operative rather than kind, a working part of her, the softest available lever. [146] Eleven years as chief negotiator of the forever war had taught her that no one signs anything for the reason they announce. [168] She poured wine for a widow who spat it out, and refilled the cup without comment; she let an old shepherd shout at her for eleven minutes and then asked, quietly, whether his brother's name was spelled with one L or two. [210] By the second hour she had eighty-nine. [217]

The clipboard was cut from a beam of the Hall of Signatures, which had burned in the ninth decade of the war. [239] Every ceasefire since had been drafted on its black face, and every ceasefire had failed, which was why nobody trusted ink anymore. [261] Paper could be burned; an oath sworn during the slow dance of celestial bodies could not be unsworn, not in the theology of either army. [286] So Ilsa had spent four years arguing calendars instead of borders, waiting for the night the two moons and the red wanderer stood in one line, an alignment that came once in forty-one years. [320] Her superiors had called it superstition; she had called it the only enforcement mechanism available, and had written that phrase into the margin of the draft. [346] An ideal that is not anchored in practice, she believed, is only a politer way of saying no. [364] Through ritual significance, then: the treaty would live inside a ceremony old enough that breaking it would cost a man his ancestors. [386]

The rite had one more clause, and it was the reason her hands were not steady. [402] Before the vow, the guilty had to read their own names aloud from the roll, and be unnamed. [420] Unnamed meant stripped of clan, rank, record, and burial rights: alive, and no longer anyone. [435] Twenty-two names were on the roll, and she had drawn up the roll herself, on the clipboard, in her own hand. [456] The twenty-second was Colonel Ilsa Verain of the Southern Sluice Command, who at twenty-six had ordered the Hemmel gates opened and drowned four hundred and six people in a single grey afternoon. [488] Nobody had made her include it. [494] That was the arithmetic she had come to accept: redemption is not a feeling one arrives at, it is a price, and the price is always the thing you would rather keep. [526] She wanted her name; she wanted, absurdly, to be remembered as the woman who ended it. [542] Peace that cost the negotiator nothing would be one more page of ink, and everyone on this terrace knew what ink was worth. [565]

The ninetieth witness arrived at moonrise, a girl of sixteen with her grandmother's shawl over her head. [582] The ninety-first was the shepherd, who came back up the stairs, sat down, and said his brother's name had two L's. [603] Overhead the moons closed the last degree of their long, indifferent circuit, and the red wanderer slid between them like a bead onto a wire. [628] Ilsa read the roll. [632] Twenty-one voices answered before hers, generals and sappers and a bishop, each one shedding a name into the warm air. [652] When she spoke her own, she heard how thin it sounded, and how much smaller than four hundred and six. [672]

The vow followed, and it was an imperfect vow: twelve years of ceasefire, three disputed valleys left unresolved, no reparations anyone would call adequate. [696] Twelve years was practice, though, and practice was how ideals learned to carry weight. [710] She handed the ebony clipboard to the girl in the shawl, since a rite needs a keeper and a keeper needs a name. [733] Then the woman who had been Ilsa Verain went down the cracked stairs, through the vines, into the first grey light of the shortest night of the year. [761] Behind her, on a terrace that had never been beautiful except in the way broken things are, the witnesses began, uncertainly, to sing. [784]
