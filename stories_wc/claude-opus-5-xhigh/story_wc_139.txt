The prep room at Halvard and Sons was the liveliest place in the city after curfew. [16] Formalin bit the air like citrus, the lamps buzzed, and under Mira's hands the dead went pink again, cheek by cheek, as if arguing with their own certificates. [44] She liked them better than the living; they never asked her to lie for them. [59] Her father did, at 2 a.m., through the breach in the north wall where a water main had burst and the plaster had never been fixed. [85] Casimir Vrana came through it sideways, coat white with dust, holding a vintage ink pot the way other men hold a child. [107]

"They have my name on the arrest lists," he said. [117] "Tomorrow the consulate on Sabak Street opens at nine, and I mean to ask for political asylum." [134]

Mira did not put down her needle. [141] Between the consulate and this room lay eleven blocks of checkpoints, and her father was a con artist whose face was famous in three ministries. [166]

"You want the hearse," she said. [172]

"I want the coffin, the transit permit, and your handwriting." [182]

He set the ink pot on the steel table, beside a dead alderman who had died of nothing more sinister than pork. [204] The pot was squat cut glass with a brass hinge, ministry issue from the old empire, and it still held the iron-gall ink he had been famous for. [232]

"Ministry ink liquefies rigid thinking," he said, the old joke, the old catechism. [245] "Show a rigid man a document in this ink and he bends, because his whole life he has obeyed things written in it." [268]

She had learned to write with that ink at nine years old, copying signatures onto widows' deeds until she understood what she was doing and stopped. [294] Stopping had cost her a mother, a house, and eight years of speaking to him only at funerals. [312] Now she wanted the arithmetic of it. [319]

"A consulate will not shelter a swindler," she said. [328]

"It shelters witnesses." [331]

"So be one." [334] She pushed a sheet of Halvard's letterhead across the table. [344] "Write down every official you bribed, every warrant you forged, every name they wanted buried; write it in that ink, so that no clerk can pretend it is not real." [374]

He laughed, then stopped, because she was doing what she always did, which was wait. [389] He had taught her that too: never chase the mark, never plead, simply wait actively, arranging the room until the mark walks himself into the only open door. [417] She had arranged the room. [422] The coffin stood open on the trestles, the transit permit lay half-filled, the hearse idled in the alley, and she had already washed and dressed the alderman for a burial the state had scheduled at dawn. [458] One coffin, one route, one signature; the only variable left was what her father was willing to become. [476]

"If I sign that," he said, "I can never work again." [487]

"That is the price," she said. [493] "You get your life, the people you sold get their record, I get to stop being the only honest thing you ever made and never used." [519] "Three terms, balanced." [522] "That is what a fair trade looks like." [530]

He looked at the alderman's pink face, that chemical vitality, the dead man looking healthier than anyone on the street. [550] Then he uncapped the pot and wrote for forty minutes, and Mira watched the ink go from wet blue to permanent black, the way it always did, the way nothing else in his life had. [585]

At dawn she screwed him under the false floor of the alderman's coffin, signed the permit in her own hand, and drove the hearse through four checkpoints. [612] Rigid young men in gray waved through a corpse with the correct paperwork, and she understood, finally, that her father had been right about ink and wrong about everything else. [642] On Sabak Street she pried the lid, and he climbed out into the consulate garden with the confession folded against his chest. [664]

"Come with me," he said. [669]

"Somebody has to bury the alderman," she said, "and somebody has to be here when they come asking who signed the permit." [691]

She kept the ink pot. [696] It sits now on the shelf above the drain in the prep room, full of ink that has convinced border guards, magistrates, and one foreign ambassador, and she uses it only to write the truth, which is slower, and which works. [737] The dead keep arriving, warm from the street, and she keeps making them look alive enough to be mourned. [756] The breach in the north wall she leaves unrepaired. [765] Others will need it, and she is very good at waiting. [776]
