Rain had rinsed the lagoon to cobalt, and Mira came down to the water as she always did, quiet as a held breath. [23] She was the shoreline poet, the last one, and the village left her alone to tend the loom under the pier's burnished hush. [46] The loom held the woven dawns: forty years of mornings, each sunrise pulled into thread and laid beside the last, so the coast would remember its own light. [74] Her only tool was a drake's tail feather, curled and iridescent, taken from a bird that had drowned in a storm the year she began. [99] With it she combed the warp, and with it she wrote, ink of lampblack and salt, in a hand nobody else read. [121]

Last night's storm had ruined the week's work. [129] The threads had gone sour: a green that fought the gray, a rust that argued with everything, three mornings that refused to sit together. [153] For an hour she tried to cut the wrong colors out. [164] Every cut left a hole, and every hole made the cloth lie. [176]

That was when the boy came along the shingle, dragging a kite the size of a door. [193] Teo, the netmaker's grandson, ten years old and loud as a gull colony. [206] He had built it from his grandfather's ruined seine and a broom handle, and it was crooked, patched, plainly unfinished. [226] Mira set down the feather and watched him, warmly attentive, the way she had once watched weather. [243]

The wind off the lagoon took the kite badly, then took it well. [256] It rose over the cobalt water in a wobbling spiral, shedding a strip of net, wholly imperfect, wholly aloft. [275] Teo laughed at it and the pier creaked and a gull screamed flat and off-key, and Mira flinched at the noise, all that discord. [299] Then, as a kite string snaps -- a small sound, like a knuckle cracking -- everything changed at once. [316]

The kite kept climbing. [320] Freed of its tether it did not fall; it went out over the lagoon in a long, unfinished arc, no longer anyone's, still rising, and Teo began to cry. [349] She did not tell him it was only a kite. [359] She sat with him on the wet stones and asked him to describe it until it was out of sight, and he did, badly, beautifully. [384]

That was when she understood what forty years had not taught her. [396] The cloth had never been a record of perfect mornings. [406] It was a record of mornings, and mornings are ragged things -- half-lit, interrupted, snapped off at the string. [424]

She went back to the loom while the boy watched, and she did not cut anything. [440] She worked by harmonizing discord: setting the sour green against a deeper green so it read as shadow, letting the rust answer the gull's flat cry, giving the three quarreling mornings a fourth thread between them that agreed with all of them a little and none of them entirely. [489] The drake's tail feather moved through the warp like a small blue oar. [502] It took until dusk. [506] When she finished, the week did not look mended; it looked lived. [518]

Teo asked what it was for, and Mira, who had spoken maybe two hundred words that year, gave him twelve. [538] "Nothing here finishes, so I tend it anyway; that is the poem." [550]

He came back the next morning before the light, and the morning after that. [564] By autumn he could comb the warp himself, and she let him keep the feather in his pocket between dawns. [584] They never found the kite. [589] Fishermen reported it twice that summer, once snagged on the far cliffs, once simply gone, and Teo liked the second report better. [611]

In the burnished hush after rain, when the lagoon went cobalt again and the pier steamed, the two of them worked without talking much. [635] The cloth grew longer, seam by imperfect seam, and the coast kept its light. [649] On the last morning of her life -- a spring one, gray-green, entirely ordinary -- she wove three inches and stopped mid-row. [669] Teo, grown, kept the row exactly as she left it, and began the next dawn beside it. [686] That, he thought, was the whole art: not to mend the break, but to answer it. [702]
