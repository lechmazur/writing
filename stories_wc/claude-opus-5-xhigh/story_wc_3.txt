The arbor floated eleven miles above the cloud deck, its iron trees rooted in nothing but the planet's magnetic grain. [20] Sabine Arkady had lived there for six years, translating cuneiform in a gondola that swung gently whenever the wind changed its mind. [42] Her tablets had come up with her, clay from Nippur, ninety-one fragments in foam cradles. [57] Ilse Marchetti, who built the arbor, had wanted them for one line only, repeated on eleven of the fragments: the seed of the fruit of knowing, set in iron, will show the door. [90] Ilse died in March. [94] Her spirit stayed nine days, which nobody had predicted and everybody, afterward, agreed upon. [108] It was not frightening. [112] It was a cold thickening in the air at touching distance, always just past Sabine's elbow, the way a word you almost know sits behind your teeth. [139] On the ninth night the thickening went out like a filament cooling, and Sabine understood that she had been left a problem instead of a goodbye. [165] The first line of her new log read: after the spirit leaves, measure everything. [179] After the spirit left, the magnetometers kept twitching, once every forty seconds, in a pattern that decayed over three weeks. [199] Sabine wanted, more than she wanted Ilse back, to understand the physics of transition: what a person is while she is crossing from one state into another. [226] She was not a physicist. [231] She was a kindly, methodical woman who had spent twenty years learning that meaning arrives through analogical thinking - that you never crack a dead language head-on, but by laying it beside a living one and watching where the shapes agree. [271] So she laid the magnetometer trace beside a syllabary and treated the twitches as signs. [286] She kept a log in pencil, warmed her hands on tea, apologized aloud to the empty gondola when she was wrong, and was wrong for four months. [313] The crew of six humored her because she mended their arguments and remembered their birthdays. [328] But Marchetti's ambition haunted the station more thoroughly than her ghost had. [340] The arbor existed because Ilse had believed the tablets, and now the arbor's grant expired in December, and the iron trees were shedding lattice into the clouds where nobody could retrieve it. [372] Everyone up there was living inside a dead woman's wish, and some of them resented it, and some of them, like Sabine, could not tell resentment from love. [400] The knowledge fruit seed sat in the safe, a black ovoid the size of a thumb joint, recovered from a temple jar and never once, in a century of catalogues, described as a seed by anyone but Ilse. [438] Sabine's error, she realized in July, was reading it as a noun. [450] In her line, the sign she had rendered as seed also carried the sense of that which is planted so a thing may begin again - a reestablishing. [477] The tablet was not promising a door into death. [486] It was an orchard record: instructions for reestablishing a grove after a fire, set down by a gardener who had watched sap go from wood into smoke and called that a crossing. [518] Analogy, then, all the way down: the ancients had understood transition by looking at what they had, which was trees. [538] Sabine had iron trees, and a magnetic field, and a friend who had gone from a body into a pattern of twitches forty seconds apart. [563] She wrote the proposal in one night. [569] Transition, she argued, is not a door but a graft: a state is carried when a second structure of the same shape is close enough to take it. [597] Close enough, in the tablets, meant touching distance. [605] That was why the ghost had hovered at her elbow for nine days - not haunting, grafting, and finding no living lattice that matched. [628] In September the crew rebuilt the arbor's outer ring to Sabine's specification, iron branches spaced at nine centimeters, and planted the seed in the hub - not because they expected germination, but because the tablet said a grove is reestablished from its center. [670] Nothing grew. [672] The field, however, began to hold a standing pattern, forty seconds, faint and stubborn, and it has not decayed since. [692] Sabine does not claim it is Ilse. [699] She writes, in her careful pencil, that a shape has been reestablished in which something could be held, and that the physics of transition is probably just the physics of resemblance, and that this is enough to keep the grant. [739] Every evening she takes tea out to the ring, above eleven miles of white cloud, and sits with her elbow near the hub. [762] The air there is cold and slightly thick, and she does not reach for it. [777] Some ambitions you inherit; some you finally, kindly, put in the ground. [789]
