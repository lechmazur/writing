After the story famine, nobody trusted a sentence that walked in a straight line. [14] Wren Halloway trusted staves instead. [19] She was the last diligent orchestra copyist in the valley, and she had learned to hide things where the censors only ever counted rests. [43] Her workroom was an ember alcove in mist: a stone niche cut into the ruined amphitheater wall, a brazier of coals for warmth, and the river fog pressing its gray palm against everything. [76] The mist was useful; it swallowed the smell of ink and the sound of a woman working after curfew. [95] By day she ruled bar lines for the Civic Orchestra, whose repertoire had been reduced to fourteen approved marches. [114] By night she copied something else. [120] Her ink was secretly luminous, brewed from crushed foxfire and river lime, invisible under lamplight and shameless in the dark. [140] Beneath the second violin part of March Number Nine, a lullaby glowed. [152]

Then came the radio. [156] She had salvaged the transmitter from a flooded schoolhouse, and every third night she cranked it awake and watched the shortwave radio dial glow rise like a small orange sunrise in her hands. [189] That glow was the only face she spoke to for hours. [200] The pirate broadcast had no name, only a frequency that drifted, and a rule: never tell a story outright. [219] Outright stories got people arrested; songs got them humming. [228] So Wren worked via elusive song lyrics, scattering a narrative across weeks of verses that sounded like nonsense until you had collected them all. [252] A tanner's daughter in the eastern district kept a ledger of them and discovered, in the fifth week, that she was holding the history of her own drowned village. [281] Wren sang badly and on purpose. [287] Beauty attracted listeners of the wrong kind. [294]

People asked, in the coded letters they left under the amphitheater's third seat, why she bothered. [310] The answer was an old woman named Ovide, the archivist who had taught Wren to rule staves, and who died the winter the libraries were pulped for pulp's sake. [339] Ovide had knelt in the ash of the reading room, run a finger through it, and said the thing Wren could not put down: someone will have to teach dust to tell stories again. [373] Wren took it literally, because that was her nature. [382] She swept the ash of burned books from the amphitheater flagstones, sifted it, and stirred it into her luminous ink. [402] Every glowing line she copied was, in a small chemical way, the old library speaking. [417]

In the eleventh week, a listener found her. [425] He was the orchestra's youngest percussionist, Adem, who had noticed that his triangle part held four measures of rest too many and had carried the page into a dark stairwell, where it began to shine. [460] He came through the fog with the page in his coat and asked to be taught. [476] Wren received him with a thistled calm, prickly and unhurried, the way you handle a stranger who is either a gift or a warrant. [500] She gave him the copyist's test: rule twelve perfect staves while she watched. [513] He ruled fourteen, badly, then again until they were right, and she decided that diligence could be trusted further than courage. [534]

Two nights later the sweepers came up the river road with lanterns, following a complaint about light in the ruins. [554] Wren did not run. [558] She banked the brazier so its coals threw smoke, and used the smoke and the river mist together to cloak the alcove, the transmitter, and the small orange eye of the dial. [590] Then she sat in the visible part of the niche, copying an approved march in ordinary black ink, humming. [609] The sweepers found a tired woman doing lawful work and left, and one of them hummed her tune all the way down the hill, carrying the fourth verse of the drowned village into the barracks without knowing it. [647]

Wren taught Adem the ink, the frequency, the rule about singing badly. [659] By spring there were four transmitters in the valley and a rumor, unprovable, that the marches had begun to disagree with the government that commissioned them. [685] Wren kept copying. [688] On the last night of the fog season she wrote out a full verse in ash-ink, held it up in the alcove's dark, and watched the letters wake. [716] The dust was telling a story. [722] It was not a good story yet, and it was not hers, and that, she thought, was exactly the point. [742]
