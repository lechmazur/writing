The world had gone quiet the year Mira was born, and no one could agree whether the silence had come from the sky or from us. [26] Birds still opened their beaks; wind still bent the grass; but the air had stopped carrying anything, as if sound had grown too heavy to travel. [52] Mira learned her trade anyway, because she was symphonically minded and could not stop hearing arrangements no one else would ever hear. [74] She was a tree-song sculptor, last of a guild that had once shaped living wood into instruments the weather played. [94] Her workshop was the siskin alder grove above the drowned rail yard, where the alders grew in fours and the siskins came each autumn to strip the cones in complete, maddening silence. [126] In the grove's center stood her great-grandmother's work: a long-forgotten sculpture, three alders trained together into a harp-shaped arch, dead now, grey as bone. [150] Mira had come to it every season for eleven years, and every season it refused her. [166] This time she brought no chisel. [172] She brought a magnifying lens, a stub of charcoal, and the porcelain insulator she had pried from a fallen telegraph pole as a girl. [196] The insulator was white and heavy and shaped like a mushroom cap, and it was the only object she owned that had ever, once, rung. [221] She had felt it in her teeth when she struck it, at eight years old: a shiver, a ghost of a chime. [243] Everything she had done since was an attempt to make the world do that again. [258] Her new method was to stop carving and start reading. [268] For a month she mapped the arch by analyzing stress cracks in the long-forgotten sculpture, tracing each split with charcoal, numbering them, measuring their depth against the grain. [296] The cracks were not damage. [301] They were a score. [305] Her great-grandmother had bent the three trunks against their own growth and let the wood answer, and where the wood had strained hardest, it had cracked in intervals - fifths, thirds, a stubborn minor seventh near the crown. [342] The old woman had not imposed a tune on the tree. [353] She had listened to how it wanted to bend, and bent it there. [366] Empathy in motion, the guild books called it, and Mira had always read the phrase as poetry; it was engineering. [386] Sound, she began to suspect, had not left the world; it had simply stopped being welcomed anywhere. [403] It needed a body shaped to its liking, a shape arrived at by yielding rather than force. [420] So she chose four living alders on the grove's south edge and began to bend them, following the cracks' logic, wrapping the strain-points in wet cloth, giving each trunk a year to agree. [453] Under the roots of the eastmost tree, a hedgehog wintered in a nest of alder leaves, and Mira set her deadline by it. [476] Not spring, not thaw: after the hedgehog uncurls. [484] The animal knew, better than any calendar, when the ground had softened enough for living wood to give. [502] March came intensely quiet, the way all months came now. [512] On the ninth morning of it, she knelt and saw the nest breathing, then the small nose testing the air, then the hedgehog unrolling itself and walking away without a sound she could hear. [546] Mira released the cloths. [550] The four trunks settled into the shape she had asked of them, and their new cracks opened exactly where her charcoal numbers said they would. [575] She hung the porcelain insulator from the topmost bend, on a wire, where the wind would take it. [593] Then she reached into the arch and drew her thumb across the four strained trunks, a strum, the only gesture she had ever really known. [618] Nothing. [619] Then - low, arriving in her ribs before her ears - a chord. [630] Four notes, and the insulator swinging into the highest trunk, ringing that ghost-chime she had chased for twenty years, over and over, as the wind kept it moving. [658] It was not loud. [662] It would never be loud. [667] But the siskins came down out of the cones and sat along the arch, heads tilted, and Mira understood that she had not made music so much as made a place willing to hold it. [702] She began sketching the next grove that evening, and the one after that, and a map of every bend the world might still consent to. [727] It would take her whole life, and the silence would outlast her. [739] But somewhere above a drowned rail yard, four alders were singing to a piece of old porcelain, and anyone who came close enough to listen would feel it in their teeth. [770]
