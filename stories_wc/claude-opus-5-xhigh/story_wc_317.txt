The storm broke over Hollow Mill at nine and was gone by ten, leaving behind the kind of storm-born silence that made every drip from the eaves sound deliberate. [29] Mira Ostrander unlocked her doors anyway. [35] She had run the Hollow Mill Escape Rooms for eleven years, always at night, always beside the quiet brook that turned the old wheel, and her regulars called the place a nocturnal haven for people who could not sleep until they had solved something. [79] The lights ran off the water; she opened the sluice each evening and let the brook flow through the wheel until the bulbs came up amber. [105] Her best room, The Drowned Library, could not be brute-forced; it opened only when two players stopped copying each other and worked at right angles. [130]

That night the mail had come late, and among the bills sat a padded envelope wrapped in customs tape she could not read, no return address, and a hand-printed label declaring the contents to be ear studs. [167] Inside was one seed, brown and flat as a thumbnail. [177] Mystery seed mail, the county extension office called it: unsolicited packets arriving from nowhere, to be burned, never planted. [196] Mira, who had been described in a local review as notably receptive to nonsense, set it on the workbench under a lamp instead. [219]

She solved it the way she built her rooms, through orthogonal approaches, three unrelated angles converging on one door. [238] The tape was folded into a triangle at the corner, a habit only one person she knew had, Dev Rao, her old designer, who folded every roll that way so the next person could find the edge. [275] The seed itself, matched against the botany plates in her prop library, belonged to Amorphophallus titanum: a corpse flower, seven to ten years from seed to its single reeking bloom. [305] And the postmark date was the anniversary of the night Dev had quit. [318]

He had quit because the Aldritch Group wanted the millrace for a parking apron, and Mira had spent that whole year in meetings instead of in the workshop. [346] "You are protecting the building," he had said, "and letting the work die inside it." [361] He had been right, and she had let him go without arguing, and the mill had kept its water rights and lost its best mind. [386]

The seed was not an apology and not an accusation. [396] It was a puzzle in his old grammar: an object whose only correct solution takes a decade. [413] Plant it, and you commit to still being here. [422] A corpse flower is a terrible business plan and a perfect promise, nothing to show for years, then something nobody who smells it ever forgets. [447]

The Aldritch letter had come in the same mail, offering to buy the water rights outright, and it sat under the seed like a trapdoor. [472] Mira carried both outside. [476] The brook ran high and brown from the storm, and the air after rain held that suspended quality she had spent her career trying to build into a room, the hush of a possibility just born, before anyone has ruined it by naming it. [520] Across that hush she made three decisions, and made them quickly, the way she always did once the angles converged. [540]

She declined Aldritch. [543] She converted the drying loft above the wheel into a workshop with no paying customers in it, ever, a room whose entire purpose was to safeguard space for innovation, funded by the four rooms downstairs that already worked. [581] And she planted the seed. [586] Not in the ground - quarantine rules, and she was lawful enough to call the extension office first, which logged the packet and let her keep it potted under observation. [615] The tenant of that pot would smell like carrion for two days sometime around 2034, and until then it would look like nothing at all. [640]

She wrote Dev one line on a postcard: "Loft is yours if you want it; the pot needs watering for ten years." [662] He came back in March, folded the tape into triangles, and never mentioned the seed. [677] Some nights, after the last group had gone and the mill was dark, Mira would open the sluice a second time and stand listening to the water flow through the wheel, keeping a little light on over a pot of dirt that had not yet decided to become anything.
