Sela wove nets on the crooked wharf at Vessel Canyon, and because her hands were busy, her mouth made poems. [20] The fishers paid her in mackerel and listened to her verses the way people listen to weather. [37] That year the sea drew back like a sleeper turning over, and after the tide pools emptied they did not fill again. [59] Salt crusted the wharf pilings; the shrine at the canyon mouth stood ruined, its offering bowls dry, its keepers gone inland. [80] Sela alone kept mending things, because she believed the town needed a path carved from ruin to reverence, and paths are only stubbornness made visible. [105] Behind the ruin lay the labyrinth: a centuries-old maze carved into the red canyon walls, corridors cut shoulder-width through sandstone the color of old blood. [130] Precedent said only the shrine's keepers might enter, and only by the Tide Route, whose turnings were sung and never written. [151] The keepers had taken the song with them. [159]

What Sela had instead was a storybook illustration plate, torn from a child's book that had washed ashore in a crate of ruined paper. [183] The plate showed the labyrinth's heart from above: a round chamber, a seep of water, and white orchids opening in the dark like small patient lamps. [209] The artist had drawn the walls but not the way through them. [220]

Then the sleepwalking began. [224] First the oyster-shuckers, then the ferryman's twin daughters, then old Tavis who had not walked far in years: they rose at moonrise and went up the canyon barefoot, hands out, and returned at dawn with red dust to the knees. [264] None remembered anything; all of them woke calm, which frightened the town more than screaming would have. [281] The town called it a curse of the dry pools. [291] Sela called it a map, delivered in pieces. [299]

Each walker went in only so far and turned back at a different wall. [313] But their routes overlapped, and where two lines agreed, Sela reasoned, the corridor was real. [328] So she followed them, night after night, dusting the sandstone floor with crushed shell so their feet would print, and she sang her poems softly so they would not wake. [358] By day she did what a weaver does: she began to sew the paths together. [373] On a sailcloth wide as a door she stitched each walker's route in its own color: madder for the shuckers, indigo for the twins, undyed linen for Tavis. [401] Where threads crossed she knotted them, and the knots made a shape she recognized from the storybook plate: a spiral folding inward toward a round room. [427] Three gaps remained, three walls no sleeper had touched. [436]

The elders forbade her to enter, citing the precedent no one could sing anymore. [450] Sela answered that a rule with no rememberers is not a rule but a ruin, and that ruins were what she mended. [472]

She went in at moonrise with the sailcloth rolled under her arm and the plate in her shirt. [490] The corridors were narrow and warm, still holding the day's heat, and her shoulders read the walls like a line of verse. [512] At the first gap she chose left, because water runs left down that canyon; at the second she chose the colder draft. [534] At the third she found the twins standing asleep with their palms on stone, and she waited until they turned, then took the turning they had refused. [561]

The heart of the labyrinth was exactly as the child's book had promised. [574] Water seeped from the red wall into a shallow basin, and orchids grew from the seam, pale and unbothered by the dark, giving off a smell like cool rain. [603] Sela sat down among them and understood the sleepwalkers' calm; it was solace, plain and green, the kind that asks nothing. [624] The maze had never been about the sea at all. [634] It was a road to fresh water, and the keepers had guarded it with a song so no drought-mad crowd would trample the source. [658]

In the morning Sela hung the stitched sailcloth on the ruined shrine where the offering bowls had been. [676] It was a broken precedent, written down where anyone could read it, and the elders stood before it a long time. [697] Then old Tavis fetched a jar, and the ferryman fetched two, and the town learned the way awake. [715] They carved the route into the canyon mouth beside the tapestry, precedent replaced by instruction, ruin turned by slow work into reverence. [737] Sela kept weaving nets, though the fish were gone; she said a net was only a poem that had learned patience. [758] Her last verse, cut above the orchid basin, was hauntingly optimistic, and the walkers who came by daylight read it aloud: The sea forgets us; the stone remembers where to weep. [789]
