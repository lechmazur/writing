The Reality Checker of Semaya carried calipers, a rain gauge, and a notebook of things she had failed to disprove. [20] Her name was Idra, and her office was older than the harbor: someone had to test the island's marvels before the island believed them too hard. [46] She climbed the ancient ridge in bloom on the last dry morning of the year, when the ginger lilies opened all at once like an argument being conceded. [74] Above her, thunderheads stacked themselves in the south. [82] Before the monsoon arrived, she had to certify a seed. [92]

The elders called it the knowledge fruit seed, the last of its line, hard as a knuckle and pale as boiled rice. [114] Semaya kept no libraries; salt air ate paper, and the wars had eaten the rest. [129] Instead the island kept a collective memory, a shared dream stored in the roots of one tree, retrieved each generation through interpreted dreams. [152] Sleepers lay beneath the branches; interpreters sat beside them and wrote down the names of ancestors, the words of songs, the shapes of old boats. [177] Then the last tree died, and the dreams thinned to weather. [188]

Idra found Wen, the youngest interpreter, kneeling in the terrace soil with the seed cupped in both hands. [206] "It has to waken before the rains," Wen said. [215] "Otherwise the terrace floods and we lose it, and then we lose everyone who is only remembered inside it." [234]

Idra measured the seed. [238] This was the part the mainland surveyors laughed at, the reason they called her empirically whimsical: she brought instruments to miracles and treated the readings as sacred. [265] Eleven millimeters long, three grams, a density that suggested nothing more holy than a mangosteen. [280] She wrote it down anyway, because her notebook was not a weapon against wonder but a fence around it. [299]

"Verdict?" Wen asked. [302] "Undetermined," Idra said, which was what she always said, and what the island paid her to say honestly. [320]

They planted it at noon in the bloom-line, where wild ginger and the old grave-lilies met. [336] Nine sleepers came up the ridge that afternoon carrying mats: fishers, a boat-builder, two children, and Idra's own mother, who had begun forgetting the names of her brothers. [363] That was the honest engine of all of it, Idra thought: not scholarship, not office. [378] Her mother's face when a name would not come. [387] She had taken this job to protect cultural memory, and she had learned that protecting it meant refusing to fake it. [408] A comfortable lie would rot faster than paper in salt air. [419]

The sleepers slept. [422] Wen sat between them with a lamp and a stack of blank leaves, and Idra sat behind Wen with her rain gauge, watching the south. [447] At the third hour the boat-builder began describing, in a flat sleeping voice, a hull design nobody living had built. [467] At the fourth, one of the children sang eleven verses of a planting song that the elders knew only four of. [488] At the fifth, Idra's mother said the names of her brothers, all six, in birth order, and then laughed at something none of them could hear. [514]

Wen wrote for six hours without lifting the pen more than to dip it. [528] Idra wrote too, but in her own column: nine sleepers, one buried seed, no measurable germination, ninety-one recovered items. [547] Alternative explanation: suggestion, ritual, and nine people permitting themselves to remember together. [559] She could not rule it out. [565] She would not rule it in. [571]

The monsoon arrived at dawn, exactly as her instruments had promised, and washed the terrace to mud. [588] They never found the seed again. [593] But the ninety-one items went down the ridge in Wen's satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, and were copied that winter onto goatskin by four different hands in four different villages, so that no salt and no war could eat them all. [633]

Idra's final entry on the matter, still posted in the harbor office, reads: The tree may have wakened; the people certainly did. [655] Cause undetermined; effect verified; recommend annual repetition. [662]

Every year now, before monsoon, they climb the ancient ridge in bloom and plant an ordinary seed and sleep on it. [683] Idra goes up with them, older, still doubting, and the doubt has become the brightest thing she owns. [701] It keeps the lamp lit, and it keeps the lamp honest. [712]
