The horizon measurement station sat at the lip of the fjord, where the water lay so still that sound seemed to be something the mountains had agreed to ration. [29] Halvard Sunde was its hydraulic clock shepherd, which is a title nobody outside the Bureau has ever needed. [47] It meant he tended the nine water clocks in the cellar, coaxing their brass throats, bleeding their air, keeping their falling weights of water honest so the astronomers upstairs could trust the moment they wrote down. [83] The clocks were eminently peculiar animals. [89] They drank meltwater in spring and sulked in the cold, and each had a temperament Halvard knew the way a farmer knows which ewe will bolt. [115] Every evening at nineteen hundred the station master, Fru Berg, blew the brass whistle on the observation deck, and the day's measurements were sealed. [139] After blowing the whistle she went home to her wood stove, and the station belonged to Halvard. [156] That hour was when he did his own work, which was not measuring anything, but trying to understand what clocks really measure. [178] He had a theory, and he had a key. [187] The key was a mantle clock key, a small steel butterfly that had wound his mother's clock in Bergen for forty years. [209] The clock itself had been sold; he kept the key, because a key is a promise that something can still be started again. [232] His theory came from the horizon. [238] The station existed to measure it: the exact angle at which sky met water, recorded twice a day, so that ships and surveyors could agree on where the world ended. [268] Through the great theodolite, on a good cold evening, the horizon showed two razor edges instead of one -- the true line and its refracted twin, laid together like the blades of shears. [300] You had to squint to see the hairline of grey between them. [312] Halvard had come to believe that time had the same doubling. [322] A clock did not measure time; it measured the gap between two edges -- the world as it was, and the world as it appeared to be arriving. [349] To test it he needed a method, and the method he had chosen was counting backwards. [365] Forward counting, he had noticed, made a man agree with the clock; he leaned into the next number, expecting it, and so heard the tick he wanted. [392] Counting backwards gave the ear nothing to lean on. [401] So each night, in the fjord quiet, he sat between the number-three clock and the pendulum regulator and counted down from three hundred, aloud, slowly, marking on paper where his voice and the water and the brass began to disagree. [441] They always did, by the low nineties. [448] On the ninth of November the meltwater ran thin and the number-three clock stopped at two hundred and six. [467] Halvard kept counting. [470] He counted down through the silence, and the silence did not correct him, and somewhere near forty he understood that he was the clock now, and that he had been keeping a shepherd's watch over instruments that only ever repeated back to him what living things already knew: that a thing is here, and then it is not. [528] At zero he stood up. [533] He climbed to the observation deck, where the fjord held the mountains upside down without a tremor, and he put his eye to the theodolite and squinted at the two edges of the world. [567] The grey hairline between them was the only honest measurement in the building. [580] It was the width of a life. [587] In the morning he wrote a letter to the Bureau recommending that the station log include, beside each horizon angle, the name of whoever had watched it, since the observer was part of the instrument. [622] Fru Berg read it, blew the whistle at nineteen hundred as always, and afterwards stayed. [637] She said the Bureau would never allow it, and then she asked him to teach her to count backwards. [656] He gave her the mantle clock key to hold while she counted, so her hands would have something to be sure of. [678] They sat in the cellar among the nine peculiar clocks, and the water went down, and the fjord outside gave back nothing at all, which was, he thought, the correct answer. [709]
