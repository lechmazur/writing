The last ship sailed at noon, taking the summer people, the mail, and the possibility of leaving. [17]
By dusk Vesna Kalder had unpacked her ink, her folding desk, and the small brass scissors she used for cutting threads. [38]
She had come to the palace at Orvet for the winter, because winter was when the living most needed their obituaries. [59]
This was her trade: explained absences. [65]
A daughter sails for the mainland and never writes; a husband walks into the fog on a Tuesday; a son is conscripted and becomes a rumor. [91]
Those left behind cannot mourn a person who might yet return, so they hire Vesna to write the absent one so vividly that the absence acquires a shape, a weather, a set of habits. [125]
She does not kill them off; she gives them a life elsewhere, and a reason for the silence. [143]
The Countess of Orvet received her in the long hall, where the palace carpet lay like a drained lake, red and black, three hundred years old. [169]
"My son Anders took the last ship four autumns ago," the Countess said. [182]
"I have stopped setting his place," she said, "and I have not stopped listening for him." [198]
She knelt and worked a fingernail under the carpet's edge, where the weft had frayed, and drew out a single black weft thread the length of her forearm. [226]
"He used to pull these loose when he was small, and I beat him for it," she said. "Take it. Work from that." [249]
Vesna took the thread and understood the commission was not a portrait but a map. [264]
She had been travelling for eleven years, island to island, learning what she privately called the geography of healing: which griefs are valleys, which are coastlines, which are simply weather that must be waited out. [299]
She had never mapped her own. [304]
For three weeks she worked in the shadowed garden behind the east wing, where the hedges had grown high enough to swallow the low autumn sun by two in the afternoon. [335]
Beech leaves came down in slow copper drifts; the fountain had been shut off for winter and held one inch of black rainwater; the whole place had the lonesome splendor of a ballroom after the musicians go home. [373]
She wound the weft thread around her left wrist and wrote. [384]
Her method was not invention but projection: she gathered every fact the household would give her - Anders bit his thumbnail, Anders hated the smell of tallow, Anders could not sleep unless a door stood open - and then she projected him forward into the years he had not spent here. [433]
Presence, she had learned, can be manufactured from detail the way heat is manufactured from friction. [449]
By projecting presence into the empty chair, one could finally see that it was empty. [464]
She wrote that Anders kept a chandler's shop in Lisbon that smelled of nothing but wax, which he had learned to bear; that he had a wife who laughed too loudly; that he did not write home because every draft began as an apology and he could not get past the first line. [517]
She read it aloud to the Countess in the garden at dusk, which is the intimate hour, when a voice carries no farther than the person it is meant for. [547]
The old woman listened with her hands folded, and when Vesna reached the apology she made a small sound, and it was done. [570]
"He is alive somewhere," the Countess said. "This is only how I will carry him until he isn't." [588]
That night Vesna unwound the weft thread from her wrist and laid it on the desk beside a blank page. [608]
Eleven years ago she had taken the last ship out of a town she never named, leaving a sister on a dock. [630]
She had written obituaries for a hundred absent people and never once for herself, because she was the one absent, and no one had hired her to explain it. [659]
So she took the commission herself. [665]
"Vesna Kalder is alive," she wrote, "and travels, and is not coming back yet, and the silence was never contempt; it was only that every letter began as an apology." [695]
She tied the black thread around the folded page so the wind would not take it. [711]
In spring, when the first ship came, she would send it. [722]
Until then the garden held her, shadowed and gold and going quietly to sleep, and she sat in it a long while, mapping the last unknown coastline she had left. [752]
