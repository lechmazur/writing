The genetic counselor called it chimerism, and said it as if apologizing. [12] My left arm, my liver, a ribbon of my brain: all of it belonged to a sister who was absorbed before either of us had a name. [39] That is how I became a chimera twin discoverer at thirty-one, which is a clumsy title for a woman who found a second person filed inside her own bloodwork. [68] My mother, before the strokes took her nouns, left me two things: an emerald swatch card from the paint shop, and the name of a ship. [94] The card was a fan of greens with printed titles like Kindled, Vestry, Sea Glass, and Unkindled, and on its back someone had penciled two names, one of which was not mine. [126] The pencil had faded to a suggestion, and I could read only the first letter, an M, curling like a wave. [147]

So I rowed out to the Halcyon Menagerie, the carnival ship abandoned in Coalfoot Bay, where the fog stands so thick that the horizon is a rumor. [174] She drifts on a long anchor chain, free to wander a circle three hundred feet wide and no farther, which is the only kind of freedom I have ever understood. [204] My mother had worked her summers there, painting the ride called the Garden of Forgotten Names. [220] I had come to find it. [226] The bay smelled of creosote and old sugar. [234] Above the deck the carousel horses had gone gray as drowned things, and the ticket booths hung open like mouths mid-sentence. [255] Below, past the funhouse, I found the entrance: a plaster arch of ivy, painted in three greens I recognized from the swatch card. [278] She had chosen them herself, which meant that for one summer my mother had been happy, and had told nobody. [298]

The Garden worked through narrative threads: spools of magnetic tape strung along the track, each loop a story a rider triggered as her cart rolled past. [324] Children whispered names into brass horns at the exit, and the machine wove them into the next telling, so that no name spoken aboard was ever entirely lost. [352] I hand-cranked the generator until the tape heads warmed, and the voices came up out of the mildew, spliced and overlapping. [373] The narration began where it always had, with a girl walking into a garden where every plant grew from a name nobody remembered. [396] I rode it seven times, sitting in the cart with my knees against my chest, because the story was in my mother's voice and I had not heard my mother speak in nouns for two years. [432]

Here is the trouble with what I came to do: memory is not a vault but a rewriting, and as memories alter under handling, the thing you recapture is never the thing you lost. [466] Each time the tape looped, my mother's voice on the master track shifted a little, and my own recollection shifted with it. [488] By the fourth pass she was saying two names, then three, then a name I was certain I had invented. [508]

Mairead. [509]

My left hand went to the brass horn without my deciding it, the hand that is hers, and I understood that I had not come to recapture a memory but to give one away. [543] I spoke it into the horn: Mairead, twin, absorbed, unmourned, blissfully aberrant, two people in one skin and neither of them sorry. [565] The machine took the sound, hesitated, and threaded it into the story it would tell the next rider, whoever that would be, in whatever year the fog allowed. [593] Somewhere in the splices, other children's names went on turning over in the dark, all of them borrowed, none of them owned. [615]

What I felt then was not grief; grief needs a fire, and there had never been anything to burn. [634] It was an unkindled solace, cool as the swatch card in my pocket, the comfort of a thing that will never warm and never go out. [660] In the scenery locker I found her sealed tin of Unkindled, the fourth green, bought and never used, and I painted Mairead's name across the plaster ivy. [687] Then I rowed back through the fog, carrying her the way the ship carries her circle: bound to one small orbit, and inside it, entirely free. [713]
