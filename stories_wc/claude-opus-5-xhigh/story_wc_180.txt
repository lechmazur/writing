The cathedral had no roof left, only orchard. [8] Pear and quince and one stubborn crab apple had rooted in the rafters two centuries ago, and now their branches did the work the vaulting used to do, holding a lush ceiling over the empty nave. [44] Yesla climbed it every autumn to collect the vows. [53] They came down with the leaves, which was the point of them. [65] Deciduous vows, the old orchard-keepers called them: a promise written on a leaf in wax pencil, hung all summer, and let go in October so it could be made again, or not, in spring. [99] Nothing was supposed to be permanent except the trees. [108] This autumn the leaves came down in August, curled and brown, because the Cistern Guild had breached the contract. [127] For sixty years they had sent water up the old lead pipes in exchange for a quarter of the fruit; in June they had sealed the valve and sold the flow to the tannery instead. [162]

Yesla was a mender by trade, hands and thread, and she had one eye of milky quartz that a glassblower had ground for her after a wire snapped in her face at nineteen. [195] Children called her the quartz-eye mender and believed she could see through walls with it. [210] She could not see anything with it, which she considered a fair trade for the respect. [226]

Her granddaughter Ines came up the ladder behind her, furious, ten years old, carrying a crowbar she could barely lift. [246] 'We break their valve,' Ines said. [252] 'That is a plan,' Yesla said, 'and it is even a good one, if you want to be jailed by Thursday.' [273] Ines was endearingly feisty in the way of children who have not yet lost an argument to the world, and Yesla did not want to be the one who taught her that lesson. [306] The girl's mother, Yesla's daughter, had gone downriver eight years ago after a shouting match about exactly this orchard, and had not come back. [330] What Yesla wanted now was narrower and harder: to hand the old knowledge across one gap without dropping it. [349]

'Bring my bag,' she said. [354] The mending bag had hung on the same nail since the accident, and neither of them had emptied it in years. [375] Ines turned it out on the altar stone: three spools of waxed linen, a fish hook, a paper of mustard seed, forty feet of copper wire, a tin of graft wax gone to amber, a folding awl, and a length of green hair ribbon. [419] 'That was your mother's,' Yesla said, before she could decide not to. [431] Ines held the ribbon up to the light coming through the branches and said nothing for a while, which was new. [452] Then: 'What's the wire for?' [457] Yesla had not known until that second. [464]

The lead pipes were dry, but the west tower still had its roof, and its gutters ran into a drain behind the sacristy that had been blocked since before Ines was born, and rain was rain whether a guild sold it or not. [507] They spent nine days on it. [513] The awl and the copper wire made a strainer for the drain mouth; the waxed linen bound split gutter joints; the graft wax sealed them; the fish hook, tied to a line, dragged out sixty years of leaf mash from the elbow of the pipe. [558] Ines planted the mustard seed in the trough soil because Yesla told her mustard roots break hardpan, and Ines wanted to know if that was true. [584] It was, mostly. [587]

On the tenth day it rained, and water ran down from the tower gutters into the rafter troughs and along the grafted channels to the roots of the crab apple, and Ines shrieked like a gull. [623] It would not be enough water for a full crop. [633] It would be enough to persist, which Yesla had come to think was the only verb the orchard actually knew. [653]

'We should sue them anyway,' Ines said, tying the green ribbon around a low branch. [668] 'Write it on a leaf,' Yesla said, meaning nothing much by it, meaning only that she was tired and the wax pencil was in her apron. [694] Ines did, and then looked at the leaf, and then said the thing that Yesla would repeat for the rest of her life: 'So a vow you can drop is one you can pick back up.' [730] The child had no idea she had said anything. [739] She was already arguing about who would climb higher next time. [750] But Yesla wrote it down that night on the back of the breached contract, and in spring she posted a letter downriver, and the letter said only: come see what your daughter taught me. [784]
