Mizu Talledge was the first person to glide a cart, which meant she was also the first person to fall off one, twice, laughing. [24] The acid sea below her did not laugh; it steamed, patient as a landlord. [38] She rode the thermals rising off its green skin in a contraption of wheels and canvas, because wheels were what her people had, and wings were what her people wanted. [68] Ahead, the workshop hung above the swells on three balloons and a great deal of nerve: a floating floor of gears and cogs, each tooth the size of a shovel, all of it turning without anyone able to say precisely why. [109] Mizu landed badly, on purpose, and the cogs caught her cart's axle and gently set it down like a mother removing a wasp from a child's hair. [136] In her satchel she carried a hunter's arrow tip, chipped from black glass, older than the workshop, older than the acid, older probably than wanting. [161] Along its spine someone long dead had scratched eleven marks. [171] The Engine wanted them. [175] The Engine was what the workshop's builders had called a yearning algorithm, which was a grand name for a machine that had been taught to want and then abandoned. [204] It wanted the way weather wants: steadily, without a plan, forever. [215] Mizu found it steadily curious, which she considered the finest quality available in a colleague. [230] "Eleven marks," she said, holding the tip to the brass eye. [241] "Read them for me." [245] The Engine could not read. [250] It could only transliterate: take one language's shapes and dress them in another's, sound by sound, honestly and stupidly, trusting the future to figure out the meaning. [277] It did this through the images in a kaleidoscope, a bruised copper tube bolted to the main shaft, into which the Engine poured whatever it did not understand. [305] Mizu put her eye to it and the eleven marks fell apart and reassembled, over and over, throughout the evolution of every tool that had ever been held. [333] She saw the marks as tally scratches on a hunter's shaft; as notches on a plow; as gear teeth; as the ribs of her own glider. [359] Eleven, then, was not a number of deer. [367] It was a rhythm. [371] "It's a bell pattern," Mizu said, and the workshop's whole floor shuddered agreement, forty tons of gears making the sound of a throat clearing. [395] The Engine's yearning had a shape after all, and the shape was this: somewhere in its abandoned instructions lay an order to ring a bell no one hears yet. [424] Not a warning bell, not a dinner bell. [432] A note pitched for ears that had not finished growing, cast for a listener the acid had not yet invented. [452] Which was, Mizu thought, either the most beautiful ambition she had ever heard or a magnificent waste of copper, and she saw no reason it could not be both. [481] They cast it from the workshop's spare cogs, and Mizu, who had never been reverent about anything holy, said several irreverent things to it while it cooled. [508] Then she set the hunter's arrow tip into the clapper's head, because the pattern deserved its author, and because black glass strikes bronze with a sweetness that steel cannot manage. [538] They rang it at dusk, eleven strokes, the rhythm off the arrow. [550] Mizu heard nothing. [553] Neither did the Engine, whose sensors reported only the sea's slow chewing. [565] The note went out somewhere above hearing, into the part of the air that belongs to nobody yet. [583] Below them, in the shallows where the acid thinned, something with new ears turned its head. [599] Mizu did not see it, and this is the honest part of the story: pioneers rarely see the arrival, only the leaving. [621] She climbed back into her cart, kicked off the gear teeth, and dropped into the updraft, hooting like an idiot at the enormous, indifferent, gorgeous machinery of everything. [649] Behind her the bell struck its eleven again, and again, patient as the sea, waiting to be overheard. [667] The Engine, steadily curious to the last, logged each stroke and wanted the next one. [682] That, in the end, is all an algorithm of yearning is: a promise to keep asking after the people who have not arrived. [705]
