The cobalt observatory kept its autumn like a held breath. [10] Leaves ticked against the great copper dome, and the sound came back doubled from the walls, an echoed silence that made everyone whisper. [33] Mirene liked it that way. [38] She was the observatory's galaxy-chart embroiderer, and silence was the only fabric she could not stitch. [54] Each night the astronomers measured the sky; each morning Mirene translated their numbers into thread, silver for old stars, ash-gray for dying ones, a knot of gold where a nebula folded in on itself. [88] They called her the Idiot of the Dome, because she asked questions a child would ask and laughed at her own hands. [110] She let them. [113] A woman who disguises wisdom as foolishness is handed every secret in the building, since no one bothers to lock a door against a fool. [138]

Her true work happened at the third gallery, where the eternal candle burned. [151] It had not gone out in four hundred years, and by law every clock, lens, and shutter in the observatory had to synchronize to the moment its flame was measured at midnight. [183] Mirene worked by that flame from the eternal candle because it never flickered, and thread does not lie under a steady light. [205]

That autumn, the charts began to lie. [212] The lie was small: an extra stitch in the margin of the Serpent's tail, a doubled backstitch under the Kiln. [232] Someone was sewing messages into her finished work after she slept. [243] So Mirene did what she had always done best, and began unraveling. [255]

She did not pull the false stitches out. [263] She read them the way she read the sky, through adjacent possibilities: given this stitch here, what stitch could come next, and what could not? [288] Every thread has neighbors; a needle can only travel so far before the cloth complains. [303] By mapping which paths the hidden hand had refused, she learned its purpose better than it knew itself. [321]

The messages named gates, granaries, and the hour the garrison changed watch. [333] They named the Chancellor's carriage route. [339] They were charts of a city, not a sky, and they were being carried out of the observatory folded inside gifts of embroidered stars, to generals who would hang them on their walls and read them like orders. [377]

Mirene understood then that she was living in the small, thin time before the coup, the pause when a country still belongs to itself. [401] She could have run to the magistrates. [408] A fool's testimony, though, weighs nothing, and her foolishness was the only armor she had. [423] So she chose the slower, stranger weapon. [430]

Mirene had spent forty years listening for unheard stories -- the ones stars told with no one to write them down, the ones servants told to the stairwells. [457] She knew that a story nobody hears still shapes the room it is told in. [472]

For nine nights she sat under the eternal flame and altered the conspirators' stitches by a single thread's width apiece. [492] She shifted the hour of the watch change. [500] She moved a gate two streets east. [507] She made the Chancellor's route run past a bakery that had burned down a decade ago -- a detail only an old woman who walked the city at dawn would remember. [537] Then she waited, humming, dropping her spools, being the Idiot of the Dome. [550]

The coup arrived on the first frost and fell apart within an hour. [563] Companies marched to gates that were not there. [571] Signals fired at hours that no longer agreed. [579] Men who had staked their lives on being perfectly synchronized discovered they were strangers to one another in the dark, and surrendered rather than be seen failing. [606]

Afterward the magistrates searched the observatory and found nothing but star charts, beautiful and useless as ever. [623] Mirene sat in the third gallery with her hands in her lap, listening to the leaves come back off the walls. [644] The flame stood straight and told her nothing, which was its way of agreeing. [658] Nobody would ever tell this story. [664] That, she thought, threading her needle, was exactly why it had worked. [676]
