Vessh was not a spoken language; it was a carved one, read with fingertips along grooves in bone. [18] Ila learned it from her grandmother in the shadow of the towers of bone that stand in the canyon of endless echoes, where the wind never stops repeating itself. [47] The towers were the ribs of animals no one had names for anymore, set upright by hands no one remembered, and every rib was inscribed from root to tip. [76] By the time Ila turned forty, only four people alive could still read them. [90] She was a storyteller by trade, quietly articulate in a place built to shout, and she understood before anyone else what was about to be lost. [116] Not the bones; bone keeps. [121] The hands were what would die: the calloused knowledge of which groove meant grief and which meant grain-in-flood. [139] So Ila set herself an impossible errand: to translate texture into sound, so that Vessh could be heard after the last fingers stopped. [162] The copper helped. [165] As a girl she had worked the green seams under the canyon floor, and the mine had left its signature in her: thin copper threads grown into the veins of both forearms, hard and bright beneath the skin. [203] Copper carries what it touches, and the camps came to call her the copper-veined storyteller. [218] When she dragged her fingertips across an inscription, the vibration ran up her arms and rang, faintly, in the small bones of her ears. [242] Faintly was not enough; she needed amplification, and for that she needed the tunnel. [256] At the canyon's north end, the rock folds into a tunnel of echoes almost a mile long, a throat of stone that takes one sound and returns it forty times. [286] Her plan was to harness that throat: to fit a bone rib inside it, drag a stylus down the grooves, and let the tunnel multiply the whisper into speech. [315] For the stylus she bought, with eleven years of savings, a flawed diamond rumored to be cursed. [332] The trader said it had blinded a lapidary and drowned a prince. [344] Ila cared only that the flaw was a feather-shaped fracture running through its heart, which meant the stone sang instead of merely scratching. [367] A perfect diamond would have been silent. [373] The curse, she decided, was simply the price of a tool that had a voice. [388] The tunnel had one condition: the wind. [395] Air moving the wrong way through the throat smeared every echo into mud, and the currents shifted hour by hour with no pattern a person could feel from inside. [424] Her grandmother had taught her the old surveyors' trick: read the air through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight. [446] The canyon swifts nested in the bone towers by the thousand, and when they rose at dusk their shape was a map of the wind. [471] A flat ribbon meant still air; a corkscrew meant crosswind; a flock that split and rejoined above the north end meant the throat was drawing clean and straight. [499] For two years Ila watched the swifts and worked only on the split-and-rejoin nights. [513] She worked across the death of a language. [521] Tomas died in the second winter; the twins, Sabe and Oru, in the fourth and fifth. [537] Each of them, before going, laid Ila's hands on their favorite passages and let her feel what they heard. [556] She was the fourth reader, and then she was the only one. [568] On the night the swifts split and rejoined above the north end for the ninth time, Ila carried her grandmother's rib into the tunnel and set the diamond in its first groove. [600] She drew it down slowly, copper arms steady, and the stone hissed a thread of noise no larger than a breath. [621] The throat took it. [625] Forty returns folded over one another, and the mud-colored hiss became a voice: rough, unhurried, speaking a word for the smell of rain on hot bone. [651] Ila did not weep, because she was busy. [659] She had four hundred ribs to read and one lifetime, and she had learned from her grandmother that a storyteller's job is not to be moved but to carry. [688] The diamond wore down a little each night, its feather-flaw spreading, and she understood that it would shatter before she finished. [709] Perhaps that was the curse; perhaps that was simply what happens to anything that speaks. [724] So she trained a boy from the eastern camp to watch the swifts, and taught him the shape of a night when the throat runs clean. [750] He could not read a single groove. [757] He did not need to; he only needed to hear the tunnel, and to know what the birds were saying about the wind. [780]
