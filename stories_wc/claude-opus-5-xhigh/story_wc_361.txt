Ceril had carried relics along the aurora-lit ridgeway for nineteen years, and her hands still shook with every step. [19] The shaking was why the guild had nearly refused her; a courier who trembles can crack a saint's finger-bone or spill a jar of century-water. [44] Mistress Halla argued for her anyway, insisting that a jittery relic courier feels the weight of what she carries, which mattered more than steady wrists. [69]

Two winters later Halla walked north to survey a collapsed shrine and did not come back. [85] She left no letter, only a dragonfly-wing pendant hung on Ceril's doorlatch, the wing sealed in resin so clear it seemed to float. [108] Ceril learned its trick by accident, at a toll-hut, mid-sentence with a stranger. [121] The pendant answered his voice by vibrating sympathetically, a thin insect hum against her collarbone, and the minute they had between them opened out like a fan. [148] They talked about his drowned brother, his mother's bread, the names of three dogs; when he shouldered his pack, the tollman's kettle had not yet boiled. [174] That was the pendant's whole gift: it extends brief encounters, stretching them from the inside without spending a grain of the world's time. [197]

Ceril used it the way Halla had taught her to use everything, to coax wonder from the routine. [215] The ridgeway was routine: eleven miles of shale spine between the Reliquary and the northern chapels, walked by night because the aurora gave better light than any lamp. [243] Green fire poured overhead like slow water, and Ceril, jittering, counted steps and hummed to the relics in her satchel so they would not feel alone. [269] In nineteen years she stopped at every cairn, every hut, and gave each herder and pilgrim an hour folded inside a greeting. [291]

In her fortieth year the Reliquary handed her a lacquered box with a label Halla had written. [308] Inside lay one grey seed from the mistress's ridge garden, and beneath it, in her cramped hand: this will germinate only in stretched time, forty years of patience in a single night. [340] Ceril sat with the box on her knees for a long while, understanding at last that she had been left a tool, and that the absent mentor had never expected to return. [372] Halla had gone north knowing the shrine was unsound, the way she did everything, and had posted her last lesson forward nineteen years to arrive when her student was ready. [402]

That night the aurora came up rose-colored, and Ceril climbed to the highest cairn, where the wind always stole her breath and her hands shook worst. [428] She scraped a hollow in the frost-loose shale, set the seed in it, and pressed the dragonfly-wing pendant into the soil beside it. [451] Then she began to talk. [456] She told the seed about Halla's laugh, about the toll-hut stranger, about the shaking she had never outgrown, and the resin wing took her voice and hummed. [483] The night unfolded and unfolded. [488] Ceril felt decades pass through her like weather while the aurora hung motionless above the ridgeway, and under her palms the seed split, rooted, and germinated, pushing up a sapling with veined translucent leaves that trembled exactly as her fingers did. [529] By the world's clock she reached the northern chapels before dawn, on time, with her satchel full. [546]

Afterward she walked the ridgeway another thirty years, and the tree grew, and the story of it traveled across the fade from memory to myth. [571] Herders who had met Halla called her a stubborn woman with dirt under her nails; their children called her the gardener who died to plant something; their grandchildren called her the Aurora Gardener, who never died at all. [609] Ceril let the name harden, since a myth walks farther than a memory and does the same work. [627] She herself thinned into rumor: a trembling old courier who stopped too long at every cairn and somehow gave you a whole evening in half a minute. [654] Pilgrims still climb to the high cairn under the green light, and if they speak to the tree, its leaves answer by vibrating sympathetically, and the hour they spend there is longer than any hour they own. [691] They come down saying the ridgeway is a wondrous place, which is exactly the lie Halla wanted told, because it was only ever eleven miles of shale, and someone had to coax the wonder out of it. [728]
