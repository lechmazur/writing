I knocked on 4B for sugar because the building told me to. [12] That is a lie we later agreed to keep, Mrs. Odile Varga and I, though on the first evening it was only a lie I told myself. [39] She opened the door with a spoon already in her hand. [50] Sugar, she said, as if we had done this for years, and perhaps in some ledger of the house we had. [71] Mrs. Varga taught a dead language for a living, which meant she taught almost no one. [87] Three students a year, she said, mostly graduate boys who wanted to read one poem to impress a professor. [106] I told her I wanted to read buildings. [113] Not metaphorically. [115] I am a surveyor of historic structures, and every old house in this city is signed: mason's marks, foundation stones, lintels cut with words nobody has spoken aloud since the last cholera year. [148] I could photograph them; I could not hear them. [157] So the sugar became tea, Thursdays, and the tea became lessons. [168] She was eighty and patiently nimble, the way a good locksmith is patiently nimble: her fingers moved fast and her voice never hurried me. [192] She would set out declensions like small stones on the tablecloth and rearrange them until the grammar stopped resisting. [211] Beside her dictionaries stood an antique desk seal in brass, the kind pressed into wax, its handle worn to the shape of a thumb that was not hers. [239] I recognized the device on its face in week six. [249] It matched the cornerstone of our own building: a wheel with nine spokes, and beneath it two words I had copied into my notebook years before finding this address. [278] I did not ask. [282] Instead I began, gently, to interrogate the grammar. [290] Why does the language have a verb tense used only for things that outlive their speakers? [306] Why do the inscriptions on our stairwell keystone use a plural that includes the listener? [321] Mrs. Varga answered every question and none of them. [329] It is a dead language, she said, so its answers are only historical. [342] That was our second agreed lie, and by then I was collaborating in it. [356] In August the news promised a meteor shower, and she suggested the rooftop garden. [370] Nobody used it but her: tomatoes in olive tins, a fig tree in a bathtub, and the old ventilation housings standing around like patient witnesses. [395] We brought the tea up in a thermos. [403] She brought the seal, which she had never once used in front of me. [417] The first meteor went over while she was pouring. [426] Read me the parapet, she said. [432] The brickwork there carried a line of shallow marks I had assumed were tool damage. [447] Under her lamp, with eleven months of Thursdays behind me, they turned into a sentence: We were raised by hands that agreed not to name us. [473] I read it twice, and the second time I heard my own voice shaking. [487] The masons were a guild, she said. [494] They were paid to build, and they built in a language the city had outlawed, and they wrote what they could not say in court. [519] My grandfather's thumb wore that handle down. [526] He sealed contracts that were lies everybody had consented to: the building is ordinary, the workers are nobody, the words mean nothing. [548] Sky streaked twice, three times. [553] She pressed the seal into the soft wax of a candle stub and handed me the impression, still warm. [572] A dead language cannot be inherited, she said, only borrowed, the way you borrowed sugar. [587] That was the third lie, and the kindest, because she was handing me the whole of it. [604] I asked why she had let a stranger with a notebook into her kitchen. [618] She said she had watched me from this roof, kneeling at the foundation stones with a soft brush, mouthing the shapes wrong for a whole spring. [644] She said, the day you knocked, my sugar bowl was full. [655] So that was the first lie, and it had been hers. [666] We sat until the shower thinned, naming the ventilation housings in the old plural that includes the listener. [684] She died in March, and the seal came to me in the mailbox with no note, because a note would have been redundant. [707] Now I survey, and I write in my reports what the city agrees to hear: sound structure, minor spalling, decorative marks of unknown origin. [731] Then I go home, press the wax, and answer the buildings out loud. [744] They have known me the whole time. [751]
