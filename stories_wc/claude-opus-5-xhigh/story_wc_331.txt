The Ferris Library of Scientific Poetry opens at eleven at night and closes when the sun clears the water tower. [20] Marguerite Vance keeps those odd hours, and has for nineteen years, because she is sanely eccentric: she has a reason for everything, and the reasons are all sound. [48] Night patrons are quieter, the boiler is honest after midnight, and grief, she has found, keeps a graveyard shift of its own. [70] The collection is strange and specific: verse about entropy, sonnets on beetle taxonomy, a shelf of cosmological ballads bound in gray cloth. [92] Marguerite catalogs it by hand. [97] She refuses the terminal the board installed in 2011, not out of superstition but out of measurement: she timed herself, and the index cards win by four seconds a query. [127] Her drawers are analog memories, ten thousand of them, each in the handwriting of whoever loved the book enough to shelve it. [149] Some of the cards are Tomas's. [155] Tomas ran the day shift for thirty years and wrote the annotations in green pencil, and when he died in March his handwriting stayed on in drawer after drawer, unbothered, filing away. [187] On the third night after the funeral she found his tattered silk scarf still knotted to the coat hook behind the circulation desk, faded plum, frayed to a fringe at one end where he had worried it while thinking. [226] She did not wash it. [231] She wears it, and she has begun, slowly, to unravel it. [242] This is the part the patrons notice. [249] Between requests, while patrons browse the stacks with their coats still on, Marguerite sits under the green desk lamp and crochets. [270] She is not making a scarf back. [277] She is making a hyperbolic plane, increasing every third stitch, the way Tomas taught her with a paper napkin and a diagram the year they met. [303] The silk ruffles as it grows, folding into itself, making more surface than the room should hold. [320] That is the point. [324] Tomas's argument, which he published in a journal nobody read and repeated at every dinner party until people stopped inviting them, was that memory is not a warehouse but a curved space. [356] In flat space, he said, everything you have lived must crowd together and crush itself; the past gets smaller as you add to it. [380] But through hyperbolic geometry, the further you go from any point, the more room there is. [396] Infinite shelf space, he called it, and then he would laugh and say that was where the stories actually live: not in the books, which are only their shipping crates, but in the widening dark behind them. [433] Marguerite is trying to find that place. [439] She is aware of how this sounds. [446] She is not confused; she does not think the scarf is a door. [459] She thinks that hands remember what heads mislay, and that if she repeats the motion he taught her, in the building where he taught it, at the hour when nothing else competes, something in her will reawaken. [496] This is a hypothesis, and she is testing it, and that is the sanest thing a person can do at three in the morning. [520] The patrons watch her without meaning to. [527] A shift nurse who comes for the ballads; a man who reads only footnotes; two students who never check anything out. [548] They glance over the tops of shelves at the small woman in the plum scarf, her hook flashing, the ruffled silk spilling over her knees like something dredged from deep water, and they feel, each of them separately, that they are witnessing a private thing done in public on purpose. [598] Nobody asks. [600] That is the etiquette of the odd hours. [608] On the night the last thread is worked in, Marguerite ties off the end and holds up a ruffled purple mass the size of a sleeping cat. [635] Nothing happens. [637] No door, no voice, no lifting of the ordinary air of a library at four in the morning. [655] But her hands are still moving, and she realizes she has been counting under her breath in his cadence, three and increase, three and increase, and that she has not thought about the hospital in six hours. [692] She has thought instead about a napkin, a diagram, a man laughing at his own joke about shipping crates. [711] The place where stories live, it turns out, is not somewhere you arrive. [724] It is the widening you make by going. [732] She sets the hyperbolic plane on the circulation desk, next to the card drawers, where the green pencil still files away in the dark. [756] The shift nurse, passing with an armload of ballads, stops and touches it, and says, without looking up, that it is beautiful. [778] Marguerite says thank you, and stamps her books, and the sun starts on the water tower. [794]
