Mira had painted the fog for eleven years, and the fog had never once thanked her. [16] In the ruined orchard district, where centuries of thick fog had settled like sediment, she was the only artist left who still worked in light. [41] Her brushes were lenses, mirrors, and a lamp with nine shutters, and her canvas was the wet air itself. [60] She painted with light because the fog held it: every beam she swept through the murk lingered for an hour, the way a bruise lingers. [85] Someone had once called the district a sheet of exposed film, and Mira thought that was exactly right. [103] Something enormous and bright had happened here, long ago, and the air had never finished developing the picture. [121] The trouble was that Mira had run out of things worth showing. [133] Her murals had grown accurate and joyless: fog, ladders, dead trees, the grey backs of neighbors hauling water. [151] Then, digging a posthole for a new lamp stand, she struck the fresco fragment. [165] It was a palm-sized wedge of plaster, and on it three tiny figures were caught mid-laugh beneath a tree so heavy with fruit that its branches bent like sleeping arms. [195] Mira sat down in the mud and laughed too, quietly, the way you laugh at a joke told by someone dead. [216] She wanted, with a suddenness that frightened her, to rediscover the lost joy that plaster remembered. [232] Alone, she could learn nothing; the district's archives had rotted generations back. [244] But the fog-towns kept a knowledge network: relay lamps on the old irrigation towers, blinking coded questions from roof to roof, each keeper adding what they knew before passing the flash along. [276] Mira climbed her tower and spent three nights blinking a description of the fragment into the dark. [293] The answers came back over nine days, out of order, contradictory, and beautiful. [306] A mason in the salt quarter knew the plaster recipe. [316] A glassmaker's apprentice recalled a rhyme about the Orchard Feast, when the district lit torches under every tree and let strangers eat until they wept. [341] An old cartographer sent the position of the vanished chapel wall, and the news that the fresco had shown two hundred faces. [363] Mira had one. [366] So she did what a mural artist does with a fragment: she guessed the rest. [381] The guessing happened within the fold of a daydream, on an afternoon so still that the fog seemed to be holding its breath. [404] She leaned against a dead pear trunk with the fragment in her fist, and the orchard opened. [421] Torchlight, wasps drunk on windfalls, a fiddler with a broken string improvising around the gap, her own grandmother young and sticky-chinned. [442] It lasted perhaps four seconds, and she came out of it with wet eyes and a composition entire in her head. [463] For a month she built the rig: forty mirrors, the nine-shutter lamp, tinted glass ground from bottle bottoms, all aimed at a single acre of standing fog. [490] She told the network the date, and the network told everyone else. [502] Two hundred people came to the display, which was, Mira thought, an unreasonable number of witnesses for a woman painting on weather. [524] She opened the shutters. [528] Light went into the fog and stayed there, and the orchard came back: trees in fruit, forty feet tall, torches under every one, and faces, faces, faces, laughing at nothing anyone could hear. [561] The crowd made a sound Mira had never heard in the district, a low collective noise between a sob and a snort. [583] It held for one hour, as fog-light does, then thinned, then went. [595] Afterward a boy asked whether she was sad that it could not last. [608] Mira turned the fresco fragment over in her palm, three laughing strangers who had outlived their whole feast by six centuries. [629] Nothing lasts, she said, except the part that gets exposed. [639] Then she asked him to hold the lamp, and told him where the mirrors went, and blinked the recipe out across the towers so anyone could paint it again. [668] The fog took the message and carried it, patient as ever, developing slowly toward some picture none of them would live to see finished. [692]
