The orchard had not seen a sunrise in ninety years, and Toma had stopped apologizing for it. [17] Under the greenhouse glass, the alchemical trees grew anyway, fed on lamplight and quicksilver mist, their pears heavy as bells. [37] They ripened only to sound. [42] Toma kept the beads on a wire above the workbench: eleven carved stone beads, each one cut in a different century, each one tuned to a different life. [70] Struck with a bone needle, they gave off resonant awakenings - a hum that traveled root to crown and told the orchard to remember what season it was pretending to be. [100] And every hum brought her. [105] She came as she always came: the ashen-veil ghost bride, gray lace crusted with the soot of a wedding that had burned before the vows. [130] She never stayed. [133] She could not: her presence worked through ephemeral cameo appearances only, a heartbeat in the doorway, a flicker between two rows of quince, a face in the black glass that was gone before Toma could turn. [169] In each appearance she did one small, exact thing. [178] She breathed on a blighted branch and the blight fell away as ash. [191] She touched a barren root and it swelled with fruit by morning - if morning were a thing that happened here. [211] Her gift was lovingly venomous. [216] Everything she healed, she also poisoned a little; the pears she blessed came out sweet and faintly bitter at the core, and the soil where she lingered longest went white and dead. [248] That was why she rationed herself to seconds. [256] Toma had read the ledgers of the eleven lives that came before this one, all of them his own handwriting in different hands. [279] The first ledger was the debt. [285] In the winter of the first life he had carried a drowning girl out of a frozen millrace, given her the first stone bead for luck, and walked home through the cold with her water in his lungs. [323] He died at nineteen. [327] She lived to be a bride, and then a widow of her own funeral, and then a ghost with an accounting problem: a life owed, and only one creditor to find, again and again, across the centuries. [364] So she came to repay a debt across lifetimes, one orchard at a time. [378] She kept his harvests alive; she kept his roofs mended; she kept him, in eleven lifetimes, from starving. [396] And she had shrouded this valley in endless night so she could keep working, because sunlight unstitched her. [414] Ninety years of it. [418] Beyond the glass, over the hush of winter-cracked fields, the neighboring farms had gone to frost and rumor. [436] Nothing grew out there anymore. [441] Her payment had become a poison; her devotion had become a climate. [453] Toma understood the arithmetic at last on the night he struck the twelfth bead - the one he had carved himself, from the millstone of the drowned mill. [480] The hum went out through the trees, and the trees answered, and she stood at the end of the pear row with her veil full of ash and her hands full of someone else's harvest. [515] Two seconds, maybe three. [519] Long enough for him to say, "It's paid." [527] She shook her head. [531] She always shook her head. [536] A debt is only closed by the one who is owed, and he had spent eleven lifetimes calling her back, striking beads in the dark, hungry for the flicker of her at the edge of the lamplight. [573] He was the reason she could never stop. [581] So he did the cruelest, kindest thing a man can do to a woman who has loved him for four hundred years. [603] He decided to ghost her. [608] No farewell, no last summoning, no note left under the quince. [619] He cut the wire, ground all twelve beads to gray dust in the mortar, and scattered them where her worst poison lay. [641] Then he walked out of the greenhouse and did not look back at the glass, though he felt her arrive behind him - once, twice, a third time, briefer each time, a cameo with no scene left to enter. [679] The night thinned like breath off a window. [687] Somewhere past the orchard wall, over the hush of winter-cracked fields, a gray line opened in the east, and ninety years of dark came apart with a sound like a struck bead, resonant, waking, final. [722] He wept, walking into it, and he was smiling, because the last thing the light did before it took her was let him see her whole - unveiled, unowing, laughing at him the way she must have laughed before the frost, sweet and a little bitter at the core. [770] The pears ripened in daylight after that, and they never needed to be called. [784]
