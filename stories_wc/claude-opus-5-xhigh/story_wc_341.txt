The tether snapped on a Tuesday, and after the string breaks, Ilse Vantry learned, the world does not fall; it simply stops being held. [24] She had been the aviation meteorologist at Kettle Ridge Station for eleven years, reading the sky for pilots who never saw her face. [47] Now the station's sky gondola drifted at nine thousand feet, its severed cable trailing beneath it like a plumb line searching for a floor. [71] The winch spool below, on the ridge, still held four miles of steel wound in perfect rings. [88] That was the thing about a spool: even cut, it kept the shape of everything it had ever carried. [107] Ilse called this spooled wholeness, and she had built a career and a grief around it. [123]

Twelve years earlier her husband, Aldo, had flown a mail run into a mountain wave cloud over the Sarn Gap and had not flown out of it. [150] No wreckage, no beacon, only a hole in the radar sweep the size of a small brave airplane. [168] Everyone else called that a vacancy. [173] Ilse called it evidence, and she meant to prove that empty spaces remember. [186]

Her method was unscientific and she never wrote it in the official logs: she categorized clouds by emotion. [204] Column A held the placid ones, fair-weather cumulus loafing over wheat. [215] Column B, the sullen stratus that pressed pilots low and made them lie about ceilings. [230] Column C she reserved for the quietly explosive: the lenticular caps and hot towers that looked like sculpture and hid enough energy to fold a wing. [256] Aldo had died inside a Column C cloud, and Ilse had spent twelve years arguing that such a cloud keeps a ledger. [278]

The gondola smelled of cold brass and older dust. [287] Along its curved rail sat her instruments: a mercury barograph, three anemometer cups, and Aldo's brass telescope, the one his grandmother had carried across an ocean. [313] Its lenses were scratched to a soft haze, so every star it showed came through like dusty starlight, half memory and half light. [336] She had used it every clear night to sight the Sarn Gap and log what was not there. [354]

The radio crackled with Petra Osk, her apprentice on the ridge, voice thin with static and guilt. [371] "The spool jammed and the drum sheared," Petra said. [380] "Valve your gas and you can be standing on scree by midnight." [392]

Ilse checked the drift: two knots, north by northwest, straight down the throat of the Sarn Gap. [409] The wave cloud was already forming there, a stack of smooth lenses glowing rust in the last light, patient as a held breath. [432] "Not tonight," she said. [436] "Log this: I am going to the Gap." [444]

She had one instrument the ridge did not: eleven years of soundings, wound like thread on the same spool of habit, every one taken at the same hour from the same altitude. [476] Laid end to end, they showed a small persistent flaw in the wave over the Gap, a pocket of warm disordered air that returned each autumn like a bruise under the skin of the sky. [511] Turbulence has no memory, the textbooks said. [518] The textbooks had never lost anyone into a cloud. [527]

At nine-forty the gondola slid into the first lens, and the basket shuddered and went strangely calm, the way rooms do after shouting. [550] Ilse read the barograph, timed the rotor bumps, and called every number down to Petra, who repeated them back like prayers. [571] There it was, exactly where twelve autumns had put it: the pocket, warm and rough, a wound in laminar air that would not heal. [595] Something had torn the flow here once, hard, and the mountain and the wind had kept rewinding the tear onto the same spool ever since. [620]

It was probably a rock spur, she knew; it was probably nothing to do with a mail plane. [638] But an absence had held its shape for twelve years, and that was the whole of what she had set out to prove: empty spaces remember. [664] She raised the brass telescope, found the Gap through the thin skin of the lens cloud, and let the dusty starlight settle on the place where nothing was. [692] Then she opened the valve. [697]

The gondola sank out of Column C, past the sullen stratus, down toward the black ridge and Petra's landing flares. [717] In the morning she filed the sounding under a new designation, Cloud Aldo, and the bureau, which had no column for grief, accepted it as a local anomaly and printed it in the regional atlas. [752] That is how you memorialize a man in a country with no grave: you get his name into the weather, where pilots must read it before they fly. [780]
