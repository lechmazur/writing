The waterfall broke light into a thousand colors, and Ilse Verran walked through it as if parting a curtain of stained glass. [22] Behind it lay the hidden orchard, older than the guild, older perhaps than the idea of guilds, and every spring it smelled brand new. [46] Her auburn hair went silver with mist the moment she stepped inside. [58] She had three hours before the final transformation, and she was, by her own honest accounting, one ingredient short. [77] The manuscripts named it plainly: a frozen dew crystal, gathered from the oldest tree, at the hour the orchard permits. [97] No clock could find that hour. [103] For forty years the guild had insisted the instruction was corrupt, a scribe's flourish, and had ground their teeth over failed crucibles. [125] Ilse had read it differently, because her mother had taught her to watch birds. [139] She set her small brazier on a flat stone and coaxed a flame from dry apple-wood, feeding it slowly so it would last. [162] Then she sat with her ledger open on her knees and waited for the starlings. [177]

They came at dusk in their thousands, pouring over the waterfall's crown, and the orchard answered them with a sound like held breath. [200] Ilse tracked the murmuration the way other alchemists tracked temperature, reading the hour through the patterns in a flock of starlings. [221] The birds folded into a narrow spiral, then a lens, then a shape her mother had called the closing hand. [241] Cold dropped through the orchard like a stone into a well. [252] On the lowest branch of the oldest tree, a single bead of dew hardened into a crystal, six-sided, holding the last of the colored light. [277]

Ilse's hands shook, which she recorded in her ledger, because she recorded everything. [290] She had come here to settle an argument that had cost her twenty years: whether logic and intuition could be harmonized, or whether one always had to be sacrificed to feed the other. [323] The guild had chosen logic and made nothing. [331] Her mother had chosen the birds and died poor, laughing, with a pressed blossom in her pocket. [348] Ilse carried that blossom still, folded in wax paper between the pages on evaporation rates, and told no one. [367] She was, beneath the vinegar and the measurements, secretly sentimental, and she considered it a flaw until tonight. [385]

She lifted the crystal free with silver tongs and carried it to the flame. [399] The manuscripts ended there, with one line she had always assumed was decoration: the work completes at the touch of wonder. [420] She had planned to substitute a measured quantity of heat. [430] Instead, with the crystal a hand's width above the fire, she stopped. [442]

The orchard was doing something she had no words for. [452] Every branch had gone white with frost, and the waterfall behind her threw its broken colors across the ice, so that the trees burned green and rose and gold without heat. [483] The starlings settled into the branches and stopped singing all at once. [495] Ilse laughed out loud, alone, forty-three years old, and the sound surprised her more than the frost had. [513] Something in her chest opened that no crucible had ever opened, and she understood, finally, that the line was not decoration but a specification. [537] Wonder was the reagent. [541] Not a mood, but a method: the moment the measuring mind stopped defending itself and simply looked. [558]

She lowered the crystal into the flame. [565] It did not melt; it rang, one clear note, and the note went into the fire, and the fire turned the color of an apple blossom. [591] The final transformation took nine minutes and required nothing further from her but attention. [605]

Afterward she wrote her report by the dying brazier. [614] She gave the guild the measurements, the timings, the frost point, the ratio of light to ice. [631] Then she wrote the instruction they would hate: read the hour in the starlings, and when the trees catch fire without burning, stop working and let yourself be astonished. [660] Both halves, she noted, in the same hand, on the same page. [672] She took out the pressed blossom, looked at it for a long time, and left it in the crook of the oldest tree. [695] Walking out through the waterfall, she let the colors break over her and did not try, even once, to name them. [716]
