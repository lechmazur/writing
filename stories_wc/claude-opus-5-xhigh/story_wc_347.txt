Mera never asked for the orchard, and the orchard never asked for her. [13] It came anyway, the way weather comes, the winter her grandmother stopped answering the bell. [28] Eighty-one trees, planted in a labyrinth cut into the cliff, every one of them bearing iron. [44] That was the curse: the trees fruited hardware. [52] Bolts in spring, hinges in high summer, and in autumn a hard rain of small nuts, hexagonal, cold, ringing off the stone paths. [75] The second half of the curse was in her hands. [84] Mera could not touch anything the orchard had made without blighting it, so she had learned the other way, the way her grandmother called working at a hand's width: holding her palm above a thing until the metal dented under the pressure of nothing at all. [130] Leaving impressions without touching. [134] It was, she admitted, a graceless gift, and the things she made with it came out lopsided and beautiful, like birds built by someone who had only heard birds described. [164] Which was, more or less, the truth of the birds. [174] Six of them still patrolled the labyrinth, brass rooks with clockwork throats, and they had been built to call travelers home. [195] Now they only patrolled. [199] Their song-drums had rusted through, and a bird with no song is only a shape crossing the moon. [217] The cliff road forked at the orchard wall, and the wrong fork went into the labyrinth, and the labyrinth went to the edge. [240] Three carters had gone over the edge last year. [249] Mera had heard the last one calling for two hours and had not found him, because she did not know her own maze. [272] In October she found the blueprint under the seed press, a sheet gone blank with age, the ink lifted off it by ninety winters of damp. [298] Blank, but not empty. [302] Her grandmother had drawn hard, and the stylus had pressed the lines through into the paper itself. [319] Charcoal dust, tipped and rolled across the sheet, brought the grooves up gray: a bird. [334] Not a rook, but a seventh bird, bigger, with a lamp for a breast and a song-drum the size of a dinner plate, and a note in the margin in her grandmother's hand: SHE LEADS THEM OUT. [371] Her grandmother had never built it. [377] There was a second note, smaller, offhandedly exact the way the old woman always was about the things that frightened her: NEEDS 214 NUTS, ALL FROM THE SAME TREE, GATHERED BEFORE FROST. [409] After the first night frost the iron went brittle; a frosted nut split under any pressure, even the pressure of nothing at all. [432] That was the last week of October, and the sky that evening had the low green look that comes before a hard cold. [455] She took the barrow into the eleventh row, to the oldest tree, and worked until her back ached, tipping nuts off the flagstones with a wooden scoop. [482] Two hundred and thirteen. [486] She counted twice. [489] The rooks came over at dusk, wings ticking, and she stood still under them and hated them, and then went down on her knees in the wet grass to look for one more small nut. [524] She found it in the moss at the root, cold as a coin and still whole. [540] In the pressing shed she laid the parts out on sacking and began to assemble the seventh bird the only way she could: hands hovering, breath held, the metal folding and biting itself shut under a palm that never came down. [581] It took until the small hours. [587] The wings came out uneven. [592] The lamp sat crooked in the breast, and the song-drum, when she tapped air above it, gave a note like a bucket falling down a well. [618] Her grandmother would have wept at the workmanship. [626] Mera carried it out on the wooden scoop, set it on the orchard wall, and watched it haul itself into the cold air like a woman climbing a ladder in skirts. [657] Frost came an hour later, whitening the flagstones, and the whole orchard began to tick and split. [674] Below, on the cliff road, a lantern stopped at the fork. [685] Mera heard the bad note go out over the labyrinth, once, twice, ugly as a hinge, and the lantern turned toward it. [707] She watched the light thread the turns, note by note, until it came out at her gate: a carter, swearing, alive. [728] He asked what that horrible sound was. [735] Mera said it was two hundred and fourteen nuts and ninety winters and a woman who had drawn too hard, and left it at that. [760] She was still not sure she wanted the orchard, but she wanted the next lantern to turn, and that, it turned out, was enough to keep her. [787]
