Mira worked only in the last twenty minutes of daylight, when the city turned to honey and the varnish on old paintings went soft with light. [26] The dealers called her the golden-hour portrait thief, and they were not wrong, though they misunderstood the reason. [44] She did not sell what she took. [51] She read it. [54] In Vaux, a portrait painted under a fleeting golden sunset keeps a splinter of the sitter's future inside the pigment, and the splinter only shows itself in light of the same color. [86] Those were the illuminated moments: a wedding, a betrayal, a hand on a train door. [101] Mira had spent eleven years stealing other people's, hoping one of them would contain her. [116] She was quietly savvy about it, the way a locksmith is savvy about doors: no bravado, no signature, only patience and a good ear. [140] Because that was the whole of it, really: to press your ear against destiny's door and hear whether anyone inside was moving. [162]

Her instructions came from Oskar, who kept the flower stall under the clock at Emerald Station. [178] Oskar never spoke a plan aloud; he wrote codes in flower arrangements and let them ride the trams across the city. [199] Marigolds meant sunset work; three sprigs of rosemary meant three guards; a single white anemone meant the owner had died and no one would miss the frame. [226] On Thursday a boy delivered a bouquet Mira had to read twice. [238] Marigolds, yes, and rosemary, but bound around a stem of moonflower, which Oskar had never used. [254] It meant, if she was reading him right, that the job continued after dark. [268]

The house on Calder Row held a portrait of a girl of seven, sold by a mother who needed rent money more than she needed a daughter's future. [296] Mira had been that girl. [301] She took it at 7:41, with the sun going down the sky like a coin down a slot, and she had four minutes to look. [326] The pigment warmed, the splinter surfaced, and Mira saw nothing at all: a woman-shaped absence, a smear of dark where a life should be. [350] The gold went out of the window like a breath, and she stood in the dark holding proof that she had no future to overhear. [375]

The moonflower, then. [378]

Emerald Station by moonlight is a strange cathedral: green tiles from floor to vault, and when the moon crosses the skylight the whole hall goes underwater and quiet. [406] Oskar was closing his stall, wrapping the unsold stems in newspaper. [417] He looked at the canvas under her arm and did not ask. [429] From beneath the counter he lifted a jar of captured moonbeams, the kind the old glassblowers made before the war, still holding a slow silver light that turned when you tilted it. [461] "Sunlight shows you what will happen," he said. [469] "Moonlight lets you reinterpret it." [474]

She had spent eleven years believing the future was a fixed room behind a locked door, and here was a florist telling her the door had hinges on both sides. [504] Mira set the portrait against the tiled wall and unstoppered the jar. [516] Silver poured out, cold and unhurried, and lay across the child's painted face. [529] The smear of dark did not become a wedding or a betrayal. [541] It became a green hall, a skylight, a woman kneeling with a jar in her hands. [557] It became this. [560]

Mira laughed, once, in luminous disbelief, the sound going up into the tiles and coming back to her doubled. [579] The painter had not left her blank because she was doomed. [590] He had left her blank because her one illuminated moment was the moment she stopped stealing other people's. [608] She was inside it now, and it was small, and it was hers. [621]

In the morning she returned the canvas to Calder Row through a window she had left unlatched, which is a thing no one in Vaux had ever done. [649] Then she went back to the station and asked Oskar for work. [661] Now she builds the bouquets. [666] Marigolds for the hour, rosemary for the guards, moonflower for the ones who need to be told, gently and without words, that the door was never locked. [693] The jar sits on the counter, dimming a little each year. [704] She tilts it whenever a customer comes in looking hunted, and she watches the silver turn, and she says the only thing she knows for certain. [730] "Whatever you saw at sunset," she tells them, "come back at moonrise and look again." [745]
