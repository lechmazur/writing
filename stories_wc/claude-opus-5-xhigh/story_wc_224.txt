The bookstore had eaten the mountain slowly, shelf by shelf, until every ore seam became a corridor and every cave a reading room. [23] Ceyla had shelved its lower galleries for eleven years and had never once found the bottom of the labyrinth. [42] She had come here after remembering became painful, after the flood took the valley towns, after the language of her childhood thinned into a hum she could no longer hold in her mouth. [75] The mountain hired anyone who could carry a crate up a ladder and ask no questions of the dark. [94]

On the ninth gallery, where the lamplight turned silver, sat the mercury-glass seer. [107] Her skin had the sheen of an old mirror's backing, and if you looked too long you saw your own face, slightly improved. [130] For one coin she would substitute reality nightly: you slept, and she rebuilt the hours you could not carry, so that you woke owning a kinder yesterday. [157] Most of the shelvers paid. [162] Ceyla did not, though she ate her bread beside the seer most evenings, because the woman's voice carried a hidden thunder, like weather waiting behind a wall. [189]

In spring a crate came up from the drowned valley, and packed in wool inside it lay a lost language stone. [210] It was black and heavy as a sleeping animal, notched in rows nobody upstairs could name. [226] The cataloguers passed it hand to hand, shrugged, and marked it ornamental. [238]

Ceyla carried it to lamp thirty-one, set her fingertips in the notches, and began to shake. [254] "Your people wrote for hands, not eyes," the seer said behind her. [266] "Fetch a feather." [269]

She found a heron's quill in a drawer of bookmarks and drew it slowly across the stone, and the notches caught the barbs one by one, the way a comb finds knots. [301] Sound came up the shaft of the quill into her palm, and the sound was her grandmother's voice. [319] It came via the texture of a feather because her people had built their writing for the blind winters, when snow sealed the doors and only touch was left. [348] She had been taught this at six years old, and had let it go at nineteen, because using it made the valley rise up in her like water. [376]

"The mountain is full of buried talents," the seer said. [386] "Gifts people put down because holding them hurt. I make a living off the putting down." [402]

For eleven nights Ceyla read. [407] The stone was not a poem or a history. [415] It was an instruction, repeated in four dialects, for anyone leaving a place they loved. [430] Hollow the stone. [433] Carry the seed of your hearth tree inside it. [442] Plant it where you mean to stay, and you will know the shape of home by what grows. [460] Ceyla turned the stone over and found the plug of wax, and under the wax a single seed, dry, ridged, patient for forty years. [484]

That night the seer offered her a coin's worth free: a valley that never drowned, a kitchen, a door that opened the right way. [508] Ceyla said no, and asked instead for the summit key, which only the old staff carried. [524] The seer was quiet so long that the lamps seemed to lean in. [536] "I substitute my own nights too," she admitted. [544] "Forty years of them. I have replaced my home so often I no longer know its shape." [561]

They climbed all the next day, through galleries of atlases, past the sealed room of unsold prophecies, up the stair that the miners cut and the booksellers finished in oak. [591] The summit door opened onto a terrace of thin soil where nothing had ever been asked to grow. [609] Ceyla knelt and pushed the seed in with her thumb. [619] Above them the sky had been holding its breath all season, and it broke at last: real thunder, no longer hidden, and rain that struck the stone terrace hard enough to sting. [651] The seer lifted her mirrored face into it and let the water run, and her skin showed no improved reflection at all, only a woman getting wet. [678]

"Read to me," she said, "the real words, even if remembering hurts." [690] So Ceyla took out the heron feather, wet as it was, and drew it across the notches, and the instruction spoke again in her grandmother's voice, and the seer wept without needing to invent a reason. [726]

In the autumn a green shoot came up through the terrace soil, crooked, stubborn, unmistakably a hearth tree, and Ceyla began teaching the feather-reading to anyone on the ninth gallery who would hold out a hand. [762] The seer stopped selling nights, and slept, badly and truly, in the only world there was. [778]
