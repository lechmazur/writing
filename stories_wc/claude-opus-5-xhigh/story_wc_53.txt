After the carousel halted, the lagoon stopped breathing. [8] For nine hundred turnings it had spun above the cloud deck, dragging tide from nothing, and Kesh had learned to read its rhythm the way other people read clocks. [37] Now the water lay flat as a dropped mirror, and she could not tell whether yesterday had happened yet. [56] A kinetic lagoon exists only while it moves; this one had stopped, and had not yet decided to disappear. [75] She waded out anyway, hip-deep in stillness, sorting the settled things. [86] Scavenging was honest work in a place that had no ground: whatever the current used to carry now sank, and Kesh got first claim on the sinking. [113] Brass fittings, ticket stubs, a child's shoe, and the yellow husk of a disposable camera. [128] She had found forty of those cameras over the years, and she had kept exactly one part from each: the wheel. [149] The little ridged thumbwheel that advanced the film, that clicked once per moment spent. [163] Kesh strung them on wire at her belt, and whenever her sense of order slipped, she thumbed one and let the click set her back in sequence. [190] Forty clicks made a day, roughly; forty days made a turning. [201] It was a poor clock, but she had built it herself, which was more than she could say for anything else. [222]

Once she had meant to build the second lagoon. [231] She had drawn it at nineteen: a wider basin, gentler gearing, a carousel that would never need to stop. [250] The drawings went into the water one spring, along with the rest of her ambition, and the current had worn them down the way it wore down everything: not violently, just patiently, until the lines were suggestions and the suggestions were silt. [292]

Her ghost stood on the ninth horse of the stopped carousel, where it had stood since the halt, waiting to be spoken to. [315] It wore her face at nineteen and it would not answer questions; it only asked them. [331] Kesh knew the rule, because everyone in the lagoon knew the rule: a ghost will trade you your future for a true account of your past. [357] The trouble was that Kesh's past had come loose from its order, and she could not tell the story of a life she could no longer sort. [384]

So she gathered it secondhand. [388] The stilled water held voices the way a cup holds tea leaves; she walked the shallows at dusk and listened to what other people said about her. [415] A ferryman telling his daughter about the girl who redrew the gearing and was laughed out of the guild hall. [435] Two lamplighters arguing about the winter she stopped submitting plans. [445] A woman she had loved, telling a stranger that Kesh had been kind and had wanted too little, in that order. [466] Fragment by fragment, she assembled a chronology she could not have produced from the inside. [481] It took eleven hundred clicks. [486] It fit in one paragraph. [491]

Then she climbed the carousel. [496] The horses were cold and the mirrors had gone honest, and the whole ring hung in a fractured stillness, as if the machine were holding a note it could not finish. [527] Her ghost looked at the wire of forty wheels and said, "You kept the counters and threw away the pictures." [547] "I kept the time," Kesh said. [553] "I only lost the order of it." [560] "Then tell me the order," said the ghost. [568]

She told it: apprenticeship, drawings, the guild hall's laughter, the winter of no plans, the woman, the water, the forty cameras, the halt. [591] She told it in the words of ferrymen and lamplighters, because those were the only words that had held their shape. [612] When she finished, she said the thing that was hers alone, the thing no one had overheard: "I regret it." [632] "Not the failing." [635] "The stopping." [637]

The ghost stepped down off the ninth horse. [645] "Regret is a gear," it said. [651] "It only works if something turns." [657] Then it was gone, and Kesh was nineteen and forty-one at once, and for the first time the confusion felt less like drowning and more like a wide, usable view. [687]

She went down into the machine room under the lagoon floor, where the great shaft sat seized and patient. [706] She had no drawings left, but she had eleven hundred clicks of other people's memory and forty ridged wheels that knew exactly how to advance one notch at a time. [736] Kesh set her shoulder against the shaft. [743] Somewhere above her, so faint she might have invented it, the water remembered how to move. [759]
