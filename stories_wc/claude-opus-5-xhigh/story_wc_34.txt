Nobody came to hear Oris sing. [6] That was the shape of his life on Kestrel Deck: a seldom-heard balladeer sweeping the misty courtyard above the clouds, humming to the flagstones while the cargo-lifts groaned. [34] He had learned to be driftingly alert, half asleep in the fog and yet listening for the one note that meant something was wrong with the weather-loom. [61] That was how he found the first graffiti. [69]

It bloomed on the north wall in soot and rust: a grid of dots, ten across, punched in careful rows, as though someone had rolled ink through a sieve. [98] Oris knew that pattern. [102] Wren, the deck's garden-engineer, had died two winters back, and her flower press still sat in the tool crib, its bolt plate perforated with the same ten holes per row. [132] He fetched the plate, laid it over the dots, and read the pattern through the holes like a lyric through a rhyme scheme. [155] It said: SING IT FOR ME. I CANNOT FIND THE WORDS. [166]

The loom-tenders had a joke about the ghost in code, the stubborn fragment that made the lightning-arrestors stutter every storm. [186] Oris stopped thinking of it as a joke. [194] He learned her window the hard way, standing out in the wet while the sky cracked white: she could only push signal in the pause between lightning and thunder, when the arrestor dumped the clock and left three seconds of unguarded machine. [236] In those seconds the soot crawled up the wall and made its dots, and the courtyard smelled of hot copper and crushed violets. [259]

So he answered the only way he could: he mixed lamp-black with lift-grease, clamped the flower press bolt plate to the stone, and stamped his reply hole by hole, learning to encode his own voice into ten-dot rows. [297] It took him nine nights to write WHAT WORDS. [306] The conversation that followed was the strangest work of his life, and the happiest: two makers passing messages encoded in graffiti art, her side blooming in soot spirals, his side pressed in tidy grids, the wall filling with a script no one else on the deck could read. [354] There was a tinkersong wonder to it, the way a good mechanism sings when it finally meshes. [371]

But Wren's fragment could not hold a whole thought. [380] She sent pieces: MIRA. THE SECOND SHELF. COLD IN THE MORNING. I MEANT TO. [394] A balladeer's whole trade is to articulate unspoken needs, to take the stammer of a grieving miner or a lovesick rigger and give it meter until the person says yes, that, that is what I could not say. [432] Oris had simply never had anyone ask. [439]

He went to the second shelf of the tool crib and unbolted the press. [453] Inside, between two boards, lay nine sky-lilies pressed flat as pages, and a label in Wren's hand: for Mira, when she is old enough to want them. [480] Mira was seventeen now, working the cold morning shift on the mooring gantry, and she had not spoken her mother's name in two years. [504] Oris understood then that Wren's need was not a message. [514] It was a song, and she had chosen a singer because a song is the only container that holds love and apology at once. [538]

He wrote it over three days, the ballad of the garden-engineer who spent her last hours patching the loom so the deck would keep its air, and who therefore never came home to say goodnight. [573] He stamped the first verse on the wall for her approval, and the soot came back with one word: YES. [593]

The next storm, Oris stood in the misty courtyard with his lap-harp and the pressed lilies in his coat, and Mira came because he had told her the wall had her name on it. [627] Lightning opened the clouds below them, white as a struck bell. [638] In the three seconds before the thunder, in the deck's one perfect silence, he sang. [653] Three seconds is one line, if the line is honest. [663]

He sang it storm after storm, three seconds at a time, and half the deck learned to wait out the flash with him. [686] Mira took the lilies on the fourth night and said her mother's name out loud. [701] After that the arrestors ran clean, and the loom-tenders congratulated themselves on a patch they had never written. [719] Oris keeps the bolt plate on his belt, in case anyone else on the high decks has something they cannot say. [740] They come to him now, one at a time, out of the mist. [753]
