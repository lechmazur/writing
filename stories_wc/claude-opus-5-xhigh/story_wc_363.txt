The raveling silence reached our village in the third week of autumn, and it took the bells first. [18] Mai Shen, a wistful woman who had spun thread on the same loom for forty years, heard the bronze tongues stop mid-swing, as if the sound had been pulled backward out of the air like a loose stitch. [56] She did not scream. [60] She had been waiting for it since her son Rui walked east with a satchel of shuttles and never came back. [81] What Rui left behind was a tree sap amber bead on a waxed cord, warm as a held breath, with a single winged seed suspended inside it. [108] Press it to your ear and you could hear him say, faintly, the orchard still holds. [124] The old women of the village called the world a tapestry, and Mai Shen had spent her life at its selvage, mending small tears in ordinary things: a marriage, a harvest, a boy's fever. [158] Now the whole cloth was coming undone, thread by thread, and through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry there was no one left to tell but the dead and the far away. [190] She found the way to him in a rummage sale box. [201] The widow Tan was selling off her husband's belongings under a tarp in the market, and among the chipped cups sat a jigsaw puzzle with its middle pieces missing. [230] Only the edges remained, and when Mai Shen fitted them together on her knees in the dust, the flat straight border made no picture at all -- it made a route. [260] Rui had cut those pieces himself -- she knew his knifework the way she knew her own hands -- and the notches along the border spelled the turnings of a path: nine left, four right, follow the water down. [297] He had left her a message across an impossible distance, and now she had to carry one back. [315] She walked for eleven days into the bamboo. [323] The stalks stood in green ranks a hundred feet high, and the silence there was not empty but hungry, licking the sound off her footsteps until she could not hear herself walk. [355] She kept the amber bead between her teeth so she would remember she had a mouth. [371] On the twelfth morning the bamboo opened onto the forgotten orchard. [382] Sixty pear trees stood in rows no one had pruned in a century, their trunks weeping sap that hardened into beads exactly like the one she wore, each drop stoically sparkling in the flat gray light, holding whatever had been said near it. [425] At the center stood the first loom, the great one, its warp strung from the branches themselves. [442] Rui sat before it, or what was left of him: a shape of woven thread in the seat, hands still on the beater, his voice long since spent into the cloth. [473] He had held the weaving alone for four years, and when he could not hold it, the silence had begun to ravel outward from here, and the bells had stopped in his mother's village. [507] Mai Shen understood the arithmetic of it before she let herself feel it. [520] Someone had to sit down and recommence the work, and whoever sat down would be woven in. [537] She was sixty-eight years old and had been spinning toward this chair her whole life without knowing its name. [556] First she pressed the amber bead against a fresh bead of sap on the nearest pear tree until the two warmed and fused. [579] Into the soft new resin she spoke her message, the only one worth an impossible distance: Rui, I came, you can rest, I have the shuttle now. [606] Then she hung the bead on the branch above him, where a hundred years of rain would not reach it, and sat down, and began. [631] In the village, the bells came back on a Tuesday, one at a time, hesitant, like men clearing their throats. [651] No one could say why, though the widow Tan wept over her tarp of secondhand things and could not explain that either. [673] Far east, in an orchard no map remembers, a wistful old woman throws the shuttle and catches it, throws and catches, humming to keep her voice a little while longer. [703] When it goes, the amber above her will still be sparkling, holding her sentence steady for the next pair of hands that find the border of the puzzle and follow it home. [735]
