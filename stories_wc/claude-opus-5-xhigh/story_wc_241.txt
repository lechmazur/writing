After the heart attack, my father gave me his lantern instead of an apology. [14] It was a candle lantern, tin, with one mica pane clouded gold from forty years of smoke, and he pressed it into my hands in the cardiac ward like a man settling a debt. [48] "Open it when you want to know how I thought," he said. [60] I teach probability at the state university, which my colleagues find funny, since everyone knows what my father is. [79] Compulsive is the clinical word; he preferred "devoted." [87] They call me theoretically rebellious: I publish papers arguing that randomness is the most misunderstood civic virtue, and then I go home and floss. [111] My rebellion has always been safely on paper. [119] Inside the lantern, folded around the candle stub, I found eleven slips of paper covered in copied graffiti: arrows, initials, a lopsided crown, the phrase SIX RIDES THE GREEN. [148] This was his system. [152] For decades my father made his wagers by interpreting cryptic scrawls on subway seats -- messages other men left in wax crayon and ballpoint, a folk oracle riding under the river all day. [184] I had known he was superstitious. [190] I had not known he was a scholar of it. [200] So in the last week of June I began to traverse the lines he had ridden, notebook open, doing fieldwork on my own father. [224] The seats still carry writing. [229] I sat under flickering strips of light copying stars, dates, a name inside a heart with the heart crossed out, and slowly the code opened: SIX RIDES THE GREEN meant the sixth horse on a green-silked jockey, and the crown meant a favorite, and the arrows meant nothing at all, which he had also recorded, honestly, in his own hand. [289] That was the part that undid me. [295] My father knew. [298] On slip nine he had written, in the tidy engineer's print he used for truth: "These marks predict nothing. I read them anyway. Both facts are true and I live in the gap." [331] In my lectures I teach lucid contradictions: the mind holding two incompatible certainties and warming its hands at the friction. [351] I had always used gamblers as my example. [359] I had never used him. [364] The eleventh slip was not graffiti at all. [372] It was a library call number and a date: midsummer. [382] So I went, on the longest day of the year, to the Carnegie branch at the end of the green line, and it had rained since dawn. [409] The rainwashed reading room smelled of wet stone and the clover that grows in the cracks of the parking lot, a sweet green smell that is somehow the exact smell of missing someone who is still alive. [446] The call number belonged to a 1961 volume on pedigree lines. [457] Tucked in the gutter of page 200 was a photograph of my mother, and behind it a folded page of my dissertation, printed and thumbed soft. [483] He had underlined one line twice: "The purpose of studying chance is not to win but to widen perspectives responsibly." [503] In the margin, in crayon -- subway crayon, the same waxy red -- he had answered me: "Then teach me. Slowly. I'm not good at slow." [527] I sat in that damp light for an hour. [535] I could have burned the slips; a cleaner daughter would have. [546] Instead I brought the lantern to his kitchen table in July, lit the stub, and let the mica pane throw its old amber square across the oilcloth. [573] We spent the summer on his oracle. [580] I taught him to score his own slips, to log every scrawl he obeyed and every race that followed, and by August the notebook showed what he had always privately known and never had to look at. [617] He did not stop betting. [622] He stopped betting the rent, which is a smaller miracle and the only kind I trust. [638] Now, when I lecture, I put the lantern on the desk and let the students look through the clouded pane at the room, everything gold and slightly wrong. [666] That is what a belief does, I tell them. [675] You cannot take it out of a person like a splinter; you can only teach them to notice the tint. [695] My father, nine years past the arithmetic his cardiologist offered, still rides the green line most Tuesdays and still copies down the scrawls. [718] He mails them to me, annotated, with the odds worked out in the margin, and at the bottom of the last one he wrote: "Still nothing. Still looking. Love, your bookmaker." [749] I keep them in the lantern, beside the flattened clover from his wallet, and I do not correct him. [768] Some contradictions you don't resolve; you carry them, lit. [777]
