Everyone agreed they were living during a period of profound transformation. [11] Nobody agreed what the world was turning into. [19] Since the Unbinding, memories had begun to entangle across strangers, so that a woman in Tarn might wake weeping over a boat she had never owned. [45] Neve Sarraf called herself a helix-minded engineer, because she could not think in straight lines and had stopped apologizing for it. [66] Every problem came to her as a spiral: the same question at rising altitudes, each turn a little truer. [85] Her notebooks were chaotically precise, margins crawling with corrections that were themselves corrected, every figure accurate to four decimals and filed nowhere. [107] She worked at night, in the moonlit canyon below Dry Harbor, where the old sea had drained away and left a submarine lying on its side like a beached whale. [137] From its conning tower she had salvaged a broken periscope mirror, a hand-sized wedge of silvered glass with a crack running its length. [160] The crack was the useful part. [166] Held up under a full moon, it split a person's reflection in two, and the entangled memories separated along the fracture: yours on the left, the stranger's on the right. [196] Neve had not built the effect; she had only measured it, then learned to modify it. [212] Memory is not a stone but a habit, and habits are written through synaptic plasticity, which means they can be rewritten by anyone patient enough to rehearse. [239] So she rehearsed. [242] She had a purpose that embarrassed her when she said it aloud, so she wrote it on the first page instead: to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams. [270] The Unbinding had turned every skull into a dig site. [280] Under the strata of daily worry lay the things people had quit: the boat unbuilt, the language half-learned, the letter never sent. [302] Neve believed those ruins deserved a curator. [309] Her own entanglement was a man named Idris Pell, a bargeman two hundred miles east, whom she had never met and knew better than her brother. [335] For eleven months his abandoned dream had been leaking into her sleep: a canyon bridge of laminated cedar, drawn in his hand, folded into a drawer the winter his father died. [366] She woke each dawn with cedar dust in her mouth and grief that had no address. [382] It would have been simple to cut the line. [391] The mirror could sever an entanglement in three nights of rehearsal, and half of Dry Harbor queued for exactly that. [411] Neve wanted the opposite. [415] On the ninth night of the moon she climbed down to the submarine, propped the broken mirror on the rusted rail, and let the canyon fill her. [442] The reverie came the way it always did, quaking, a tremor starting in her jaw and running down the spiral of her thinking until the rock walls seemed to breathe. [472] In the left half of the glass she saw herself at nineteen, quitting the bridge program because the mathematics of trusses had felt like a cage. [498] In the right half, Idris folded his drawing away. [507] Two abandonments, entangled, and she finally understood the physics of it: the Unbinding did not pair people at random, it paired the same wound. [531] So she rehearsed the memory differently, over and over, the way a hand learns a chord. [547] Not erasing the drawer, not lying: she added one detail, true and small, that neither of them had let themselves keep. [568] The drawing had been good. [573] Nine nights, four hundred repetitions, corrections to the corrections, until the new pathway held. [587] In the spring, a letter came upriver, addressed to the engineer in the canyon. [601] Idris Pell wrote that he had woken one morning certain of something he could not explain, and had gone to a drawer he had not opened in twenty years. [630] Enclosed was a photograph of cedar laminations clamped in a shop, and a question: did she know anything about load calculations. [651] Neve laughed until the canyon threw it back at her. [661] She began a new notebook that night, its first page ruled into columns: dreamer, dream, depth, date of abandonment, condition on recovery. [683] The queue at Dry Harbor changed shape by summer; people came now not to be cut loose but to be excavated. [704] She met them in the moonlit canyon, the broken mirror steady on the rail, the world still transforming around them into something nobody could name. [729] She could name her part of it, at least, and she did, once, aloud, to a stranger who had come to recover a garden. [753] Archeologist, she said, of abandoned dreams. [759] Then she picked up the glass, and they began. [768]
