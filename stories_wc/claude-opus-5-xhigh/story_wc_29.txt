The university radio station sat on the roof of the old physics building, a shed of cinderblock and duct tape that smelled like ozone, burnt coffee, and forty years of other people's cigarettes. [33] Mireille Osei had worked the two-in-the-morning slot for eleven months, and in that time the campus had learned to call her the celestial signal interpreter, half joke, half warning. [62] The name fit better than her actual one, which appeared on no faculty roster since the astronomy department let her contract lapse. [84] Her astronomical charts covered the console, taped over the fader labels, annotated in three colors of grease pencil and one long coffee ring. [107] They showed a patch of sky in Ophiuchus that had begun, in March, to whisper. [122] Not in radio, but in pressure. [128] The signal arrived through subsonic frequencies, eleven to seventeen hertz, too low for an ear and too patient for an instrument that only listened for an hour. [155] Mireille had listened for eleven months, with her hand flat on the studio window, feeling the glass buzz in the small hours. [177] What she found was not a message but a lever. [187] Below hearing, those frequencies work on the body like a stranger's hand at the base of the spine: dread, suspicion, the certainty that someone in the room means you harm. [217] The town had been splitting all spring, and everyone had a reason for it. [231] The chancellor blamed outside agitators; the graduate union blamed the chancellor; the churches on Vine Street blamed each other. [250] Mireille had the charts, and the charts said the arguments spiked when Ophiuchus cleared the water tower. [267] She took it to Dean Vargas, who wanted the recordings for a grant proposal, and to Petrossian at the union, who wanted them as proof the administration was gassing protests. [297] Both of them, in their own way, wanted to own the sky. [309] Mireille had grown up on the north side of Vine Street, where two churches had stopped speaking in April; her mother now called her aunt an enemy without being able to say why. [342] Mireille went back to the roof, because power plays were exactly what the thing above Ophiuchus fed on, and she refused to hand it another one. [368] She had stopped calling the source an intelligence and started calling it what it behaved like: cosmic tricksters, patient and delighted, plucking at a species that argued beautifully. [396] You do not defeat a trickster by shouting. [404] You outwit them. [407] The station's transmitter could not radiate at fifteen hertz, but the theater department's subwoofer array, borrowed for the spring opera and stored one floor down, could. [433] Her plan was simple physics and stubborn arithmetic: to interfere with the incoming wave, cancel it, meet each trough with a crest of her own. [458] The charts gave her the timing; the water tower gave her the horizon; a graduate student named Bo Kettering gave her forty minutes of stolen power from the elevator circuit. [488] They ran the cancellation at three-forty in the morning, as the community divided into two crowds on the quad below, one with candles, one with bullhorns, forty yards apart and closing. [519] Mireille kept the microphone open, because if it failed she wanted somebody's tape to have the reason on it. [538] The array did not make a sound. [545] It made an absence. [549] The buzz in the window quit. [555] The hair on her arms lay down. [562] Down on the quad, a bullhorn wavered mid-sentence, and someone laughed, startled, the way people laugh when a toothache stops. [582] Then the charts betrayed her: the source shifted, four hertz up, testing her, and she chased it by hand on the oscillator dial with Bo shouting numbers. [609] It shifted again, and again, and each time she was faster, until at last it stopped shifting, and the silence above the water tower felt, unmistakably, like someone deciding to be interested rather than amused. [644] By dawn the crowds had gone home, still angry, but angry about rent and tuition, human-sized things they could name. [664] Mireille signed off the station at six, cautiously hopeful, which was the most any interpreter of celestial signals could honestly be. [685] She rolled the charts, kissed the dust off them, and left a note taped to the console for the next voice on the air: KEEP THE HAND ON THE GLASS. THEY COUNT ON US ARGUING. [720]
