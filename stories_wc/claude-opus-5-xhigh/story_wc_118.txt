The last human dream was dreamt on a Tuesday, by a fisherman's daughter in Kirkmoss, and nobody had dreamt since. [20] Vesper Oakes, who had been called an odd historian even before the sky went crooked, did not mourn it the way others did. [43] She had her own method for filling the empty hours: she collected the stories walls had heard. [60] Her library was a reclaimed silo on the Hessel road, sixty feet of riveted steel that had once held barley and now held everything else. [85] Shelves spiraled up its inner wall like a staircase for someone patient, and at noon the rust threw a bronze quiet over the reading floor, a hush with a metal taste in it. [118] Vesper was sensitively perceptive in a way the surviving doctors had no word for; she could lay a palm on plaster and feel the arguments soaked into it. [146] Brick remembered weather; wood remembered weeping; stone remembered almost everything and forgave none of it. [161] That autumn - if the word still meant anything, with the seasons stumbling out of order - she went to the Ardry farmhouse to probe a chimney breast. [187] The walls there hummed with something older than gossip, and she found the source in the fireplace: a nest of half-burned diaries, their spines fused, their margins bitten black. [216] Someone had tried to burn them and lost their nerve. [226] She carried them back to the silo in a flour sack and worked through them for eleven days, wearing cotton gloves, reading with her fingertips as much as her eyes. [256] The diaries belonged to four generations of Ardry women, and every one of them wrote about the same thing near the end: the stars had moved. [282] Not drifted - moved, sharply, the way a table moves when someone leans on it. [296] The official story was that the axis had been displaced eighteen years ago, all at once, and that the dreaming had stopped because the human body could no longer find north in the dark. [330] The Ardry diaries said the displacement had happened before, in smaller shoves, and that each time a generation had lost something it never named out loud. [356] Vesper needed something older than paper to test it against, and she had one thing. [371] Pinned under glass on the silo's third turning hung a fragment of a medieval tapestry showing dragons - three of them, wool gone the color of weak tea, coiling around a wheel of stars. [404] She had always read the dragons as decoration; scholars did. [414] Now, with a diary open beside her, she saw that the beasts' tails converged on a point in the embroidered sky, and that the point was not where the pole stood now, nor where it had stood eighteen years ago. [454] It was a third place entirely. [460] Some weaver, seven hundred years dead, had marked the sky's true hinge with monsters so that nobody would mistake it for ornament, and everybody had. [485] Vesper laid her bare hand against the silo's steel that night and asked it, the way she asked all walls, what it had heard. [509] The steel was young and honest: harvest talk, a boy hiding from a strapping, a woman singing to barley in 1974. [530] Under all of it, faint as a pulse through a mattress, was the thing the diaries had circled and the dragons had pinned down: a long slow grinding, patient, indifferent, the sound of a world turning on the wrong pin and getting used to it. [575] She understood then why she had never mourned the dreams. [585] Dreams had been the mind's way of practicing displacement, of going somewhere that wasn't where you lay down; the species had simply lost the axis it dreamed around. [613] But walls dreamed nothing and forgot nothing, and she was the only one left who could listen. [630] In the bronze quiet of the following noon, Vesper began the catalogue she would spend the rest of her life on. [651] She hung the fragment at the silo's top, where the ladder ended, so that the dragons' tails aimed through the vent at the empty patch of sky where the hinge belonged. [682] Beneath it she shelved the half-burned diaries, uncopied, unrepaired, because burnt things tell the truth about how much people can bear to know. [705] Visitors came, sometimes, sleepless and hollow-eyed, and she read them a wall. [717] A kitchen in Kirkmoss that had heard a fisherman's daughter describe her last dream aloud at breakfast; a cellar that remembered a proposal; a stairwell that had held one long argument for forty years. [751] They went away calmer, carrying something borrowed to sleep beside. [761] It was not dreaming, Vesper admitted, but it was an axis, and a turning world needs one. [778]
