They called Yrsa the lunar-eclipse locksmith because she could only open the difficult things while the moon went dark. [19] The rest of the month she mended nets, sanded driftwood into spoons, and missed her brother. [35] Ivar had been a clockmaker, and clockmakers, he liked to say, are only locksmiths who work from the inside. [54] He left her a clockmaker's gear assortment in a tin that still smelled of his pipe: forty-one brass wheels, none of which fit each other. [79] She had tried for two years to make them turn together, and had learned only that grief is a mechanism with no teeth to catch on. [105]

The rumor came to her the way all useful rumors come to coastal towns, secondhand and salt-stained: there was a library above the clouds, frozen, where the world kept its instructions. [136] She went not for wealth or vengeance but to learn the rhythm of repair, the timing Ivar had in his hands and never wrote down. [161] The stair was old ship-timber, driftwood grey, bolted into the mountain by someone who had clearly loved the sea and left it. [183] She arrived under the soft hush of a distant morning, the sunrise happening somewhere far below her boots, muffled by cloud like a bell wrapped in wool. [210]

The library had no door, only a frost-welded gate, and the frost was a lock like any other. [228] Yrsa breathed on the hinge until it wept, and went in. [239] Inside, everything was ice: ice shelves, ice ladders, books furred white and stiff as gulls in winter. [256] Nothing could be read whole; the cold had broken the pages into flakes. [269] So she learned the place the only way it allowed, through scraps of prophecy hidden in a library's corner, drifted there like sand in a doorway. [295]

She knelt for three days sorting them, matching torn edges the way she matched gears. [310] A scrap said: the eclipse is the only key that fits the mirrored hall. [324] Another said: uncap the oil before you look, or you will only remember. [337] A third, in a hand she almost recognized, said: repair is not a skill, it is a tempo. [355]

The mirrored hall lay at the library's spine, and it was, plainly, a hall of mirrors: forty-one panes of black ice, curved and facing each other, each one holding a slightly older version of her. [390] Forty-one, like the gears. [395]

She waited eleven nights for the eclipse, eating snow and dried fish, sleeping with the tin against her ribs. [414] When the moon slid into shadow it did not go dark at all. [427] It turned copper, and the mirrors caught it and passed it hand to hand until the hall was hauntingly bright, brighter than noon, a light with no warmth and too much memory in it. [461]

In every pane she saw a workshop, and Ivar in it, at a different hour of his life. [479] She remembered the second scrap, drew the flask from her coat, and uncapped the clock oil before she let herself look. [500] The smell of it, thin and sweet, was her brother's whole workshop, and it kept her in her body while the mirrors tried to keep her in the past. [529]

So she watched his hands instead of his face. [537] Forty-one Ivars, forty-one motions, and every one of them the same underneath: touch, seat, quarter-turn, pause. [553] The pause was the part she had never known was there. [563] She had been forcing the wheels together in one breath, and Ivar had always given the metal time to agree. [583] That was the rhythm of repair: two beats of work, one beat of waiting. [597]

She sat down on the ice with the tin open in her lap and began, and the copper light held long enough for thirty of the forty-one wheels to find their partners. [629] When the shadow slipped off the moon the mirrors went blind, and she was alone in an ordinary frozen room. [649] She did not mind. [653] The remaining eleven she would finish at home, slowly, in the pauses. [665]

Going down the ship-timber stair, she thought about how driftwood is just a boat that learned patience. [682] The clouds took her, then gave her back to the noise of gulls and rope and market bells. [700] She still worked only under eclipses, because that was her gift and her limit. [714] But in the long bright ordinary months between, she fixed clocks. [725] Touch, seat, quarter-turn, pause. [730] People said her repairs held better than new, and asked her secret, and she told them the truth, which nobody believed: that she had learned it in a frozen library above the clouds, from a hall of mirrors, in a light that was too bright to be the moon. [779] And that the waiting was the hardest part to teach. [789]
