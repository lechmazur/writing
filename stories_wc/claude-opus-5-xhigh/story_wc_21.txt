The letters surfaced first, forty-one of them, sealed inside a chapel wall in Bay Saint Mercy. [16] Everything afterward belonged to Ondine Marsh. [21] She was the solemn civil-rights archivist aboard the Verity, a floating library slung beneath a zeppelin that had drifted the Gulf coast since the county courthouses began burning their own basements. [52] Her work was simple to state and impossible to finish: gather what the fire wanted, and keep it. [70] Among the letters lay a diary, and the diary was encrypted. [81] Not code from a spy manual, but a homemade cipher of tide tables and hymn numbers, the sort a man invents when he trusts no one and still needs to remember. [112] The author signed himself Abel Cray, field secretary, missing since the August of the voter drive. [128] Ondine broke the tide-table half in eleven days at the reading tables, the airship's shadow crossing the dunes below like a slow eclipse. [151] The tide tables pointed to one place: the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes. [164] Locals called it the Glass Eye. [170] A sinkhole in the barrier island, black water over black stone, so still that noon lay on it like a coin on a shut mouth. [195] The hymn column stayed shut, but the last line she could open said: I went down to see it plain, and now I see nothing else. [221] She took the mooring basket down at dawn. [229] On the sand between the dunes and the lagoon, someone had arranged the beach debris into words. [246] Bottle glass, crab claws, a rope of dried wrack, bleached vertebrae laid in careful rows: WHO FOUND THEM. [264] He already knew the letters had been found; he only wanted to know by whom. [279] Abel Cray stood at the lagoon's rim, twenty-six years old and sixty years late, his shirt still wet from a rain that had fallen in 1963. [305] He was retrocognitively trapped: he could see only what had already happened, the beach as it had been some hours before, so the living were invisible to him and the dead were his neighbors. [339] Speech reached him as nothing. [344] But debris stayed where it was put, and eventually became the past, and so he could read it. [362] That was the parley: two people negotiating across a six-hour lag with driftwood and vertebrae, the sun hammering the dunes white. [383] By noon the heat had turned the air to water, and she moved through the reverie of it, laying words she could not speak to a man she could not touch. [414] ONDINE MARSH, ARCHIVIST, she spelled in bottle glass. [422] I WANT THE KEY. [426] By afternoon his answer lay beside hers, kelp letters trembling in the wind: WHY. [440] She thought about lying. [444] The Verity's board wanted the diary decrypted for an anniversary exhibit, wanted Abel Cray restored to sainthood, the sixty-year rumor of the stolen drive fund finally scoured off his name. [474] But the letters, once read together, had a shape she disliked, and the shape had a name inside it. [493] So she laid the truth out in shells: THERE WAS AN INFORMANT, THEY SAY IT WAS YOU, I MUST KNOW EITHER WAY. [515] You learn, holding a movement's papers, that reverence and evidence are not friends, and that a truth withheld to protect the dead begins, in a generation, to protect the wrong people. [546] The lag came back at dusk. [552] Abel had spent his hours dragging driftwood into place, and the message ran forty feet along the tideline: NOT ME, MY BROTHER, HE DID NOT KNOW WHAT THEY WOULD DO. [582] Beneath it, smaller, in periwinkles: KEY IS THE THIRD HYMN, SUNG BACKWARD. [594] She stood in the searing red light with her notebook open and did not write for a long minute. [613] The brother's name was on a boulevard, a scholarship, a bronze plaque in the Verity's own archive room. [631] That was the tension, and the tension was where the truth had been hiding, as it always is. [649] Her last arrangement took until moonrise: I WILL PRINT ALL OF IT, YOUR BROTHER TOO, AND YOUR NAME ON THE SAME PAGE. [671] Then she rode the basket up into the cool of the library, where the decrypted pages would smell of glue and salt for years. [695] At dawn the Verity swung out over the dunes, and she went to the observation glass with her coffee going cold. [716] Below, the obsidian lagoon held the sky like a struck bell, and around it, in white stone and black wrack, Abel had written his last line. [742] TELL THEM I DID NOT RUN. [748] The tide was already coming for it. [755] Ondine wrote the words down, dated them, and filed them under evidence, which is the only kind of mourning she had ever been any good at. [781]
