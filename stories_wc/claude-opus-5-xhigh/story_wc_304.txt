The grotto hung nine miles above the tundra, a skyborne bubble of hollow stone that the old cartographers had refused to name. [22] Vessa Orme had named it anyway, in her own ledger: the Quiet Room. [35] She was a beautician mortician, the last one licensed to work above the cloud line, and she had painted four hundred faces beneath the aurora. [60] Tonight the aurora came in green ribbons that pulsed like slow breathing, and the grotto filled with a radiant hush, light without heat, sound without noise. [86] On the basalt table lay Ilke, who had taught her the trade. [98] Ilke had been dead for eleven minutes. [105] The dead, Ilke used to say, do not leave at once. [116] They pause at a threshold of stillness, a narrow interval between the last heartbeat and the true silence, and in that interval a person is still, in some fashion, lucidly watchful. [147] That was why the grotto existed: at this altitude, the aurora pressed close enough that the interval could be heard. [167] Vessa switched on the copper receiver and let the static pour out of its horn. [182] Static was the whole craft. [186] A dying mind, brushing against the charged air, printed itself on the hiss the way a thumb prints itself on glass, and the work was finding patterns in static: a cadence, a repeated stutter, a rhythm that did not belong to the sky. [229] Find the pattern, and you could infuse it into a stone. [240] Vessa opened her kit past the brushes and the tinted waxes, past the powders she used to give the dead the flush of an afternoon nap, and took out the emerald chip. [272] It was no bigger than a fingernail paring, flawed with a white cloud at its heart, and it had cost her nine years of wages. [297] Only emerald held a pattern; everything else let it drain away by morning. [310] She set the chip in the cradle at Ilke's temple and listened. [322] The hiss rose and fell, the aurora combing through it, and for a long while there was nothing but weather. [342] She was not trying to save Ilke; nobody could be saved from oblivion, and Ilke would have slapped her for trying. [363] She wanted only to capture one last spark before oblivion took it, one honest gesture of a mind, so that the grotto's shelves would hold, among four hundred strangers, the one person who had mattered. [398] At minute fourteen, the pattern came. [404] Three short bursts, a gap, three more: the rhythm Ilke had drummed on the table's edge whenever a student's hand shook. [425] Slow down. [427] Look. [428] Vessa's hands went steady, the way they always had under that drumming. [440] She turned the dial a hair and let the pattern pour into the chip. [454] The white cloud inside it dimmed, brightened, and settled into a faint pulse, three and three, patient as a metronome. [474] Then the hiss did something it had never done in four hundred nights. [487] It answered her. [490] The three-and-three broke off, hesitated, and returned doubled, not habit now but insistence, the drumming Ilke used when she wanted an answer instead of obedience. [515] Vessa looked up from the receiver to the woman on the table, and understood that the watching went both ways. [535] Eleven years she had lived in the Quiet Room, breathing filtered air, painting strangers, telling herself the altitude was necessary to the craft. [558] Ilke had come up twice a year and gone down again, and every time she left she had drummed on the doorframe, three and three, and Vessa had pretended not to hear. [590] A person could stand at a threshold of stillness for eleven years and call it a profession. [607] The signal thinned, frayed, and went back to being only weather. [618] Ilke was gone. [621] Vessa finished the work properly, because that was the last courtesy: the wax, the powder, the faint rose at the mouth, the hair combed back the way Ilke had worn it in the years before she stopped caring. [659] The aurora went on breathing above the grotto, green folding into violet, filling the stone bubble with that light that made every dead face look merely thoughtful. [686] She did not put the emerald chip on the shelf with the others. [699] She wound it into a wire and hung it at her throat, where it pulsed three and three against her collarbone, cool and stubborn and awake. [725] At dawn the supply gondola came, and for the first time in eleven years she got into it. [743] The grotto kept its radiant hush behind her, and she went down toward the noisy, unlovely, breathing world, lucidly watchful at last, carrying one small green spark that had refused oblivion.
