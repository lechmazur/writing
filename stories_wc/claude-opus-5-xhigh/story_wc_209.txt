The amber pavilion stood at the low end of the park, its glass panels the color of steeped tea, and every October it filled with leaves that nobody swept out. [30] Nesrin swept it out. [34] She had a commission for the pavilion's inner wall, forty feet of plaster the city wanted covered in something cheerful, and she was quietly driven in a way that made committees nervous. [66] She did not argue; she simply came back, morning after morning, with her ladder and her jars. [83] In her coat pocket she carried a collection of old postcards, rubber-banded, soft at the corners as cloth. [101] They had belonged to her grandmother Adile, who had spent fifty years as a night nurse and had collected postcards of cities she would never visit. [127] On the back of each, in pencil, Adile had written the name of a flower that opened after dark: moonflower, tuberose, evening primrose, night-blooming cereus. [152] The last card, from a city Nesrin had never heard of, said only: ask why they bother. [169] So the mural became a question instead of a decoration. [179] She wanted to understand why flowers bloom at night, and she had decided the wall would not be finished until she could answer. [202]

Autumn gave her the underpainting: rust, ochre, the pavilion's own amber light lying across wet plaster like syrup. [220] A botanist she telephoned told her about hawkmoths and bats, about scent conserved for cooler hours, and it was true and it was not enough. [245] Facts explained the mechanism; they did not explain Adile, who had loved such flowers because she worked when the rest of the city slept. [269]

In December the answer came sideways, as answers do. [278] A glazier named Toma, hired to reseal the pavilion panels, mentioned that he had spent his apprenticeship on the cathedral across the river. [301] The north window there, he said, was older than the church's records, and no one had ever fully read it. [321] Its maker had worked in stacked plates, and the design revealed itself only through the hidden layers of etched glass, each layer legible at a different angle of light. [350]

Nesrin went the next morning at five, and every morning after, across the melting of winter's last snow. [368] The nave was cold as a cellar and smelled of wax and thawing stone. [382] She learned the building's dawning hush, that ten-minute stillness when the night bells had stopped and the traffic had not begun, when even the pigeons in the clerestory held their breath. [413] In that hush the light came in low and slantwise, and the window began to give up its layers. [432] First a lily, ordinary enough. [437] Then, as the sun rose a degree, a second etching surfaced beneath it: a moth, wings open, its proboscis threading the flower's throat. [460] Then a third layer, faintest, only visible in the last minute before full daylight drowned it: a woman with a lamp, walking a garden path. [485]

Nesrin sat in the pew with the postcards in her lap and tried to reckon the thing plainly. [503] The window's maker had understood what the botanist could not say: that some things are made for a narrow audience. [523] A night flower does not bloom for the crowd at noon. [534] It blooms for the one creature awake to meet it, and it spends everything it has on that meeting. [553] Adile had been that creature, going ward to ward with her lamp, and no one had come at five in the morning to watch her do it. [580] That was the reckoning: the debt owed to unwitnessed work. [590]

By April the snow was gone from the park's north beds and Nesrin, who had always been called a vivid mural artist, painted the quietest wall of her life. [619] She kept the amber ground beneath, so the whole surface held a banked warmth, umbral and tender, like a room where someone has just turned down a lamp. [647] Over it she laid a garden in three depths, the way the glazier's ancestor had: flowers at the surface, moths beneath, and far back, barely there, a woman walking with a light. [679]

The committee came at noon and found it dim and disappointing. [690] Nesrin only asked them to return before sunrise. [698] Two of them did. [702] In the pavilion's low light, in the hush before the city started, the amber caught and the third depth rose out of the wall like breath on glass. [730] Nesrin stood with the postcards in her pocket and did not explain. [742] Some blooms only want one witness, and she had learned how to be one. [756]
