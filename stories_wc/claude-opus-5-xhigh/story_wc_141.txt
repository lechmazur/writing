I debunk things for a living, which is a cheerful job if you enjoy funerals. [15] Ghost lights, weeping icons, wells that cure gout: I arrive, I measure, I write the small sad true thing in my ledger, and everyone hates me by Tuesday. [43] The Institute's word for it is myth buster. [51] The last entry was supposed to be the ephemeral compass. [61] Fishermen at Craighalt swore that once in a person's life, if they asked the sea a question worth its salt, a compass would form in the air for the length of one breath and point at the answer. [99] Then it was gone, and you could never ask twice. [109] Halloran believed it. [112] I told him he was a romantic with a barometer, and he told me I was a woman who mistook a locked door for proof there was nothing behind it. [142] I have reimagined that conversation eleven hundred times since he died, and in most versions I am kinder, and in a few I am so kind that he stays. [171] The observatory on the cliff had been abandoned since the war, its dome cracked open like an egg somebody gave up on. [193] Gulls nested in the telescope mount. [199] Rain came sideways off the water and the whole ruin smelled of iron and kelp, and I whistled while I climbed, because gloom is best carried at a jaunty angle. [229] Halloran had spent his last winter here, and he had left two things. [242] The first was a cello he never finished: ribs bent, neck fitted, no varnish, no strings, a body with a mouth and nothing to say. [267] The second was a seashell, painted. [273] He had lacquered it white and lettered it in his awful draftsman's hand: WHAT POINTS EVERYWHERE AND HAPPENS ONCE? [292] He called it the universal riddle, and he meant it: he thought every myth in my ledger was one clumsy retelling of that single question, and that if he could decode it he would have the grammar of everything. [331] I had laughed at him. [336] Now I sat on the floor of a dead observatory with his notebook on my knees, reading his last page by the light of a storm. [362] It said: Do not measure it, retell it, out loud, to the wood. [375] So I closed the notebook, and inside the whisper of those closing pages, that small papery hush which is the sound of a person finishing, I heard the cello hum. [405] Unstrung wood should not hum. [410] I laughed, delighted and miserable, and did the unscientific thing. [420] I put my mouth near the open sound hole and retold the story the fishermen told me, all of it, badly, with the wind in it. [446] The half-finished body took my voice and gave it back as resonant hums, layered, as if three of me were agreeing. [467] Dust lifted off the floorboards in the hum and stood up in a ring. [481] Salt grains, gull down, ash: a compass rose, hanging in the air of a room where men once mapped the sky, turning slowly to find its north. [508] I had one breath, and one question, and I had spent nine years deciding I did not believe in either. [528] Every reimagined version of our last argument crowded my throat, wanting to be asked: did you forgive me, were you right, would you have stayed. [553] I asked his riddle instead, because it was his, and because a debunker should at least test the claim as stated. [574] "What points everywhere and happens once?" [580] The dust compass swung, and every needle of it, because it had many, pointed at me. [596] Then the hum thinned, the ring fell, and the floor was only a floor with grit on it. [614] I sat there a long while, cheerfully wretched, rain coming through the cracked dome onto my boots. [631] Here is my ledger entry, and it will get me fired. [642] The compass is real and it is useless, which is the joke Halloran spent nine years trying to tell me. [663] It points everywhere at once because the answer to a universal riddle is not a direction, it is a witness: a thing that happens once, that cannot be measured twice, that is only ever available to whoever is standing there breathing. [704] A life, in other words. [709] So I have stopped rewriting our last argument into kindness; he does not need my better lines, and I would rather keep the real ones, sharp as they were. [738] I took the painted seashell home, and the cello too. [748] I am learning varnish, badly. [753] When it is finished I will string it and retell this whole gloomy delightful business to anyone who asks: the myth is true, you get one, and I used mine on a dead man's question. [788] I do not regret it, which is the cheerful part. [798]
