The greenhouse had been rusting since before the war, and Ilse Varga liked it that way. [16] Rust let the light in sideways, through holes where panes had fallen, and things grew crooked and honest under it. [36] She had one rule, which she told anyone who ducked through the gap in the wall: never trust a cook who has nothing to be sorry about. [63] Ilse tasted memories. [66] Not metaphorically; a carrot pulled from a dead man's plot carried the ache of his knees, and bread from the Commission bakeries tasted of nothing at all, which was the point. [97] The Commission had smoothed the city after the war, sanding grief off people the way you sand a splinter off a rail. [119] Her neighbors could not remember their own dead, and so they were calm, and so they were easy, and so they were less. [142]

Ilse had kept the seed for nineteen years in a tin that once held saffron. [157] The old orchardist who gave it to her called it a knowledge fruit seed and said it would not sprout for a keeper who wanted power from it, only for one who wanted breakfast. [191] He had been informally wise in the way of men who fix things with wire, and Ilse had inherited both the seed and the habit. [216] She planted it the spring the astronomers announced the alignment: seven bodies strung along one line, a thing the sky would not do again for eleven hundred years. [244]

The tree grew fast and wrong, throwing out sixty small green fruits like a nervous host offering too much. [263] On the last morning Ilse took her shears and began to prune. [275] Tomas, who swept the ruins for scrap and had adopted her, watched her cut away branch after loaded branch. [294] "You're throwing away food," he said. [300] "I'm throwing away sixty mediocre things to get one good one," she said. [313] "That's all cooking is, and most of living." [321] By dusk one fruit remained, hanging low and heavy, and the sugar of the whole tree ran into it. [340]

The orchardist had explained the fruit's trick once, over bad coffee: it worked through synaptic plasticity, prying open in an adult brain the soft window that closes at seven, so that what had been sanded away could be laid down again. [381] It did not install memories; it only made the mind able to hold what it had once refused. [399] The rest was the cook's problem. [405]

Ilse could have made a weapon of it. [413] She thought about it while the planets climbed: one fruit, ground fine, dropped into the Commission's reservoir; a whole city clawing at its own history in a single night; riots, and the smug satisfaction of being right. [450] Instead she made soup. [454] She had been salvaging the ruins all year for it: onions from the cratered allotments, thyme from a windowbox that had outlived its house, bones from a butcher who did not ask questions. [487] She tasted as she went, as she always did, and the pot filled with other people's lives: a wedding in the allotment soil, a boy's fever, a woman singing to a stove. [519] She grated the fruit in last, when the alignment stood overhead and the greenhouse rust glowed like a struck match. [539]

Then she carried the pot out to the yard, where thirty neighbors had come because she had told them there would be free dinner, which was true. [566] She told them the rest too, standing on a crate with the ladle. [579] "This will give you back your dead," she said. [588] "Not tonight, and not all at once, but over months, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth." [606] "You may eat or not eat." [612] "There's bread if you would rather." [618] Nine walked away. [621] Twenty-one held out their bowls, and Tomas went first, and no window broke anywhere in the city that night. [640] That was the whole rebellion: soup, in a yard, under a sky that would never look that way again. [659]

Ilse ate last and tasted everything at once -- the sanded years, the smoothed griefs, her own mother's hands -- and she sat down hard on the crate and wept for a while, and then she scraped the pot, because waste is waste. [700] In the morning she went back to the rusted greenhouse and looked at the pruned tree, ugly and bare and alive, and began planning the next thing she would grow out of the ruins. [734] Someone had to make something beautiful here, and it was not going to be the Commission. [750]
