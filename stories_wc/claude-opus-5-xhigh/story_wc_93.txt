Friction left the world on a Tuesday, and by Thursday nobody could hold anything, not even a promise. [18] Knots slid loose, wheels spun without moving, and the sea went glassy and mean, sliding up the cliffs like oil. [38] Dust never settled anymore, so the sun stayed swaddled in a gray veil, and our only morning was the aurora - a green, borrowed dawn. [62] Maren Sill had herded eels in the tidal channels below our bluff for thirty years, and she was the only one who did not seem surprised. [88] That was because she was precognitively cursed, which in her family was described plainly, the way others might mention a limp. [109] She saw what was coming and could never alter it; she had known about the friction for eleven years and had spent them painting rocks. [134] She also knew my mother's death, and had promised her, at the end, to teach me the patterns - a promise that had lingered between us like smoke ever since, never quite delivered, never withdrawn. [168] So when the eels vanished from the channels, I went looking for her. [181] Finding Maren was famously difficult - she moved like her animals, always in the seam between water and rock - but the aurora that night lit the whole tidal bluff, and I saw her shadow bent over the stones. [218] The bluff was covered in painted rocks, hundreds of them, ochre and white and cobalt, arranged in careful lines that ran down to the waterline. [243] "One, one, two, three, five," she said without looking up, "then the primes, then the doublings - say them back to me." [264] I did, badly, and she moved three stones a hand's width to the left. [278] The rocks did not slide, though nothing else on that slope would stay put. [292] Under each one, she told me, was a grain of collapsed star dust, sifted from the crater out past the shoals, so heavy that a thimble of it weighed as much as a boat. [326] It was the only thing left that gripped the world. [336] "The patterns are older than me," she said. "My grandmother's grandmother laid them in the mud for the eels. I only ever wanted to understand why the sacred ones work." [366] Eels, she explained, do not swim in straight lines; they follow spirals and intervals, and a herder who lays the right sequence can turn a whole run of them the way a fence turns sheep. [401] Now the eels were gone, spun out into the frictionless dark, and she was not herding eels. [418] She was herding the aurora. [423] The green light came down where the star dust called it, and the stones caught it, and along the sequence I could feel something like grit returning to the air, a roughness, a hold. [457] "You can harness it," I said. "Grip, out of light." [467] "One person cannot," she said. "The line has to run from the high stone to the sea, and the sea takes the low stones every six hours." [494] "Someone must stand in the water and set them again, and again, and know the order without thinking." [512] Then she looked at me for the first time, and I understood that she had seen this conversation eleven years ago, and had promised my mother because she already knew my hands would be in that water. [549] The curse had never let her choose; it had only let her prepare. [562] I waded in up to my thighs, where the glassy water slid off my skin like it had somewhere better to be, and I began setting stones: one, one, two, three, five. [594] By the third tide my fingers knew the order better than my mouth did, and the roughness had spread from the bluff to the shoals, and a single eel came back, feeling its way along the spiral like a finger down a scar. [637] Maren watched from the high stone, where she had set the last grain of dust, and she did not tell me what came next, and I did not ask. [666] Above us the borrowed dawn hung green and patient, lighting a pattern older than either of us, and for the first time I thought I could see the shape of why it was sacred: not because it was beautiful, but because it held. [709] The dust never settled, but we did. [716]
