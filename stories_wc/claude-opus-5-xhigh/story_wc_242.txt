The booth stood where no booth should: forty feet under Delaney Street, in a utility tunnel that smelled of ozone and wet iron. [23] Mirem had inherited it the way other people inherit debt. [33] Track crews and night people called her the operator, though there was no switchboard down there -- only one booth, one line, and Mirem deciding who got the window. [61]

Every night she unlocked the folding door, sat on the milk crate, and waited for the window between the hum of streetlamps. [83] The city's grid cycled at 2:14, and for ninety seconds the lamps above went silent -- no ballast buzz, no filament whine -- and in that silence the old copper lines beneath the platform would loop. [117] A call placed then did not go where it was dialed. [128] It went somewhere adjacent, somewhere wrong, and a stranger picked up. [139] Mirem's mother had called this a defect. [146] Mirem called it a practice. [151]

On the shelf above the coin return lay a glass harmonica disc, the largest from her mother's broken instrument, rimmed with dried gold paint. [175] Wet a fingertip, ride the edge, and it gave a tone that sat under the skull rather than in the ear. [196] The tunnel was melodically haunted because of it; the tone hung in the tile curve for minutes, thinning, refusing to end. [217] Her mother had ended that way as well, mid-sentence, on a Tuesday, without warning anyone how to begin the ending. [237]

So Mirem dialed wrong numbers on purpose. [244] She had rules. [247] Nine calls to a stranger, no more. [254] On the first she apologized for the wrong number; by the fourth they were telling her things they had never told the people who slept beside them; on the ninth she told them, plainly, that she would not call again, and why. [296] This was cultivated estrangement, deliberate as a bonsai: intimacy grown fast, pruned on schedule, so that the parting could be handled with both hands. [320] She was teaching endings how to begin. [327] The strangers, she believed, learned it too. [334]

The trouble arrived in February, when the loop delivered her to the same man three separate times, on three separate numbers. [355] His name was Osric; he drove a salt truck; he had a laugh like a dropped wrench. [372] "You again," he said, the third time. [379] "I think your machine likes me." [385]

She should have ended it at nine. [392] She reached nine and told him the rule and he listened all the way through, and then asked, gently, who had taught her that leaving needed rehearsing. [419] Mirem hung up and ran the disc until the tunnel sang. [430] The tone found the tiles and multiplied and would not stop, and she understood that she had been keeping her mother alive in the worst way -- as a sound that never resolved, a call that never got its ending, only its interruption. [472] Nine calls was not a lesson. [478] It was a loop with better manners. [485]

On the tenth night she came down early, before the trains stopped, and sat with the disc in her lap while the tunnel ticked and cooled. [511] At 2:14 the lamps overhead went quiet, and forty feet down she felt the absence like a held breath. [530] She dialed the salt depot on Ninth, correctly, the way anyone would. [542] The loop took it anyway -- it takes everything in that window -- and carried it somewhere adjacent, and Osric answered, because by then the machine did like him. [569]

"Tenth call," he said. [573] "You broke your own rule." [578]

"My name is Mirem Vais," she said. [585] "There's a phone booth under Delaney Street, in the utility tunnel behind the electrical closet, and the door is unlocked at two in the morning." [610]

That was the whole of it: an ending given a beginning, the introduction she had skipped nine times over. [629] Then the ninety seconds closed, the streetlamps took up their hum again, and the line went to dial tone. [648] Mirem set the glass harmonica disc on the booth's little shelf, wet her finger one last time, and drew a single tone, and did not wait for it to fade. [678] She climbed the maintenance ladder into a street that smelled of salt and February. [692] Somewhere behind her the tone was still going, thinning, learning at last how to stop; somewhere ahead a truck was turning down Delaney with its yellow light going around and around, and she stood under the lamps and let it find her.
