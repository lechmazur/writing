The Golden Arcade woke the way old things do, slowly and with a rattle, its glass roof turning from ash to honey while the sweeper-drones went quiet for the dawn interval. [31] Mira Vann had ninety minutes before the letter in her coat had to be mailed, and she intended to spend every one of them being careful. [57] For eleven years she had been a pseudonym creator, licensed, bonded, the last one working the Arcade. [74] People still wrote surface mail -- paper, stamps, the brass slot at the Arcade's east end -- precisely because paper could not be indexed, and paper needed a name on it that was not yours. [107] Mira supplied the names. [111] She kept them on an ebony clipboard, black as a piano lid, its spring clip biting a hundred index cards: Orville Teak, Susanna Grieve, Halcyon Pratt, Mr. B. Almondine. [140] A good pseudonym had to be carefully inventive: plausible enough for a postal clerk, strange enough that no living person would ever answer to it and be dragged into a stranger's trouble. [172] She had been proud of that craft once. [179] Then, four winters ago, her brother Ash walked out of a hearing room and out of the ledgers entirely, and letters began arriving at her flat signed Halcyon Pratt. [208] Halcyon Pratt was hers, card thirty-one, invented on a bored Tuesday and never sold to anyone. [224] The letters themselves were dull: weather, recipes, a joke about turnips. [235] The margins were not. [239] Pencil scratches ran up the edges of every page, tiny groups of ticks and loops, and Mira had spent four years trying to discover meaning in the margins the way other people pray. [272] The answer arrived, as answers do, from children. [279] Every morning the Arcade's caretaker kids chalked the flagstones under the golden roof -- hopscotch grids, spirals, lopsided suns -- and every evening the cleaning mist took it all away. [307] Three weeks ago Mira had knelt to tie her boot and noticed that one hopscotch grid was numbered wrong: 1, 2, 4, 8, then a gap, then 31. [335] Card thirty-one. [337] She had begun to juxtapose the two things every dawn, laying a letter flat on her ebony clipboard beside the chalk, margins against numbers, until the grammar surfaced. [365] Codes in children's sidewalk chalk: the ticks in the margins told you which chalk figure to read, and the chalk told you which tick meant which letter. [392] Neither half survived alone; neither half could be seized. [401] It was the most beautiful thing her brother had ever built, and he had built it out of turnip jokes and a nine-year-old's blue chalk. [426] This dawn the honey light came through the glass and found today's grid already drawn near the brass slot, and Mira crouched with her clipboard and worked. [453] The message took forty minutes and said: STILL HERE. STOP SENDING. THEY READ MARGINS NOW. [468] In her coat was her reply, written last night on paper she had aged over a kettle, addressed to a boarding house in the flooded quarter, signed Susanna Grieve. [497] Its margins were crowded with ticks that meant: I found you, I am coming Thursday, wait. [513] The brass slot was nine steps away, and the collection drone came at seven. [527] She stood there a long while in the amber quiet, holding the two halves of her life up beside each other: the woman who invented names so that people could disappear safely, and the sister who wanted one person to stop disappearing. [569] Then she knelt, took the stub of blue chalk the children left in the planter, and drew a hopscotch grid of her own beside theirs. [594] Squares one, two, four, eight, then a gap, then thirty-one, and in the margin of the last square, small enough to be nothing, four ticks meaning WAIT. [621] She tore her letter into eight pieces, dropped them into the planter's soil, and mailed nothing at all. [639] The cleaning mist would take her chalk by evening, and the children would copy it tomorrow without knowing why, and somewhere in the flooded quarter a man reading sidewalks would learn that his sister had understood him. [676] At seven the drone came, found the slot empty, and hummed away over the golden roof. [692] Mira clipped a fresh index card to the ebony board and began, carefully, inventively, a new name for a woman who would need one on Thursday. [718]
