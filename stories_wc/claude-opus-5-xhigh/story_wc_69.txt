The velvet library smelled of wet leaves and old glue, and Mirela Antic unlocked it every Thursday in October, expectantly resigned to whoever did or did not come. [28] The curtains were the color of dried plums, the chairs still wore their green upholstery, and the whole room held what she privately called traveled stillness: the hush of a place that had been shelled, patched, and forgiven. [66] She had been the town's choir teacher for thirty-one years, a harmonious woman by temperament and by stubbornness, which meant she had taught children who later refused to sing with each other's children. [99] Her whole working life had unfolded across unlearning hatred, and the unlearning was slower than any curriculum. [116]

On the reading table lay the zither her grandmother had smuggled out of a burning village in a wheat sack. [136] It was ugly, honestly, water-stained and warped, except for the zither soundhole rosette: a carved wooden wheel of interlocking letters, sixteen of them, cut by a maker whose family name nobody could now pronounce. [170] The letters did not spell a word; they were meant to denote intervals, a tuning notation from an art that had almost finished vanishing. [194] Mirela had spent eleven autumns in this library reconstructing the method, the way other people rescue vanishing arts: from letters, marriage records, and one cracked wax cylinder. [221] The old singers had not tuned by adding a drone or a piano; they tuned by subtracting noise. [239] You sang a chord, then removed whatever roughened it: a pressed jaw, a hurried breath, a voice shoving itself above its neighbor, until only the ringing remained. [266] The rosette's sixteen letters named sixteen kinds of noise to take away. [278] That was the whole art, and it was the only pedagogy she had ever found that did what she wanted, which was to braid diverse voices into harmony without asking any of them to become quieter versions of someone else. [318]

Seven people came that Thursday. [322] Three were Halic women from the east bank, two were Dorvan men from the mill road, one was a boy who came for the radiator heat, and one was Emin Halic, who had lost a brother in the summer of the roadblocks and had not spoken to a Dorvan in nineteen years. [374] Mirela did not mention any of that. [380] She set the zither on her knees, plucked one string, and let it die out completely before she said anything. [400]

"Sing the vowel you use when you are tired," she said. [411] They did, badly, seven separate tirednesses colliding. [418] She held up one finger and touched the first letter on the rosette. [431] "Take away the pushing," she said. [437] The chord thinned, then steadied. [442] "Now the listening-for-mistakes." [445] "Now the wanting to be finished." [451]

By the ninth subtraction the seven voices had stopped being seven. [462] Emin's voice was still unmistakably Emin's, dark and slightly nasal, and the miller's son still sang like a farm gate, but the sound in the room had gone smooth and enormous, and the dried-plum curtains buzzed faintly against the window glass. [503] Nobody moved when it ended. [508]

"What was the tenth one," Emin asked finally, not looking at anyone. [520] Mirela turned the instrument so the lamplight went through the rosette and threw sixteen bright letters onto the table. [539] "The tenth is the noise of the story you tell yourself about the man beside you," she said. [557] "It is the loudest one, and it takes the longest." [567]

Emin sat with that. [571] Then he asked whether they would meet again next Thursday, and the miller's son said he could bring his aunt, who had a low voice like a cellar. [599]

Mirela wrote the sixteen letters on the library slate before she left, badly, from memory, because the rosette was walnut and walnut does not last forever. [625] She was sixty-four; she had perhaps a dozen autumns left to hand the thing over, and she had learned not to expect the town to hurry. [651] Still, walking home through the leaves, she counted seven voices instead of five, and revised her arithmetic upward. [669] Vanishing is not a single event; it is a rate, and rates can be changed. [684] Behind her the library windows went dark one by one, holding their velvet, their old glue, and their patient quiet. [704] In her coat pocket, folded small, was the list of sixteen subtractions, and she had already decided that Emin Halic, who could hear the tenth one, would be the person she taught to read it.
