The commission came in October, when the first monarchs drifted through the valley like sparks off a slow fire. [19] Thirty-one names, one stone. [23] The council could not agree on a shape, so they handed the problem to Ilse Brandt, the village gravestone carver, who was thoughtful in the way of people who spend their lives spelling other people's last words correctly. [61] The bridge had gone down in August with a busload of harvest workers on it, and grief had settled over the valley like sediment. [85] What the council wanted, though nobody would say it, was for one woman to discover the shape of collective grief and then cut it into rock. [111]

Ilse worked in the ancient greenhouse behind the churchyard, because the light there at dusk turned stone the color of skin. [132] Its iron ribs were older than the church; its glass was patched with mica and hope. [148] Monarchs had used it as a waystation for as long as anyone could remember, and they came in through the broken pane above the door and hung on the dead grapevine in orange shingles. [182]

Her grandmother Bettine had carved there for fifty years, and had been dead for three. [197] She still came at dusk. [202] Ilse did not find this strange, only inconvenient, since Bettine had opinions. [214]

"You are carving a list," the ghost said the first evening, standing where the light thinned. [230] "A list is not a shape." [236]

"Then tell me the shape," Ilse said. [243]

"I never had thirty-one at once," Bettine said, "so you will have to find it, and I will tell you when you are wrong." [267]

That was the whole of the spectral mentorship: no answers, only corrections, delivered in a voice like a hand on the back of the neck. [292]

So Ilse went to the families for supper, one house each evening, all across the butterfly migration. [309] The Brandts had always traded favorite poems at dinner, a custom her grandmother had enforced with a wooden spoon, and Ilse carried it into other people's kitchens like a lantern. [339] You give a poem, you get a poem; that is the trade, and nobody has to speak plainly about the bridge. [360] The Kessler boys offered a hymn about wheat. [367] Old Marta Voss recited nine lines of Rilke and then corrected her own translation twice. [382] A widower named Ferenc said only a couplet his wife had used to wake him: the river is early, the bread is late. [405]

Ilse wrote nothing down. [409] Instead she brought her woven ribbon roll, the one Bettine had used for sixty years to measure the length of a name against the width of a stone. [437] At each table she unrolled a hand's length of it and let the family tie a knot where their poem ended. [458] By the end of the migration the ribbon held thirty-one knots and hung heavy as a rosary. [475]

In the greenhouse she coiled it on the bench and looked at it for four evenings, fiercely meditative, refusing food, refusing the vicar, refusing her own hands. [502]

"You are waiting for it to speak," Bettine observed. [511] "It is a ribbon." [515]

"It is thirty-one people's held breath," Ilse said, and did not look up. [528]

On the fifth evening the monarchs left. [534] They did not go all at once; they went in a long uneven thread, lifting off the grapevine, spiraling once around the greenhouse's warm middle, then out through the broken pane, south. [566] Ilse stood in the middle of it with her arms down. [577] The sound was enormous and there was no violence in it at all, a thundering calm, thousands of small departures agreeing on one direction. [601]

That was the shape. [605]

She cut a single slab, low and long, and carved into it not a list but a spiral: the thirty-one names running outward from the center, each spaced by the knots on the ribbon, so that a mourner had to walk around the stone to retrace the whole turn. [654] Between the names she set the poems' last lines, cut small, so that fingers found them before eyes did. [673] Ferenc's couplet fell at the outermost curve, where the spiral opened and stopped pretending to close. [689]

"Too clever," said Bettine on the last night, and then, after a long while, "no, it is right." [707] She did not come back after that, which Ilse had expected, and mourned anyway, quietly, in the proper order. [726]

In spring the monarchs' children came north again and hung on the grapevine, and the village came to the churchyard to walk the spiral, and Ilse kept the ribbon roll on the bench where the light could reach it, thirty-one knots, unmeasured now, and enough. [771]
