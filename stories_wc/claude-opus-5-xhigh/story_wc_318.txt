They called Odile Vance the leaf blower symphony conductor, and she did not correct them, because a title is a kind of promise. [23] Her orchestra was four men from the county grounds crew, mournfully eager, holding their machines like cellos they were afraid to bow. [45] Her concert hall was the echo chamber tunnel beneath Halloway Road, a storm culvert wide enough to walk in, curved so that a whisper at one mouth arrived at the other as a chord. [79] Above its far end, through a rusted grate, sat the Kessler house. [91] The Kesslers had not laughed in a year. [99] Odile knew the exact length of that year, because Ruth Kessler had told her about the hourglass: a tall one, blown for a ship's chandler, that took twelve months to empty and had been turned on the morning they buried Wren. [140] Mourning, Ruth said, was supposed to end after the final grain slipped through the hourglass. [155] The last grain had fallen on Tuesday, and the house had gone quieter still, as though the silence had been waiting for permission to become permanent. [181] So Odile came on Wednesday with rakes, barrels, and a plan she could not say out loud. [198]

First the tuning. [201] They gathered wet oak leaves by the barrowful and let them macerate three days in rainwater until the blades went soft as wet paper. [225] Then they pressed the pulp along the tunnel's north wall in patches, thick here, thin there, because Odile had learned that a concrete throat sings too many notes at once and must be muffled selectively, the way you damp a piano string with a fingertip. [270] By Saturday the tunnel held only one register: the low, breathy middle of a woman's humming voice. [287]

The blowers were old two-strokes, and one of them, the smallest, had a pull cord handle worn into a shallow hollow by a child's grip. [312] Wren Kessler had spent two autumns helping the crew, mostly starting engines, because he loved the moment the machine caught. [332] Odile had kept the machine. [337] She had never been able to give it back, and she had never been able to explain why she had it. [358] That was the work, she thought: tending to the unspoken, keeping it fed and swept until someone was strong enough to hear it. [381]

Sunday at dusk they took their places at the tunnel's mouth. [392] Odile lifted both hands. [396] The men did not play music; they leaned into their intakes and half-sang the lullabies their own mothers had used, and the engines chewed those fragments up and pushed them out as an off-key wind that traveled the curved concrete, gathered the damped walls' single register, and rose through the grate into Ruth Kessler's kitchen. [451] It did not sound like a boy. [458] It sounded like something older and patient standing just outside the window, breathing in a key no living throat could hold. [479]

Ruth came out onto the porch with a dish towel in her fist, and her husband came behind her, and neither of them spoke. [503] Odile signaled the smallest machine forward. [509] She set the pull cord handle in Ruth's hand. [518] "He always started them," Odile said. [524] "I have been keeping his sound for you." [532]

Ruth pulled. [534] The engine caught on the second try, coughed, then settled, and the tunnel took the noise and returned it soft, and the wind that came up through the grate carried her own voice, because she was humming without knowing it, the same four notes she had used over a crib eleven years earlier. [587]

Her husband laughed. [590] It was a terrible laugh, cracked and wet, more like a hinge than a sound of joy, but it was the first one, and the tunnel repeated it four times, each fainter, as if the house's old laughter were walking back up the road from a long way off. [639]

Odile lowered her hands and let the engines die one by one. [651] In the morning she would scrape the macerated leaves off the wall and the tunnel would go back to being ordinary. [672] That was necessary. [675] A thing like this must not be kept, only given, and then the keeper must let go of it. [694] She left the smallest machine on the porch steps with its cord coiled neatly, its handle facing the door, waiting for whoever needed to hear the wind next. [722] Behind her, in the tunnel, something hummed the last four notes back, and Odile, walking home in the dark, did not turn around. [745]
