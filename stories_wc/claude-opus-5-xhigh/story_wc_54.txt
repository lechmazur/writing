The carnival left on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the field behind the spaceport was only mud, ticket stubs, and the ghost-rings where the tents had stood. [27] Mira Oyelaran arrived on Thursday with a clipboard, a laser measure, and instructions to certify the passenger terminal as unsalvageable. [47] She was three weeks into an internship that everyone told her was a filing job with boots. [64] The terminal had been closed for nineteen years, since the last shuttle lifted for the outer stations and never scheduled a return. [86] Pigeons owned the departure boards. [91] Mira did not find it sad, exactly; she found it unfinished, which was worse and better at once. [109]

In the dry basin of the concourse fountain she found the sextant piece. [122] It was a brass arc, an index limb snapped from an explorer's sextant, the graduations still crisp, the maker's stamp reading KOWALSKI, SURVEY OF THE THIRD APPROACH. [149] Captain Ada Kowalski had used it to bring the first colony barge down through the dust, and the town had mounted it in the fountain's plinth as a promise. [178] Someone had pried it loose; the carnival's midway had run four hundred meters from the terminal doors. [195] Mira put the brass arc in her jacket pocket and kept surveying, because that was the job. [212]

The job, however, kept turning into a puzzle. [220] The terminal's plumbing made no engineering sense. [227] Storm drains that should have run straight to the flats instead looped under the waiting hall in long, patient switchbacks. [247] Greywater from the old cafeterias fed a cistern that fed nothing. [258] Three days of tracing pipes with chalk convinced her that she was standing inside a maze of intentions, and that every wrong turn in it had been deliberate. [286]

In the mechanical archive she found the reason, in a binder swollen with damp. [300] The terminal's architect had been Kowalski's daughter. [307] She had designed the building to run a beacon on nothing but weather: rain and runoff collected, sent through redirected flows down the switchbacks, spinning a turbine no larger than a barrel, lighting a lamp on the control tower that could be seen from the highway and, on clear nights, from orbit. [359] The margin note was in pencil: For travelers who have not yet decided to come home. [375]

Mira sat on the floor with the sextant piece in her hand and did arithmetic. [390] The rains had failed for six years; that was why the beacon was dark, why the terminal was dead, why the town's young people took the highway out and did not look back. [423] But the carnival had left something behind besides mud. [432] To fill its water slide and its cotton-candy stalls, it had tapped the municipal main and cut a diversion trench across the field, and nobody had bothered to close it. [462] Fourteen thousand liters a day of irrigation overflow was running out onto the flats to evaporate. [478] It was not rain. [482] It was, Mira thought, close enough to rain to argue with. [493]

Her supervisor, Okonjo, said the word she expected: unsalvageable. [502] Then he read her four pages of calculations, sighed, and said she could have the weekend and whatever was in the yard. [524] What was in the yard was culvert pipe, a broken sluice gate, and the carnival's abandoned pump skid. [542] Mira had always been functionally creative rather than beautifully so; she built things that worked and looked like what they were. [563] By Saturday dusk she had married the trench to the storm inlet with sixty meters of culvert, shimmed the sluice with the sextant's brass arc because its curve fit the gate seat exactly, and coaxed the switchbacks full. [601]

Water moved under the waiting hall for the first time in nineteen years, a sound like a crowd arriving. [620] The turbine complained, then turned. [625] At 8:14 the tower lamp came up amber, steadied, and held. [636] Mira stood in the mud and watched it with her hands in her pockets, feeling no triumph, only the clean wonder of a thing obeying its own old logic again. [666]

Cars slowed on the highway. [671] By ten there were forty people in the concourse, and Mr. Adeyemi was telling anyone who would listen that he had sold tickets under that lamp. [697] Nobody had resurrected a spaceport; a beacon is not a shuttle, and Mira said so plainly when they asked. [716] What she had done was prove a number: that a dying building could still pay for its own light, and that the maze under it had been built by someone who expected the future to need directions. [753]

On Monday she filed her report, recommending survey, not demolition. [763] Then she began the next set of drawings, because there were other dead flows in that town and she wanted to puzzle out new equations for hope while the water was still arguing in her favor. [799]
