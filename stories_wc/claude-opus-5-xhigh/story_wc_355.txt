Ilsa Vantree kept her workshop on the hill where the shadows of the pines fell across the roof and the stars did the rest of the lighting. [27] The workshop inside was eclectic to the point of comedy: harmoniums, seed jars, tuning forks in a boot, a Buddhist bell beside a broken metronome. [52] She was a pacifist of the theatrical sort: in sixty years she had never struck a living thing, and she said so often, both hands pressed to her heart, as though confessing to an audience of one thousand. [90] Tonight her audience was her grandnephew Teo, fourteen, holding a lantern he had been told not to light. [108] "Listen," she said, "because in eleven minutes the fireworks ignite, and the finest silence of the year will be gone forever." [129] For thirty years Ilsa had worked to catalog the varieties of silence, and she had eighty-one of them, etched on wax cylinders and labeled in her small slanted hand. [158] She was intuitively descriptive, naming them the way other people breathe: the hush of a dog deciding whether to trust you, the pause inside an apology, the flat quiet of a room where someone has just stopped shouting. [196] Teo thought the names were showing off. [203] Ilsa insisted they were merely accurate. [209] On the bench between them lay her brother's diary, water-stained, no bigger than a hand, its locked clasp a blackened knuckle of brass. [232] Anton had carried it through a war she had begged him not to enter, and had come home quiet, and had died quiet, and had left the key nowhere. [261] "You could pry it with a screwdriver," Teo said, for the ninth time that week. [276] "I could," Ilsa agreed, "and I would learn what he wrote, and I would learn nothing about him at all." [296] She had built the machine instead. [302] It stood on three legs like a heron: a copper horn, a coil of hair-thin wire, and a dial she had scavenged from a wrecked radio, because a lock, she argued, is only a shape holding a note, and every note can be found by tuning frequencies until the metal agrees to let go. [356] Force broke things; resonance persuaded them. [362] It was, she said, the only politics she had ever trusted. [373] But the workshop was never quiet enough to hear the clasp answer. [385] Wind, moths, the town, the road. [391] Only once a year, in the final stillness before the fireworks ignite, did the whole valley hold its breath at the same instant: shutters closed, dogs indoors, four thousand people looking up and saying nothing. [426] Silence number eighty-two, unrecorded, and the only one strong enough to let her recover her brother's voice. [443] "Now," she whispered. [446] The valley emptied of sound. [451] Ilsa turned the dial with a slowness that was almost unbearable, and the coil began to sing, so faintly that Teo felt it in his teeth before he heard it. [481] Higher, higher, and then the brass clasp shivered, hummed, and opened like a small mouth. [496] Ilsa did not touch the pages yet. [503] She had rehearsed this moment for years, the way an actor rehearses a death, and she found now that the theater of it had drained away, leaving only an old woman with cold hands. [537] The diary held no battles. [542] Anton had also been a cataloger, in his fashion. [551] Page after page listed silences: the silence after the guns before anyone dared move, the silence of a boy holding a photograph, the silence of a village that had learned not to answer questions. [585] Eighty-one of them. [588] And on the last page, in handwriting that leaned exactly like hers: "Ilsa would have names for all of these." [608] The first firework went up and broke the stillness into gold. [619] Teo, lantern forgotten, saw his great-aunt's face and understood that she was not crying over the words but over the thirty years she had spent building a machine to reach a man who had been walking toward her the entire time. [660] "The path to empathy," she said, when she could speak, and she said it grandly, because grandness was how she survived things, "is never a shortcut, Teo, and it is never a crowbar." [693] "It is a frequency you must hunt for until the other person opens." [706] Then, more quietly, in a voice with no theater in it at all: "He heard me." [722] Above them the sky cracked and cracked again, and the eighty-second silence was lost for another year. [739] Ilsa did not mind. [743] She had eighty-one of his, and she intended to spend what was left of her life giving each one its name. [764]
