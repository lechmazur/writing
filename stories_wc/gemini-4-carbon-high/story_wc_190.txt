The iron mountain locomotive backed into its granite tunnel and loosed one final blast before vanishing down the grade.  High above Cloudwater Gorge, everything waited in the quiet that follows a train whistle's echo, wrapped within a vaulting hush that stretched from the valley mist to the pale alpine zenith.  Three thousand feet above the canyon floor hung the drifting prism terrace, a hexagonal transfer deck of beveled quartz suspended between the cliffside railhead and the upper cableway.  Riding slackened stabilizer cables to absorb mountain wind shear, the clear platform swayed gently through slow ribbons of refracted violet and amber sunlight.  Beside the open hatch of the aerial cabin stood Marek, the veteran gondola operator, patiently monitoring noise levels on a brass decibel dial wired to ribbon microphones along the glass rails.  Directly overhead clung the Singing Cornices, immense shelves of cantilevered rime ice so fragile that any sharp acoustic vibration could drop tons of frost across the haul cable.  By strict engineering design, the magnetic docking clamps would not release the car until ambient sound within the gorge fell below eight decibels. 

Near the center of the luminous deck stood Clara, a weary clerk from the lowland ironworks clutching a worn canvas satchel against her coat.  Bound for the convalescent sanctuary on the summit plateau, she existed entirely in transit, suspended between a clamor she had fled and a peace she did not yet trust.  Everything around them shared that threshold state, from the buoyant floor swaying between cliff and cloud to coal smoke thinning overhead.  Marek recognized the rigid set of her shoulders because twelve winters earlier he too had arrived from the deafening stamping mills below, raw with grief and terrified of stillness.  He had remained at this aerial station ever since, working each crossing to teach silence how to comfort travelers who mistook the absence of noise for an empty abyss. 

As the final memory of the locomotive dissolved into the chasm, that immense stillness pressed inward upon Clara with sudden weight.  Deprived of the constant factory roar, she heard her pulse hammering in her ears and stared down through the transparent quartz into dizzying depth.  Her heavy boots scraped sharply backward across the beveled panes while fast, ragged gasps broke from her throat, threatening to swell into a cry of vertigo.  On the stanchion gauge, the slender brass needle climbed from eleven decibels to seventeen, holding the gondola clamps locked while stray echoes gathered beneath the overhanging ice.  Years of tending mountain currents had given Marek a fluidly logical mind that treated human fear much like wind pressure, something to be guided around an obstacle rather than opposed by force.  Shouting a warning would breach the acoustic limit himself, while stern gestures would only deepen her panic, stranding them in transit beneath unstable snow.  If he wanted the canyon air to settle, he needed to give her trembling hands something fragile that thrived only in stillness. 

From the binnacle, Marek unhooked his storm glass vial, a sealed tube of camphor, salts, and clear spirit used to read upper air currents without noisy spinning anemometers.  Chilled by the gorge breeze inside its cushioned gimbal, the supersaturated solution rested just below its crystallization point, remaining transparent only because nothing had yet disturbed its equilibrium.  Crossing the deck on soft soles, Marek knelt beside Clara, placed the cool tube upright inside her mittens, and tilted her wrists so the liquid kissed an etched quartz ring.  That microscopic roughness served to catalyze an immediate, silent transformation throughout the supercooled fluid.  Luminous white crystal ferns blossomed upward from the base of the glass, unfurling delicate feathery fronds that caught the refracted violet and gold beams rising through the deck beneath them. 

When Clara shifted one boot, the tallest plume quivered at its tip, showing her instantly that harsh motion or sound would shear the intricate lattice into cloudy dust.  Drawn outward from her terror by an instinct to protect that silent bloom, she steadied her palms and allowed her jagged gasps to lengthen into slow, noiseless breaths.  As her heartbeat eased, she looked across the gorge without dizziness, realizing this hush was not an empty void, but a gentle cradle where fragile things could mend.  Returning to the helm and monitoring noise levels one last time, Marek watched the brass needle sink smoothly past eight decibels and settle at five.  The magnetic clamps parted with a soft sigh, Clara stepped calmly aboard cradling the glowing plumes, and the gondola rose silently into the waiting clouds.
