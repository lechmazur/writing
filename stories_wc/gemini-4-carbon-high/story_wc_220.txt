Five dry winters after the last resource war, a wild hush lay over Iron Reach, a sprawling scrapyard settlement spanning a cracked wasteland of alkali clay and skeletal cranes.  Wind moved without clatter through rusted turbine shells, fierce and untamed yet softer than breath.  Inside a shack welded from corrugated hulls, Mara cleaned a brass horn while Bram slept beside her boots.  Bram was an aging bloodhound retired from finding bodies beneath bombed viaducts, and since the armistice he had refused to track anything tainted with ash or grief.  Mara shared his quiet vigil, repairing salvaged players for scattered families who sent spoken cylinders by mule courier across the fissures.  Her entire craft existed to translate distance into closeness, turning leagues of dead silt into intimate human presence while she waited for word from Eli.  Two years earlier, Eli had walked north beyond the dry basins, swearing to find unpoisoned water and living timber that charcoal barons had missed. 

At dusk, a dust runner dropped a burlap sack of unclaimed northern salvage onto her threshold and vanished into the gathering stillness.  Most pieces inside were brittle black paraffin smelling of machine grease and scorched earth, relics Bram ignored without lifting his scarred chin.  Then the old hound stood, ribs expanding in a silent, trembling draw of air that raised the hackles along his neck.  Instead of the low bay he once gave over graves, he pressed his damp muzzle urgently against a bundle of oiled linen buried beneath the scrap.  Mara unwrapped a pale hollow tube cast from golden beeswax and fragrant pine resin, catching the unmistakable scent of fresh forest loam and familiar hands.  Curving deep within its translucent wall lay an autumn leaf preserved in wax, every serrated edge and crimson vein intact. 

Proof of living trees could draw armed scavengers overnight, so Mara bolted her iron shutter before sliding the cylinder onto the mandrel of her player.  She wound the spring, lowered the sapphire stylus, and leaned close as a faint hiss bloomed in the quiet room.  Eli spoke through the horn, his voice weathered yet steady, choosing to riddle rather than name a sanctuary that highway thieves might intercept.  He chanted softly, "What speaks aloud is empty chaff, so wake a sun within the path, and let the captive scarlet bleed across the silent trench you read."  The stylus reached the end of the track and clicked rhythmically while Bram rested his heavy ears against her knee, listening to the breathless wasteland outside. 

Mara studied the verse, realizing how Eli had used her own repair habits to conceal his trail.  Overhead lamps always cast harsh white glare across polished beeswax, blinding the eye to shallow marks inside the audio tracks.  Following the clue, she slipped the cylinder off the brass spindle, extinguished her lantern, and wired a tiny filament bulb inside the hollow wax core.  When current touched the wire, warm light filtered outward through the preserved scarlet foliage, softening the glare into rich amber and ruby hues.  Lifting her watchmaker lens to that glowing band, Mara beheld a series of tinted revelations emerging from the heart of the relic.  Backlit in crimson shadow, delicate hairline letters stood out along the silent troughs between spoken vibrations, invisible under ordinary daylight yet radiant through colored wax.  Slowly turning the warm tube in her palms, she uncovered the northern route by reading secret texts in the grooves of a phonograph cylinder. 

Because artillery craters and shifting rust dunes had ruined prewar maps, the needlepoint script was intuitively descriptive, charting the land through living sensation rather than dead numbers.  Eli wrote of walking where morning wind hummed a low third through split aqueduct pipes, then climbing blue shale that smelled of crushed mint after frost.  Beyond a ridge of fused glass lay a hidden box canyon fed by snowmelt, thick with wild maples and sweet water.  Every vivid phrase bridged the barren miles between them, drawing Eli so near that Mara could almost feel canyon mist cooling her wrists.  What her trained ear recognized in the humming pipes, Bram would confirm along the ridges with his nose, tracking mint and sap instead of ruin.  When Mara packed her canteens, wrapped the cylinder in wool, and unbolted the door, the old bloodhound stepped eagerly onto the pale clay without making a sound.  Under cold stars and an untamed silence, woman and hound turned north together toward the living trees.
