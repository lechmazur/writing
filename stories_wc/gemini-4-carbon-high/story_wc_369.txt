Working the night shift as a labyrinth maintenance worker, Cole treated mundane miracles with the same practical care he gave a squeaky wheelbarrow.  Every evening after the iron gates locked, he raked the gravel paths, trimmed the ancient yew hedges, and emptied the cellar pans of quantified sorrow.  People traveled from distant towns to walk those winding green alleys, confessing griefs they could no longer carry alone.  The roots drank that heaviness from their footsteps and condensed it into grey mineral silt that trickled down into subterranean brass scales.  A soured friendship weighed roughly as much as a walnut, while a buried marriage filled a tin scoop.  Cole logged the nightly gram totals in a ledger stained with thermos rings, then wheeled the harmless dust out to mulch the cabbages. 

Yet tidy arithmetic felt like an incomplete record after twenty years of tending troubled strangers.  The scales measured only mass, stripping away every face and voice, and Cole had grown quietly determined to discover what trees witness while turning human ache into chalky grit.  He knew the yews held those living impressions inside their dense heartwood, though no saw could ever open a bough to check.  Living wood in this maze heals in moonlight, sealing every pruning cut into seamless bark before steel can reach the core.  Even cuts stained with yew sap across Cole's knuckles closed the same way whenever he rested his hands on a bright windowsill during supper.  Since the bark would not yield to tools, Cole spent six patient months preparing an optical chart by mapping scattered dreams across the grounds.  Weary visitors often dozed on stone benches, leaving pale filaments of sleep snagged on nearby twigs like luminous wool.  Before sweeping those wisps into his dustpan, Cole noted their exact coordinates on vellum, tracing the hidden angles where the canopy stored its clearest memories. 

Tonight, everything came together high in the stormlit tower by moonlight.  Outside the open casement, distant lightning flickered through departing thunderheads while a crisp shaft of lunar silver poured across Cole's workbench.  From his canvas apron, he unwrapped the astronomer's broken sextant, salvaged years ago from a dark bin behind the cellar stairs.  The scholar who originally designed the maze had mistrusted messy emotion, building brass instruments to flatten sorrow into geometry until a fallen rafter crushed his favorite tool.  Its numbered arc was snapped in two, and its specialized mirrors, cast from hardened yew resin, had stayed spiderwebbed with cracks inside that windowless bin.  Taking a pocket screwdriver, Cole began to deconstruct the ruined housing, unscrewing the rigid vernier clamp and prying free each piece of resin glass.  He pushed the bent brass scraps aside and stood the fractured mirrors upright in dabs of putty along the inked nodes of his chart.  When he slid the vellum directly into the moonbeam, the amber resin drank the pale light, and every jagged crack melted shut into flawless glass. 

All that remained was the brief mechanical pause between one day's tally and the next.  Deep beneath the tower ran a long calibration passage, insulated with three feet of river clay so not even storm winds broke its hush.  Each midnight, when the final grain of silt settled and brought the master scale level, a tiny brass pin slipped from the counterweight latch onto the flagstones.  Cole leaned his ear toward the copper speaking tube beside his bench, knowing the roots unclenched their grain only at the moment a pin is heard in a silent corridor.  A faint metallic chime drifted up the pipe.  In that same heartbeat, a broad sheet of lightning lit the drenched maze below, bouncing a sudden shimmer off the relaxed yew needles straight through the casement.  The flash caught the mended resin mirrors along the dream map and unfolded across the whitewashed wall in living color. 

Cole held his breath, bracing himself for scenes of bitter weeping, yet the plaster glowed with quiet tenderness instead.  There knelt a daughter on damp gravel tying her frail father's shoelace, beside two estranged brothers sharing hot tea, and a young widower smiling as a fat robin scolded his boots.  The ancient trees were not hoarding misery at all.  They filtered away the crushing weight of grief into cellar dust only so they could preserve the ordinary love that had made the grief hurt.  After three seconds, the subterranean valves clicked shut for the new cycle, and the wall faded back to plain limewash.  Smiling to himself, Cole brushed the loose brass screws into an empty tobacco tin, tucked his screwdriver away, and headed downstairs to check on the cabbages.
