For eight centuries, the benthic city of Halcyon had known only the crushing dark five miles beneath the ocean surface.  Built beside volcanic vents after ancient storms drowned the continents, its reinforced domes sheltered a humanity that navigated solely by sonar echoes.  Everything changed six weeks after discovering aliens in high orbit above the clearing clouds.  Seven vast, silent vessels hung motionless above the sea, drinking every radar and acoustic ping sent from automated surface buoys without returning a single wave.  Instead, the visitors pulsed slow optical patterns aligned with the constellations, signaling that any ascent craft approaching them would fly completely blind to active instruments.  That quiet ultimatum brought Jonas, the senior navigation instructor of the academy, alone into the upper cupola long past midnight. 

Overhead, a newly tethered surface lens piped raw night sky down through miles of glass cable onto the high ceiling.  Before Jonas could train the crew selected for first contact, he had to rewire fifty years of trench instincts and fulfill a daunting duty: to learn to navigate by starlight.  Staring upward made his pulse race with ancestral vertigo.  Abyssal minds understood enclosed tunnels and solid rock, not the terrifying majesty of unfolded horizons stretching into emptiness without a wall to return sound.  Half the city council wanted to sever the optical tether and hide in the silt, while reckless young pilots begged to launch immediately without charts.  Jonas remained balancedly curious, approaching the alien sky like an unmapped chasm that demanded equal measures of rigor and reverence. 

Yet after four sleepless nights, the projected stars still blurred into meaningless white dust before his eyes.  Without pressure gradients or thermal currents to anchor his perception, he could not judge how fast the planet turned beneath those cold fires.  Seeking a familiar weight, Jonas reached into his heavy coat and drew out an ancient boat anchor chain link salvaged decades earlier from the mud outside the south airlock.  Forged before the deluge by surface mariners, the weathered oval of pitted iron featured a thick central crossbar that split its interior into twin narrow slots.  He had carried the relic for years as a quiet tribute to ancestors who once sailed under open wind.  Turning the iron between his fingers beneath the starlit dome, Jonas noticed how centuries of strain had pulled its inner sides completely straight and parallel. 

A sudden clarity steadied his breathing.  In murky submarine canyons where sonar scattered, he always taught pilots to rely on edge geometry, fixing their craft against the crisp linear intersections of basalt ledges and structural hull ribs.  Open space possessed no stone walls, yet he held rigid boundaries right in his hand.  Jonas raised his arm to full extension and aligned the flat iron crossbar against a titanium beam of the cupola.  Instantly, the chaotic scatter of stars snapped into disciplined triangles bounded by dark metal.  Each narrow slot of the link subtended exactly three degrees of arc, allowing him to clock the angular drift of bright beacons as they grazed one straight iron bevel and crossed toward the next.  Emboldened, Jonas stepped to the wall console and retracted the lower safety shutters, commanding the optical array to expand the feed all the way down to the deck. 

The comforting gloom of the room vanished as silver ocean swells met a wheeling vault of stars on every side.  For one fierce heartbeat, his knees weakened at the illusion of standing unprotected atop five miles of black water.  Then he lifted the corroded iron sight toward the eastern rim, locking the flat horizon neatly along the bottom edge of the link.  Framed within the upper aperture, seven pale amber lights pulsed in solemn unison beside a tilted winter constellation.  A heavy ache filled his chest as he realized that the sheltered childhood of his species in the deep dark was forever finished.  Yet watching those patient strangers gleam through iron forged by forgotten sailors stirred a profound, quiet wonder that steadied his old hands.  Jonas opened his logbook under the faint celestial glow and began plotting the first true course toward the surface.  When the heavy hatch unlatched at dawn and his youngest cadet stepped hesitantly onto the starlit deck, Jonas held out the ancient link and beckoned her forward.
