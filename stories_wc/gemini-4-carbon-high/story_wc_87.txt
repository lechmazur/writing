Before dawn broke over Blackrock Sound, Julian dragged his sea kayak down the slick concrete of the coastal kayak launch, his steel leg brace clicking against the stones.  Six years had passed since an electrical failure blinded his survey ship in a gale, tearing open her hull on a reef and leaving him a broken navigator ashore.  Bolted to his fiberglass foredeck sat the last relic of his former command: a brass astronomical compass that ignored the magnetite bluffs warping local needles and found true north solely by the rays of the sun.  Two miles across the channel on Osprey Island, his daughter Clara was packing the family cottage before the noon freight boat arrived to carry her away for good.  Since the wreck, Julian had loved her fiercely inside his chest while hiding behind excuses on the mainland, and he had come to the water today for one reason: to align heart and action before distance became permanent. 

For weeks he had rehearsed a dignified crossing under the clear sunrise promised by every coastal forecast, picturing himself dialing latitude into the brass vernier and gliding straight to her pier with some piece of his old mastery restored.  Instead, he stood in that cold, humbling silence when expectations collapse.  A dense wall of sea fog poured around the headland just as first light greyed the east, swallowing Osprey Island, the channel buoys, and every trace of the rising sun.  Without a shadow line across its translucent target glass, his prized astronomical compass was no better than a paperweight.  Julian let out a merrily melancholic laugh, humming a brisk tavern jig about blind helmsmen while he saluted the useless brass instrument with two wet fingers.  Years of misfortune had given him that buoyant, sorrowful armor, greeting fresh disasters like old drinking companions who arrived right on cue. 

Up the ramp, two weekend paddlers strapped their hulls onto roof racks, quick to gravitate toward hot coffee and dry pavement now that visibility had died.  Julian felt his own bruised spirit pull in the same direction, his resolve shrinking to a flickering faith that quivered with every cold slap of tide against the concrete.  Yet the instant he imagined limping back to his truck to compose another careful letter, a sudden backlash of regrets struck him harder than the wind.  Every missed birthday, every unanswered call, and every cowardly retreat of the past six years recoiled upon him at once, warning that love hoarded in safety was indistinguishable from abandonment.  The open channel was suicide without sky sights, yet half a mile south lay the shallow mudflats, where tall creosote timbers carried power lines across the drowned marsh to Osprey Island.  Deepwater officers mocked mudflat hugging, yet Julian wedged his braced leg into the cockpit, shoved off from the coastal kayak launch, and committed himself to navigating by utility poles. 

Ten strokes out, the mainland vanished, leaving only brown chop smelling of sulfur and eelgrass.  He found the first crusted timber looming at the marsh edge, read the tin numeral one nailed above the barnacles, and sighted along the sagging black cables before they dissolved into white mist overhead.  From pole two to pole six, oyster shoals rasped beneath his keel while each brief span of blind water demanded that he trust unseen wires to still be waiting thirty yards ahead.  Halfway across, where a deeper tidal gut split the flats after pole eight, a crosswise current caught his bow and shoved him sideways into featureless fog.  Both the eighth timber behind him and the ninth ahead vanished completely, and his cramped leg locked hard against the hull.  Panic rose in his throat, nearly snuffing out the small ember of belief that had carried him from shore, as the seaward rip threatened to sweep him past the island altogether. 

Julian held his paddle still, closed his eyes against the blank glare, and let his senses gravitate toward the faintest earthly clue above the tide.  Ten yards to his left, damp ceramic insulators buzzed softly in the mist, and heavy drops of condensation pattered from invisible copper spans onto the dark water.  Digging his blade deep against the current, he pulled beneath that steady drip until one cold bead splashed squarely across the blind face of his astronomical compass and pole nine loomed dark out of the foam.  Pole by numbered pole, streaked with marsh mud and stripped of every grand illusion, he followed the humming line onto the gravel shingle beside the island pier.  Clara stopped on the wet planks with a wooden crate in her arms, staring in quiet astonishment as her father hauled himself upright in the fog and reached out to take half the weight.
