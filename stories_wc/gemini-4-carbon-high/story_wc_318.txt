Thirteen months after young Sam died, silence has turned the house cold and feral.  The boy has not left, and because neither parent dares speak his name aloud, his lingering spirit mistakes their quiet for anger.  Frost blooms across the parlor mirror, latched doors rattle from within, and damp sneaker prints surface on the hall runner whenever Nora weeps.  Every evening at nine, Nora flips the brass timer on the mantel, enforcing sixty minutes of mute vigil while small, weightless footsteps pace overhead.  Walter waits with folded hands until after the final grain slips through the hourglass and his wife retreats behind her locked bedroom door.  Only then does he pull on his wool coat and descend through the cellar bulkhead to begin tending to the unspoken. 

Plain speech fails inside those rooms, where even a whispered apology sends icy drafts down the stairs and drives Nora deeper under her quilts.  Behind the garden bluff lies an abandoned brick carriage passage known to the family as the echo chamber tunnel, its arched mouth aimed squarely toward the back windows of the house.  Before illness claimed their son, Walter was a school band teacher and the proud leaf blower symphony conductor of their block, donning a frayed tailcoat every October to lead yard machines in wheezing parades.  During the last autumn when Sam grew too weak to leave bed, Walter strapped three salvaged gas blowers onto wooden sawhorses deep inside this brick vault.  He fitted dented stovepipes of stepped lengths over their snouts and wired their throttles to a crossbar by his stand, turning engine clatter into a cavernous brass drone that made Sam howl with delight. 

For over a year the contraption has gathered rust in the dark, untouched since the funeral.  Yet tonight Walter returns with one clear purpose: to restore lost laughter to a household slowly freezing into a tomb.  Solemn prayers would only frighten the ghost and harden the grief of his wife, whereas the tender absurdity their boy loved might crack the ice.  Dry oak leaves drift thick across the tunnel floor and would rattle like shrapnel against the intake grilles if left loose.  All week he has swept those brittle heaps into the stone seepage troughs along the walls, submerging them in rainwater to macerate until their stiff fibers soften into silent, sweet pulp.  Now he stirs the dark mash beneath the stovepipe mouths, releasing a rich scent of steeped cider and damp earth into the draft. 

As Walter steps onto a crate before his rusted stand, his lantern dims to an amber thread and the air turns bitter cold.  From the deeper shadows comes the soft scuff of one untied shoelace dragging across wet brick, just as Sam always wore his left sneaker.  A pale plume of breath hangs beside the nearest sawhorse with no body behind it, the unseen child hovering there mournfully eager for a game interrupted by death.  Walter swallows hard, steadies his shaking knees, and wraps cold fingers around the scarred plastic pull cord handle of the center machine.  He yanks once against the stiff compression, then again, until the cylinder coughs awake in a low sputter and he coaxes the flanking engines to a ragged idle.  Raising his white baton, he tugs the throttle twine to blend their airflow inside the curving vault, where harsh engine buzz softens into a vast, wobbling organ chord.  He knows he can bridge the gulf between the living and the dead only via half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind, offering memory without demanding that anyone speak too soon.  Leaning into the heart of the brickwork, Walter croons the silly bedtime verses about sailing teapots that Sam loved, his rusty baritone cracking on the middle notes.  The vault catches his voice, braids it through the wheezing triad of pipes, and pours the warm, fragrant autumn gust across the dark lawn toward the house. 

Beside the sawhorses, the crushing chill loosens its grip.  Invisible fingers tug twice at the hem of his wool coat in time with the clumsy beat, and woven inside the echoing drone comes a faint, breathless giggle from empty air.  Up on the second floor, a dark bedroom sash slides open.  Nora leans over the sill in her shawl, staring down at her husband waving a white stick before three sputtering yard blowers in the dark.  For one heartbeat the ravine hangs breathless, and then a startled, ragged laugh breaks from her throat, half sob and half delight, echoing all the way down into the brickwork.  In the sudden warmth that follows, the cold weight beside Walter finally lets go and slips peacefully into the trees, just as the back porch light clicks on.
