Oren woke at dawn's earliest whistle, when the sea wind fluted through a rusted stovepipe rising out of the sand above his head.  Pale light spilled down the narrow skylight into his feral loft beneath the dunes, once the high attic of a merchant house before drifting ridges buried the old harbor village.  Now wild marram roots hung like silver fringe from the cedar beams, ghost crabs scuttled across the tilted floorboards, and nesting terns murmured in the eaves.  Sitting up under his patched wool coat, the vagrant penny poet breathed in the lonesome splendor of his sunken sanctuary, where shafts of amber sun turned falling grains of sand into pillars of fire. 

Along a plank shelf beside his cot stood forty stoppered vessels, the secret tools of a trade that went far beyond selling scribbled rhymes for copper coins on the cliff roads.  For twelve years Oren had practiced the delicate art of capturing sounds in glass jars, slipping corks free inside his satchel whenever townsfolk spoke what pride forbade them to explain.  In his reckless youth, he had mocked his own silent father and walked away from home, realizing too late that the old fisherman had spoken love only through gruff warnings about weather and nets.  That grief had forged his sole purpose: to become the translator between generations, carrying the weathered truths of coastal elders to the restless children eager to flee inland.  He could never mend his own past, yet gathering stray human echoes from the wind offered him a gleaned redemption, harvested scrap by scrap from other families before silence claimed them. 

This morning, however, a heavy green jar on his crate troubled him deeply.  Inside its curved walls churned the quarrel he had caught last dusk at the bluff cottage between blind Widow Marta and her granddaughter Linnea, who planned to board the morning packet steamer forever.  Pressing his ear to the cool glass, Oren heard the brittle scolding of old Marta scrape against the sharp slam of a wooden latch.  If he handed the girl that raw echo at the pier, its salt bitterness would only drive her faster toward the sea.  Just as coastal wives boiled wild honey into sour marsh berries for winter storage, he needed some resonant grace to sweeten the trapped fury so Linnea could taste the terror of abandonment hiding beneath it.  Yet every verse he murmured over the rim rang hollow and thin. 

A sudden cascade of sand hissed from the eastern gable, where night gales had pried loose a rotted wainscoting panel from the buried house.  Kneeling in the fresh drift, Oren remembered town lore saying this very merchant attic had belonged to the family of Marta sixty summers ago, before the dunes marched over their threshold.  His fingers struck carved walnut inside the wall cavity and drew forth a forgotten music box wrapped in faded linen.  After brushing grit from its brass cylinder, he gave the tarnished key a careful turn.  Slender steel teeth plucked out a halting harbor lullaby, its bell notes positively wistful, aching with the tender vigilance of mothers watching small boats vanish into fog.  Oren caught his breath as his trained ear for meter recognized the exact rhythm of those chimes.  Even in blind anger, Marta had shaped every clipped accusation to the cadence of the cradle song she had once hummed above a sleeping child. 

Moving fast before the steamer bell could sound, Oren eased the cork from the green jar and held the singing brass comb directly over its open mouth.  Harsh words rose like wasps toward the rafters, met the falling silver notes, and slowly settled back into the glass as their jagged edges dissolved into melody.  He sealed the vessel tight, tucked a fresh couplet beneath its twine, and climbed the ladder through the skylight onto the windblown crest of the dunes.  Down on the timber wharf, Linnea stood beside her canvas trunk, staring back at the silent bluff while sailors cast off the bowline.  Oren pressed the jar into her hands without asking for a penny, then retreated up the golden slope before she could question him.  From the high grass, he watched her twist the cork free and flinch at the first sharp syllable, only to press her palm over her heart as the ancient cradle tune bloomed beneath that weathered voice.  Leaving her trunk on the planks as the steamer pulled away, Linnea ran weeping up the cliff path toward the cottage door.  Alone once more against the vast gleam of tide and sky, Oren pocketed the silent brass box and walked onward along the empty shore, rich beyond measure in the light of morning.
