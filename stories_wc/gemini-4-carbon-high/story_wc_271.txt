Cold salt spray drifted across the terraced beds of the arboretum perched on a seaside cliff.  Nadia knelt beside a stunted hawthorn near the limestone brink, her grafting chisel poised above its dark, weathered bark.  Five winters had passed after the last lighthouse keeper locked the iron door of the neighboring tower and surrendered the headland to automated beacons.  Since then, only the botanical staff and the machines of the maritime authority shared this high margin between pasture and sea.  Behind her, young Julian set down a crate of Damascus apricot saplings and wiped mist from his forehead.  He still wanted to plant the young fruit trees directly into the open bluff meadow for the spring dedication, convinced that rich compost and good intentions could defeat an Atlantic gale.  Nadia did not scold him for wishing the climate gentler than it was.  Quietly talented at structural propagation, she preferred to teach with timber and twine rather than lectures.  Other horticulturists had failed to grow stone fruit on this exposed promontory, yet her grafts thrived because she bound fragile inland scions onto native coastal rootstock.  Her purpose in every cut was to anchor optimism in realism, giving delicate blossoms the scarred, windward roots required to endure actual storms. 

She understood the cost of unshielded beauty better than Julian knew.  Years earlier, a clerk at a resettlement clinic had summarized her history on an intake form in four stark words: drone strike wedding survivor.  The phrase held none of the music from her cousin Tariq and his bride dancing beneath orchard lanterns, nor the sudden erasure that dropped from a cloudless afternoon sky.  Inside the breast pocket of her canvas smock rested a crumpled photograph from that celebration, pulled from her own clenched fist at the field hospital.  White fault lines crossed the glossy paper where laughter met scorched dust at one corner.  When Julian had glimpsed it in the potting shed last month, he had offered to scan the image and digitally smooth every crease.  Nadia had refused gently, knowing that erasing the damage would turn real memory into a fragile lie. 

Now a bank of grey sea fog rolled toward the cliff, leaving them scant minutes to seal the graft before dampness spoiled the exposed wood.  Nadia tapped her chisel to open a clean cleft in the hawthorn bough and pared a budded apricot shoot into a pale green wedge.  Just as she lifted the scion toward the split bark, a sharp mechanical whine rose from the cove below the unmanned lighthouse.  An automated coastal survey quadcopter crested the chalk ledge, its four rotors pitching shrill and tight against the updraft as its cameras mapped rock erosion.  Nadia knew the yellow machine belonged to geologists, yet her nervous system answered the acoustic frequency long before reason could speak. 

Years of patient rehabilitation had trained her to navigate panic through somatic markers, reading bodily signals as weather to steer by rather than commands to surrender.  Every alarm flared in her flesh at once: ice cinched her ribs, bitter copper flooded her tongue, and phantom heat prickled across her collarbones.  Her hands began to freeze inches from the parted limb, tendons locking around the bare steel of the grafting knife.  Seeing her rigid posture beside the sheer drop, Julian stepped forward to pry the blade from her fingers.  If he jarred her arm now, the hawthorn cleft would tear and the exposed apricot tissue would wither in the salt mist. 

Unable to call out through her constricted throat, Nadia pressed her left wrist slowly inward against her smock pocket.  Through the heavy canvas, she traced the stiff ridges of the print against her chest, answering old visceral terror with immediate physical truth.  Wet limestone chilled her knees, sea spray stung her knuckles, and the creased paper confirmed she had already survived the day the sky broke.  Just as an oyster coats a sharp grain of grit in smooth nacre, she wrapped conscious breath around the shrill rotor noise until her paralysis became a pearl of stillness.  The quadcopter still hummed twenty yards off the bluff, yet the tremor drained from her wrists and Julian halted his outstretched hand in quiet wonder.  With steady fingers, Nadia seated the tender apricot wedge deep inside the scarred hawthorn wood, aligned their living cambium layers, and wrapped the union tight with waxed twine.  As the automated beacon on the headland flashed its first cold pulse into the gathering fog, Julian lifted his crate of saplings to carry them back for coastal grafting, understanding at last what held a blossom to the cliff.
