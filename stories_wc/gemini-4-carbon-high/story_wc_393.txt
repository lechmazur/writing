Stepping out of a spring squall into Elena Moreau's antique music box repair shop felt like entering the body of a cello.  Unlike the dead concrete silence of the solitary tier where Caleb Ward served as a penitentiary chaplain, this narrow room held a vibrant hush.  Dozens of walnut and rosewood cases lined the walls, their coiled springs and tuned steel combs humming faintly in sympathy with the rain against the glass.  Caleb approached the counter, unfolded a square of damp linen, and set down a tarnished clockwork heart.  No larger than a closed fist, the brass musical movement had been cast in the anatomical curve of two chambers, its pierced filigree crusted with green oxidation and dried mud. 

Elena looked up from her workbench, offering the mild nod she might give any neighborhood clergyman bringing in an attic curiosity.  Because she did not yet recognize the corroded shell, they stood together in the calm before realization would remake the afternoon between them.  Twelve years earlier, during the midnight burglary that took her father's life, a panicked teenage lookout named Jonah Bell had fled this very room clutching a velvet pouch he mistook for jewelry.  Finding only brass gears inside, Jonah had buried the movement in a biscuit tin beneath a rail culvert hours before police arrested him.  Six months ago in the prison infirmary, dying of lung failure, Jonah had begged Caleb to dig it up.  Hardened by twenty years of cellblock schemes, Caleb had dismissed the story as an angle for a sympathetic parole recommendation.  Then Jonah waived his parole hearing entirely, writing to the board that he did not deserve release, only that the craftsman's daughter deserved what had been stolen.  When Jonah died in February, Caleb had stood beside an empty steel bunk and whispered that he was sorry for doubting him.  Regret meant little without labor, as Caleb often reminded convicts, so on Saturday he had carried a shovel into the soaked ravine, determined to turn an apology into action. 

He had hoped to return the piece already singing rather than burden Elena with a ruined lump of metal from her worst night.  Yesterday, however, an uptown horologist had glanced at the seized cylinder, declared the mainspring snapped and the pins hopeless, and suggested the scrap bin.  Prisons were monuments to final verdicts, places where damaged lives rarely received second opinions, and Caleb refused to repeat that failure here.  If this movement was truly dead, he would hand Elena Jonah's confession letter and soften the blow gently; if the uptown expert was wrong, her father deserved to speak to her first.  "Another repairman told me it was beyond saving," Caleb said softly.  "I wanted someone more patient to decide." 

Elena fitted a black loupe to her eye and cradled the clockwork heart on a leather pad.  "Men call delicate things ruined when they resent the hours required to listen," she murmured, reaching for wintergreen oil and a sable brush.  Her touch was aptly deliberate, suited to fine tempered steel where one impatient slip could shear a tuned tooth forever.  Drop by drop, she dissolved the crusted silt choking the worm gear, then slid a brass pick beneath the seized governor fan.  Around them, the shop seemed to lean inward, its quiet alive with the crisp pulse of wall regulators and the scent of oiled cedar.  With a tiny metallic sigh, the governor spun half a turn and freed itself.  "Every pin on the cylinder is straight," Elena whispered, unscrewing the small top bridge to inspect the dark barrel housing. 

Inside the curved chamber, however, her overhead bench lamp cast only a harsh white glare off the wet oil.  Clicking off the bulb, Elena carried the open piece to the front window, reading the shadowed interior by the way light breaks through storm clouds, angled low and clean without surface reflection.  Just then the squall parted above the wet street, sending a narrow blade of amber sun sideways through the filigree ribs.  That slanting beam revealed an unbroken mainspring coiled tight within, beside three tiny words etched in familiar script along the inner wall: For my Elena.  As her fingers tilted the brass, stored tension nudged the cylinder awake, and three steel teeth plucked the pure opening phrase of a childhood lullaby into the quiet.  For one breathless second more she remained still, sheltered on the edge of that vanishing calm before grief and wonder could overtake her, while Caleb reached into his coat for Jonah's letter and waited to meet her eyes.
