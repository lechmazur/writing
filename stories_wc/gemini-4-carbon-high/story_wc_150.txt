Nadia knelt beside the collapsed burial vault at dawn, pressing palms raised in a distant harbor city against the cool limestone dust of her ancestral homeland.  Around her stretched the sunken catacomb orchard, a cavernous hollow where wild fig and pomegranate trees grew beneath narrow skylights and pale roots snaked between bones stacked in tiered wall niches.  As a diaspora returnee reclaiming a valley her grandparents had fled two generations earlier, she carried a tarnished bronze pointer in her coat and a quiet resolve to restore lost faith.  Decades of exile had convinced her elders that their sacred covenant with this soil had perished, leaving even Nadia uncertain whether anything remained beyond fragments of dead custom.  Lifting the first slab of fallen shale from the grave, she began the delicate work of excavating the tomb. 

Warmly curious rather than fearful among the dead, she tilted her head to admire how amber sap gleamed like tiny lanterns above smooth skulls and how woody tendrils cradled fragile clavicles.  A hushed wonder hung in the damp air, making her brush away the upper layer of chalk with tender restraint, as though waking a sleeping house.  Beneath the dry leaves emerged the cracked cedar lintel of the vault, still bearing one jagged third of a circular limestone sundial base etched with ancient hour lines.  According to family lore, the mourners burying the final village timekeeper had fled mid rite when sudden landslides struck the pass, turning both the funeral and their exodus into interrupted journeys.  In their haste, the pallbearers had left the grave half sealed and dropped the remaining curved stones of the monument somewhere along the dim corridors. 

Scraping down to the middle stratum, Nadia found thick silver fig roots binding hardened clay tightly around the inner stones of the tomb.  Refusing to hack through living wood, she poured water from her canteen to soften the packed earth and used the interval to seek the missing stonework through purposeful wandering.  Because no map had survived the ocean crossing, she drifted down the western gallery without a rigid route yet with attentive intent, letting subtle currents of sweet air and the lean of ancient branches guide her steps.  She paused often with gentle delight to study tiny blind beetles, luminous moss, and unlit clay lamps set on ledges by walkers who never finished their rounds.  Where gnarled roots looped through a row of femurs toward a trickling cistern, she discovered a carved wedge of pale limestone resting beside a rotted wooden barrow. 

Carrying the heavy fragment back to the grave, Nadia eased away the softened clay until the skeleton of the timekeeper appeared in the cool dimness.  Fine white roots snaked peacefully between the ribs and curved through the finger bones, which still cradled an unfinished pilgrim bowl.  The sight stirred a careful magic in her chest, as if the orchard itself had spent decades guarding the halted traveler until someone returned to witness it.  Pouring her remaining water over the stubborn silt at the base of the chamber, she noticed faint mason marks pointing east and set out once more, meandering deliberately beneath ruby pomegranates that glowed like quiet coals above ancestral skulls.  Following the gentle slope where hurried footsteps had once scattered gravel toward the exit stair, she lingered where fern fronds quivered in an unseen draft and uncovered the final third of the sundial base. 

Back beside the open vault, Nadia brushed the last loosened earth from the stone floor, completing the excavation of the tomb without harming a single bone or living root.  She kneaded damp subterranean clay in her cupped hands and fitted the three heavy limestone arcs together atop the lintel to rebuild the sundial base.  Drawing the tarnished bronze pointer from her coat, she slid its narrow foot into the central socket just as the overhead sun cleared the jagged rock lip.  A single shaft of golden light, freed at last from the overhanging cliffs that blocked its morning passage, plunged straight through the leafy canopy onto the mended circle.  The sharp shadow of the exiled metal fell true across the carved meridian line, while warm air sighed softly through the hollow flutes of bone and rustled the silver fig leaves in a quiet, thrilling benediction.  Touching the sunlit stone, Nadia felt her own doubt dissolve, certain now that no halted path was truly broken and that word of this living measure would rekindle belief among her kin waiting across the sea.
