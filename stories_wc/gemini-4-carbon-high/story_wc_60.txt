Dusk settled cold and dry across Vaelen, a city of forgotten fountains where three hundred marble basins stood cracked and waterless beneath the curfew lamps.  Counting her hours from the pause of migrating geese upon the dry plazas until their departure at dawn, Mara knew she had only one autumn night to act.  She wrapped dark wool tight around her wrists to hide the pale blue glow of her skin.  Years of grinding phosphor lenses in the municipal vaults had left that chemical radiance in her pores, turning her into a luminous skeptic among fearful neighbors.  While the ruling Wardens preached that her shining hands were a mark of spirits and that the vanished past was a divine punishment, Mara trusted only optics, acoustics, and human motive.  Moving in a defiant hush past shuttered tenements, she treated the enforced silence of the streets not as submission, but as a cloak for treason. 

Forty years earlier, the Wardens had drugged the public wells to induce civic amnesia after capturing the voices, faces, and water maps of Vaelen inside slow mineral glass.  The master glazier forced to build that reliquary had hidden it inside one fountain moments before drinking the drugged water himself, erasing his own knowledge of the hiding place.  Other rebels had hunted in vain for secret ciphers or omens, but Mara possessed a realistically imaginative mind that paired bold conjecture with plain observation.  She reasoned that bodily habit survives where conscious thought is wiped clean.  When the northern flocks descended at twilight, the frail old glazier had risen from his porch without knowing why, drawn toward the birds he had once fed beside his greatest work.  After he shuffled back to bed, Mara located the exact courtyard among three hundred dry squares simply by following breadcrumbs of dark rye scattered along the cobblestones. 

Now she slipped into Heron Court, where dozens of grey geese tucked their beaks beneath tired wings inside a dry basin of carved granite.  Stepping slowly so the resting flock would not raise an alarm, she reached beneath the dry bronze spout and twisted a loose keystone.  From the hollow pedestal she lifted the stained glass box, its ruby, amber, and cobalt panes dark with forty years of hoarded light.  Mara had risked the gallows for one purpose: to liberate trapped memories so the citizens of Vaelen would remember their stolen charters and reopen the mountain aqueducts.  Yet as she unwrapped her glowing fingers to inspect the brass frame without a lantern, she found the sliding shutters fused tight with hardened pine pitch and mineral scale.  If she forced a blade into the seams, the brittle panes would shatter and spill their stored images into useless dust.  She needed to unstick each corroded channel gently while waiting for the exact celestial alignment that could trigger the glass. 

According to her optical calculations, slow glass sealed at midnight in late autumn would invert its refractive index only when struck again by the zenith light of Fomalhaut.  Opening the reliquary therefore demanded sidereal patience, binding her hands to the slow, indifferent clockwork of the turning sky.  Crouched motionless among the warm feathers of the geese, Mara brushed single drops of citrus oil along the brass grooves with a sable hair.  Twice, heavy boots echoed down the outer avenue, and she cupped her body over her luminous hands until the Warden patrol passed.  Minute by minute, the white star crept across the narrow gap between slate rooftops while the oil softened forty winters of resin.  When the pale starbeam finally touched the central prism, Mara pressed her thumbs against the loosened latches and slid the four shutters wide without a whisper of sound. 

Instantly, ribbons of colored daylight unfurled from the leaded panes, projecting crisp reflections across every wall and upper window of Heron Court.  Vibrating slightly in the cold air, the glass breathed out a low chorus of vanished voices reciting the civic charter, naming forgotten kin, and describing the buried floodgate wheel in the hills.  Behind thin curtains, sleepers woke, pressed palms to wooden frames, and wept in quiet recognition as the drugged fog broke inside their minds.  Before first light greyed the east and the geese lifted skyward with a sudden rush of wings, dozens of citizens had emerged carrying iron wrenches toward the mountain gate, neither shouting nor running, united in silent resolve.  Leaving the empty prism beside the dry spout soon to roar with clear water, Mara wrapped her shining wrists in wool and joined their march.
