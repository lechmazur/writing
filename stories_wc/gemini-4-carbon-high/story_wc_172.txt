Anselm Vane stood alone at the center of the blinding white salt flat, listening to the dry crust tick beneath his boots.  Two hundred miles north, newspapers were praising him as a visionary philanthropist whose Mercy Aqueduct now delivered free river water to six arid industrial cities.  None of those grateful editorials lingered on the Orelia Basin downstream, where Vane's upstream diversion dams had choked three rivers into dust to create a manufactured drought.  Twenty thousand marsh farmers had watched their ancestral orchards wither into gray bone before accepting his foundation's generous relocation grants.  Vane had signed the checks with amoral serenity, trading human communities for dead mineral light without a flicker of guilt. 

For fifty years, murky backwaters and agricultural silt had drowned this plain, burying the sunken halite caverns where vesper curlews once gathered at dusk.  Dense saline clay had blinded every subsurface radar survey Vane commissioned, leaving forty square miles of featureless white pan hiding a single subterranean throat.  Only slow desiccation could betray the hollow chamber below.  As the basin baked over two rainless summers, remaining groundwater had drained downward toward that deep sinkhole, pulling the shrinking mud along buried fault lines.  At dawn Vane had hiked across the basin following a map drawn by cracks in dried riverbeds, tracing where three miles of branching fissures converged like compass needles at one ragged vent in the salt. 

To his board of directors, Vane was an iron utilitarian who measured mercy in spreadsheets, yet beneath that cold armor he remained secretly sentimental.  He knelt beside the dark vent, unlatched a titanium case, and lifted out an extinct bird's egg resting on faded velvet.  Pale gray and speckled with rust, the shell weighed less than a dry petal in his palm.  Before agricultural runoff flooded this plain and killed the last vesper curlews in his youth, he had crouched on these crystal flats as a hungry boy, transfixed by their twilight choir.  Decades later he had outbid three national museums at a private auction for this lone specimen, hollowed a century ago through two beveled pinholes at its poles.  In living curlews, the inflated vocal sac had matched the exact ovoid volume of their clutch, tuning the porous mineral shell to resonate at the precise pitch and dry overtones of the parent's call. 

Museums possessed silent feathers under glass, yet no machine had ever captured how these crystalline ridges and salt chimneys braided one thin whistle into a lingering harmonic chord.  Vane had uprooted thousands of lives for one private purpose: to preserve the echo of extinct birds.  Around him in a wide circle stood twelve archival microphones wired to a quartz recorder, waiting while purple storm clouds bruised the western peaks.  Soon a monsoon deluge would sweep across the basin and dissolve his dry stage back into mud.  Already the plunging barometric pressure ahead of the squall was drawing a steady, vertical plume of cool air upward from the deep salt cavern.  During the lull of distant thunder, every surface breeze died, sealing the flat beneath a still dome of expectant silence. 

Vane pressed the record switch and extended his hand into the rising column of subterranean breath.  Synthetic replicas had failed in his laboratory, and any wire mount or metal clamp damped the fragile shell's vibration to a dead hiss.  To sing cleanly, the ovoid had to ride untethered inside the updraft.  Feeling the smooth current tug at the speckled curve, Vane parted his fingers in a deliberate release and drew slowly back.  Cradled midair by the rushing jet, the hollow egg spun and trembled, pouring a clear, fluted note from its tiny aperture across the gleaming expanse.  Instantly the exposed salt ridges and hidden chambers woke in answer.  One solitary pitch multiplied into overlapping waves of silver sound, until the empty plain rang with the phantom chorus of a vanished flock soaring through ruined light.  Vane closed his eyes, drinking in that brief, impossible resurrection while the recorder needles danced.  Then the storm inhaled, the cavern's breath faltered, and the spinning shell began to drop.  Lunging forward to catch it would have scuffed the crust and marred the fading reverberation still shimmering across the microphones, so Vane stood motionless with his hands at his sides.  The egg struck the hard salt in a whisper of pale dust, leaving only its pure, ghosted echo lingering across the white waste as the first cold rain began to fall.
