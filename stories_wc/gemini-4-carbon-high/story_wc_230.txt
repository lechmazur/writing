High in the hanging orchard, Oren knelt upon a swaying iron grating and tested the wet limestone soil with chilled fingers.  All around his suspended terraces of inoculated hazelnut trees, the narrow ravine echoed with thunder, its slate walls battered by storms that swept relentlessly inland from the coast.  As a rational truffle forager, he had engineered these aerial root cradles for pure agricultural logic, knowing that suspended gravel drained torrential rain fast enough to keep prized black fungi from rotting.  Yet six years after the empathy virus rewired human neurology, the gorge served an even more urgent purpose, isolating him and Mara from the suffocating emotional din of the lowland towns. 

Solitude alone could not cure what the pathogen had done to their minds.  The infection had fused visual recognition directly to the limbic cortex, leaving every survivor involuntarily truthful the instant two gazes met.  No passing ugliness or animal panic could be filtered before reaching the tongue.  During a fierce gale the previous night, when steel anchor cables groaned against the cliff, Oren had glanced across their stone cabin at Mara and heard his own voice coldly calculate how long the flour would last if her winter cough killed her.  Tears spilling, she had stared back and confessed a fleeting wish that the canyon wind would tear the whole orchard loose so she would stop feeling his constant terror.  Neither of them endorsed those stray shadows of an exhausted brain, yet forced candor turned every storm into an interrogation.  Oren wanted nothing more than to cultivate psychological safety within their home, restoring the quiet mercy of choosing which thoughts deserved speech. 

Sheltering beneath a dripping cedar beam near the cabin door, he drew a brass tin from his coat and unwrapped a smuggled relic of the old world.  Resting on damp sphagnum moss lay an antique glass eye, blown by a nineteenth century artisan from dense potash and lead crystal, its painted iris a pale, luminous hazel.  Lowland wardens treated masks, dark spectacles, and prosthetics as heretical contraband, crushing any artifact designed to hide human expression, so Oren had bartered four pounds of rare winter truffles to slip this fragile shell past the river checkpoints.  For a man devoted to soil chemistry and verifiable data, staking their peace on such an object felt uncomfortably close to superstition.  He had discovered the idea only through rumored cures in discredited textbooks, salvaged in mildewed bundles from an academic pulping barge two autumns ago.  Orthodox physicians had mocked those early outbreak monographs, dismissing their claim that the viral feedback loop required light striking both optic nerves simultaneously while an observer perceived an intact gaze.  Ordinary blindfolds or leather patches always failed because visible concealment alarmed the hijacked mirror neurons of a partner, provoking even wilder spirals of involuntary dread. 

Oren stepped into the cramped vestibule and tilted a cracked mirror toward the grey squall.  Three years earlier, a snapped support wire in the lower orchard had lashed his left eye, shrinking the globe into a scarred remnant that perceived no shapes yet still absorbed enough stray brightness to ignite the neural compulsion.  If the ridiculed authors were right, he could occlude that damaged receptor beneath heavy leaded glass, starving the reflex of light while offering Mara a calm, unthreatening face.  He rinsed the curved shell in boiled rainwater, steadied his breath against the roar of wind funneling up the ravine, and drew back his scarred eyelid.  With the same deliberate precision he used to unearth delicate mycelium from chalky stones, he slid the cold crystal rim beneath his brow to occlude every last photon.  A dull ache bloomed behind his temple, followed by a profound, asymmetrical stillness, as though a screaming wire inside his skull had finally been snipped. 

He lifted the latch and entered the warm room where Mara sat mending a willow harvest basket beside the stove.  Thunder rattled the shuttered panes, and she looked up reluctantly, flinching in advance of whatever raw cruelty the tempest might wrench from their mouths.  Their eyes met across the narrow floorboards.  Sharp anxieties still flickered through Oren, grim estimates of rusted turnbuckles and flooded roots, yet his jaw remained mercifully under his own command.  Mara studied the mismatched, painted gleam resting in his left socket, her shoulders softening in wonder as no compulsive confession tore through her chest to answer him.  "The hazelnut cradles held fast against the wind, and we are safe tonight," Oren said softly, offering the deeper truth he chose to keep, while rain drummed like forgiveness upon the roof.
