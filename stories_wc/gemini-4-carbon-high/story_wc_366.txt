Along the grey archipelago, Bram worked thirty winters as a reticent lighthouse supply skipper who spent scarcely a dozen spoken words between one gale and the next.  Silence suited him, yet his warmly observant gaze missed nothing along the circuit, noting frayed cuffs on a young keeper, an untouched ration tin, or the faint tremor in fresh ink.  Beside his wheelhouse compass rested a waxed canvas pouch carrying an anonymous letter chain from rock to lonely rock aboard his launch.  Years earlier, a snowbound tender had sealed a comforting note inside an amber bottle for the next island, only for winter surf to shatter it and cast back one smooth piece of sea glass.  Keepers thereafter trusted their unsigned pages to Bram, keeping that frosted amber shard inside the pouch while still trying to bottle the feeling of safety in quiet accounts of dry wool, rising bread, and sheltered coves. 

This murky November day, the maritime board had retired Bellwether Light on the noon slack tide, leaving just enough oil burning through the haze to bridge afternoon traffic until a new automated buoy woke at nightfall.  On the harbor pier before Bram sailed out, the evacuated keeper had returned the mail pouch, and Bram recognized his slanted script in an anonymous addition mourning the unseen friend left behind inside the haunted lantern room.  Bram knew well who lingered up there.  Old Mara had lost her hearing to the steam foghorn decades back, so she and young Bram had devised a way of stacking stones as messages along the jetty and high gallery, where trays of flat shingle still sat.  Twenty winters after her burial, living keepers still felt icy drafts turned gently from their necks and saw twin ovals of melted frost open beside them on the curved panes. 

Passing Bellwether in gathering twilight, Bram raised his brass spyglass toward the dimming tower and spotted three wet slates newly leaned inward upon the upper rail, forming their old stone warning for failing light and mortal danger.  Mara had poured out both life and death to protect every hull on these shoals, and Bram could not abandon her to panic; tonight, someone had to protect her in turn.  He brought his launch into the leeward cleft, tied fast to the slick iron ring, and climbed the hollow spiral stairs carrying the canvas pouch under his arm. 

When he entered the haunted lantern room, the starved wick held only a trembling blue bead inside the still lens, and within seconds that thin spark wavered and died.  Every keeper knew the hollow dread that claims a tower after the lamp flickers out, yet tonight that grief belonged to the dead.  In the sudden dark, sharp frost raced across the glass while spare signal stones inside the sill box rattled and spilled as Mara darted unseen from pane to pane, frantic for ships she believed were sailing blind.  Half a mile seaward, the new channel buoy began blinking its stark white strobe every five seconds, too surgical and brief to convince an old watcher that the reef remained guarded. 

Bram did not speak, knowing a woman deafened in life had no use for spoken words now.  Noticing where the distant flash sliced across the eastern sill, he knelt first beside the cold brass pedestal and spread the pages of the anonymous letter chain in a pale circle around the dead burner.  Generations of unsigned keepers surrounded her there with quiet proof of schooners anchored whole and crews sleeping safe behind latched doors.  Gathering fallen cobbles from the iron deck, Bram then built his reply upon the eastern sill in their shared stone code: two flat basalts for harbor, bridged by wide slate meaning all vessels sheltered.  At the peak of the cairn, squarely in the path of the offshore beam, he wedged the thick amber piece of sea glass.  When the white strobe swept the lantern next, the frosted shard drank that harsh glare and spilled a warm, honeyed radiance over the stacked stones and open letters, filling the glass room with the gentle gold of old oil flame.  The rattling pebbles fell still, and beside his woolen sleeve two soft ovals melted through the rime on the pane, leaning close to the amber pulse.  Out of broken glass, borrowed light, and strangers sharing ink, Bram had managed at last to bottle the feeling of safety for the watcher who spent everything she had upon the sea, and he stayed quietly beside her while that small luminous heart beat against the night.
