After the carousel halts for the night, the roaring turbines beneath the kinetic lagoon above the clouds subside into a fractured stillness.  High over the pale stratospheric mist, the iron basin holds acres of restless, electrified water that never goes wholly flat, breaking instead into silent geometric panes that twitch with residual charge.  Along the dripping perimeter catwalk, Arthur moves as a resolute scavenger, dragging his hooked mesh pole through the intake grates with practiced patience.  Decades of breathing the ionized vapor off the water have left him chronologically confused, untethered from any reliable sequence of years.  When he glances at his scarred knuckles on the brass rail, he feels startled by their age, half convinced that the foundational survey finished yesterday and that winter will arrive before noon. 

Forty summers ago, or perhaps next Tuesday in his tangled mind, Arthur designed this suspended reservoir to power a gleaming research metropolis in the upper air.  Timid investors and brutal atmospheric winds whittled that vision down until his eroded ambitions survived only as a novelty pier, where airship tourists paid silver to ride painted tin seahorses above the storm tops.  Rather than descend in disgrace, Arthur stayed beneath the decking, salvaging copper filaments, dropped coins, and waterlogged trinkets from the currents.  His prized creation rests inside his coat pocket: a small magnetic wire recorder cobbled from dynamo scrap and fitted with a notched disposable camera wheel pried from a sunken tourist casing.  Because the tiny plastic ratchet was built to advance film in a single direction, it clicks strictly onward, preventing Arthur from accidentally spooling backward whenever his grasp on time slips. 

Now that the last passenger dirigible has vanished into the cloud bank, blue static gathers by the central pylon and knits into a lean young man wearing a draftsman's apron.  On the night the city blueprints were canceled, the kinetic reservoir captured an electrostatic imprint of Arthur's younger fury, cleaving his memory in two.  Every evening, Arthur walks across the hushed boardwalk to negotiate with his own ghost, seeking the severed anchor of his timeline so he can remember his life in order and finally pilot a skiff down to solid ground. 

"Open the emergency sluice and sink this carnival," the phantom says, his voice sharp against the glassy hush of the water.  "Drain the basin, and I will give you back your years." 

In earlier seasons, Arthur tried arguing engineering tolerances, only to falter when his disordered mind mistook past compromises for future plans.  Tonight he tries a new offer, having built his case by collecting fragments of overheard stories that drifted through the carousel floorboards while the turbines spun.  Arthur sets the brass box on a bollard and presses his thumb against the disposable camera wheel until it yields one crisp click.  From the taut copper spool comes the crackle of wind and a widow's quiet voice, recounting to her grandson how a harbor fire took everything her family owned before she whispers that the sunlit cloud sea below makes loss feel survivable.  Arthur thumbs the serrated plastic rim forward another notch, releasing the laughter of two estranged brothers trading half a tale about an orchard storm as lagoon spray hit their coats.  A third click plays a trembling sailor describing a shipwreck he survived, breaking off in awe at the horizon.  Each salvaged snippet hangs briefly in the thin cold air, fragile ripples disturbing the dead calm between machine and sky. 

The luminous phantom stares down at his shimmering boots, his fierce posture wavering for the first time.  The ghost asks, "Do you expect me to believe these scraps justify the towers we never raised?" 

Arthur looks across the dark, twitching mirror of the basin and realizes that false pride will never buy his freedom.  "They do not replace the city," Arthur replies, allowing the full weight of regret to steady his voice.  "I regret every unbuilt spire, and I regret even more the forty years I squandered hiding in the rafters instead of living in the world that remained."  He nudges the recorder toward the glowing figure, offering his final terms: he will spare the lagoon for the strangers who still need its view, carry the grief of their failed blueprint himself, and never pretend the compromise was painless. 

For a long breath, only distant static ticks across the quiet water.  Then the young draftsman reaches out, advances the plastic wheel one last tooth forward, and dissolves into Arthur's ribs like cold rain.  Memory snaps cleanly into place from youth to old age, leaving yesterday behind him and dawn ahead as he walks toward the descent skiff.
