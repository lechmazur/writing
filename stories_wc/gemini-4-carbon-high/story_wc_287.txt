Four stories above the wharf cobbles, Clara tends her crates as a fire escape gardener, coaxing sage and winter kale from narrow boxes bolted to the iron slats.  Below her landing stretches a luminous harbor by moonlight, its calm tide doubling the autumn moon into a broad sheet of silver.  There is a protected vulnerability to everything she keeps on this high perch, where tender roots sit swaddled in burlap behind black iron bars.  All season, Arthur's tangled climber has claimed the seaward corner post, and she has vowed to alter nothing until after the final petal drops from the old rosebush.  Tonight, loosened by the November chill, that last crimson curl detaches from its stem and drifts into the shadows below. 

Six months of widowhood have made Clara wistfully pragmatic about endings.  She presses one dry leaf into her pocket for Arthur's memory, then lifts her oiled shears to cut back the dead canes before frost rot reaches the crown.  Clearing the corner opens a narrow sightline around the brick wall into the tenement airshaft, where her young son Jonas lies awake behind his parted sash.  Ever since the spring gale took his father at the sea gates, the boy has dreaded the starless shaft that swallows his room once autumn dies.  Kerosene costs too much to burn past supper, and Clara also shelters her tender rosemary cuttings on the sunless sill outside his glass.  To answer both needs, she has salvaged a beveled washstand mirror and an iron clamp, determined to reflect light into dark corners that the sky forgets. 

As she kneels on the cold grating, a quivering hush settles between the trembling bay and the breathless brick shaft.  The silence feels taut as wire, vibrating faintly with the tide against the pilings while Jonas listens from under his wool quilt for any sudden clank of metal.  Sharp noises in the night still remind him of the harbor alarm bell ringing through rain.  Down on the silver channel, the watchman's skiff glides past the piers, its wooden blades dipping and lifting in steady measure.  Clara paces every snip of her shears with the rhythm of oars in water, cutting on the low splash below and pausing through the quiet glide.  She wants the gentle cadence to instill a durable calm in her son, proving that night holds working patterns rather than chaos. 

Once the thorny stems lie bundled for kindling, she wipes salt haze from the glass and fits the iron clamp onto the corner stanchion.  Yet decades of sea spray have pitted the top rail into an uneven slope.  Each time she tightens the thumbscrew, the heavy mirror sags a fraction of an inch, throwing its pale beam high against blind mortar instead of Jonas's sill.  She tries wedging a pruned rose twig beneath the bracket jaw, only to feel the brittle wood crush flat.  A sliver of slate from a drainage pot slips straight off the damp iron and vanishes into the alley.  Breath misting in the cold, Clara reaches deep into her canvas apron and fingers the lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth she has carried on twine since April.  Snapped long ago from the notched brass tool Arthur used to read key cuts for the harbor locks, the tiny piece bears a flat, exact step machined to a sixteenth of an inch.  Untying the string, she hooks that notched brass shoulder directly over the rusted lip beneath the mirror mount. 

The precision wedge bites cleanly into the pitted iron without sliding.  Still timing her wrist to the distant oars crossing the bay, Clara turns the thumbscrew three slow strokes until the brass holds fast.  Instantly, the wide silver radiance of the water catches the glass and bends around the brick corner into the shaft.  Cool, shimmering bands wash across the rosemary leaves on the sill and glide through the raised window to paint wavering ripples across Jonas's ceiling.  Around the corner, she hears the rustle of quilts as the boy props himself up, letting out a long, unshaken breath at the harbor's borrowed glow.  The fragile stillness in the courtyard softens from dread into wonder, holding mother and child inside the same silvered quiet.  Clara packs dry straw over the pruned rose roots for winter, rests a gloved thumb against the tiny brass tooth bearing the mirror's weight, and goes inside to sleep.
