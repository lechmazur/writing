Oren carried two leather valises across the marble lobby with the poised grace of a former cathedral tenor.  Outside the arched windows, relentless blizzards buried the cobalt fortress under snow.  High in the border pass, the blue citadel now served as a neutral hotel where envoys and officers waited out the war.  After a failed uprising, twenty singers had been spared prison provided an army surgeon deadened their vocal nerves each year with freezing phenol.  Rendered incapable of treasonous hymns or gossip, Oren worked here as a bellhop of the silent choir.  Trained for sacred processions, he and his brothers performed chores that were seraphically mundane, brushing sleet from collars, aligning brass keys, and bowing without a sound.  Yet March had arrived, and during nerve regeneration, the lean week before the surgeon returned with fresh needles, splinters of heat woke in his throat and brought back the ragged ghost of his voice. 

Tomorrow morning the entire staff would submit to the syringe again, yet Oren walked the dark halls past midnight because he needed to understand the cost of safety.  Three times that winter, refugees lacking papers had begged shelter from storms, and each morning the Warden claimed they had departed before daybreak, though unbroken drifts sealed the gates.  Yesterday a hunted pianist named Mira had checked in alone, and tonight her room stood stripped and empty while her wet leather boots still steamed beside the mudroom stove.  Carrying a shaded tallow lamp into the deserted music parlor, Oren noticed the cushioned bench beside the concert grand tilting crookedly on three legs.  At dusk he had seen Mira anxiously twisting a loose piano bench bolt beneath the seat while studying the locked keys, working the thick iron fastener free as her sole weapon.  Lowering his lamp to the wainscoting, he found that heavy piano bench bolt lying beside a fresh gouge in the oak panel concealing the ruined north keep. 

Oren slipped the threaded iron into his pocket and eased through the panel, where three unheated turret stairs coiled upward into blackness.  Whoever had dragged Mira from the parlor had carefully swept fallen mortar grit back across their bootprints to hide which tower they climbed.  Holding his lamp low, Oren found the trail via the slightest discoloration on a dusty spiral staircase, where brushed grey powder lay thin over the dark cobalt stone underneath.  He followed those faint cerulean smudges to the windblown summit, where an iron grille sealed a narrow watch cell.  Inside huddled Mira in a thin wool shawl, shivering violently, her wrists bound with braided linen cord from the hotel laundry.  Scratched into the blue masonry around her were thirty dated names of vanished guests, proving the Warden bought immunity for his hotel by trading trapped fugitives to dawn patrols. 

Oren stared at the carved wall as a revulsion colder than glacier wind settled into his chest.  The choir had comforted itself with the belief that surrendering their voices harmed nobody else, yet averting their eyes from abandoned boots had bound them all in an unpardonable silence.  Drawing the heavy piano bench bolt from his pocket, Oren wedged its square head deep into the frozen iron staple of the cell latch and threw his weight downward until the brittle metal snapped.  He sawed her cords apart on the jagged bracket, led her down to reclaim her warm boots, and wrapped his thick bellhop coat around her shoulders.  At the leeward coal postern, where scoured granite dropped toward the timberline trail, Mira gripped his sleeve and begged in a raw whisper for him to flee beside her. 

Oren looked down at the sheltering pines, then back up toward the dormitories where nineteen fellow choristers slept in ignorance before their morning injections.  If he fled now, ski guards roused by his empty post would overtake Mira before dawn, and the surgeon would numb the rest of the choir into another winter of blind complicity.  Closing his fist around the cold piano bench bolt like an iron tuning fork, Oren shook his head and chose to remain.  He watched Mira vanish safely among the shadowy ledges, barred the postern from within, and climbed toward the vaulted atrium where every singer and foreign envoy would gather at first light.  His waking throat burned like thawed flesh against the certainty of chains, yet as he breathed a first clear note into the freezing stone dark, his chest rose with a fierce, frostbitten hope.
