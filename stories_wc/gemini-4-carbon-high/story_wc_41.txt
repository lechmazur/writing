Along the salt peninsula, people still called Jonas the junkyard prophet, though years had passed since anyone climbed the rusted hills of his salvage yard seeking an omen.  His gift worked backward from ordinary sight: he was precognitively forgetful, losing his memory of any object or person shortly before time broke or claimed it.  Once, fishermen brought him cracked engine blocks or tarnished wedding bands, watching his eyes for the sudden blankness that foretold a wreck or a parting.  Now the townsfolk had moved inland, and Jonas lived alone amid the hush of old videotapes left unwatched, his corrugated shack insulated with crates of discarded black cassettes that nobody cared to reclaim.  Among those silent plastic bricks sat reels of his own vanished youth, unplayed because static images could only mock the slow, rhythmic pull of the sea outside his door. 

Beyond the scrap heaps stretched the tidal flats where Jonas had spent thirty years welding his fog sculpture garden.  Dozens of tall frames built from split fenders, brass boiler tubes, and bedsprings rose from the wet silt to carve incoming ocean mist into drifting, ghostly figures.  Most evenings he wandered through that labyrinth clinging to echoes, pressing his ear against cold steel chambers that trapped and repeated the distant clang of harbor bells or the cries of departed gulls.  Yet echoes merely rehearsed what had already passed, leaving a hollow ache in his chest that swelled and receded with every tide.  What drove him back to the salt marsh was a deeper yearning: he wanted to capture the sound of growing older, the live and unrepeatable sigh of matter yielding to years.  Decades earlier, he had built one hollow column among the sculptures with iron walls rolled thin as paper, designed to corrode until a high tide compressed the air inside and parted its seam in a single resonant chord. 

Tonight, as equinox fog rolled ashore, Jonas reached for his coat and froze at a sudden void inside his mind.  He remembered every outer arch of the garden, yet the shape and place of that aging iron column had vanished completely, along with the memory of how his own voice had sounded that morning.  The twin erasures meant only one thing: before high water, both the forgotten sculpture and his own weathered body would cross an irreversible threshold into frailty.  Taking a pocket audio recorder and a piece of plain chalk from a salvaged schoolhouse desk, Jonas waded into the blinding whiteout to locate the missing monument before peak tide split it unheard. 

Tons of magnetized scrap rendered any compass useless, and dense vapor shrank his world to the length of an arm.  He searched by mapping the borders of his amnesia.  Whenever a familiar silhouette loomed through the mist, he drew a bold white cross upon its rusted flank with the chalk so he would not circle back to monuments whose futures remained intact.  Between those marked towers, he forced himself to ignore the comforting, familiar reverberations of surf and listen instead for an unrepeated metallic strain.  Each time a faint groan reached him through the shifting gray curtains, he knelt on the wet sand, arranging driftwood as directional markers to anchor the bearing before the next wave or lapse of memory swept his orientation away. 

Cold brine swirled around his ankles, retreating with a whispered pull toward the deep only to return heavier and higher.  Gradually his trail of bleached sticks converged upon an unmapped hollow at the heart of the flats.  There, sculpting the dense mist into a bowed, breathing torso, stood a tall cylinder of corroded sheet iron that Jonas did not recognize at all.  Its utter strangeness proved he had found his work.  The wide drum hummed evenly in the wind, concealing where the metal was ready to fail, and his chalk had worn down to a crumbly stub.  Calling on an old junkyard habit, he rubbed the white stub broadly across the leeward curve until pressurized rust water seeping from within stained a hairline fault straight down the pale streak. 

Jonas pressed record, held the microphone beside that darkening vein, and rested his cheek against the trembling shell.  Surf rose to his knees, tugging seaward with a slow tidal longing as rising water compressed the trapped air inside the drum.  The corroded seam parted in a low, crystalline sigh that wove into the rasping hum of his own throat, neither an echo of yesterday nor a dead image, but change itself caught in full breath.  As mist washed the last chalk into the water, Jonas stood still in the ebb and let the tape turn.
