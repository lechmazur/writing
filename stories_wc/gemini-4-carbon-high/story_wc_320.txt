Maren tightened the grounding clamp on her copper railing and looked out across the abandoned radio tower city suspended over a gorge.  Beneath her insulated rubber boots, greased suspension cables wide as tree trunks held forty rusting lattice spires and tin relay shacks steady above the black chasm.  She worked alone during lunar perigee, when the swollen silver moon hung low enough to press upper sky currents straight down between the basalt cliffs.  That heavy tidal pull made the night air serenely electric, coating tarnished guy wires in pale blue glow without a single harsh spark.  As a celestial signal interpreter, Maren valued nights like this for sheer mechanical thrift, since the close moon bent distant starlight into her parabolic dishes for free.  She poured hot chicory tea from a dented flask, blew gently across the tin rim, and stepped inside her swaying listening booth. 

Five winters earlier, the northern Vela cluster had collapsed into cold ash, and her guild had packed its quartz receivers and left the span.  Her colleagues saw only a dead sector choked by old canyon echoes, yet Maren stayed behind to find the beginning in every ending.  Just as she had turned a derelict switch house into a dry home warmed by surplus resistor coils, she trusted that ruined stars gathered their own wreckage into fresh suns.  Proving it required catching a whisper from the Vela ash cloud through miles of roaring static.  The gorge below acted like an acoustic funnel, trapping stray broadcast ghosts from vanished human cities alongside the lingering hiss of stellar collapse. 

On her workbench sat her latest gamble, a Hollywood star fragment bought from coastal salt diggers for three spooling motors and a jar of solder.  The relic was a jagged wedge of coral pink stone inlaid with a strip of tarnished brass that formed one sharp point and part of a stamped name.  Standard sympathetic craft called for meteoric iron in the bridge circuit, since sky metal naturally drew sky voices.  Too poor for true meteorite, Maren had wired her alligator clips onto the ancient brass point, betting that an artificial star built by legendary broadcasters would amplify faint light.  She slipped on her leather headphones, opened the main relay switch, and began to tune the primary dish toward the Vela ash cloud.  Instantly a deafening squall of canyon static and lunar hum slammed her eardrums, while acrid smoke curled from the rosin around the brass contact. 

Wincing, Maren cut the power, pulled the headphones down around her collar, and stared hard at the smoking chunk of stone.  Every manual she owned insisted on forcing more voltage through metal conductors to overpower interference, yet brute amplification only fed the roar.  If she wanted a clean reading before the moon climbed away from the gorge, she had to rebuild her circuit by discarding assumptions.  Tilting her oil lamp closer, she examined the coral pink surface under a magnifying lens and noticed thousands of tiny heel scuffs ground into the polished aggregate.  This emblem had never hung on a tower to project noise; it had rested flat in a sidewalk, walked over for ages after the mortal performer it named fell silent.  Under sympathetic law, centuries of ended applause had cured that scuffed masonry into a shard of hush, a solid wedge of aftermath that absorbed clamor instead of broadcasting it.  That realization exposed her second error: she had been hunting for the crackle of ignition, forgetting that before any fire catches, the hearth draws a quiet, inward breath. 

Working briskly with needle pliers, Maren unclipped the leads from the brass trim and clamped them straight onto the dull pink stone.  She reversed the phase coils on her receiver so the circuit would measure acoustic drag rather than voltage spikes.  Outside the frosted window, the suspended towers hummed in the serenely electric breeze while lunar perigee reached its peak directly overhead.  Settling the headphones back over her ears, she began to tune her vernier dials downward, steering past the shrieking canyon ghosts into the lowest trough of the band.  The Hollywood star fragment drank the static like dry mortar drinking rain, carving a crisp, sheltered pocket of silence right through the clamor.  At the very bottom of that quiet, her brass needle dipped in a slow, steady cadence.  Deep inside the ruined Vela cloud, cold dust had begun spiraling inward under fresh gravity, silent and dense and unmistakable, gathering mass for its first future blaze.  Maren locked the dial setscrew, reached for her grease pencil, and logged the birth coordinates on a clean page before finishing her warm tea.
