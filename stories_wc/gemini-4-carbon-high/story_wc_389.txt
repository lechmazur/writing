In his stormlit observatory above clouds of greasy coal smoke, Oswin boiled turnip peels over a sputtering spirit lamp and listened to the sky clear its throat.  Being a lonely visionary paid poorly in firewood, though it compensated him handsomely in bottled ozone and uninterrupted views of eternity.  He wore mismatched rubber galoshes to keep stray voltage out of his toes and shared the rusted iron dome with three whistling vacuum tubes named after his dead aunts.  At the western rim of the tower jutted an antique cobalt glass portico, framed in tarnished lead and cantilevered boldly over the boiling tempest.  Centuries before the lower valleys forgot the sun, nameless glassblowers had etched those blue panes with fables of toppled tyrants, generous harvests, and clever beasts.  Whenever lightning split the thunderheads, fierce indigo silhouettes stretched inward across the greasy floorboards, and Oswin studied them with watering eyes, driven to shape a new dawn using ancient shadows. 

Reaching the smogged cities miles below required cunning, because the perpetual storm chewed any steady signal into meaningless static.  Oswin solved this obstacle by whispering through fragmented radio frequencies, pedaling a salvaged bicycle dynamo while spinning his brass tuning dial across three dozen broken bands a minute.  Plain speeches perished in the ionospheric crackle, yet cadence and metaphor slipped cleanly between the thunderclaps.  Therefore he translated the dancing blue silhouettes into crooked couplets that sounded lyrically prophetic, singing of iron towers melting like tallow and seeds cracking cobblestones.  Down in the cinder alleys, a young foundry sweeper named Malachi began catching those scattered verses on a homemade crystal receiver wired to a bedspring.  Nobody guessed that a wheezing hermit in galoshes stood behind the miracle, keeping lonely watch across the rise of a prophet. 

During the first lean winter, Malachi chalked the airborne riddles onto factory walls, stitching three words from forty megahertz to five words from ninety until starving workers heard a coherent promise.  Listening to intercepted police chatter on his tin headphones, Oswin beamed through his frosted beard and decided the boy earned deeper parables.  He bundled himself in frayed wool, grabbed an oilcan, and shuffled out into the cobalt glass portico while hailstones drummed a frantic waltz overhead.  By cranking a rusted worm gear, he made the outer leaded shutters unfold like the wings of a mechanical beetle, exposing recessed panes that had not tasted stormlight in three hundred years.  Furthermore, the angled lead frames bent his antenna waves in fresh patterns, letting forgotten silhouettes of shared loaves and broken fetters spill across his transmitter desk.  Fueled by the verses Oswin crooned from those projections, Malachi marched ten thousand singing strikers through the Magistracy gates before autumn ended. 

Yet triumph proved heavier than soot, and by the second summer the humble sweeper had draped himself in velvet, crowning himself High Mouthpiece of the Thunder.  Malachi outlawed unauthorized crystal sets, locked questioning poets in iron cages, and broadcast a booming demand up into the clouds, ordering his unseen angel to speak solely on one official military band.  Up in the rafters of the sky, Oswin sighed, salted his watery turnip broth, and tapped the glass belly of Aunt Mildred until her filament glowed steady amber.  Long ago he had sworn himself to variant allegiance, a stubborn creed holding that fidelity belonged never to a ruler, only to the next necessary awakening.  Whenever a liberator grew fond of chains, devotion had to jump frequencies just like a rebel song dodging static. 

Braving a midnight squall that smelled of burnt pennies, Oswin crept back onto the slick cobalt glass portico with a crowbar wedged under his arm.  With numb knuckles he pried loose the highest, oldest transom, watching its frosted blue medallions unfold into the teeth of the gale like a lotus blooming in hellfire.  Violet lightning blazed overhead, hurling wilder figures across the observatory floor: a laughing magpie stealing a scepter, a cracked throne sprouting dandelions, and children dancing on rubble.  Leaving the official military channel dead and silent, Oswin hopped back onto his bicycle seat and pedaled furiously, scattering subversive nursery rhymes through fragmented radio frequencies that Malachi and his censors could never scrub clean.  Down in damp cellars, outlawed crystal sets began to tick and hum again as fresh dissidents pieced together the riddle of a prophet grown hollow.  Grinning behind his copper microphone, his rubber boots squeaking on the pedals, Oswin sang to the dark below until morning began to mend itself.
