Ten autumns had passed on the orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches, bringing the colony into the quiet, scarred era after strangers become family.  When their battered lifeboats first dropped into this luminous nebula of respite, hiding from a distant war inside violet clouds of stellar dust, the survivors had hoarded rations behind bolted doors and spoken six suspicious tongues.  Years of nursing orphans through spore fevers and pulling stubborn roots from acidic loam had bound those wary refugees into kin, though survival beneath the magnetic sky spans remained jagged and unforgiving.  Now a cold shift in the cosmic mist had starved the terraced valleys of rain for forty days.  Worse, when Jora and her field crews diverted river water into deep irrigation trenches, the native pear trees sealed their blossoms inside ironclad husks of black thorns.  Convinced that blight had killed the crop, the council voted to fell half the groves at noon for winter firewood. 

Only Ansel refused to surrender the trees.  Once the quiet potter and pollinator keeper of the settlement, he had fallen from a slick limestone arch three harvests back, yet he lingered as an altruistic ghost rather than dissolving into painless starlight.  Each touch of solid matter burned his thinning spirit like lye, and he lacked breath to speak aloud or strength to lift heavy iron in daylight.  Instead, he spent the freezing hours before sunrise scraping soft white stone across flagstones, guiding his living kin only through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn.  When Jora stepped outside carrying a sharpened felling axe, pale diagrams stretched down the empty street beneath the violet glow of the sky.  Phantom markings might have sparked panic in an older, fractured camp, but earlier warnings about leaking cisterns and spoiled seed sacks had proven this unseen watchman strangely trustworthy. 

Lowering her axe, Jora knelt over the central sketch.  Slanting script begged the stewards to reexamine both the thorny buds and the forgotten butterfly garden watering bell hanging inside the dusty glasshouse near the square.  Jora hurried to the shed, unhooked the bell, and carried it back to the chalked cross sections of roots and hovering stone arches.  Crafted by Ansel before his fall, the terracotta dome had a narrow thumb vent on its stem, pinhole perforations across its flat base, and a tiny copper clapper suspended inside.  Studying the botanical notes beside the clay vessel, she grasped their terrible mistake at last.  On a steep world prone to violent mudslides, drenched soil triggered the orchard trees to lock their flowers inside defensive brambles.  The buds unfurled only when greeted by two gentle cues together: a featherlight mist like condensation dripping from overhead rock spans, and the faint chime frequency that mimics native glasswing swarms.  High inside the porous basins of the floating arches, trapped nebula dew and dormant butterflies were still waiting, prevented from spilling downward only by the dry seasonal wind. 

Yet one woman holding a single butterfly garden watering bell could never wake four hundred acres of armored boughs before winter set in.  Following the white lines down the brightening lanes, Jora realized why Ansel had spent every shred of his spectral strength before daybreak.  The sprawling mural branched to every doorway in town, meticulously planned to orchestrate collective effort across every pair of calloused hands.  Outside the kilns lay exact proportions for firing sixty new clay bells by midday, while rope trajectories before the climbers hall mapped safe ascents to seven sky basins.  Even the flagstones by the infirmary and schoolhouse bore numbered orchard rows where elders and children could carry pails and release the gentle vacuum rain. 

Dropping her blade, Jora struck the iron assembly gong until sleepy faces filled every porch.  Nobody asked for guarantees, for shared grief had taught them to move as one household whenever a slim chance appeared.  By afternoon, potters pulled hot terracotta vessels from smoking embers while climbers dangled beneath magnetic stone arches, scraping their knees raw to lower canvas hoses brimming with cold sky water.  Shoulder to shoulder in the tangled aisles, villagers filled the bells, sealed the vents with blistered thumbs, and reached into the hostile canopy to loose soft showers and copper chimes.  Sharp spines tore sleeves and drew blood across outstretched wrists before the black husks split open, unfurling pale blossoms as awakened butterflies spiraled down from the heights.  Runoff soon washed the chalk dust from the stones as a faint shimmer dissolved peacefully into twilight.  Pressing her bleeding palm to a living trunk, Jora welcomed the brambled hope of a sanctuary that demanded scars yet kept her family fed.
