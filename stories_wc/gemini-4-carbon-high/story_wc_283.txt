Deep in the amber warehouse beneath cliffs of sealed limestone, Oren works inside an unearthed silence so complete that falling sand sounds like rain.  Without sun or stars reaching the subterranean city, every baker, weaver, and resin miner depends upon him as the night hourglass turner to measure the dark watches.  Around his walnut desk rise towering blocks of fossilized golden resin cut from the mountain roots, glowing faintly beside crates of dry silt hauled from high drainage fissures.  Centuries of friction polish old quartz grains too smooth and make the great glasses run fast, so Oren spends each solitary shift sifting fresh canyon silt through fine copper mesh. 

On a stone plinth beside the central sand clock rests the sacred canyon echo in a jar.  Three hundred years ago, when a tempest and rockslide choked the surface pass, an archivist sealed that hollow vessel while its inner lining of warm amber remained soft enough to trap sound vibrations.  Whenever Oren presses a brass ear trumpet against the curved amber belly, four faint voices murmur inside, each preceded by a solemn call of Bone, Horn, Shell, or Iron.  Flowing together in the order they strike the ear, those voices recite the grim law of the city: "Dread the sky; our kin must remain entombed, and never rise from the vaults; swear never to break the cliff seal."  City elders teach that four ancient Fates spoke this warning, binding every generation to harvest resin in obedient gloom.  Watching pale apprentices haul heavy amber sleds past his door, Oren aches to spin a prophecy into raw possibility, yet he sees no flaw in the sacred words. 

His only solace comes from small relics caught in his copper sieve while refining silt from the numbered fissure crates.  Preserved in dry amber dust lie dozens of cloak fasteners torn loose during the ancient gale and washed into crevices at varied heights along the buried gorge.  Earlier keepers tossed such scraps into the waste chute, but Oren feels quietly inspired by these intimate traces of neighbors who once walked beneath open clouds.  Between hourly turns of the tall glasses, he steadies his thoughts by cataloging lost buttons in a linen ledger, recording the material, thread color, and exact fathom depth stamped on each barrel.  Over seasons of patient sorting in the golden hush of the resin stacks, four unmistakable layers appear across his table.  Silt from the highest rim shafts yields only iridescent river shell discs, whereas crates from twenty fathoms down hold carved horn rings, forty fathoms produce polished sheep bone, and the deepest fissures just above the vault yield forged iron studs. 

Tonight, Oren brushes dried clay from an iron stud bearing a tiny watchtower mark, and his breath catches in the cool air.  As the truth unravels across his open ledger, three centuries of dogma fall away in a single heartbeat.  Shell, Horn, Bone, and Iron were never divine Fates, but four cliffside guard stations uniformed in distinct fasteners, descending from the sunlit canyon rim down to the subterranean gate.  During the collapse, jagged limestone bends bounced the middle canyon shouts into the wet amber jar ahead of the distant rim cry, tangling a human relay that traveled from sky to cave.  With trembling fingers, Oren lines up the four button types from highest elevation to lowest, using the true order of Shell, Horn, Bone, and Iron to unscramble the trapped phrases on fresh parchment.  Read from top post to bottom gate, the exact same words transform before his eyes: "Rise from the vaults; swear never to remain entombed, and never dread the sky; our kin must break the cliff seal." 

Tears sting his eyes as he understands that the vessel holds no wrathful curse, only shared wisdom shouted from post to post by ordinary guards guiding their families back toward daylight once the stones settled.  When the morning turner arrives with a crew of quarrymen carrying chisels and lanterns, Oren does not hide his ledger away.  Instead, he sets the shell, horn, bone, and iron fasteners beside the humming jar, hands the brass ear trumpet to the eldest mason, and points down the corrected page.  One by one, the workers listen in hushed wonder, passing the trumpet from hand to calloused hand until quiet nods ripple across the vault.  As the last grain slips through the neck, Oren inverts the heavy glass, no longer counting eternal night, but measuring the first hour of their climb toward the sun.
