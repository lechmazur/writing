Alden climbed the cliff stairs onto his cobalt terrace at dusk, clutching a sodden billet of cedar beneath his wool coat.  Around him, the glazed blue tiles darkened toward slate as twilight settled over the cold strait.  Near the low stone parapet, his grown daughter Mara sat folding dried linen, serene and untroubled by the evening wind.  They had lived a full decade together under the weight of unspoken words.  Before retreating to this lonely coast, Alden had worked in the capital as a guilt-ridden black market genealogist, forging spotless bloodlines for hunted citizens.  In a state where Censorate auditors probed a suspect's mind alongside parish ledgers, ink alone could never save an outcast.  To pass inspection, a true specialist in his illicit trade manufactures false memories, using neural tinctures and whispered suggestion to graft warm, seamless recollections over severed roots.  Alden had hated playing butcher to real histories, yet his hardest work had been performed upon his own child.  Ten years ago, his wife Elena had come home pale from her clerkship at the Hall of Lineages and burned a sealed warrant in their stove.  Before Censorate guards broke down their door, she refused to name her crime so Alden could pass interrogation, begging him instead to excise her from young Mara's mind.  Over his vials that night, Alden replaced a fierce, laughing mother condemned for treason with synthetic memories of a gentle woman lost to winter fever.  The alteration spared Mara from state orphanages, but it left their home wrapped in a sieved hush, a strained quietude from which every jagged grain of real grief had been filtered away. 

Across the water lay the prison sawmills where convicts, denied paper or visitors, whittled secret final testimonies into scrap wood and dropped them into the millrace.  Winter currents dragged the surviving pieces into the perilous, unvisited cove below Alden's cliffs.  Every evening he sought to quell his gnawing torment by sifting through driftwood carvings along a forgotten shore, driven to discover the weight of a withheld truth that Elena had carried into exile. 

Mara gathered her folded linen, noticed the wet timber against his ribs, and asked, "Did the tide bring another sailor's tally?"  "Something like that," Alden answered quietly as she stepped inside the glass doors to fetch the oil lamp.  Alone in the deepening blue light, he set the grey cedar billet upon the iron table.  Guards burned any plank bearing a name, so the carver had etched only a small clock dial with twelve pinpricks, an hour pointer at four, and an empty groove scored at seventeen minutes past.  With trembling fingers, Alden reached into his vest pocket and drew out a bent brass watch hand.  It had snapped from Elena's watch when the patrol forced her against the hearth at four seventeen, the sole relic of her he had dared to keep.  When he laid the tarnished brass into the carved wooden channel, its length and crooked bend matched the cut precisely. 

Beneath the dial ran a cramped row of Registry clerk shorthand, choked white with dried sea salt.  Using the fine point of the watch hand as a pick, Alden carefully scraped the crusted brine from each tiny incision.  One by one, the clerk symbols emerged: Kestrel ledger faulted, claimed your seal, keep Mara whole.  Below Elena's neat marks, a rougher knife had gouged the winter cross that prisoners added before casting a dead cellmate's board to the tide.  Alden braced his palms against the cold cobalt stone as the breath left his lungs.  Elena had joined no rebel plot; she had intercepted an audit tracing a flawed black market lineage Alden sold the Kestrel family, taking his sentence so he would remain free to shield their daughter. 

For years he had imagined that learning her secret might ease his conscience, yet feeling the full measure of her sacrifice anchored him in quiet, irreversible sorrow.  Inside the house a match flared, and Mara returned carrying a lit brass lamp whose amber glow pooled across the terrace.  Looking into her peaceful eyes, Alden felt a fierce impulse to confess everything and beg forgiveness for the mother his carelessness had cost them both.  Instead, he forced himself to quell that selfish hunger for absolution, knowing Elena had died to keep this burden off Mara's shoulders.  He slipped the watch hand back into his pocket, accepting with tempered regret that his penance was to carry their real history alone.  "It is only a watchmaker's note from someone keeping time for a house they could not come home to," Alden said softly.
