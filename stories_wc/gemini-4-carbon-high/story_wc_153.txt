Elias poled his narrow skiff into the mangrove labyrinth inhabited by singing spirits, where black roots arched like flooded cathedral ribs.  High in the salt mist among the leaves, pale shapes hummed overlapping melodies woven from drowned ink and rainwater.  Above these marshes stood an old cliffside town whose people tucked their unsent letters into the stone jaws of chapel gutters, trusting storms to pulp the paper and wash their secret griefs into the tide.  The spirits drank that bitter runoff and sang back every swallowed truth.  Elias moved through their eerie choir in the space where an apology waits unspoken, that fragile hush between a wound and its hardening.  An hour ago in their cliff cottage, his wife Mara had set her valise beside the unlatched door and asked quietly if anything remained of their marriage.  He had stared at her thin shoulders, said nothing, and fled down the steps toward the water. 

At the dead heart of the channels floated a tall houseboat enclosed by clear glass panes, home to Maren, a cryptic medium who traded in riddles because plain answers let proud men keep their armor.  When Elias climbed dripping into her dim cabin, she wrapped his trembling hands in a rough wool shawl while scolding him like a wayward boy.  "A man who rows away from an open door usually brings the lock inside his ribs," Maren murmured, studying his raw eyes with fierce mercy.  Since a winter fever had nearly stolen Mara a year past, Elias had grown flinty and distant, picking small quarrels because loving someone so mortal felt like holding a lit candle in a gale.  Standing at the cottage threshold tonight, he had felt a cold certainty clamp shut inside his chest, convincing him their bond was already dead.  Terrified by that numbness, he begged Maren to dredge up his drowned confessions, desperate to find the moment before deciding to let Mara walk away.  If he could touch the fear beneath his silence before moonrise brought the harbor ferry, perhaps his throat would unfreeze. 

Maren lifted a curved gray stone from a velvet cloth: a gargoyle's rainspout shard, broken years ago from the very chapel jaw where townsfolk wedged their folded pages.  Porous and dark with mineral memory, the limestone had drunk decades of dissolved ink pouring from the cliff.  "Stone remembers the storm, yet only living heat can evoke one household's ghosts from ten thousand singers," she said softly.  Decades of breathing luminous marsh salt had altered Maren so that her flesh heals in moonlight, closing any wound the instant silver rays clear the canopy.  Standing in the deep shadow of the mangrove boughs, she pressed Elias's knuckles to the shard so it knew his grief, then drew its jagged edge across her own weathered palm.  When Elias protested at her pain, she hushed him sharply, willing to bleed in the dark for a love he was nearly too cowardly to save. 

She set the crimsoned stone into a hot brass dish beneath the tall portside pane and held her bleeding hand beside it in the gloom.  Rich vapor hissed from the ancient grooves, fogging the cold glass, while outside the singing spirits surged downward from the branches in a sudden, aching harmony.  Translucent fingers pressed against the exterior hull, tracing swift messages in condensation on windows clouded by the stone's awakened breath.  First appeared Elias's cramped script from unsent letters shoved into the chapel gutter last autumn: I speak harshly only so she will not see my terror whenever she coughs.  Reading it, the iron band around his chest cracked open, revealing the bruised, ferocious devotion he had mistaken for indifference.  Then a second, slanting handwriting bloomed across the wet pane beside his own.  Elias gasped at Mara's unmistakable hand, drawn from pages she too had hidden in that stone throat: He guards me so fiercely he starves beside me, so tonight I pack my bag to shatter our silence before either of us chooses to let go. 

A pale shaft of moonrise pierced the tangled leaves and struck Maren's upturned wrist.  Instantly the crimson line across her palm knitted into smooth, whole skin, the steam thinned, and the fading letters wept clear drops down the glass.  "Vapor only borrows a window, boy, whereas lungs were made to spend their warmth," Maren said, shoving his shoulder gently toward the deck.  Neither of them had crossed into finality yet; both were still standing inside that trembling threshold.  Leaping into his skiff, Elias drove his pole hard into the channel and raced the rising moon toward the cliff steps, ready to spend every fierce, guarded breath at the unlatched door.
