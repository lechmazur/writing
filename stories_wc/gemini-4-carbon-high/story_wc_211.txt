High above the veiled harbor, Cassian never lit his lamp until after the foghorn fell silent.  All evening, its low brass groan shuddered seventy floors upward through the ancient orchard garden atop the skyscraper.  Only at slack tide, when sea mist thickened into a plush, breathless dark among twisted quince and black fig boughs, did those tremors die away.  In that heavy hush, where fallen fruit sweetened the damp moss like spilled wine, the venerable oath scribe unlocked his glass pavilion and opened a cedar box. 

Inside rested a single wax cylinder phonograph shaving, coiled pale and weightless as a shed eyelash.  That afternoon, the tower syndics had stood before his recording horn to renew the bicentennial covenant guarding these rooftop trees from demolition.  By law, the engraved cylinder went straight to the Chancery vaults, while the threadlike spiral pared from its groove stayed in his catch tray.  Cassian was the last port official fluent in Hittite, Akkadian, and Etruscan, translating every civic vow into extinct tongues whose frozen meanings barred legal loopholes.  Yet a recorder who speaks dead languages in an oath chamber, he had discovered long ago, hears far more than law.  When citizens repeated unfamiliar ancient syllables after him, they could not tell which sound meant gold, blood, or forever, so they could not time a false inflection.  Unguarded feeling bled evenly through their throats and carved microscopic contours into the waste wax.  Driven by a private desire to archive emotional patterns, Cassian had spent forty years salvaging those curls, preserving the pure geometry of grief, greed, and devotion. 

Handling such remnants required rare patience, because a dry wax spiral shattered at the lightest brush of skin.  He had solved that difficulty decades earlier via a curious third-grade field trip memory from his coastal boyhood.  Slipping behind a velvet drape at the Natural History Hall while his classmates crowded the whale bones, young Cassian had watched a preparator immerse brittle, coiled sea fern specimens in warm glycerin.  Buoyed and softened by the dense bath, the parched spirals had unspooled flat without cracking, while a mirror beneath the glass cast their translucent ridges across the ceiling.  Tonight, breathing shallowly so the surface stayed still, Cassian leaned over his crystal trough to immerse the Hittite shaving from the syndics in warmed glycerin and brine.  Slowly, soundlessly, the pale curl yielded its tension and lengthened into an unbroken amber ribbon suspended in the liquid. 

Opening a narrow lens under the trough, he projected the magnified waveform of the ribbon across sensitized linen pinned to the wall.  Forty velvet folios along his shelves held thousands of such prints, begun solely as an honest record of what human hearts felt while promising.  Yet over the decades, comparing old prints against court annulments and broken treaties had confronted Cassian with an accidental prophecy woven through his collection.  Months or years before a speaker consciously contemplated betrayal, one specific mark surfaced in the projected wax: a hushed, feathered double hollow between vowels, too faint for any ear to detect.  Unlike the jagged teeth of deliberate lies, that smooth undulation belonged to people who believed their own vows completely, even while some deeper exhaustion in the body already rehearsed surrender.  Without intending it, his quiet museum of present sincerity had become an oracle of future forfeitures, mapping fractures long before resolve began to fail. 

Cassian turned the brass focus wheel, expecting the new ribbon to display the crisp, steady crests of earnest civic stewardship.  Only hours ago, those young trustees had laid reverent palms against the gnarls of the oldest quince tree, chanting the Hittite clauses of perpetual care with shining eyes.  Across the illuminated linen, however, their chorus showed no steady crests at all.  Beneath every archaic syllable of protection glided that unmistakable, velvety double hollow, soft as dark water closing over a stone.  None of the syndics suspected it tonight as they slept in the gilded apartments below, certain of their own honor.  Yet within a few short seasons, when steel and rentable glass grew dearer still, they would discover sensible reasons to petition the courts, summon the saws, and strip this skyward sanctuary bare.  Cassian stood motionless beside the warm bath, listening to sea mist drip from ancient leaves onto roots that could not know they were already condemned.  With steady silver tongs, he fixed the linen print, blotted it dry, and filed the quiet sentence of his orchard into its proper album before the harbor horn began to groan again.
