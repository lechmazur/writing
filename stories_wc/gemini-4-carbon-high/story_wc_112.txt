Aboard Vesper Six, a high altitude xenobiology research station suspended in the freezing stratosphere of Kestrel, Toma was known as an obliging sky mechanic.  All afternoon he had answered trivial maintenance calls without complaint, thawing jammed airlock seals for impatient interns and bleeding pressure lines for senior biologists who rarely remembered his name.  Most of the crew mistook his quiet compliance for a lack of ambition, never guessing why he volunteered for every subzero exterior shift.  Stepping onto the lower gantry as evening approached, he clipped his safety tether to a frosted rail and looked beneath the hull.  Thousands of wild glacier moths were rising from the cloud abyss to cling against the copper turbine housings and exhaust manifolds, drawn because the outer plating stayed mechanically warm amid the lethal gale. 

Only Doctor Linnea had asked Toma for something risky rather than routine, and he had agreed before she finished explaining.  Inside Observation Bay Four rested thirty calcified moth cocoons dredged from polar glaciers below, specimens the director planned to discard tomorrow as dead fossils.  Linnea believed the species survived deep freezes by shifting broods into adjacent temporal states bridged by the crystalline wings of living adults, though the linear stabilizer of the station blocked that link.  Every other technician had mocked her theory, yet Toma shared her quiet need to prove that time moves sideways.  Three winters earlier, a snapped cable had dropped him into an ionized squall where he lived a windless hour watching frost ferns bloom and vanish across his visor while deck clocks moved half a second.  The flight surgeon had blamed hypoxia, and Toma had politely signed the report, kept quiet, and spent every spare evening studying the currents that briefly carried him beside ordinary seconds. 

Climbing down the rime coated ladder, he reached the magnetic nexus at twilight, a massive induction ring suspended below Bay Four where the dusk terminator twisted planetary flux into ribbons of violet light.  Through the overhead viewport, he saw Linnea waiting beside the specimen racks in her heavy parka, her posture composed and unsentimental.  An icy warmth bound the two outcasts, sparing of comfort or soft words, yet steady enough that neither let the other face the cold alone.  All day the gathering moths had beaten their broad pinions against the updrafts, shaking the steel trusses with a low thunder that scrambled delicate wave alignments.  As the last red sliver of sun sank under the cloud deck, the entire roosting flock folded its mineral veined wings flat against the heated belly of the station.  Sudden stillness claimed the catwalk, and Toma knew they had to act within the hush of beating wings at rest, the brief twilight pause when vibration ceased and folded sails formed a flat grating. 

Two soft clicks sounded in his earpiece, a wordless signal from Linnea because any motor whine or spoken broadcast might startle the sleeping flock.  Glaze ice had locked the drive servos on the primary field diverter, leaving manual force as his only silent option.  Toma unstrapped a heavy steel bar from his harness, braced his cleated boots against the frostbitten girder, and wedged the chisel tip beneath the brass stator collar.  Inhaling thin, freezing air that stung his lungs, he leaned backward over the dark gulf to lever the massive guide ring three degrees off linear north.  Frost sheared from the joint with a muffled click, and the heavy ring settled cleanly into its auxiliary detent.  Immediately, sheets of violet current rose from the tilted coil and skimmed across thousands of crystalline moth wings pressed flat against the hull.  Operating through holographic principles, the two dimensional interference pattern formed across that resting surface reconstructed a full three dimensional temporal field inside Bay Four, overlapping the cold room with an unbroken lateral season. 

No shells cracked open, and no alarms disturbed the stratospheric night.  Instead, gazing upward through the rimed glass, Toma witnessed thirty subdued awakenings unfolding quietly along the steel shelves.  Chalky husks grew translucent as faint golden vessels began to pulse inside them, folded antennae stirred by slow millimeters, and warm breath misted the inner walls of jars that had stood frozen for centuries.  Above him, Linnea did not cheer or reach for her instruments, only pressed one bare palm gently against the nearest clouded cylinder before looking down through the dusk toward the gantry.  Resting his gloved hand on the humming brass coil while frost gathered along his lashes, Toma raised two fingers in silent reply, content to know his lost hour was real and nothing surrendered to winter was ever truly gone.
