Seven months after the last visit of the highland beekeeper, the terraced grotto city behind the monumental waterfall smelled of damp limestone and vanishing roses.  Outside the cavern mouth, a colossal curtain of glacial water thundered into the gorge, hiding three hundred displaced persons from the armies that had burned their capital.  For two years, only the old beekeeper had dared the slick basalt stairs behind the cataract, bringing mountain honey, grain, and flasks of floral distillate from the high orchards.  On his final descent in autumn, coughing from valley smoke, he had delivered one last demijohn of rosewater alongside news that the passes were sealed and every hive was dead.  Since then, the exiles rationed that sweet essence to mask the mildew on shrouds and blankets, until the delicate scent of summer gardens became the perfume of quiet extinction. 

No one felt that creeping erasure more acutely than Julian, a cultured museum guide who had once led titled patrons through the Royal Conservatory of Decorative Arts.  Deeply historically oriented, Julian measured calamity not in lost coin but in severed centuries, cataloging the lineages and provincial idioms of his fellow refugees before grief could flatten them.  Around him on the glistening stone tiers, master ironworkers, lacemakers, and illuminators were dying in the spray without looms, anvils, or dry parchment to pass on their craft.  To rescue dying arts had become his sole reason to wake beneath the roaring vault, yet the one surviving pattern book remained locked in river ice.  The beekeeper had chipped that frozen folio from the flooded conservatory cellar during winter and hauled it down to the frost ledge beside the cold updraft of the falls. 

Now spring warmth was swelling the cataract above into a deafening torrent, sending tremors through the cavern roof and dripping meltwater across the upper frost ledge.  If the block melted unattended in the dark, the carmine inks of the Grand Guild Alphabet would dissolve into pink slush, erasing seven hundred years of ornamental geometry.  Even in the warmer tiers below, perpetual mist rotted paper within days, leaving no surface on which to copy the sacred curves of fourth dynasty scrollwork and lace.  Standing beside the communal brick ovens where families queued for grey ration loaves, Julian remembered a family custom of baking bread shaped like initials for winter feast days.  His grandmother had braided sweet dough into intricate ancestral monograms, teaching young fingers the exact classical proportions of calligraphy and woven knots long before children ever held a pen.  Where vellum failed, living hands could become the archive. 

That evening, Julian carried the heavy slab of ice down to the flour tables and summoned the displaced artisans and their pale children around the glowing hearth.  With steady curator grace, he placed the encased folio near the warm flue vents to thaw.  Water hissed onto hot brick as the crust of ice thinned and cracked, exposing the first sodden page of illuminated capitals.  A murmur of dismay rose from the crowd when the crimson pigment immediately bled across wet calfskin, fading into ghostly washes nearly invisible under dim tallow lamps.  Unflinching, Julian reached into his threadbare waistcoat and drew out a single emerald tinted opera glass lens, the sole unbroken remnant from the night the capital theaters fell.  Trained in gallery restoration, he knew that deep green glass filters out red glare, forcing the faintest trace of carmine ink to stand out crisp and black.  Peering through the green circle as the ice continued to thaw, Julian saw the drowned flourishes sharpen into brief, miraculous clarity before washing away forever. 

In his measured lecture voice, he dictated every historic curve and ratio to the waiting bakers while turning the melting leaves with chilled fingers.  Dozens of scarred hands rolled and pleated the ration dough into the initials of their lost houses and guilds, shaping an M with ironwork spirals and braiding an S with sevenfold lace.  Before sliding the trays into the coals, Julian uncorked the final demijohn and brushed each braided letter with rosewater.  Soon a wave of warm floral sweetness billowed through the grotto, exquisite and suffocating against the rising roar of the floodwaters outside their fragile stone sanctuary.  By midnight the ancient folio lay blank and ruined in a puddle of pink water, and the demijohn stood nearly dry beside the cooling ash.  Yet as children traced the glazed crests of their hot loaves before eating, repeating every twist in the dark, Julian knew their hands would remember what the river tried to drown.
