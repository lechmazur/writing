Solder smoke curls past the dim amber dials of the basement university radio station like nebula dust trapped in a bottle.  Mara wipes blackened rosin onto her wool sleeve and leans over the scarred mixing console, listening to the cold hiss between worlds.  For six years she has worked on this campus as a celestial signal interpreter, paid to sift pulsar rhythms from dead static, yet nothing in the archive ever sounded so sly. 

Three nights ago, the observatory dish caught a shimmering transmission falling out of a dark rift near Cygnus.  The Provost called it a miracle of higher mathematics and promptly locked the astrophysics building behind hired guards to hoard the prestige.  Within hours, leaked recordings sparked bitter power plays across every hall and street corner.  Each listener hears a flattering promise of dominion hidden inside the harmonic lattice, convincing rival deans, town councilors, and student societies that the stars have crowned them alone.  Above her head, sleet rattles against the narrow concrete window well as the community divides into hostile camps around the muddy quad.  Red road flares bleed through the icy slush outside, illuminating timber barricades where former neighbors shout threats over bullhorns and fight for access to the relay towers. 

Nobody upstairs wants to hear what Mara found in the lower sidebands before security revoked her clearance.  The broadcast is no gift of wisdom, but rather a snare cast by ancient minds that treat young civilizations like glass jars full of stinging ants, shaking them for sport.  When the Provost patched the telescope feed into the campus intranet to study the upper melody, he unknowingly piped its true venom straight into the buried copper utilities of the valley.  Beneath the luminous chime that enchants the human ear, a predatory rhythm pulses through subsonic frequencies, vibrating at fourteen cycles per second to inflame terror and ambition in the mammalian brain. 

Cutting the main power at the substation would fail.  The signal carries a reflective telemetry ping, meaning an abrupt blackout would warn the distant senders to shift their beam toward a larger city grid.  Mara knows the only way to outwit cosmic tricksters is to let them believe their joke is still working while quietly stealing its punchline.  Locked inside this forgotten student booth among peeling punk stickers and dusty vinyl crates, she has the one unguarded transmitter wired directly into the deep bedrock grounding rods of the hill. 

Because department rivals wiped the digital star catalogs during their afternoon turf war, she spreads her heavy paper astronomical charts across the dual turntables and pins their curling corners into the acoustic foam.  Coffee rings stain the pale ink of Cygnus, yet her red grease pencil marks the exact sidereal drift and lensing angle of the void source against the rotation of the Earth.  Every degree of planetary turn alters the phase of the incoming wave, requiring manual calibration down to the millisecond.  Measuring the arc across her astronomical charts with brass dividers, she bypasses the audio limiter on the aging tube amplifier and tunes the oscillator to an exact mirror of the alien pulse. 

Heavy boots pound down the stairwell outside the steel fire door, accompanied by angry voices demanding the backup keys.  Mara braces her shoulder against the rack and throws the copper knife switch to interfere with the parasitic wave before anyone breaks the lock.  No sound comes from the studio speakers, yet the air turns thick as cold iron.  Driven through subsonic frequencies, her inverted current pours into the bedrock and climbs the wet utility lines, meeting every crest of the stellar tremor with an equal trough.  Glass vacuum tubes halo the dark booth in pale violet fire while fine gray dust lifts off the floor tiles, hanging weightless like a miniature galaxy.  Her jaw aches with the silent collision of worlds, old copper and damp earth holding fast against the whisper of the void. 

Outside the studio door, the pounding abruptly stops, replaced by a bewildered murmur in the stairwell.  Mara climbs onto a milk crate and presses her bruised knuckles to the icy casement glass.  Across the dim, slushy quad, two opposing lines of townsfolk and faculty lower their iron pipes, blinking through red flare smoke as the phantom fever breaks inside their skulls.  Up in the observatory, the surface melody still shimmers untouched, feeding a harmless lie back to the dark sky so the watchers never suspect their grip has slipped.  Morning will still bring bruised pride, broken windows, and the constant need to keep adjusting the phase as the stars wheel overhead, yet watching a young guard help a shivering student over the barricade leaves Mara cautiously hopeful.
