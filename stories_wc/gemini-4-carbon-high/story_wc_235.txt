Finding your own mossy headstone in an alpine clearing tends to ruin your sense of schedule.  Since brushing frost off the granite carved with his name, Callum had grown hopelessly chronologically confused, unable to tell whether his death waited next winter or had already happened three autumns ago.  Inside the drafty mountain ranger cabin perched high above the timberline, he brewed pine needle tea for a version of himself who might have drunk it yesterday.  Forty years as a lucid dream chronicler had already worn his grip on linear time dangerously thin.  He had spent decades walking wide awake through sleeping architecture, mapping impossible cities of spun sugar and singing bone until miracles felt as routine as tax forms, though still undeniably gorgeous when the light hit them right. 

Seeing that cold stone marker, however, had shattered his mind into a howling blizzard of unlinked moments.  Childhood fevers bled into old heartbreaks and unlived tomorrows without rhyme or sequence.  Before the grave claimed its due, Callum needed to distill chaos into narrative, forging one continuous story that proved his scattered existence actually meant something.  Mice had devoured the paper forestry ledgers in the abandoned outpost years ago, which suited him fine, because turning separate pages only worsened his temporal vertigo.  Instead, he had peeled pale sheets from the surrounding trees and stitched them with sinew into a long birch bark scroll stretched across the cabin floor.  Only an unbroken ribbon of tough woodland skin could hold his whole timeline in view at once.  Yet whenever he crouched over the bark with a piece of woodstove charcoal, his disordered waking memory stalled in circles. 

Just as rangers once used this high station to spot distant wildfires across tangled valleys, Callum needed altitude inside his own subconscious to see how his history connected.  He stretched out on the narrow iron cot, listened to the wind rattle the frosted panes, and slipped deliberately into lucid sleep.  Immediately, the dreamscape unveiled its usual theatrical splendor: violet oceans hanging upside down above canyons of luminous quartz.  Callum gave an appreciative shrug, weary of his own flamboyant imagination yet forced to admit that the inverted surf caught the starlight magnificently.  Around his boots, however, his personal memories formed a claustrophobic maze of shifting stone corridors where decades collided blindly.  In his twenties, pure childlike delight had lifted him into dream skies, but a veteran chronicler who had seen his own burial plot could no longer summon innocent joy.  Gravity pinned him down until a dark, feral nostalgia sank its teeth into his ribs.  There was nothing gentle or sentimental about this longing; it paced inside him like a starving winter wolf, snarling for the unmeasured pulse of his vanished youth. 

Callum knew only one reliable method to harness that predatory ache for lift: getting jealous of past selves.  Peering through a crack in the labyrinth wall, he spotted himself at nineteen, sprinting barefoot across warm river stones without a single thought of granite headstones.  Bitter resentment flared hot in his chest as he despised that boy for squandering effortlessly boundless afternoons.  His boots rose six inches off the quartz floor.  Turning down another corridor, he watched himself at thirty, laughing over cheap wine in a sunlit kitchen, rich with decades that ignorant fool took entirely for granted.  Callum fed greedily on that petty, scorching envy, coveting every unbroken bone and uncounted breath until the heat inflated his ribs like a silk balloon.  Propelled by fierce thermals of spite toward every younger incarnation, he broke free of the maze and began to soar.  Upward he rocketed toward the inverted violet sea, rolling his eyes at the melodrama of golden lightning while breathlessly admiring how the whole chaotic labyrinth shrank beneath him.  From this freezing altitude, the tangled corridors finally aligned into a single silver river winding logically from his first cry to the snowy ridge outside. 

Every mistake, dream, and loss bent the current deliberately toward the mountain clearing, revealing that the grave was not an interruption, only a punctuation mark.  Callum snapped awake in the frigid ranger cabin, lungs stinging with woodsmoke, before waking confusion could scramble the aerial map.  Dropping to his knees, he dragged black charcoal swiftly across the birch bark scroll, translating decades of fractured static into one clean, unbroken arc.  Beyond the icy windowpane, either dawn or dusk painted the birches in absurdly showy crimson, another tired miracle that nevertheless stole his breath.  He tied the finished scroll with twine and sipped his cold tea, content that whenever his headstone became accurate, it would mark a story that finally made sense.
