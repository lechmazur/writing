Six months after making first contact, public shrines still surrounded the university gates, yet Sabine remained a luminous skeptic.  An accidental leak during the orbital handshake had seeded her epidermis with harmless alien photophores, leaving her palms and collarbones glowing like pale embers in dim rooms.  While onlookers mistook that radiance for grace, Sabine trusted only molecular bonds and testable reactions.  She had always been academically rebellious, publishing sharp critiques of the Xenology Syndicate for treating the visitors as inscrutable mystics rather than biochemical organisms.  When the Syndicate banned all field interference around the solitary envoy walking through the northern valley, she disabled her faculty transponder and slipped under the perimeter wire.  A single ambition drove her trespass: she needed to understand the chemistry of homecoming, convinced that belonging was not a miracle of spirit, but an exact equation of scent and receptor. 

The travelers had lost their cradle planet eons ago, surviving endless transit by staging an artificial pilgrimage on every living world they encountered.  Each envoy selected an abandoned terrestrial spire, marched toward it alone, and breathed native moisture to trick its ancient nervous system into feeling the physiological relief of return.  On Earth, however, the synthetic rite kept failing.  Three previous envoys had reached their chosen landmarks only to collapse in tremors of unfulfilled recognition, unable to complete the neurochemical lock.  Studying leaked telemetry late at night, Sabine realized modern air supplied wet soil geosmin, which satisfied half the alien receptor, yet lacked the dense, resinous chitin esters of their vanished prehistoric forests.  Before leaving campus, she had unlocked the paleobiology vault and taken a specimen of Eocene fly amber, along with a pocket thermal coil.  Suspended inside that golden tear of fossilized sap was a tiny winged dipteran halted midflight forty million years ago, holding ancient conifer terpenes and arthropod hydrocarbons together in unbroken stasis. 

An April squall had just swept the arboretum, leaving behind a vast, bronze quiet under low metallic clouds.  No wind moved the budding birch boughs; water gathered on oxidized memorial plaques and fell in slow, resonant intervals onto moss.  Sabine tracked the fourth envoy via dusty footprints left on an overgrown sculpture path that coiled up the hillside.  Because the ritual required pilgrims to coat their soles in hydrophobic silicate ash ground from the asteroid hull of their starship, each step shed dry, pale ochre powder atop the wet slate.  The trail wound between tarnished bronze figures half swallowed by creeping ivy, rising steadily toward an old stone observatory, a rainwashed tower in spring whose dark masonry gleamed against the clearing dusk. 

Climbing the exterior stair while the envoy ascended the interior ramp, Sabine felt their separate inquiries converge at the open parapet.  The creature stood beside the slick crenellations, tall and slender in woven copper filaments, its thoracic spiracles flaring rapidly as it drew in the damp smell of sprouted grass and limestone.  Already a fine tremor ran along its limbs; the spring rain offered living renewal, yet without an ancestral anchor its nervous system was stalling on the brink of solace.  When Sabine stepped into view, the envoy did not recoil, recognizing the familiar amber luminescence of its own shipboard flora shining softly across her bare hands.  Honoring the stillness of the high stone deck, she knelt, placed the fly amber upon her thermal coil, and dialed the brass ring just warm enough to volatilize the surface without scarring the fossil inside. 

Within seconds, a faint plume of warm succinic acid, ancient pine resin, and preserved insect cuticle drifted upward to converge with the cool geosmin rising off the wet parapet.  The envoy leaned over the glowing palms of the scientist and drew one deep, unbroken breath.  Instantly the violent shivering ceased, replaced by a low harmonic hum like a struck bell settling into rest.  Its dermal plates relaxed, and a drop of clear saline gathered near its crest as forty million years of terrestrial memory completed a circuit begun under another star.  Watching the ancient fly gleam inside the cooling resin, Sabine felt her strict materialism vindicated yet unexpectedly softened.  Reducing the ache of exile to carbon chains did not cheapen the peace now filling the dusk around them.  Home was neither a mystical birthright nor a fixed coordinate lost to time; it was a delicate, reproducible reaction that two displaced minds could assemble together in the rain.
