At nautical twilight, when the sea turned to slate while a faint silver horizon still lingered, Oren stood high on his lighthouse gallery with a brass spyglass pressed to his eye.  Thirty years as the headland's eccentric lighthouse keeper had tuned him to odd habits: he wore three tarnished barometers belted over his oilskins, saluted passing cormorants by naval rank, and kept ledgers of silences rather than ships.  Far below his tower stretched the opal dock in mist, its pale silica slabs quarried from coastal cliffs so that every sweep of his newly lit beacon struck milky blue fire through the vapor.  At the jetty's end idled the muffled hospice launch that carried dying mainlanders across the sound, and beside it stood a gaunt old passenger with no one to see him off.  Before boarding, the frail man turned back, gazed straight past the glaring lens at Oren's shadowed figure, and laid a small white token upon an iron bollard.  Then he lifted his wool sleeve, rubbed a slow, deliberate circle against the empty air before his face, peered through that invisible ring at Oren, and stepped into the vanishing boat. 

Unsettled by being so directly seen after decades of quiet invisibility, Oren locked his lens gear on automatic rotation and hurried down the iron stairs to investigate.  He rarely set foot beyond his tower door, yet an urgent need to decode a stranger's final gesture pulled him out onto the slick, shimmering stones before full night erased the trail.  Fog curled thick around his boots, flashing brief violet and pearl inside the wet mineral grain each time the overhead beam swung past.  Resting on the cold bollard was a dried Edelweiss boutonniere wrapped in tarnished silver wire.  Oren recognized the dense, woolly highland blossom immediately from harbor curiosity shops, where brokers sold alpine stems steeped in human neural impressions.  Unlike lowland petals that rotted quickly in coastal dampness, desiccated mountain felt could preserve borrowed memories intact against salt air for half a century.  Oren kept two faded stems in a tin upstairs himself, pressing them to his wrist during winter gales to taste summers that had never belonged to him. 

Pulling off one wet glove, he pressed the brittle stalk against his pulse.  Instantly, sunlit glaciers blazed behind his eyelids, followed by fiddle music in a pine tavern and warm hands pinning the fresh flower onto a bridegroom's lapel.  Oren frowned into the drifting sea smoke, baffled.  Nothing in that joyous mountain youth explained why its owner had died alone by tidewater, nor why he had mimed wiping air toward a secluded keeper.  An ordinary person would have searched the bright alpine scenes again for a clue, yet Oren had spent half a lifetime identifying coastal beacons by the dark eclipses between their flashes.  Trusting his strangest instinct, he closed his eyes and chose to examine the flower's history by observing gaps. 

He let the dazzling snowfields rush past unheeded and anchored his attention inside the brief, unrecorded seams where one mountain memory ended and the next had not yet begun.  Beneath the loud alpine joy hummed a softly persistent undertow, the faint residue that accumulates whenever a lonely buyer replays a secondhand blossom thousands of nights in the same quiet room.  In the first narrow pause between glacier and tavern, Oren smelled coal oil and heard harbor rain ticking against cold glass.  In the next dark seam, frail knuckles lifted a wool cuff to rub a small clear circle into a steamed garret windowpane across the cove.  Through that wiped ring of glass shone Oren's own lighthouse half a mile over black water, and pacing its high gallery in bulky oilskins stood Oren himself, faithfully tending the lamp.  With the image came a quiet rush of gratitude from a solitary tally clerk who had endured decades of borrowed mountain dreams by checking, between visions, that his fellow watchman across the bay was still awake. 

Oren opened his eyes as the last silver thread of twilight surrendered to night, leaving the pale jetty shimmering beneath his revolving light.  Two men had lived entirely apart behind separate panes of glass, yet neither vigil had gone unshared.  Carefully pinning the dried blossom above his three brass barometers, Oren faced the dark garret across the cove, raised his damp sleeve, and rubbed a clear circle in the mist.
