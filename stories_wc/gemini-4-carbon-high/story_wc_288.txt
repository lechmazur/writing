Four days after the reincarnation memory surfaced in his mind, Ansel still could not hold a pen without his fingers locking into tremors.  Citizens reaching adulthood normally recalled lives from a century or two earlier, slipping easily into familiar histories.  Ansel instead remembered walking twelve thousand years ago through green valleys long drowned beneath greenhouse vapor, making him the sole living contemporary of extinct species.  His mind brimmed with the weight of tame falcons, the rustle of silver birches, and wild bees that survived now only as dead taxonomic entries.  Physicians called such deep recalls brittle awakenings, warning that an ancient sensory map forced into a modern nervous system would fracture both minds.  Nothing of that vanished biosphere remained outside this tidal library above clouds, anchored to a granite peak where solar heat drove dense vapor seas up the cliffs every afternoon. 

To shield fragile parchment from those caustic surges, the outer terraces and towering heliostat shutters were clad in atomically reflective alloy.  Engineered to subatomic smoothness, the metal bounced every acidic molecule away without wetting, funneling pure sunlight into the deep stacks at low tide before sealing the halls in gleaming silence when mist rose.  Inside those soundproof wards, newly awakened archivists healed by gathering in chorus groups to share overlapping memories of recent decades.  Ansel sat alone, surrounded by flawless mirrors that showed only a pale youth while rejecting every trace of age or weather.  Convinced that such radical solitude would shatter him, the chief physician scheduled a chemical pruning at dusk to erase the older persona.  Ansel refused to let half of himself die merely because no peer could validate it, resolving instead to translate isolation into independence. 

Under library charter, any keeper who operated the exposed Crown Array solo through a full tidal cycle earned autonomous tenure, exempt from medical oversight.  Other archivists shunned that summit post outside the communal domes, yet Ansel climbed the stone stair at dawn to claim the watch.  The duty demanded exact timing: angling the massive heliostat blades to feed light downward all morning, then winching them edgewise before the afternoon cloud surge cracked their rigid lattice.  Because electronic sensors corroded quickly in the unshielded upper air, Ansel brought an artifact from the relic drawers, a pocket brass sundial salvaged long ago from the lowlands.  Setting it upon the parapet, he studied its coat of green verdigris, tangible proof that metal could react with harsh air, change color, and still endure.  Through the clear morning hours of low tide, tracking a genuine solar shadow across weathered engravings calmed his ancient instincts and steadied his hands on the bronze gearwheel. 

Then an equinox updraft drove the afternoon tide upward thirty minutes early, boiling over the cliff rim in a dense whiteout before the shadow reached the fourth mark.  Robbed of direct sun, the brass sundial went instantly blank, while the surrounding atomically reflective blades scattered the swirling mist into a blinding, horizonless glare.  Bereft of sky and earthy scent inside the acrid fog, his prehistoric memory panicked like a cornered wolf, triggering a severe spasm that locked his muscles against the lever.  If the heliostats stayed broadside to the unseen crest, turbulent wind shear would snap the mirrors and pour corrosive vapor straight down into the vellum stacks.  Forcing his eyes shut against the dazzling fog, Ansel stopped fighting his divided pulse and listened to the mountain ridge above him.  Twenty paces upslope stood a prehistoric monument older than the archive, and as rising currents slid without friction up the library hull below, compressed air rushed through its fluted granite pillars.  Blind in the vapor, he read the incoming gale by the hum of an ancient stone circle, tracing its rising harmonic chord as the pressure shifted westward. 

To the trained archivist, that deepening resonance served as an exact acoustic barometer marking the velocity and angle of the cresting tide.  To the man from the green earth, the vibrating megaliths sang with the remembered cadence of wind through highland pines and beating wings, feeding a sensory hunger that sterile silence had starved.  His tremor vanished the instant both halves interpreted the same vibration, unlocking his grip so two lifetimes turned the heavy bronze wheel together.  Feathering the array safely into the peak current, Ansel understood that neither self needed to overwrite the other when both could coexist in shared purpose.  When the cloud sea ebbed at dusk, rolling cleanly off the mirrors while leaving fresh moisture upon the tarnished brass beside him, he stood whole on the summit, needing no chorus beyond himself.
