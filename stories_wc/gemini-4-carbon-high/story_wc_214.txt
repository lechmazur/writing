Poised above a nameless ocean world, the shooting star tracking station lingered through the hush of an overlooked era.  The fierce forging of the first suns had ended ages ago, yet no waking voices had risen from the dark waters below to name the constellations.  Within those basalt halls ribbed with tarnished brass, Oren labored alone as a cosmic janitor.  Each cycle he swept stray meteoric ash from the observation terraces, scraped cold slag from the gravity anchors that held the keep aloft, and polished the great quartz apertures left behind by vanished architects.  Those ancient builders had shaped their citadel for absolute permanence, worshiping unyielding iron and despising everything that wore away.  Oren, however, had been formed from humbler silicate and mortal breath, meant only to tend the silence between glorious ages.  Lately, fine pale fissures had begun to spread across his palms, threatening the steady grip required by his broom and plumb lines. 

Ashamed of his thinning shell, he coated his knuckles with black pitch after every shift, yet in secret he longed to find strength in vulnerability rather than sealing himself inside dead armor.  Though bound to menial upkeep, Oren remained spiritually curious, convinced that the universe wasted nothing without purpose.  Where the old automated ledgers recorded falling stones merely as mass lost to friction, he paused over every drift of star dust in his pan, wondering what final truth visited a stone as the sky undid it.  Still, whenever he peered through the primary quartz lens of the dome, the atmospheric entries below appeared only as blinding white slashes, too fierce and clinical to reveal their inner grace.  His answer surfaced while he was clearing ancient grit from a collapsed watchtower along the outer hull.  Beneath a mound of oxidized wire lay an unbroken pane of yellow lantern glass, curved like a cupped palm and warm as aged honey when held against the starlight.  Drawn by its gentle hue, Oren carried the relic up the spiral stair and wedged it directly into the brass sighting frame above the central well. 

That very watch, a winter stream of primordial fragments crossed into the pull of the dark planet below.  Looking through the yellow lantern glass, Oren caught his breath.  The amber pane drank the merciless glare of burning plasma, softening harsh annihilation into steady golden fire and laying bare the delicate mineral veins inside every plunging rock.  Leaning close, he raised his cracked fingers to the warm surface.  Watch after watch, by tracing the path of falling stars across that golden curve, matching his slow pulse to each erratic tremor and luminous flare, Oren began to empathize with the perishing travelers.  Beneath his fingertips, their descent was no longer an accident of blind mechanics, but an intimate passage from frozen solitude into radiant release.  Then twin bolides entered the upper air side by side, and the glass revealed a deeper law.  The first was forged of solid nickel and flawless iron; refusing the atmospheric heat, it plunged unbroken into the black abyss, sinking mute and sterile to the ocean floor without changing the world around it.  The second stone was porous and deeply fissured, scarred by eons of drift much like the hands of the custodian watching from above.  As Oren followed its arc across the yellow lantern glass, he felt the fire enter its seams not as ruin, but as awakening.  Rather than hoard its form, the fragile stone opened completely, blossoming into a vast canopy of golden vapor that seeded the barren clouds with carbon, phosphorus, and ancient salt. 

Oren lowered his hand and scraped the dried black pitch from his knuckles, letting the amber glow touch his open cracks.  Only what dared to fracture could give itself to what came next; hardness was merely another name for barrenness.  Taking up his broom, he walked to the outer terrace and swept the gathered star ash not into the disposal furnaces, but outward into the gentle downward pull toward the sea.  Far below, silver rain began to gather for the first time over the dark horizon, drinking the rich dust of broken worlds.  Oren rested his broom against the basalt rail and sat beside the golden sight, no longer dreading the slow surrender of his own clay.  No trumpets sounded across the void, and no empire rose in that quiet shadow, yet through the silent observatory and the seeded waters beneath it settled a flourishing stillness, deep and fertile as winter earth waiting calmly for spring.
