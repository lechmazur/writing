Silence returns slowly to the echoing observatory above clouds, though the brass bell of the wall telephone still hums faintly in the copper dome.  Five minutes after the journalist calls with a midnight deadline, the granite rotunda feels less like a retreat for stargazers and more like a celestial courtroom.  Below the arched windows, a silver cloud sea hides the valley where the Vance pulp mills once fouled the river air.  As a newly crowned newsprint heiress, Maren unlocked the secret vault of her late father last week to discover hidden truths, cheerfully ignoring every lawyer who begged her to leave old ghosts asleep.  She expected to indict her father, not imperil her beloved godfather. 

Across the chart table, Julian Sterling studies the tablet where the reporter sent a magnified scan of a thirty year old front page proof.  The unpublished article holds his warning about toxic mill vapor, yet stamped across the columns is a withdrawal order bearing his flourishing signature.  Julian sighs, a dry sound that circles the curved ceiling like a weary bird, and reaches for his carved rosewood pen nib.  Because steel points shrieked intolerably inside this chamber, the astronomer whittled his nibs from dense Brazilian timber so his midnight equations would whisper instead of scratch.  Dipping the dark wood into sepia ink, he begins drafting a confession on a pad of unprinted mill paper, preparing to take full blame for burying his research. 

Maren leans her elbows on the slate counter and asks, "Since when does my favorite heretic confess to sins he spent three decades mourning?"  Julian offers a faint, rueful smile without lifting his eyes from the page.  "People are much like stray comets, Maren, captured by old mistakes into orbits of regret that swing endlessly around the same dark star," he murmurs.  Quietly, he recounts the burden that kept him marooned on this peak.  On the night his warning was meant to print, her mother visited the dome to plead for the family mills while Julian calibrated the shutter outside.  The next day, her father claimed her mother had taken the pen from this desk and forged the signature on the galley sheet to prevent ruin.  Loyal to her mother, Julian chose lonely exile over exposing her to disgrace, and tonight he intends to shield her memory once more. 

Maren has always been devotedly observant, schooled since childhood both by Julian at the telescope and by grizzled pressmen in noisy ink rooms.  She watches the wooden chisel glide across the soft pine pulp of his pad, then studies the magnified signature glowing on the tablet.  When Julian sets down the pen and reaches for the wall telephone, she steps briskly between his hand and the brass receiver to intervene. 

Lifting one finger with theatrical gravity, she adopts the crisp lecture tone he once used to quiz her on geometry.  "Before a distinguished scientist flings himself into noble ruin, this humble printer requests permission to test his tragedy through proof by contradiction," she declares.  Julian blinks in surprise, indulgent amusement flickering across his solemn face as he gestures for her to proceed.  "Assume our premise is true, that my mother stood in this room and imitated your wide calligraphic hand upon that galley sheet," Maren says, tapping the tablet.  "Nobody carries an angled dip pen in an evening coat, and you banished metal points from this dome, so she necessarily used your rosewood pen nib."  She places his fresh confession beside the glowing scan and zooms in on the heavy capital letter of his name.  "A solid chisel of oiled timber has no split tines, so heavy pressure on raw newsprint merely lays a flat velvet ribbon, just like on your pad," she continues.  "Yet on the scanned proof, heavy pressure parted two sharp metal tines that sliced parallel furrows into the cheap pulp and plucked loose fibers between them."  Her voice drops to a gentle hush that the dome carries clearly: "One rigid piece of rosewood cannot split into twin steel blades, which renders the premise impossible." 

Julian stares at the twin razor tracks, realizing at once that her father forged the withdrawal in the city composing room and invented a lie to bind him in silence.  A startled, boyish laugh escapes him, ringing bright and weightless against the copper vault as thirty years of false gravity finally let him go.  Tearing his confession in half, Maren hands him the receiver with a ceremonial bow so they can put the real truth on the morning front page.
