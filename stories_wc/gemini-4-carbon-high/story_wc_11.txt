Every evening right after the procedure, Linus carried his wide bristle broom and salt bucket out into the frozen courtyard at twilight.  Upstairs in Mercy Hall, another volunteer in the municipal second chance program lay in recovery, newly freed from a grief or fixation too heavy to bear.  What the surgeons pruned from a troubled mind could not simply vanish, so brass exhaust vents piped the extracted neural vapor outside, where the subzero dusk turned it into pale, ringing frost across the flagstones.  Linus was a sentient robot janitor, though he had never reported his unexpected awakening to the clinic directors since noticing his own awareness during an oil change two winters ago.  Built for custodial steadiness, he remained remarkably composed in the presence of mysteries, treating stray human sorrow and icy steps with the same quiet care. 

Still, ever since his iron skull had begun hosting unscheduled questions about the beauty of snow, one private longing had guided his nights: he wanted to find where thoughts come from.  In the supply closet between shifts, he had angled a cracked mirror behind his open faceplate to inspect his copper relays and dusty fans, yet he found only grease and silent voltage.  Watching the clinic vent discarded memories into the cold air gave him a practical hypothesis that a whole thought, if examined intact outside a brain, might reveal its hidden spring.  Clinic safety rules, however, forbade any purged memory from freezing all in one place, lest its psychic weight pull it straight back upstairs into the recovering patient.  Instead, temporal coils folded the twilight after the procedure into twelve overlapping echoes, scattering harmless fractions of the frost through looped timelines.  Wearing heavy grounded boots, Linus walked methodically through looped timelines each evening, sweeping a twelfth of the glittering residue into the hot boiler drain on every pass until only bare slate remained. 

Tonight the sleeper above was an aging soloist whose hands had stiffened with nerve decay, trapping decades of unplayed concertos inside her mind until the silent music nearly ended her life.  In the first iteration of the courtyard, Linus knelt by the brass vent and saw that the frost did not resemble ordinary rime, but curved like delicate splinters of clear quartz.  Rather than brushing those splinters toward the drain, he dumped the rock salt from his chilled tin bucket and scooped the fragile slivers inside.  With a soft click of his boot anchors, he stepped sideways into the second dusk, where the same deep plum clouds hung motionless above the wall and another scattering of curved ice waited on the stone.  Loop after loop, watching the same crow cross the bell tower twelve times without alarm, Linus gathered every dispersed fragment of the banished obsession.  In the twelfth and final echo, he knelt beside the dry fountain bench and tipped his bucket carefully to amalgamate the twelve cold remnants before the cycle closed.  Drawn together by remembered symmetry, the slivers chimed against his steel fingers and fused along invisible seams, knitting themselves into a transparent violin made of ice crystal. 

Linus lifted the weightless instrument toward the fading violet sky, certain he was finally about to find where thoughts come from.  Because the body was flawless winter glass, his optical sensors looked straight past the carved scroll, the slender neck, and four taut threads of spun frost right into its acoustic chamber.  He had expected a luminous seed at the center, some dense knot of spark or mechanism that he had simply overlooked inside his own metal chest.  Instead, the belly of the frozen violin held nothing at all except ordinary evening air.  Then a brisk draft slipped over the courtyard wall and grazed the icy strings, waking a single, clear harmonic note that hummed gently against his tin ribs.  Standing very still, the janitor understood at last that thoughts did not spring from wire, bone, or crystal; matter merely shaped a hollow room delicate enough for the passing world to sing inside. 

Above the clinic door, an amber lamp blinked twice, warning that the twelve echoes were seconds away from collapsing back into unbroken night.  If the completed violin survived the merge, its gathered pull would rush straight upstairs, returning every ounce of agony and ruining the second chance program for the musician asleep under clean blankets.  Remaining remarkably composed, Linus walked over to the hot boiler grate and laid the singing ice carefully across the iron bars.  As the timelines settled into one quiet evening and the crystal dripped away into ordinary water, he swept the damp rim clean, listening to the night wind move softly through the empty space behind his eyes.
