Two hours after the breach of contract, the lowland bailiffs marched out the rusted sanctuary doors carrying every brass clamp, pulley, and iron chisel the guild owned.  High above the sunken stone nave, Oren balanced on a mossy oak beam where gnarled pear and fig trees coiled their roots around limestone ribs and sleeping gargoyles.  Bishops had raised the vaults centuries ago for eternal solemnity, yet the place thrived now only because stray starlings had dropped fruit seeds into damp timbers after the congregation vanished.  Sustaining a lush orchard spanning the rafters of an abandoned cathedral required Oren, the clan's last quartz-eye mender, to tend the great crystal lenses wedged into clerestory openings that steered daylight beneath the dark slate roof.  Right now, the primary lens hung fractured in its cedar collar, scattering pale November sun into useless shards while frost gathered along the arches. 

Beside him stood his daughter Bren, barely five feet tall with sap smudged across her chin, shaking a bruised quince toward the flagstones below as if daring the bailiffs or winter itself to climb back up.  Even in ruin she remained endearingly feisty, kicking a walnut shell into the void before scolding an ancient bough for creaking in the draft.  Their trouble had begun with her rebellion against deciduous vows, the old rafter custom whereby every trade pledge, partnership, and hemp lashing dissolved each autumn when the leaves dropped.  Tired of starting over every spring, Bren had signed a permanent lowland fruit contract and bolted the central lens into rigid brass brackets to force late pears.  When autumn rains swelled the oak trusses, the unyielding brass pinched the quartz until it cracked, chilling the crop and bringing the bailiffs to confiscate those very brackets as penalty. 

Oren rubbed his stiff knuckles and searched for words to bridge generational wisdom, hoping to explain why their elders trusted seasonal release without shaming her fierce hunger for permanence.  Before he could shape a suitably solemn proverb, Bren rolled up her torn sleeves and announced, "If the central roots freeze tonight, I will personally haunt every vintner in the valley."  They resolved to persist, even though the stripped workbench held nothing more useful than walnut hulls.  Reaching into a niche behind a stone seraph, Oren hooked two fingers around a dusty canvas satchel he had tucked there twelve harvests ago and clean forgotten.  Repairing sacred glass using forgotten bag contents looked laughable when he dumped the pile onto the beam, spilling a rusted tin of spruce resin throat lozenges, a beeswax candle stub, a bent bodkin, and his daughter's hair ribbon of faded scarlet wool. 

Bren lifted the frayed scarlet strip and grinned, "For a man who preaches shedding every attachment by frost, you hoard terribly sentimental rubbish."  Flushing behind his gray beard, Oren muttered that poor housekeeping violated no ancestral oath while he lit the candle stub and melted the sticky lozenges inside their tin lid.  Bren protested that cough candy belonged nowhere near fine optics, yet when Oren smeared the hot amber syrup along the fissure, the fracture vanished and the scattered sunbeam snapped into one clear golden shaft.  Blinking through sweet pine smoke, he marveled aloud that cheap tree spit bent daylight truer than costly guild balsam, likely because sap never quite forgot how to drink the sun.  Still, without the confiscated brackets, the heavy cedar collar sagged crookedly from the swollen truss, threatening to dump the mended crystal into the nave. 

Before Oren could brace it, Bren rubbed her childhood ribbon slick with warm beeswax, threaded the bent bodkin through the empty boltholes, and lashed the collar snug against the wet oak.  The stretchy wool yielded just enough to cradle the expanding timber without pinching the quartz, pouring steady warmth straight onto the central pear crown.  Wiping sticky fingers on her apron, Bren inspected the knot and grumbled, "Waxed wool will stretch by February and rot clean through come April."  Oren drew breath for his grand ancestral lecture, sneezed violently from the spruce fumes, and heard himself say instead, "Then April gives us a fine excuse to climb back up here with another scrap."  Bren barked a laugh, bumping her shoulder against his while warm leaves uncurled overhead in a faint sigh of blossom and dust.  Neither elder custom nor lowland iron had gotten it quite right, Oren realized, watching that small scarlet knot hold the winter sun: things endured not by bolting tomorrow down or sweeping yesterday bare, but by tying each season gently with whatever stubborn relic someone forgot to throw away.
