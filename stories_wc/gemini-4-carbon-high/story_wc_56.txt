Julian poled his narrow punt along the twilight barge canal at Fenwick Cut as violet dusk settled between willow banks.  Across a dozen counties, boatmen and tavern keepers knew him as an errant legend seeker who drifted from port to fen with grappling irons and oilcloth notebooks.  Yet Julian never hunted treasure or miracles.  Ten years earlier, discovering that his revered mentor had forged a famous maritime chronicle shattered his youthful faith, leaving him a single quiet purpose: to understand the architecture of lies.  He studied how small fabrications were mortared into enduring folklore, convinced every myth was a snare built from vanity or greed.  Through a decade of patient fieldwork, stripping away illusion without anger, his judgment had grown thoughtfully clear. 

Tonight the water board had lowered Fenwick Cut to waist depth for downstream culvert repairs.  Beside Julian, a battery receiver murmured with the automated night broadcast from the lock house upstream, reciting shallow draft warnings over soft static.  At dawn, an automatic relay would cut power to that transmitter so the aging generator could drive the heavy sluice motors and refill the channel.  Any wading or salvage in the exposed bed had to conclude by the silence of morning radio, which gave barely three minutes of warning before water surged back down the cut.  That brief window suited his investigation into Isaac Mora, an aging horologist who generations earlier allegedly scuttled his workshop barge during a midnight squall to keep bailiffs from seizing guild gold and a wondrous clock said to hold the sun at midnight. 

For decades, treasure hunters had followed a ballad verse claiming the wreck lay where the fourth hour touched the lock wall.  Sighting from the granite masonry along the southeast angle of four on a watch face, they had dragged the deep rocky weir pool and recovered nothing.  Julian ignored ballads; he uncovered submerged truths by training instincts to inhabit practical human haste rather than theatrical spectacle.  Reading the dark channel, he knew a lone old boatman scuttling his craft in secret would never fight currents toward roaring stone, but would let the gentle towpath eddy nudge his hull into the quiet reed bay northeast of the lock.  Wading into that cold silt in hip boots, he probed with an iron rod until seasoned white oak rang dull beneath three feet of mud.  All night under faint stars, accompanied by the monotone gauge broadcast, he trenched around the buried sternpost and worked a hand winch until a tarred copper cylinder pulled free from the rotten ribs.  At that exact moment, as pale amber light touched the eastern reeds, the voice on the receiver clicked into dead air. 

Julian hauled his punt ashore and climbed the stone steps cradling the cylinder just as upstream chains clanked and fresh water hissed back down the channel.  Sitting on a dry granite bollard above the rising basin, he chipped away the brittle pitch and drew out an oiled chamois bundle.  Inside rested no bullion and no geared marvel, only a tarnished brass antique pocket sundial folded flat beside a vellum sheet.  Curious, Julian raised the hinged gnomon and placed the instrument upon the lock wall, aligning its tiny inset compass needle due north.  Unlike a clock dial, the engraved afternoon mark for the fourth hour angled sharply northeast, pointing straight across the filling cut toward the exact reed bay where the oak ribs slept. 

He unfolded the vellum and read a calm confession from Isaac Mora to his apprentice son Daniel, who had struck a corrupt bailiff in self defense that night and fled downriver toward an outbound coastal steamer.  To stop constables from wiring harbor police to blockade the estuary, Mora had staged a wreck at the lock and shouted of lost gold, sealing his famous heirloom dial inside the hull so a search of his wet coat would prove the disaster real.  Every officer and boatman in the valley abandoned the coast road to dredge the weir for two days while Daniel escaped to sea, and tavern gossip soon inflated a solar dial lost in darkness into a clock that held the midnight sun.  In a sudden wave of hindsight clarity, Julian recalled the dozens of fables he had dissected across ten years and realized those inventions were rarely traps for the gullible, but roofs raised in desperation to shelter someone loved.  The premise of his solitary life gave way in a serene upheaval, leaving him quietly whole beside the brimming canal as early sunlight struck the brass gnomon and cast its first true shadow in generations.
