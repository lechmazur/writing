At dead low tide, Nora Vance climbed over the barnacled timbers of a grounded schooner and looked down at the sunken orchard greenhouse ringed by shipwrecks.  A century ago, a winter landslide had dropped an estate conservatory intact into this rocky tidal kettle, where stray vessels had snagged on the outer shoals ever since, encircling the submerged glass dome in a palisade of bleached oak hulls.  Only the conservatory's peaked iron ridge poked above the October surf, breathing a sharp draft of brine and fermented russet apples into the night air.  As a hands-on folklorist who trusted muddy boots over library archives, Nora knew this smell in her bones, even if she had spent half a lifetime unable to place why it made her throat tighten. 

She had first descended through these frosted panes at twenty, driven by the chronically curious temperament that pushed her to test every coastal superstition on her own skin.  Local crabbers had warned her that a fungal ring thrived beneath the drowned apple trees, feeding on shipwreck timber and stealing whole seasons from trespassers who breathed its spores.  Young Nora had stepped straight into that mossy circle with her sample jars open, and by morning an entire autumn had vanished from her mind.  Now, decades later, silver hair tucked beneath her wool cap, she had returned to the shoal to recover stolen memories of the final voyage she had shared with her father before that winter took him.  She still kept his brass dividers and his neat October logbooks on her desk, proving they had charted this very cove together, yet she could recall neither the timbre of his laugh nor a single word they had spoken aboard his sloop. 

Through years of patient fieldwork among aging islanders, Nora had finally uncovered the second half of the reef legend.  Whenever the orchard mycelium stole memories, it sealed the theft by planting a rigid daily compulsion in the empty space.  Ever since that forgotten October, Nora had placed her father's pocket compass on her windowsill pointing west every night before sleep, gripped by nameless dread if she forgot.  According to the lore, the buried ring kept its hoard locked away until that substitute routine shattered, fruiting for a single low tide only when habits break.  Timing her move to the year's lowest autumn tide, Nora had locked the compass inside her satchel last night and sat awake with trembling hands until dawn snapped the spell. 

Now she pried open a rusted roof vent, lowered herself onto the drowned peat floor, and clicked off her headlamp.  Lantern beams or torchlight would shrivel a sensitive fairy ring mushroom into barren slime before fingers could touch it.  Around her, gnarled russet trees stood in four inches of black seepage water, sheltered from the ocean wind by the submerged glass walls so the surface lay flat as obsidian.  In such pitch gloom, a cap the color of wet slate was impossible to see directly, yet Nora knew how to detect it by studying broken constellations mirrored in the brine.  Overhead, clear October starlight filtered through cracked conservatory panes and the canted spars of the surrounding wrecks, scattering bright fragments of Cassiopeia and Perseus across the dark water at her feet. 

Wading slowly between mossy roots, she paused after every step until her ripples faded and the starry reflection settled.  Most gaps in the reflected star chains were straight and sharp, sliced cleanly by iron mullions or the ribs of wrecked trawlers outside the glass.  Near the trunk of the oldest apple tree, however, Perseus bent apart along a smooth ring, its brightest star bowing outward where a submerged crown dimpled the surface film from below.  Kneeling in the freezing brine, Nora reached into that warped cluster of stars, felt the firm, pleated cap of the solitary fairy ring mushroom, and snapped it cleanly from its stem.  When she broke the cold cap beneath her nose and inhaled its crisp puff of spores, nearly four decades of blankness dissolved in a rush of salt wind and apple smoke.  There was her father at the tiller in his oilskin coat, his dry, unhurried laugh ringing across the bay as he pointed his brass dividers toward Perseus rising above the shoal.  As the turning tide began to whisper against the outer hulls, Nora climbed back toward the open air, carrying that brief autumn whole and bright inside her head at last.
