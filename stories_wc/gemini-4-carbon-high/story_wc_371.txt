Every November, after the birds fly south, the municipal bureau dispatches surveyors to inspect derelict lots exposed by the falling leaves.  Arthur watched from behind a curtain of winter ivy as a young clerk approached the iron gate of the Old Palace.  Thirty years at the drafting tables of that same bureau had turned Arthur into a creeping normality noticer, cursed to remember every baseline the city forgot.  Decade by decade, the administration had replaced orchard lanes with gray concrete, herbalists with ration kiosks, and songbird hedges with bare walls, moving so gradually that citizens accepted each loss as natural.  For most of his career, Arthur had simply inked those incremental revisions onto official maps and collected his pay. 

In retirement, haunted by his long compliance, he sought a tenuous absolution that could hold only so long as his hands kept repairing small corners of the ruin.  Over seven quiet years, he had reclaimed this royal rubble through guerrilla gardening, pressing stolen seeds of holly, yew, blackberry, and wild chamomile into every damp fissure.  The dense evergreens now baffled street patrols even in late autumn, sheltering a secret tea house in an overgrown palace courtyard.  Inside the restored cedar pavilion, ignoring the broken marble statues of forgotten monarchs outside, Arthur kept a tin stove and clay cups to find the sacred in the ordinary.  In a city stripped of texture, steeping real leaves in hot water had become his quiet liturgy. 

Now that refuge stood at the mercy of the young woman pausing outside the rusted bars with her survey ledger.  If she peered past the outer briars and logged an intact structure, demolition crews would flatten the thicket before winter set in.  Yet Arthur recognized her wool coat and the canvas satchel dusted with white flour at her hip.  At dawn on Mercer Street, he had seen this same clerk staring at wet gray plaster where the last hearth bakery in the district had stood the night before.  While commuters stepped past the erased doorway without blinking, she had stood trembling, clutching a dark round loaf rescued from the back alley before the masons sealed the brick.  Her satchel still held that heavy loaf, real bread from vanished bakery ovens that no longer appeared on any municipal register. 

Arthur knew the isolation behind her eyes, because solitary grief always curdled into surrender if nobody confirmed that the missing thing had once been real.  Hiding behind his holly screen would preserve his solitude, yet leaving a fellow witness alone in the cold would forfeit the fragile pardon he had worked years to earn.  Setting down his pruning shears, Arthur stepped to the woven trellis of living ivy and chose to uncloak both himself and the hidden path.  When the branches parted, the clerk gasped and clutched her clipboard against her chest.  Coaxing winter seedlings out of poisoned mortar had taught Arthur to be heuristically gentle, testing small mercies and adjusting to the slightest tremor rather than following any rigid script.  He kept his palms visible, retreated two slow paces when her shoulders stiffened, and spoke quietly of the warm rye scent drifting from Mercer Street before sunrise.  Seeing her grip loosen on her pen, he held back the yew boughs a little wider and invited her out of the wind. 

She followed him along the mossy flagstones into the cedar pavilion, where dried mint and chamomile hung in neat bundles from the rafters.  Without ceremony, Arthur lit the alcohol burner, warmed two coarse cups, and poured pale golden tea across the low wooden table.  The clerk unbuckled her satchel, drew out the blistered dark loaf, and tore the crust into uneven halves with steady hands.  They ate in silence while frost gathered on the latticework outside.  Nothing grand passed between them, no oath of insurrection or lament for fallen dynasties, yet the bitter warmth of wild blossoms and the sour chew of honest grain restored a weight to the afternoon that no decree could flatten.  When the cups were empty, the clerk opened her ledger, classified the entire quadrangle as flooded subsidence unfit for heavy machinery, and closed the cover.  Before leaving, she accepted a paper twist of wintergreen and chamomile seeds, sliding them beside the remaining bread in her bag.  Arthur fastened the ivy screen behind her footsteps, knowing that spring would bring new inspectors and that his old maps still lined the municipal vaults, yet grateful for a grace that held through one more night.
