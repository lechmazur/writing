Three centuries after the collapse of civilization, bitter wind off the iron crags drove wild thistle seeds through the gaps of the glass humming pavilion.  High above the darkened valley, tall quartz panes vibrated in their weathered bronze frames, filling the circular chamber with a ceaseless, mournful drone.  In the lost age, those singing walls had harvested mountain gales to feed a sleepless lantern, until seismic ruin snapped the spire off its true axis and snuffed the light.  Now only Oren remained at his post as the last beacon tower custodian, wrapping his threadbare coat tight while silver plumes of steppe grass hissed against the sill. 

Down in the thorny scrubland, scattered clans huddled over peat fires and mocked his vigil, insisting that humanity survived by blind fluke and would vanish the same way.  Even his sister had packed her mule two autumns ago, telling him that every broken thing beneath the sky was merely wreckage colliding in the dark.  Oren stayed in the cold aerie for one fierce purpose: to prove that coincidences have meaning.  Unlike his neighbors, Oren was retrocognitively blessed, gifted from birth with a calm sight that let his bare fingers read the living past imprinted upon stone, brass, and glass.  Where others saw random decay, his blessing revealed patient threads of continuity weaving through the centuries. 

Touching the cracked granite plinth at the center of the floor, he had felt the ancient earthquake that tilted the tower three degrees toward the north.  Touching the dormant mineral core inside the brazier, he had seen its makers design it to reject common fire, waking only when struck by a needle of pure stellar light.  Directly above that core hung the broken crystal prism, cloven diagonally during a savage winter freeze a hundred years after the cities fell.  An intact prism suspended in such a crooked spire would have aimed incoming rays uselessly onto the flagstones.  Yet Oren had measured the frost fracture with brass calipers and discovered that its steep, accidental shear compensated for the tilted masonry with mathematical grace. 

Still, the scarred lens required a bright celestial alignment along one narrow, uncracked pane near the zenith.  Seven lean winters Oren had spent by tracking star paths across the curved glass with white chalk, calculating how orbital drift carried distant suns into new positions.  Tonight, three brilliant stars of the Northern Crown would converge upon his final mark for less than a minute.  Even then, one stubborn problem remained: without an active core to drink its power, the unchanneled vibration of the glass humming pavilion rattled the bronze cradle and blurred every focal beam into faint, useless mist. 

Reaching inside his woolen tunic, Oren drew forth the triangular wedge of crystal that had split from the prism during that distant winter.  When his retrocognitive touch sank into those pale facets, he felt no storm, only the breathless midnight after the blizzard when deep drifts buried the ruined world and locked a shard of hush inside the parting lattice.  The severed fragment held true acoustic stillness, drinking up stray tremor wherever it rested.  His gift had even shown him why the falling piece survived unbroken: decades of wind had heaped soft drifts of dry thistle down over the granite floor just thick enough to cushion its drop.  As the Crown stars glided toward his chalk crosshairs, Oren wedged that shard of hush tightly into the vibrating joint between the iron support ring and the lens frame.  Instantly, the restless shuddering around the cradle ceased, as stored winter quietness spread through the metal and steadied the hanging crystal. 

High overhead, the three stars touched the mark together.  Pale light pierced the clear pane, entered the broken crystal prism, and bent sharply off its frost scar into a single, unwavering thread of silver radiance.  The needle struck the center of the cold core, and after three breathless heartbeats, deep inner warmth surged upward to rekindle the ancient beacon.  Hungry at last for the gale, the awakened stone drank the wild energy of the walls, turning their mournful drone into a rich, steady chord as golden brilliance flooded the sky.  Across leagues of shadowed waste, thorny purple thistles caught the sudden dawn on their spines, and tiny figures stepped from isolated huts to stare upward in wonder.  Resting his scarred hand on the warm brass rail, Oren looked toward the pass his sister had taken, certain now that an earthquake, a blizzard, drifting stars, and windblown weeds had conspired across three centuries to mend the dark.
