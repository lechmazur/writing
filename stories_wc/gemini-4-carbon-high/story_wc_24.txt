Every day at noon, Halvor stepped onto the granite parapet of the horizon measurement station and lifted the brass signal whistle to his chapped lips.  Immediately after blowing the whistle, he watched two herring skiffs far below cut their engines and trail their oars until the dark water flattened into black glass.  Maritime law granted the headland five minutes of undisturbed surface each midday so no boat wake would blur the distant rim where ocean met sky.  When the shrill blast finished echoing off the sheer basalt cliffs opposite, the narrow inlet settled into a profound fjord quiet. 

In that hush, Halvor could hear his charges breathing along the mossy terraces behind the stone observation hut.  Twelve cisterns capped with copper domes stood scattered up the steep slope toward the high tarn, venting trapped air with soft, animal sighs as dense brine coursed from one regulator to the next.  Because someone had to climb those icy granite paths at all hours to bleed sluggish valves and coax balky siphons, the Royal Hydrographic Office listed Halvor as a hydraulic clock shepherd.  Using falling water to track rising rock was an eminently peculiar enterprise, yet northern geology left the crown surveyors little choice.  Freed from the crushing weight of prehistoric glaciers, the coastal bedrock was still rebounding several millimeters each year, tilting ordinary pendulums out of plumb while winter cold shrank brass gears.  Only heavy salt water, cascading step by step to damp every gust of wind, could keep an honest pulse on a rising continent. 

The ministry cared only for updating nautical charts before hidden shoals lifted beneath merchant keels, whereas Halvor had taken this solitary berth to understand what clocks really measure.  For twelve winters he had pondered the secret shared by his cisterns and the optical instrument inside the hut: both relied entirely upon razor edges.  Within each stone tank, brine rose until it sheeted over honed steel blades so keen that surface tension could not cling or drag, shearing fluid into uniform intervals.  Inside the hut, the sighting tube held a matching pair of blackened razor edges set a hairbreadth apart, one bolted to the granite pier and the other floated on mercury, stripping away glare until only the sea rim remained. 

Stepping indoors while the inlet finished calming, Halvor closed the ministry ledger where clerks tallied seconds upward as though duration were coins piled in a vault.  Because snowmelt fed the high tarn without pause, his cascade never ran dry, sustaining the illusion that clocks marched endlessly into tomorrow.  To test his suspicion during the remaining four minutes of stillness, he had placed an old oak parlor clock beside the viewer, its hands missing and its mainspring slack for years.  From his wool vest he drew a tiny brass mantle clock key, a delicate heirloom among his heavy iron spanners.  Bench trials in his quarters had shown that a single tooth of its worn ratchet stored precisely two hundred forty beats of the dry escapement, matching one full cycle of the master siphon.  Halvor fitted the square socket of the mantle clock key onto the winding post and turned it until one crisp click caught against his calloused thumb. 

Immediately the brass balance wheel woke with a brisk chatter beside the slow, wet pulse behind the wall.  Bending to the eyepiece, Halvor met pale subarctic glare reflecting off the sound, which forced him to squint until his watering eye narrowed to match the slit between the twin razor edges.  There the distant horizon shone like a taut silver wire.  Adjusting the micrometer drum to keep that wire centered, he measured the sighting not by adding seconds, but through counting backwards from two hundred forty with every tick of the uncoiling spring. 

One hundred, fifty, twenty, he whispered into the cold brass hood.  Simply through counting backwards, the old fiction of accumulating hours dissolved like frost on warm slate.  Nothing in the station was manufacturing a future; everything was spending a disturbance.  The wound steel strip was unbending the twist of his fingers, the elevated brine was falling back toward sea level, and the ancient cliff beneath his boots was shedding the memory of vanished ice.  Three, two, one.  At zero, the parlor clock sighed into silence at the exact instant the master siphon emptied and the silver horizon steadied between the blades.  Clocks did not measure time at all, Halvor realized in the lingering stillness; they measured relief, the rate at which displaced matter returned to level rest.  Far below on the inlet, an engine coughed to life across the water, ending the midday truce, and the shepherd pocketed his key to go tend his flock.
