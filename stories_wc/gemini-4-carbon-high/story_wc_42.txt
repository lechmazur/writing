High in the lunar observatory above the clouds, Oren listened to the ancient mountain fracture beneath his boots.  Far below that luminous sea of mist, continents were buckling in a catastrophic age when foundations shake and empires turn to dust.  Yet through the open dome overhead, the moon hung changelessly constant, its pale craters untouched by earthly ruin.  Hours earlier, the astronomers had fled aboard the final airship, leaving Oren alone beside his brass lenses and tiny screwdrivers.  For fifty years they had valued him as a sage camera repairer, a quiet artisan who mended celestial shutters by day and broken village cameras by night, teaching grieving families that light outlives what it touches.  Where the scholars worshiped permanence, Oren had spent his life learning to harness the ephemeral, convinced that a vanishing smile or a slant of autumn rain held a fierce grace eternity could never rival. 

At the center of the chamber hummed the celestial sling, a vacuum centrifuge built above the dense lower atmosphere so a launched capsule could reach escape velocity without burning to ash in thick air.  Before sealing the rotor, Oren had emptied the capsule of cold astronomical tables and filled it instead with hundreds of translucent silver negatives salvaged over decades: laughing children, hearth smoke, his late wife singing in sunlight, and wet blossoms.  He stayed behind so those fragile human instants might rest safely in lunar dust forever.  Yet the moment he engaged the coils, a violent tremor wrenched the stone floor and sheared the regulator pin inside the Master Chronometer.  Behind its cracked crystal, Oren stared at a gilt clock hand that won't stop spinning, driven directly by the accelerating rotor beneath the floorboards. 

Worse, the quake had shattered the electronic strobe meant to flash through the drum viewport and trip the photosensitive latch holding the capsule on the spinning arm.  Without a precise microsecond pulse of light to release it upward through the launch chute, the centrifuge would soon tear itself apart and obliterate fifty years of rescued memory.  Oren knew rigid machines always failed when foundations shake, so he worked swiftly to restructure the ruined strobe housing into a purely mechanical camera shutter in front of his blazing workbench lamp.  He mounted six gossamer titanium blades on delicate bronze hairsprings salvaged from his finest focal assemblies.  Glancing at the chronometer, he noticed centrifugal force bowing the slender tip of the runaway needle slightly outward as speed climbed.  Because the dial matched the rotor axle turn for turn, Oren anchored a fine copper tripwire at the top mark of the outer bezel, positioned where that bowed tip would graze it at exact escape velocity while the capsule pointed straight up the chute. 

One fatal flaw remained: if all six bronze hairsprings did not hold identical tension, a lagging blade would smear the light pulse and hurl the capsule sideways into granite.  Yet every scale and pendulum in the dome jittered wildly from aftershocks, and brass test weights would dent the thin titanium foil.  In the tense hush between tremors, Oren turned to a glass bell jar on his bench where a withered lowland birch stood above dry shed foliage.  Lifting one crisp leaf with tweezers, he centered an open glass cylinder upright over the first shutter blade and released it from the rim.  Air resistance slowed the brittle curl to a gentle, unvarying terminal drift.  Pressing his brass ear horn against the restructured frame, Oren tuned the entire shutter by the sound of a leaf falling, dropping that same dry remnant onto blade after blade and turning tiny screws until each spring answered its whisper with one identical, crystalline note. 

A final, monstrous roar rose from the abyss as the mountain spine tore loose.  Granite blocks broke from the dome ribs, showering Oren in pale dust while silver clouds surged upward around the crumbling peak.  Beneath him, the centrifuge climbed to a piercing, luminous whine.  On the dial, the clock hand that won't stop spinning blurred into a solid ring of gold until its bowed tip kissed the copper wire.  Six titanium blades parted and snapped shut cleaner than a blink, slicing a single diamond sliver of lamplight through the viewport.  Bursting through the thin muzzle foil, a streak of silver lanced straight out of the collapsing observatory, tearing free of gravity toward the changelessly constant moon.  Dropping to his knees as the stone floor fell away, Oren watched that brief spark vanish into the quiet stars, his heart aching for the lost earth yet radiant with peace, knowing its most fleeting mercies would shine on forever.
