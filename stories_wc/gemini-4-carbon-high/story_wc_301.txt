Three weeks after stopping the medication, Callum felt every cold gust off the Cinder River as if his skin had been peeled to the bone.  For two years those white pills had blurred the valley into static, dulling his memory of the autumn flood that took his younger brother Jude.  Now, clearheaded and trembling, Callum climbed the shale embankment toward the disused railway station overgrown with ivy at Mile Sixteen.  As a truth-seeking river chronicler, he had spent a decade documenting every unlawful culvert, toxic outfall, and shifting bar along this watershed.  Yet precision had ruined him when county deputies used the map references in his published squatter survey to clear the Mile Fourteen camp during a storm, driving Jude upriver toward the flooded trestle.  Callum had come to this gorge not merely to measure flood marks, but to find a path to self-forgiveness in the last shelter Jude had reached alive. 

Currents had chewed deep beneath the limestone bluff, leaving half the timber platform hanging sixty feet above churning rapids.  Ivy wrapped woody coils around leaning chimneys and hung in wet curtains where the slate roof had collapsed.  Haste here meant falling into the gorge, while fear meant turning back, so Callum grew daringly methodical as he entered the ruin.  He knotted a climbing line around an iron mooring ring, probed every mossy plank twice with his steel sounding rod, and logged each rotted joist before shifting his weight.  Foot by measured foot, he crossed the groaning ticket hall until he shouldered open the oak door of the passenger washroom, the sole windproof room left on the bluff. 

Inside, Callum switched on his headlamp and swept the beam across cracked porcelain and glazed tiles crowded with decades of vagrant ink.  Drifters waiting out freight schedules or winter gales had turned the damp stalls into a public ledger of bleak street philosophy.  Callum read the faded couplets with the eye of an archivist, until slanted blue grease pencil beside the dry basin stopped his breath.  He knew those tall block capitals from childhood birthday cards and borrowed maps.  Wary of naming kin on a wall checked by railway police, Jude had chosen to allude to both Callum and a hidden dry cache through bathroom graffiti wisdom.  Directly beneath an older scrawl claiming that every river eventually drowns its witness, Jude had written, "Nobody owns the rain, brother, so do not curse the fool who drew the chart when the mud gives way."  Underneath that mercy, mimicking the crude stall notices around it, a second line promised clean air and the final verse at 419882 plus twelve paces north.  Strangers would dismiss the digits as nonsense, yet Callum recognized those exact map references from the private benchmark grid of his own watershed atlas. 

His pulse hammered as he checked his compass, realizing the coordinates pointed past the broken awning toward the cantilevered signal alcove jutting over the abyss.  Refusing to rush, Callum clipped a second carabiner to the iron balustrade, hacked through knotted ivy vines that masked missing boards, and counted each deliberate stride along the rim.  Twelve paces north of benchmark 419882, tucked inside the dry signal housing beneath tied oilskin, waited the burden Jude could not haul across slick trestle girders in a gale.  Callum eased the bundle onto his knees and unwrapped the canvas to uncover the folk squeezebox inherited from their father, its accordion bellows patched with tape from the yellow waterproof chart kit Callum had once shared with him.  Even after years of silence, Jude had trusted those repairs to keep the river damp out, leaving the instrument safe above the floodline because he knew his relentless brother would eventually survey Mile Sixteen. 

Braced beside the ivy curtain where mist drifted into the gorge, Callum slipped his palms beneath the worn leather straps and drew the wooden ends apart.  The mended pleats held tight without a leak, drawing a breath of cold autumn air before pressing out a low brass chord that vibrated against his ribs.  Without medication to blunt the edge, sorrow still cut through him like winter wind, permanent and real as the undercut limestone beneath his boots.  Yet holding what Jude had saved, and reading the grace left behind on that stall wall, gave Callum a raw solace that two years of chemical numbness had never touched.  He opened his ledger to grid 419882, wrote the final testament of his brother into the true history of the river, and sat breathing above the rapids until the last note joined the roar below.
