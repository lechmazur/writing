Panic always seizes a country parish after the substitute body is found, though Clara had never expected to see one dragged from her own brother's flooded crypt.  Six months ago, while Clara was away setting a quarryman's crushed ankle, young Ansel had supposedly succumbed to winter fever.  As a forgiving skeptic trained in anatomy and optics, Clara had gently pardoned the village elders for whispering about fairies when Ansel stopped growing at twelve.  Tonight, however, a burst water main had inundated the churchyard vault, and running water had stripped the glamour clean off Ansel's corpse.  On the wet flagstones lay an effigy of woven alder wood, bog wax, and the miniature brass bellows Clara had soldered for Ansel's toy steamboat years earlier.  Seeing her own childhood metalwork nested inside wooden ribs slick with living sap opened a cleft in certainty right down the center of her strictly rational world.  Material logic had not vanished, yet a narrow, irreversible fissure now admitted everything her textbooks denied. 

Because her stamped initials marked those brass lungs, the bailiff was already gathering lanterns and hounds to hunt both the changeling and his suspected accomplice.  Grabbing her surgical satchel, Clara slipped past the orchard toward the secret garden hidden behind a hedge maze on their neglected estate.  Strangers always lost heart among those shifting yew corridors, but years ago she and Ansel had wired salvaged bicycle chimes beneath the roots to ring along the true path.  Following those faint, familiar notes into the walled enclosure woke an ache of fond ingenuity.  Above wild quince boughs stood their childhood observatory, an inverted copper wash boiler coupled to rusty gramophone gears so candlelight could wheel homemade heavens across the stones.  Whenever young Ansel's chisel had slipped and pierced errant holes in that copper dome, Clara had linked the stray punctures with silver wire, guided even then by a quiet need to turn mistakes into constellations. 

Beneath that crooked dome sat Ansel himself, cradling a swollen left forearm beside a fallen wrench.  Hearing the parish alarm bell, he had climbed the mossy plinth to rescue their star projector before fleeing north, only to slip and snap his wrist against the stone rim.  He tilted his unaging face upward, looking shrewdly innocent as he murmured, "My brass lungs wheezed convincingly enough to fool old Doctor Vance, did they not?"  Behind that guileless, boyish plea for praise, ancient survival instinct kept his dark eyes fixed on Clara's hands, weighing whether truth had turned her toward splints or cold iron.  He confessed that he had staged his death to spare her the scandal of an ageless brother, yet he had hidden here all winter because mortal kinship had taught his kind to yearn.  Night after night he had cranked their tin sky alone, aching for stove warmth and Clara's inked thumb tracing new orbits beside his. 

Six months of needless grief stung Clara's throat, yet blame dissolved the instant she saw him shivering; she knelt on the damp gravel and unstrapped her kit.  She scolded softly, "You wasted my finest valve leather on a coffin trick," and switched on her pocket inspection lamp.  Years earlier, when Ansel was seven and shed a translucent, prismatic milk canine, Clara had scoffed at folklore, labeled the changeling's baby tooth a rare fluorite inclusion, and mounted it as her lamp's focusing lens.  Now, as its matched beam touched his bare wrist, refracted light pierced his remaining glamour and shone clean through the skin.  Hollow shafts of luminous bone, slender as silver reeds, gleamed inside his forearm where the radius had snapped.  Letting empirical observation steady her wonder, Clara trusted the disciplined craft of bone setting: she braced his elbow, drew firm traction until the crystalline ends chimed softly into alignment, and bound cedar trellis slats snug with waxed linen. 

Ansel exhaled as distant hound bays echoed beyond the outer hedge, whispering, "Even mended, a crooked callus will always prove what a botched copy I am."  Clara glanced up where her upturned lamp shone through that milk prism into the copper boiler overhead, scattering their invented star patterns across the dark yew walls.  Everything binding them together violated the expected order: a swapped cradle, a flooded wooden funeral, a fractured dogma, and a splinted inhuman arm.  Yet her deepest impulse remained unchanged: never to erase a flaw, but to map stray marks until they formed a navigable light.  Unbolting the copper dome to sling beside her satchel, Clara took her brother's uninjured hand and led him toward the forgotten drainage culvert, ready to chart a new sky beyond the parish.
