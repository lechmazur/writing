Six paces wide and a full mile long, the blooming jetty at twilight stretched like a slender ribbon of wildflowers and scarred basalt into an infinite pewter sea.  Above and around that thin spine of stone hung a boundless dusk where water and sky dissolved into a single violet abyss.  Brenn, once a gloom-hill coachman who drove black timber wagons and military hearses along fogbound mountain ledges, walked the narrow path alone.  Behind him, pale sea lavender, salt briar, and evening campion spilled from shattered cannon embrasures, sweetening air that had once choked on gunsmoke.  For seven years since unhitching his upland team, he had worked outward from the shore with one quiet vow: to let a garden reclaim a battlefield. 

Tonight, timed to the lowest ebb of the autumn tide, he reached the broken outer bastion where the masonry ended above swirling shallows.  Just beneath that dark chop lay the submerged concord that had ended the coastal war twelve years ago.  Exhausted admirals from both bleeding nations had chained their remaining siege guns around a carved limestone plinth and lowered the whole mass into the living coral.  Because neither army trusted the modern speech of its rival, they had summoned Brenn, who spoke dead languages preserved in the ancient highland barrows, to compose and recite the binding truce in Old Pelagic.  So long as coral encased both plinth and iron in one seamless vault, neither fleet could raise its weapons or renew the slaughter.  Yet weeping copper and bitter saltpeter from the drowned munitions had slowly poisoned the polyps.  Now, in the bleak months after the reef died, brittle white skeletons were crumbling under every tide, threatening to loose the rusted chains and cast unspent shells back upon the shore. 

Brenn knelt upon the damp flagstones and unbuckled his heavy leather waybill satchel to reveal a collection of dried seed pods salvaged from forgotten monastic conservatories in the hills.  Inside the oiled compartments rattled horned ironwood mangroves, coiled sea figs, and hooked bladder thorns whose woody husks had slept in dry highland air for generations.  Once rooted in saltwater, their tough taproots could bore downward through dead calcium and cage the sunken ordnance far tighter than coral ever had.  Running his thumb across the archaic script incised on bone tally slips, Brenn murmured the vanished Barrow tongue of the upland monks, matching thick pods to heavy surge and slender husks to deep crevices.  Yet bare basalt and bleached coral held no nourishment for waking embryos, and half the submerged craters leaked lethal verdigris from split brass casings.  In the deepening dimness, human sight could neither spot the poison slicks nor locate the rich pockets of organic silt needed to feed the seeds. 

Here his old craft along the mountain chasms guided him.  Up on those blind highland passes, where dense fog erased the cliff edges on both sides of a coach, drivers steered six horses past sheer drops solely by understanding bird calls echoing through the mist.  Brenn closed his eyes against the immense horizon and listened to the creatures wheeling around the slender pier.  From the eastern breach rose the clipped, metallic chatter of sandpipers recoiling from fouled shallows; copper scum pooled there, certain death for tender roots.  Beneath the western pilings, however, sounded the low, contented croon of roosting cormorants and night herons settling over clean eddies thick with herring scraps and ledges crusted in white guano.  Guided through the dark by those avian cries, Brenn climbed down the iron service rungs with his canvas bucket to gather heavy scoops of damp bird lime, fish offal, and decaying kelp.  Kneading that pungent mire into sticky balls around the dry husks, he prepared a rich mortar that would cling against the current and fertilize each seed the moment seawater cracked its shell. 

Wading onto the submerged western shelf in cold brine up to his knees, Brenn wedged thirty coated pods deep into the honeycombed fractures of the dead coral right where rusted siege chains met the carved plinth.  Above him, the last pale seam of twilight narrowed to a hairline between black ocean and an immense vault of stars.  Hauling himself back onto the narrow tip of the pier, he stood dripping before the emptiness and spoke the closing verses of the submerged concord aloud in the dead coastal tongue.  No living soldier remained to answer those ancient syllables, only the quiet purr of cormorants beneath the stone and the rising tide submerging the planted shelf.  Shouldering his empty satchel, the old driver turned shoreward along the fragrant thread of blossoms, walking home through the vast dark while unseen roots began their watch over buried iron.
