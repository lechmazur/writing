Minutes after the hydraulics fail, dark oil stiffens into frost across the iron catwalk of the starlit signal station.  Clara hauls on the release lever until her palms burn, yet the massive foghorn turret stays locked toward the inland hills.  Below the crag, black swells churn over three miles of vanished headland where her husband Jonah drowned with his survey boat in autumn.  Admiralty printers simply stamped blank warnings across that ruined water, so Clara stayed behind and became a patchwork navigator.  Inside the glass cupola, torn charts from the nineteenth century lie stitched with waxed twine to sonar strips and penciled crabber soundings.  She sews those broken coastal records together not only for stray winter skiffs, but to navigate the geography of loss that swallowed her life. 

Tonight, human memory is not the only thing drawn back to missing ground.  Forty fathoms beneath the whitecaps, collapsed basalt sea caves boom with low echoes whenever deep currents surge through their drowned mouths.  Because that subterranean tide fluctuates with moon phases, the sunken caverns stay mute at the quarters yet roar under the winter full moon with the exact infrasound of summer surf.  Lured from two hundred miles away by the phantom song of their ancestral rookery, thousands of gray petrels have abandoned southern waters in a fatal reverse migration.  Exhausted and rimed with ice, the flock flies north through freezing darkness toward ledges that no longer stand above the sea. 

Clara tracks them only through cryptic phone calls at midnight from Oren, an old shipmate who sailed beside Jonah and now mans the mainland naval hydrophones.  Defense censors tap every coastal wire to keep seabed acoustics secret, so Oren rings precisely at twelve while the logging reels reset.  Fifteen minutes ago, his voice murmured over the rotary line in tavern code, hiding radar bearings inside cribbage scores and cancellation frequencies inside hymn numbers.  Plotting his clues across her stitched chart, Clara saw the vanguard approaching eight miles out and tuned the station oscillator to shatter the false surf.  Seconds later, the brittle pressure hoses ruptured in the cold, stranding the main horn. 

Unable to swing the heavy turret, she must build an improvised acoustic projector on the seaward rail before the birds hit the swells.  Beneath crisp winter constellations, she hauls a spare electromagnetic driver, lead batteries, and a flared copper megaphone bell onto the parapet.  She lashes the bell toward the sunken trench and clamps the driver behind it, needing a dense coupling bridge to carry vibration from the striker into the flare.  When she tests an iron wrench between them, the magnetic coil snatches it flat and kills the stroke.  When she wedges an oak block into the gap, the fierce oscillation splinters the frozen timber immediately.  Desperate for metal that ignores magnets yet rings true, Clara reaches inside her coat for the final relic Jonah salvaged from the drowned chapel.  She draws out a patina bronze censer foot, shaped three centuries ago like a curved talon and crusted in pale green salt.  All winter she kept that cold artifact unwashed in her pocket as proof that something solid could endure the sea.  Now she braces the arched bronze claw tight between the armature and the bell rim, binding it with heavy copper wire until her knuckles bleed. 

Thin cries drift down from the starlight as the first weary ranks drop toward the dark grave of rock and bone.  Clara sets the oscillator to the hymn frequency Oren named and slams the knife switch shut.  Cast bell metal answers immediately, shedding green dust across her skinned fingers while driving a jagged, shivering drone far across the moonlit strait.  The brazen pitch collides with the deep cavern boom and splits the acoustic mirage of standing cliff apart.  Out in her lantern beam, hundreds of pale wings flare in sudden alarm, banking hard above the freezing spray before wheeling back toward the open south.  For twenty minutes Clara braces the bucking rig with numbed hands as ribbons of birds turn from the vanished shore, relinquishing a home that cannot hold them anymore.  When the sky finally clears, the ancient bronze bears deep gouges of bright gold where steel beat against it, and the water below stays as empty of Jonah as ever.  Yet resting her forehead against the frosted rail, breathing salt and cold iron into her chest, she feels a raw solace in letting the living fly onward while the dead keep the tide.
