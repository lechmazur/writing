Every autumn, millions of monarchs descended upon the ancient greenhouse at dusk, pressing warm wings against the high glass panes until the sky turned to pulsing amber.  Beneath that vast, rustling vault stood Silas, a thoughtful gravestone carver who had inherited the overgrown conservatory because its slanted twilight revealed the finest shadows on cut stone.  Before him rose an untouched pillar of black granite quarried after the spring flood swept forty villagers from the valley, taking his father Oren among them.  Rather than scatter forty separate markers across the hillside, the survivors had asked Silas for a single monument, leaving him to discover the shape of collective grief.  Trained to honor solitary lives with quiet dates and brief epitaphs, he stood before the monolith unable to strike the first blow, afraid that one unified form would erase forty distinct souls. 

On the first evening of the butterfly migration, when shadows pooled among the cracked terracotta pots and wild ferns, Silas set two tin plates of bread upon his timber workbench.  As the amber light thickened into a deep, resonant hush, Oren appeared on the opposite stool, rendered in pale quarry dust and steady twilight.  The spirit could neither lift an iron mallet nor speak plain commands, yet spectral mentorship required no physical force between two masons who shared an older language.  Throughout his boyhood, Silas and his father had kept a family custom of trading favorite poems at dinner, believing that spoken meter taught a carver where stone needed breath and stillness.  Lowering his eyes, Silas recited a cherished stanza given to him that morning by the grieving miller, and Oren answered in a voice like deep water rolling smooth boulders, offering an ancient hymn whose cadence echoed and widened the sorrow. 

Rising from the table, the ghost stepped beside the dark granite and drew a long, luminous curve through the powdered stone with translucent fingers, shaping the shared rhythm of those two verses before the mark began to fade.  Desperate to preserve the fleeting arc, Silas reached for an iron peg holding a heavy woven ribbon roll that masons steeped in chalk to plot sweeping lines across uneven rock.  As he unspooled the band and pinned it with lead weights to retrace the vanishing gesture of his father, Silas noticed how dozens of separate linen threads crossed within the cloth, each strand remaining distinct while bending into one unbroken wave.  Understanding broke over him with the quiet immensity of distant thunder.  Lifting his mallet and chisel, he entered a fiercely meditative focus, matching his slow breath to the silent pulse of ten thousand folded wings overhead.  Every measured blow rang against the vaulted glass in a thundering calm, carving not a single wide trench, but the first slender strand of a great braid. 

Across the butterfly migration, through twenty gilded dusks as new multitudes settled upon the warm panes and departed at dawn, Silas gathered favorite verses from every bereaved household in the valley.  Each twilight over supper, he spoke the treasured lines of a lost weaver, shepherd, or child, and each twilight Oren replied with a remembered poem that bound the new sorrow into the larger chorus.  Whenever the pale hand of his mentor swept across the granite to join one cadence to the next, Silas unwound another length from the woven ribbon roll to retrace both the fresh curve and the grooves already cut, ensuring that every ridge leaned upon its neighbors for strength.  Night after night his shoulders ached, yet his discipline remained fiercely meditative, stripping away grief and haste until only the deep music of steel on stone endured beneath the darkened glass. 

On the final dusk of the passage, only a trailing veil of monarchs lingered along the iron rafters of the ancient greenhouse before lifting southward into the violet sky.  Across the workbench, Silas spoke his own favorite elegy for his father, and Oren answered with a quiet couplet about mountain streams meeting the sea.  Instead of rising to mark the stone, the ghost rested a peaceful gaze upon the finished pillar and dissolved into the clear evening light as the last amber wings vanished overhead.  Silas set down the bare wooden core of the woven ribbon roll and laid his palm against the cold granite, where forty distinct channels rose from separate roots to intertwine in a single, soaring current across the stone.  In the immense stillness left behind, he knew that any mourner could follow one beloved thread from base to crown and feel the unbroken weight of the whole valley holding it aloft.
