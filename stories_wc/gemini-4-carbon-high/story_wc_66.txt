Three winters after the story famine, Bram climbed each concert evening to an ember alcove in mist high above the valley slate roofs.  Built by old turf cutters, the stone niche held flameless peat coals that kept dampness out of his transmitter tubes while fog hid the ridge from patrol vans.  Working solely by the shortwave radio dial glow, he unfolded fresh symphony sheets beside a brass microphone and scraped dried ink from his thumbnail with a penknife.  Thirty years as a diligent orchestra copyist had given him stiff knuckles, sharp eyes, and little patience for wasted motion. 

On a flat stone before the microphone rested a pinch of grey sweepings gathered from the concert hall rafters.  Before the Censorate burned the libraries and outlawed spoken tales for two decades, the iron and mica dust of this valley had absorbed generations of hearthside narration.  Saturated with human cadence, old household dust had grown secretly luminous, looking like ordinary grit by day yet shimmering in pitch dark and murmuring preserved voices whenever a broom or draft disturbed it.  Then came the twenty silent years of pulped books, scrubbed rafters, and strictly wordless state symphonies.  Though the bonfires had ceased, citizens still feared plain speech, Warden vans still policed the airwaves, and newer dust on sills and speaker cloths lay dark and blank.  It vibrated into sterile straight lines during orchestral broadcasts, knowing rhythm but nothing of plot.  Bram climbed the hill each week to teach dust to tell stories. 

At nine sharp, his single earpiece hissed with the opening strings of the Third Civic Symphony from the municipal hall.  Having copied all sixty instrumental parts by hand without a single blot, Bram knew every oboe rest, timpani swell, and harmonic gap in the score.  He flipped a copper toggle to launch his pirate broadcast on a shadow frequency pressed tight against the official state wave.  Blunt spoken prose would alert automated Warden scanners instantly and scatter the untutored grit on his stone.  Instead, he used the surging cello line to cloak his forbidden history via elusive song lyrics that bridged pure music and speech.  Matching his dry baritone to the low strings, he sang oblique verses of a winter weir, a baker hiding brass weights beneath river ice, and three lanterns crossing flooded pasture.  Down in the dark attics of the town, anyone tuning slightly beside the symphony would hear the buried ballad, while the dust on their silent radio cabinets drank the measured vibration. 

On the slate ledge before Bram, the grey motes shivered, abandoning their rigid rows to curl into linked narrative spirals.  Then the snare snapped shut in his earpiece.  Without warning, the state conductor skipped sixteen bars of brass fanfare, dropping the orchestra straight into a bare, exposed flute solo.  It was a deliberate Censorate ambush designed to strip acoustic cover from shadow transmitters.  Halfway up the misty switchback road below, twin yellow headlamps halted at the quarry turnoff, a brass loop antenna revolving on the van roof. 

Killing his power switch in mid verse would send a sharp carrier click straight to that spinning loop, yet singing full phrases over a lone flute would betray his ridge position within seconds.  Bram met the ambush with thistled calm, more irritated by the butchered architecture of the score than by the hunters on the road.  He leaned toward the red peat warmth of the alcove so the wet chill would not tighten his throat, angling the flute sheet into the green shortwave radio dial glow.  Having inked the first flutist part himself, he knew the older player paused a fraction early before every high trill to catch air.  Paring his voice to a breathy reed hum, Bram tucked single clipped words inside those exact overtones and breath marks: thaw, current, weights, dawn. 

For twelve delicate measures, contraband verse and silver flute moved as a single instrument.  When the horns crashed back in for the finale, Bram eased his output dial smoothly down into background static.  Down at the quarry bend, the loop antenna ceased turning, and the yellow headlamps rolled away into the valley mist.  Bram slid a wooden shutter over the radio glass, plunging the stone niche into utter darkness.  There, hidden from any daylight inspection, the spiraled grit on his ledge shone with a pale silver sheen, secretly luminous at last.  He brushed one inked knuckle across the stone, and the rising motes whispered back the completed tale of river ice and recovered weights, just as dust on a hundred dark attic sills below was learning to murmur again.
