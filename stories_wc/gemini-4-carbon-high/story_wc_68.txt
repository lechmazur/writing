History always rots fastest right after the plague ends.  Two weeks after our last fever barge sank into the bay, the survivors perched atop the sunken city ruins of Vane stopped coughing and started inventing convenient myths.  Down inside Culvert Six, a vaulted brick storm sewer jutting dry above the drowned marble colonnades, I preferred the company of barnacles.  Barnacles never misquote dead thinkers to excuse stupidity, which I consider my exclusive privilege.  Citizens on the upper cornices mock me as a wannabe philosopher living in a sewer because I wear patched sailcloth and recite mangled axioms from damp, half eaten scrolls.  I endure their jeers with the abrasive calm of a man who pays zero rent and sees what their kitchens flush.  As the ancient sage Menon probably meant to write before mildew claimed page nine, high perches breed delusion while gravity delivers truth to the drain. 

All morning, two competing narratives had echoed down through the iron plaza grate above my kettle.  Up on the dry Basilica steps, the Sanctum Censors claimed the survey ship Vesper had imported the sickness from foreign seas through forbidden longitude craft, demanding the noon smelting of every navigational instrument left in Vane.  Against them, the Mariners Guild swore the Vesper had docked weeks after the outbreak began carrying only relief salt, blaming stagnant rooftop cisterns instead.  Their shouting ruined my quiet until Pell, the youngest Guild horologist, splashed up my tidal stairs clutching an oilskin roll of master escapement drawings.  Pell knew I charted culvert mud out of a rationally rebellious refusal to bow to civic dogma.  More crucially, she knew my stubborn aim was to preserve ancient methods, both mechanical sea timekeeping and the lost discipline of testing rhetoric against physical matter. 

"Cut a dated silt core before noon to prove the Vesper arrived late, or the Censors melt our lathes," Pell panted.  "You are dripping brackish slime onto my sleeping sack, which advances neither navigation nor logic," I said flatly.  Even so, I knelt with my flat mason trowel beside the compacted outflow bank beneath the Basilica shaft, where plaza runoff and lagoon tides settled into an unbribed archive.  I sliced straight down through sedimentary layers to read what Vane had actually done.  On top rested grey wood ash from bonfires lit when the dying stopped.  Beneath that lay three dense fingers of white quicklime from peak quarantine, capping a greasy black band of marsh spores and dead foreign bilge mites that marked day one of the fever.  Right where that black band met clean summer shell marl underneath, my trowel grated on metal.  Scraping away dark muck, I uncovered a seafarer's chronometer cog of bright phosphor bronze, its involute teeth filed for ocean gimbals and its hub stamped with the Vesper anchor. 

When Censors smashed confiscated instruments on the customs anvil overhead, this gear had washed down the chute alongside unboiled bilge water before a single citizen fell ill.  The Guild Masters had lied: their flagship really had carried the mites, even if filthy bilge rather than brass clockwork had spread the rot.  "Press it two inches higher into the white quicklime band, Daro, or every horological manual in Vane burns today," Pell whispered in pale dread.  My practiced detachment cracked, leaving me kneeling in cold slime to agonize over two inches of mud.  Playing the cynical sage is effortless until reality forces you to choose which principle dies.  If I hauled the honest block upstairs, superstitious zealots would use my proof to abolish ocean navigation forever, confusing dirty hulls with cursed mathematics.  Yet if I moved the gear into the quicklime to save the Guild lathes, I would corrupt empirical observation itself, becoming just another liar rigging dirt for politics. 

Overhead, the leaning belfry bell tolled noon, and my breathing settled back into dry calm.  Only an amateur surrenders sound evidence to a crooked tribunal when he already owns a dry vault underground.  I stood, wiped my blade, and dropped the iron storm gate across my tidal stairs, locking both loud factions out of Culvert Six.  "Let the Censors melt hollow brass upstairs while we keep those drawings safe here," I told Pell, nodding toward my stone bench.  I left the bronze cog half embedded in the cut wall: its protruding teeth would gauge the new chronometers we filed in secret, while its buried half stayed fixed in the black seam to teach future navigators to boil their bilge before blaming the stars.  Then I ladled out two cups of mussel broth, mildly irritated that real philosophy required sharing dinner.
