Maren sets the heavy iron kettle over the gas flame and balances a tiny ladle of crushed Baltic amber on the burner grate beside its base.  Above her workbench in the rain-beaded skylight loft, November drizzle gathers in silver drops that blur the wet brick chimneys across the alley.  The filtered dusk settles over her ash shavings and oiled steel in a warm, oxidized hush, filling the workshop with a rust-hued peace.  From cold tap water to a rolling whistle takes four minutes, the exact duration of her late husband's act inside the circus cage.  Being a lion tamer's widow has made Maren hauntingly practical about sorrow: she touches the past only while the kettle boils, rationing memory so her hands stay steady for the woodcarving trade that feeds her. 

Turning to her bench vise, she tightens the iron jaws around a dense ash wood carving blank.  Julian bought a crate of these white ash blocks in September to turn spare legs for his training chair, trusting no other timber to absorb a lion's heavy swipe without shattering into lethal splinters.  His creed in the ring was always here and gone, a reminder that presence among predators was a struck match: step in, claim the center, give the cue, and step out before the cat remembered its weight.  During a damp October matinee, a slick patch of sawdust cost him half a heartbeat against the youngest male, and he lived his maxim one last time, whole in his braided coat one second and gone the next.  Maren sold the canvas gear before winter, yet she kept his chair lumber, determined to harvest lessons from loss rather than burn good wood on an empty pyre. 

As a low hiss starts inside the iron kettle, she pulls her drawknife briskly down the corners of the ash wood carving blank.  Over the past six afternoons, four minutes at a stretch, she has bored its center and pared its sides toward the shape of a palm handle for her favorite mortise gouge.  In the first weeks after the funeral, terror paced this loft like an uncaged beast whenever rain struck the skylight without warning.  Knowing that everything she loved was here and gone made the air itself feel treacherous, and her gouges slipped across maple panels because she braced all day against the next unseen blow.  Julian spent twenty seasons trying to contain savagery behind iron bars and ash staves, convinced vigilance could hold ruin at bay.  His death taught her that no cage holds forever, yet her ruined carvings taught her a second truth: if you do not contain grief within strict borders, it claims the entire house. 

Eight clean strokes finish the octagonal facets of the grip, and Maren taps the bare steel tang of her gouge firmly into the bottom socket.  In the top crown of the handle sits a shallow circular recess she hollowed yesterday.  From her apron pocket she lifts the bent brass balance wheel of Julian's stopwatch, crushed under his ribs at three minutes and twelve seconds, and lays it inside the wooden well beside a pinch of rusted ring sawdust.  At the stove, the kettle knocks and rumbles toward its boil while the ancient amber in the adjacent ladle slumps into a clear, glowing pool.  Years of gilding circus wagons taught Maren how fossil resin outlasts iron, preserving fragile wings and broken gears alike, and now she means to contain that jagged final second through crystallized time.  Gripping the hot ladle with iron pliers, she pours forty million years of liquefied sunlight into the ash crown, watching the golden resin swallow the stopped wheel and level into a smooth lens. 

Within seconds the surface skins over and hardens, locking brass and dust beneath a transparent dome that will never snag her skin.  Here is the lesson she worked so hard to claim: a bare steel tang bruises the palm, and loose sorrow ruins the cut, yet sealed inside resilient ash and hardened amber, the sharpest remnant of tragedy becomes the grip that gives her leverage.  Across the room, white steam jets from the spout as the whistle rises to a clear note, marking the fourth minute before vanishing into the rafters, here and gone.  Maren turns off the flame, pours dark tea while rain beads quietly across the darkening glass overhead, and breathes in the steady warmth of a life ready to begin tomorrow's cut.
