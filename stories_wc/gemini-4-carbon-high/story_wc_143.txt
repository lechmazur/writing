High on the moonlit summit of Sentinel Peak, Walter Finch knelt beside the perimeter gate with a bottle of photographic fixer and a camelhair brush.  Even during the space age, while July newspapers below celebrated astronauts preparing to walk upon the Sea of Tranquility, electronics remained forbidden near this volatile test basin.  Unshielded lunar lander thrusters left lingering hydrazine frost across the ancient meteor crater, and cold mountain air slowed vapor dissipation in ways no mechanical wristwatch could predict.  Instead, crews coordinated safe entry using chemical reactions for timed messages painted onto porcelain witness slates.  Colorless iodate and starch solutions reacted at the exact thermal rate propellant vapors sublimated, turning stenciled words dark blue only when the air grew breathable.  As chief crater inspector, Walter mixed extra thiosulfate fixer into a second line beneath the vapor notice so his grading release would materialize ten minutes after sunrise, holding the concrete trucks back without forcing him up the trail early. 

Shouldering his wooden bellows camera, he descended the silvered scree into a bowl scarred black by two years of rocket fire.  Younger engineers measured only blast depth and strut stability, eager to pave this battered basin into a calibration pad for lunar dishes at dawn.  Walter was purposefully curious, driven by a methodical habit of recording whatever progress prepared to erase.  Months earlier, when bulldozers demolished an abandoned meteor lookout on the north ridge, that deliberate inquiry had led him to sift the splintered floorboards and rescue a single nightwatch's hourglass sand grain wedged inside a crushed brass collar.  Old lookout journals stored in the county archive confirmed what his hand lens suspected.  Long before radar satellites replaced human eyes, solitary nineteenth century sky watchers had filled their thirty minute glass with pale impact spheres sifted directly from the pristine crater floor.  Now every native silicate bead in the basin below had been melted into sterile black slag by descent engines, leaving this tiny sphere the sole unspoiled remnant of the falling star that originally carved the peak. 

Walter had come into the cold shadow alone to photograph the last of something precious before wet concrete sealed the basin forever.  Shocked meteoritic glass carried microscopic fracture planes forged under cosmic pressure, and where faint moisture breathing from the unsealed crater floor met thin alpine cold, those lattices grew a fleeting six rayed crown of frost.  Yet the delicate ice needles could neither form without shearing nor survive a twelve second macro exposure until the mountain entered the dawning hush.  For barely three minutes between moonset and sunrise, rising canyon warmth balanced the sinking cold of the crater, silencing the relentless summit wind that otherwise trembled through his extended lens. 

Kneeling beside a cracked basalt vent at the basin center, Walter braced his tripod legs and set the tiny glass bead onto the stone with brass forceps.  Westward, the pale Moon sank toward jagged pines, looking strangely lonely now that humanity had mapped its quiet seas into landing coordinates.  Along the eastern rim, bruised indigo thinned into a sorrowful band of ash and rose, washing the scorched blast pits in faint silvered twilight.  All at once, the dry hiss among the cinders died away.  The dawning hush settled across the peak so completely that Walter heard his own pulse against his collar while bitter air kissed the basalt.  Beneath his ground glass viewfinder, vapor gathered along the invisible shock scars of the grain and blossomed outward into six luminous feathers of rime, catching the gray sky like a miniature star reborn in its cradle.  Holding his breath, Walter drew the dark slide, squeezed the cable bulb, and counted twelve silent beats into the stillness before closing the shutter. 

Seconds later, a pale gold ray spilled over the eastern lip, and the crystalline crown collapsed into a microscopic bead of water.  Gently, Walter slipped the damp grain into a corked specimen vial, folded his tripod, and began the slow climb out through brightening cold air that smelled of sage and spent propellant.  High at the perimeter fence, his delayed thiosulfate mixture finally exhausted its buffer, snapping his release order into stark blue letters across the porcelain slate right on schedule.  Heavy diesel engines coughed awake beyond the ridge, ready to bury the ancient hollow beneath gray cement so men might aim steadier dishes at another world.  Pressing the wooden plate holder against his coat as morning wind returned to mourn across the rim, Walter felt a quiet solace settle beside his grief.  Concrete pads would crack and steel antennas would eventually rust, yet this silver negative and the tiny witness sealed beside it would outlast every monument built to conquer the sky.
