Six months ago, I knocked on Miriam's seventh floor door under the pretext of borrowing sugar, a transparent neighborly ploy that nonetheless blossomed into our weekly tea sessions.  Neither of us ever sweetened our cups, and that untouched sugar bowl became the cornerstone in our delicate architecture of agreed upon lies.  She pretended to be nothing more than a retired dead language tutor passing quiet Tuesday evenings with an inquisitive tenant from downstairs.  In return, I pretended my curiosity stopped at Etruscan funerary verbs and brittle Hittite clay.  Yet from our very first meeting, an uncanny recognition hummed between us, as though we were old accomplices resuming a conversation paused a century before either of us was born. 

Tonight, Miriam had carried our porcelain teapot up to the apartment's rooftop garden during a meteor shower.  Far above the hum of streetlamps and apartment wiring, beds of rosemary, climbing nightshade, and damp thyme pressed their roots against the mossy stone parapet.  Overhead, pale green streaks of cosmic dust flared and died in utter silence, while seven sunbaked stories of nineteenth century limestone cooled beneath our boots and exhaled up the clustered brick chimneys. 

That rising thermal sigh was the exact reason I had sought Miriam out: I wanted to learn the language of old buildings.  Since childhood, I had sensed deliberate grammar in the midnight settling of heavy oak joists and intricate syntax in the drafty whispers of narrow stairwells.  Early stone scripts had borrowed their oldest angles from master masons, artisans who stacked granite in enduring sentences meant to hold memory long after flesh tongues vanished.  On that first afternoon while waiting in Miriam's foyer for unneeded sugar, I had spotted an antique desk seal resting beside her dictionaries.  Its tarnished silver handle was worn smooth, and its flat bloodstone base bore an engraved lattice of notches identical to the mason marks hidden behind our lobby radiators. 

Now, to my silent astonishment, Miriam drew that very seal from her pocket and placed it beside our brass lantern.  Pouring our chamomile in the near dark without spilling a single drop, she turned the silver stem until its carved bloodstone caught the brief white flare of a falling meteor.  By bringing the cipher into the open where every flue of the house converged, she was daring me to shatter our polite truce.  Setting my cup down, I leaned across the iron table to interrogate her at last. 

Using the rigorous philological method she had drilled into me all spring, I pressed her on why her custom Hittite exercises matched the exact acoustic rhythm of our third floor landing during rainstorms.  Without giving her room to evade, I demanded to know why she paused on the stairs each dusk with her palm flat against the central keystone, and why a scholar of parchment guarded a mason's seal.  Under my pointed questioning, Miriam neither flinched nor retreated behind academic riddles.  Both her mind and her fingers remained patiently nimble, unhurried yet deftly agile as she turned each sharp query back upon me until my own evidence aligned like fitted archstones.  "You already hear it," she said softly, her gaze lifting as twin meteors scored bright parallel lines across the zenith.  She confessed that modern cities survive on agreed upon lies: that mortar is mute, that dead languages belong solely to human throats, and that walls forget the lives they shelter.  Because stone speaks slowly across decades while drafts shift in a heartbeat, she had used our months of tea to verify I possessed both the stillness and quickness required to listen. 

Lifting the antique desk seal, Miriam pressed its bloodstone face firmly into the soft wax pooling beside our lantern wick.  Unlike ink on flat paper, the stamped impression demonstrated how architecture forms meaning through downward pressure, mapping weight, shear, and resonance across our building's seven stories.  Guiding my fingertip along the warm wax ridges, she murmured the phonetic values of load and counterbalance just as cooling night air fluted up the nearest chimney pot.  All at once, the faint vibration beneath the garden pavers resolved from random settling into a measured, welcoming cadence.  The old house was recounting the autumn storm during which its foundation was trenched, greeting the burning iron in the sky as kin from an older quarry.  Strange as those lithic syllables were, they slid into my mind with a haunting, intimate familiarity, like a lullaby recalled from a room I had inhabited in a half forgotten dream.  Another silent ribbon of fire crossed the constellations above the thyme beds while Miriam refilled my cup and taught me how to answer the walls.
