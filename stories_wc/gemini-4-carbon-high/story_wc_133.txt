Five years after the water wars, erratic storms had finally returned to scour Marrow Holm, a steep tidal island cut off twice daily by the rising grey sea.  In the ruined signal house on the crest lived Oren, an insomniac painter whose body had forgotten rest during the final dry siege.  Back then, closing his eyes meant missing the scrape of mainland picks beneath the cistern or a neighbor stealing from the ration barrels.  Now he spent the sleepless dark stretching salvaged sailcloth and painting stark panels of the scarred bluffs, laboring to thresh meaning from experience before grief hollowed him out.  By beating the chaff of panic from memory, he hoped some kernel of human truth might remain.  Yet the salt fog rotted his mixture of wild flax oil and raw clay, flaking each canvas into dust within months for lack of resin varnish. 

After a fierce midnight cloudburst hammered his slate roof, Oren went out at pale dawn while the ebb tide laid bare the black basalt causeway.  With commercial tubes long extinct, he gathered his pigments by following rain trails down the terraced hills, where fresh runoff sorted iron oxide and ochre into vivid crimson and gold seams.  Whenever the water washed tarnished brass shell casings into the clay pools, he tossed the metal into a leather pouch to keep his jars pure.  Halfway down the central gorge, he spotted a lean woman in patched oilskins climbing upward from the flats.  Mainland scavengers also worked old battlegrounds after storms by following rain trails, tracking stray rivets and copper wire upstream to buried dugouts. 

Oren traced his crimson rill around a slumped earthen breastwork just as the woman emerged from a pale limestone gully below.  Both stopped short at the lip of a steaming washout where their two runoff channels collided.  The night deluge had torn away the hillside to reveal an unearthed convergence that neither side had known existed.  Sliced open in raw cross section were two wartime shafts, the desperate well drift of the island defenders descending from above and the mainland sapper tunnel angling up from the beach.  Five years ago, two starving crews had clawed blindly toward the same subterranean fault, halting less than an arm span apart when seawater breached the seam and poisoned both shafts at once.  In the narrow rib of stone between the rotted timbers, opposing pick scars pointed directly at one another around a dry pocket of salt crystal.  Wading up to their ankles in swirling red and white mud, Oren and the scavenger stared in haggard awe, two gaunt survivors silenced by the terrible geometry of men dying inches apart for nothing. 

Movement glinted in the collapsed sapper wall, and the woman pried an amber brooch from a shred of decayed wool.  She rinsed the heavy oval in the runoff, revealing warm golden resin with a single prehistoric seed husk suspended at its core.  "All this mud, and I pull up parlor finery that will not buy a cup of barley on the coast," she muttered, introducing herself as Maren.  Oren stared at the thick lump of fossil sap, his sleepless pulse quickening as he recognized the ancient ingredient for waterproof glaze.  Crushed and fused into hot flax oil, that resin would seal his flaking paintings against the salt damp for a century.  His tone was wryly earnest as he explained, "I am the local lunatic who intends to boil that heirloom into stinking varnish so my pictures of this rock stop peeling."  Behind his dry humor burned such plain devotion that Maren looked from his jars of dark red earth back to the breached tunnels. 

Down on the flats, white surf began licking across the basalt spine as the tide turned, leaving her minutes to cross before the sea locked the island shut.  Parched from climbing slopes where ground puddles remained fouled by salt and old munitions, Maren glanced toward the stoneware flask at his belt.  Without ceremony, they made their exchange directly above the dry seam where two armies had failed to meet.  Oren handed over his pouch of heavy brass casings and his full flask of sweet cistern rainwater, and Maren pressed the warm jewel into his muddy palm.  "Paint this ditch while you are at it, so somebody remembers how close they dug," she said after drinking deep, then hurried down toward the narrowing causeway.  Climbing alone toward his studio as the sea closed around Marrow Holm, Oren held the ancient husk in its golden drop against the morning sun, ready to make the memory endure.
