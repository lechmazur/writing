Maren is a sea-foam recollectionist who whistles merry dirges while cataloging the end of things.  Every morning at ebb tide, she sets up her desk in an iridescent cave inside a giant crystal geode along the crumbling coast.  Centuries ago, a falling star cracked the hollow boulder open to the surf, lining her damp study with violet quartz that shimmers like oil on water.  It is a splendidly doomed place to work, and Maren adores how each retreating wave reminds her of inevitable oblivion.  Whenever storms churn the bay, rain washes the lingering feelings of the inland valleys down into the brine and whips them into pale froth.  She must harvest those fragile suds quickly, working before memory fades into dry salt and empty air. 

Her essential instrument is a smooth slab of crater glass, fused from beach sand by the very meteorite that split her crystalline cavern.  When she spreads fresh lather across the smoky green surface, light bouncing off the surrounding facets reveals tiny tremors within every bubble.  By rare talent and patient training, Maren is an archivist who transliterates emotions, turning wordless human moods into the phonetic script of an ancient coastal alphabet.  Sharp grief hisses on her parchment as crisp sibilants, while nervous hope hums in clusters of liquid consonants.  Though townsfolk rarely read her damp ledgers, she pursues a singular obsession above all else: to discover why some paths disappear.  All across the misty headlands, familiar cart tracks and shepherd lanes have been vanishing beneath thick turf, leaving villagers delightfully bewildered by their shrinking world. 

Maren pours herself cold nettle tea and smiles fondly at the creeping erasure, convinced the answer hides in the runoff from those lost trails.  Yet every sample gathered near a vanished road suffers from infuriatingly stubborn missing pieces.  She can trace the phonetic syllables of weary footsteps, chilled ankles, and autumn melancholy with brisk precision, right up to the moment a lane ceases to exist.  Then the foam always goes flat on the crater glass, leaving hollow rings where final feelings ought to shimmer.  Deprived of endings, she is forced to conjecture, penciling cheerful guesses into the margins about what grim fate has swallowed each route.  Perhaps travelers sigh so heavily that the earth folds over their boots, she muses brightly, or perhaps sorrow simply dissolves gravel into mist. 

On this blustery Tuesday after the Old Briar Way vanishes overnight, Maren ladles her richest catch yet onto the dark lens.  Prismatic gleams dance across the cavern walls as her pen races to capture the sighing consonants of two wanderers walking homeward in the dusk.  Just as the script nears its climax, the middle bubbles pop without a whisper, opening the familiar, maddening void while the remaining suds shrink fast.  Overhead, a gust rattles the jagged arch of the geode, and a soaked thrush flutters inside to rest its wings on an amethyst ledge.  Maren glances up to bid the damp visitor a pleasant journey toward winter, only to witness the mystery unravel via the scattered seeds dropped by a migrating bird.  Shaking spray from its feathers and beak, the thrush dislodges a dozen speckled hawthorn pips that patter straight onto the wet crater glass. 

Where each seed touches the thin film of brine inside the blank spaces, the smoky glass flares with amber light.  Leaning close before the last moisture vanishes, Maren gasps in merry astonishment and transcribes the warm, rounded vowels radiating from the hulls.  Rainwater only carries heavy, weeping emotions down to the sea, she realizes, whereas quiet contentment stays dry upon the hills, soaking into roots and wild berries.  Those completed lines spell neither panic nor tragedy, but the deep, settled peace of wanderers who have finally reached home and no longer need a road.  Once footsteps stop, passing flocks feast on the hedges along the silent ways and sow green forgetfulness over the stones.  Maren wipes the dry salt and speckled pips from her lens with a wistful chuckle, mildly disappointed to cross out all her splendidly dismal theories.  Still, she takes bright comfort in knowing that even the happiest arrivals are swiftly overgrown, forgotten by the tide, and surrendered to the weeds.
