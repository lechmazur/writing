Three weeks after the power grid stabilized, the Laboratory of Lost Sounds stopped flinching.  Its reels turned without stuttering now, and the old hoist house on Kettle Ridge hummed with recovered noises: canary chatter, mule harness, the cough of a shift whistle nobody living had heard.  Maren Voss, the laboratory's prism-song bell ringer, missed the flinching. 

Her instrument was forty glass bells, each tuned to wake under a single color, and she played them without touching them, by tilting a prism in a beam of light so the spectrum slid across their rims.  People called her playing powerfully gentle, because a tilt no wider than a breath could hush a whole room.  During the outage years she had lit the beam with her grandmother's carbide lamp, and the town had climbed the ridge on foot, in the dark, to sit in its amber spill. 

Now the arc lamps worked again, and Director Hale had built what he proudly called the artificial pilgrimage.  It was a wooden ramp spiraling four hundred steps around the inside of the hoist house, the exact length of the miners' walk from the gate to the Number Nine cage, lined with speakers that played the lost sounds in the order a miner would have met them.  At the top waited Maren's bells, which were to recreate the one sound no tape had kept: the Number Nine shift bell, silent since the collapse. 

"A walk to nowhere," Maren said at the rehearsal.  "The mine road is real, and it's still out there." 

"The mine road ends at a concrete plug," Hale said, not unkindly.  "Somebody has to build the place people arrive." 

She had no answer, so she rang.  Under the arc lamp the bells sang in perfect pitch, bright as cutlery, and the sound went through the test listeners like light through a window, leaving nothing behind.  Old Tobias Reck, who had ridden the Number Nine cage as a boy, shook his head. 

"Right note," he said.  "Wrong bell." 

That night Maren stayed among the dimmed machines, turning the problem the way she turned her prism.  She had come to this work to discover the texture of connection, the actual grain of it, what passed between a ringer and a listener or between the living and the lost, and for the first time since the lights came back she could feel none.  So she did what the laboratory had taught her to do with any dead sound: she questioned every assumption holding it up. 

She had assumed a lost sound was a pitch.  But Tobias had heard the pitch and refused it.  She had assumed clean light was better light. 

She took down the carbide lamp from its shelf and unscrewed the lens, a thick glass disc pitted by sixty years of grit and scored with one long scratch where her grandmother had dropped it running from a roof fall.  Maren had always thought of those marks as damage.  She held the lens in the arc beam, ahead of the prism. 

The light broke, softened, went the color of lamp oil, and the bells answered with a burr in their voices, a rasp like rope through a pulley, while the shadows of the pits swam gold across the rafters.  Her hand trembled, and the song trembled with it. 

That tremble undid her last assumption, the one she had defended hardest: that a pilgrimage was something a road gave you.  Nobody had climbed Kettle Ridge in the dark for the scenery; they had come to be carried by each other's footsteps, and every holy road was artificial on the day someone first decided to walk it. 

At the opening, she did not wait at the top.  She stood at the bottom step with the lens and asked the pilgrims to line the ramp, then placed the glass in the first open palm. 

"Carry it ten steps," she said, "then give it away." 

Hand to hand they propelled it up the spiral, past the canaries and the mules and the whistle, each stranger warming the glass for the next.  Maren climbed beside it and watched their faces change as they let it go. 

Tobias Reck received it last, at the top, and held it in the beam with both shaking hands.  Maren tilted the prism, a breath, no more. 

The bell that sounded was hoarse and warm and unsteady, with forty palms and one old man's pulse in it, and gold shadow poured down the spiral over everyone who had carried the glass. 

"That's her," Tobias whispered. 

Maren felt it then, under her fingertips where the prism hummed: connection was not smooth.  It was pitted and scratched and passed along, and it rang truest in hands that shook.
