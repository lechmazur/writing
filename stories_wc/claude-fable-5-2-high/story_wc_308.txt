Oret had skimmed stones across the Slack for fifty years, and her knees had paid for every one.  The Slack lay still as poured glass along the canyon floor, between towers of bone that some forgotten giant kind had left standing like a ruined comb.  A flat stone thrown well would skip nine times, each touch ringing a harmonic, and the canyon of endless echoes would hand the notes back changed.  From the changes a skimmer could read rockfalls, floods, and strangers three days off.  That was the trade. 

The duty was older.  Somewhere among the towers wandered a single beat that belonged to no skimmer, a dull knock that had never come home to whatever made it.  Every other sound in the canyon circled back to its source eventually, even if it took a lifetime.  This pulse had been left wandering before the first skimmer cut the first ledge, and the vow of every skimmer since was to walk it home.  Oret had kept the names of all forty who tried, and she spoke them each dawn before she touched a stone. 

She had walked after the pulse herself for decades, up the canyon and down, and it had always moved on ahead of her boots.  Now she could not walk past the end of her own ledge.  "Then I will learn to go without going," she told the towers, and they told it back to her until it sounded like a promise. 

She began with what she could reach, which was down.  The ledge was floored with dust, and the dust lay in bands, one for each skimmer who had sat there chipping stones and shedding skin.  She dug with a spoon and a brush, through centuries of layered dust, naming each band as she opened it and setting aside its chips in order so they could be laid back.  It took two winters.  Below the fortieth name, below the first ledge-cutter's ash, her brush found a stone arrowhead. 

She did not lift it for a full day.  When she did, she turned it in her cracked fingers to appraise it the way she would appraise any skimming stone: weight, balance, edge.  The tip was crushed flat, and bone meal was still packed white in the flake scars.  She flicked it with a fingernail, and it gave the dull knock she had chased all her life. 

So a hunter had loosed at something, long ago, and struck a tower instead, and the arrowhead had dropped here and been buried while its echo was still out.  The pulse had been coming home for centuries to a source it could not hear, and forty reverent skimmers had sat on top of it, raising dust.  Worse, they had walked after it, and every bootfall had thrown fresh echoes that shouldered it aside.  Oret laughed once, without bitterness, and the canyon kept the laugh. 

Uncovered, the arrowhead still rang too softly to call anything in from miles away.  It would have to be skimmed across the Slack, nine loud touches, at the one moment the pulse was passing close, and a stone can only be skimmed once.  She needed to know where the pulse was without taking a step toward it. 

The swifts showed her.  They nested by thousands in the hollow towers and rose each evening in one dark sheet, and she had watched them for fifty years as weather.  Now, sitting still, she saw the dent: a place where the flock flinched and folded, as if a hand had pressed it, and the dent traveled.  Through the patterns in that flock of birds in flight she tracked the pulse from tower to tower all summer, scratching its road on the ledge wall beside the forty names.  By autumn she knew the canyon's far bends better than she had when she walked them. 

On the first cold evening the dent came down the near towers and leaned out over the Slack.  Oret held the arrowhead and felt what it was to throw away the oldest thing she had ever touched.  But a relic kept on a shelf was only more dust on the source, and she had not dug through forty lives to add a forty-first.  She spoke the names, set her bad knees, and threw. 

The arrowhead struck the water nine times, knock after knock, and on the ninth the pulse answered from directly overhead and fell into step.  Stone and sound went under together.  For one beat the canyon held a rest it had not held in living memory, and above it the swifts closed into a sheet with no dent anywhere.  Oret laid the dust back band by band, in order, and picked up an ordinary stone.
