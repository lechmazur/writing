Odalys Venn had lived eleven years in the Pell Observatory, which sat on a peak so far above the clouds that the storms grumbled beneath her floorboards like bad neighbors.  Lightning lit the place from below every night, flaring through the cobalt glass portico where the founders had once stood to stare safely at the sun.  Her only company was a goat named Kepler and the ledgers of the dead astronomers, nine centuries of eclipse records they had called, with professional gloom, the Shadow Books.  From those ancient shadows Odalys had worked out one bright fact: in three years the moon would cross the sun directly over the mountain.  The smelter towns below had not seen the sun in four generations, only a ceiling of soot and rain.  If she could coax them up the mountain for the eclipse, they would watch the old darkness pass and a first true morning arrive, and nobody could ever sell them the ceiling again. 

So she broadcast.  The storm chewed every transmission to scraps, so she learned to write forecasts in verse, because a rhyme can lose half its words and still limp home.  "The black coin covers the gold coin's face," she sang into the hissing microphone, "then morning is minted in a higher place."  It was prophecy by arithmetic and lyric by necessity.  Kepler ate the first draft. 

Down in Slagmouth, a scrap dealer named Tobiah Crake caught her fragments on a receiver built from a soup tin and a dead man's dentures.  He recited them from a barrel outside the pawnshop, and people who had been promised nothing for a century stopped to listen.  Odalys heard of him through the valley's crackling chatter and, for the first time in years, laughed out loud.  A megaphone with legs was exactly what a hermit needed, so she gave Crake her loyalty the way one lends a ladder.  She fed him verses; he grew a beard, a following, and a hat. 

By the second year the hat had a collection plate in it.  Crake, now the Prophet of Slagmouth, had begun mending the gaps in her fragments with his own inventions: the coming dark was punishment, he preached, and safety lay indoors, on your knees, with a tithe.  Nobody was to climb anything. 

Odalys sat a long while among the Shadow Books, feeling her allegiance come loose like a tooth.  She had been loyal to the stars, then to the man, and it turned out both were only stand-ins for the people squinting up at soot.  Loyalty, she decided, was allowed to have variants, provided the dawn stayed the same. 

She could not outshout him, so she would out-fragment him.  Until then the storm had done the breaking; now she broke the verses herself, one line to each frequency, so that no single radio in the valley could hold a whole prophecy.  Crake's soup tin got "the black coin covers" and nothing more.  The laundress three doors down got the gold, the ferryman got the higher place, and a girl with a crystal set got the date.  To hear what was coming, neighbors had to carry their radios into one kitchen and let the message unfold between them like a map with many owners. 

On the eve of the eclipse, lanterns came winding up the mountain road, and at the front, puffing, hat askew, marched Tobiah Crake, because a prophet whose flock is leaving must lead or be left.  They broke through the clouds soaked and blinking, with the storm flashing under their boots and a sky overhead that most of them took for a trick.  Odalys herded them under the cobalt glass portico, four hundred strangers and one goat packed into a long blue porch built for staring down the sun. 

The moon took its bite.  The ancient shadow came racing over the cloud tops where nine centuries of dead astronomers had said it would, and the valley people, who knew darkness well, discovered they had never seen it arrive from somewhere, which meant it could also leave.  Crake opened his mouth to claim it.  "Don't," said the laundress, not unkindly, and he shut it. 

Then the edge of the sun came back, one hard bead of fire through blue glass, and four hundred people who had been taught that light was a rumor made a sound Odalys would spend the rest of her life trying to put into verse. 

Afterward Crake asked what his share was, and she handed him a broom.  He swept, and stayed, and proved a decent cook, which was allegiance of yet another variety.  The radios stayed too, a shelf of soup tins muttering in pieces, and most evenings someone climbed up to help her assemble the sky.
