The Marigold Queen had snapped her anchor chain eleven winters ago, and ever since she had circled the fogbound bay like a thought nobody could finish.  Free of every dock, the carnival ship still could not find the sea. 

Sabela rowed out to her at dawn with three spools of green thread and an emerald swatch card buttoned inside her coat.  The card was her mother's, a paint strip of four greens from the years she had been the ship's scenic painter.  Three shades were labeled in pencil: Carousel, Absinthe, Mermaid.  The fourth, the deepest, had no name at all. 

Six months ago, a marrow registry had written to say that Sabela's blood belonged to two people.  She was a chimera; a twin had dissolved into her before either of them was born.  The discovery had not frightened her.  She had always been blissfully aberrant, a woman with one hazel eye and one green, a seam of darker skin down her belly, a childhood habit of saying we.  The letter only told her who we was. 

What it could not tell her was the name.  Her mother had known, from an early scan with two heartbeats and a later one with one, and had spoken of it only sideways, in bedtime stories about the ship.  Somewhere aboard, she said, was the Garden of Forgotten Names, where carnival folk painted their lost ones onto tin leaves, and where she had painted one leaf in a green she mixed herself. 

Her mother had died in March, and since then the stories would not hold still.  Grief kept repainting them.  Some nights Sabela remembered the garden beneath the Ferris wheel; other nights it lay behind the mirror maze, or down where the calliope slept.  She could not trust any single telling, so she had come to recapture the truth where the tellings crossed. 

She climbed the boarding ladder into fog that tasted of rust and old sugar.  At the ticket booth she knotted the first thread to the turnstile and spoke the first version aloud, walking exactly where the story walked: past the ring toss, under the wheel.  The thread paid out behind her and ended at a locked winch house. 

She went back and began again with the second spool, the second telling, through the mirror maze, where a dozen clouded Sabelas turned their mismatched eyes toward her.  Even as she spoke, the memory shifted under her tongue; her mother's voice said stairs where yesterday it had said ramp.  She let it shift and kept unspooling, because the thread remembered what she had said even when she could not. 

The third telling took her below, past the calliope with its pipes full of gull nests.  By noon the ship was strung like a loom, and the fog had closed the bay so completely that her rowboat might have been a rumor.  She was stuck aboard until it lifted, with the run of every deck.  It felt, she thought, a good deal like having a body. 

Then she saw it: all three threads, wandering by different routes, passed through one doorway on the promenade deck, a glass-roofed room no version had ever called the garden.  Each story had hurried through it on the way to somewhere false. 

Inside stood wire trees hung with hundreds of tin leaves, and the fog came through the broken panes and moved them so they whispered.  Most had rusted blank.  Sabela held the swatch card against leaf after leaf, Carousel, Absinthe, Mermaid, until the fourth green answered from a low branch. 

The leaf said Noa, in her mother's upright hand. 

Beside it hung a small brass lantern with a clean, unburnt wick.  She remembered this part in every version: the carnival folk lit a lantern on the day their mourning was done, and let it burn out.  Her mother had never lit hers. 

Sabela took out her matches, and then stood a long while without striking one.  You could not finish mourning someone whose blood was still doing half the work of your heart.  Perhaps her mother had understood that, and left the solace unkindled on purpose, a comfort kept whole by never being spent. 

Sabela put the matches away.  She took a pencil from her pocket and wrote Noa beneath the fourth green, the name recaptured at last, where no grief could repaint it. 

Outside, the fog held the bay shut, and the Marigold Queen turned on her slow, unanchored circle.  Sabela sat down under the tin leaves, beside the unlit lantern, in the one body she would never leave and had never been alone in. 

"We found it," she said, and waited, unhurried, for the tide.
