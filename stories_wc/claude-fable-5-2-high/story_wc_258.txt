Three weeks after the cryptocurrency crashes, the loft above the shuttered exchange cafe still smelled of burnt espresso and other people's hope.  Rain beaded on the skylight, and each drop threw a small trembling lens of gray light onto Odile Marrow's worktable.  She was a cartomancer who drew her own decks in onyx ink, grinding the black stone herself, because her grandmother had, and her grandmother's grandmother before that.  What Odile had never managed to copy was the deck she inherited.  Grandmother Vesna's cards lay in their cedar box, seventy-eight rectangles of black on bone, and no two were quite the same size.  Vesna had left no instructions, only the box and a tailor's measuring tape coiled inside it like a sleeping yellow snake.  For years Odile assumed the tape was an accident of packing. 

Then the coins fell, and the city began climbing her stairs.  They came because a card reader asked for no passwords: programmers, a dentist, a grandmother who had mortgaged her bakery on a token named after a dog.  They did not really want the future; they wanted to confide the past to someone who would not laugh.  Odile listened, and because she was steadily curious rather than suddenly so, she kept a ledger of what they said.  The same phrases surfaced night after night: everyone was doing it, I thought I was early, I only wanted to stop being afraid.  She wanted to map the pattern, to chart how a whole culture had leaned over the same cliff together, but her own decks could not hold it.  Her spreads narrowed every sorrow down to one card, one answer, one road. 

Vesna's deck, she remembered, had never narrowed anything.  Villagers used to leave her grandmother's kitchen looking not answered but enlarged.  So on a night when the rain drummed hardest, Odile opened the cedar box and decided to take the inheritance apart the way the programmers took apart dead code. 

She uncoiled the measuring tape and began with the Fool.  Its height matched no standard, yet the tape answered at once, because the tape was not marked in inches or centimeters.  Vesna had inked her own divisions along it in faded onyx, and the Fool's edge ended exactly on a tiny drawn doorway.  Card by card, Odile measured, noted, and compared, working backward from the finished object toward the mind that made it.  Each card's length landed on a different symbol on the tape: a well, a bridge, a loom, a market stall.  Each card's width, she found near dawn, matched the notches cut into the long edges of other cards.  The deck was not meant to be laid in a line.  It was meant to be joined, notch to edge, every card opening onto two or three others, a reading that worked by multiplying paths instead of pruning them.  Vesna had not been telling fortunes at all; she had been surveying a village, measuring how its fears and weddings and debts connected. 

The baker came back that evening, shaking rain from her scarf, and Odile tried the old method for the first time.  "Tell me again," she said, "and this time I will not stop you at one card."  As the woman confided the loan, the dog token, the son who had urged her on, Odile fitted card to card across the table.  The market stall joined the bridge; the bridge forked toward the loom and the well; nothing closed. 

"It looks like streets," the baker whispered. 

"It is streets," Odile said, "and you are standing on only one of them."  Naming no one, she showed her where a dentist's story had crossed the same bridge, where programmers had drunk from the same well, where a hundred private shames ran together into one public road.  The baker laughed, once, wetly, the laugh of someone discovering she had been lost in a crowd rather than alone in the dark. 

When she had gone, Odile found one length of tape she had not yet used, a final hand-inked division beyond the last card's measure.  No card in the box was that long.  The symbol beside it was a square with dots across it, and it took her a moment, looking up, to recognize a skylight in the rain.  Vesna had left room for one more card, and had somehow known where its maker would be sitting.  Odile did not try to solve that part; some inheritances are meant to be carried, not opened.  She cut a strip of bone-colored board to the measure, ground fresh onyx, and began to draw the crash as a crossroads.
