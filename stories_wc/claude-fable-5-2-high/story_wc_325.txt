At slack water the sea stopped booming against Skerry Head, and the hush fell on Ines Okoro like a wave.  She had forty minutes between the ebb and the flood before the cave's throat filled again.  In her chest pocket, folded in glassine, rode a fleck of gold no larger than a comma.  She had stolen it by accident, and she was going back to return it. 

Three weeks earlier she had followed a dead telegraph cable into the rock, the way she followed everything, by what could not be seen.  Ines tracked drafts.  Moving air meant a second opening, and a second opening was the only promise that let a claustrophobic woman crawl underground at all.  She had learned the fear at nineteen, pinned eleven hours in a Welsh squeeze while a rescuer she never saw talked to her through the stone.  She never got his name.  She had been looking for ways to repay a stranger ever since. 

The cable had led her to a door, and behind it to the Halloran Testing Station, a research facility cut into the headland in 1871 and missing from every map since the company folded.  Here clerks had once hunted faults in undersea cables, reading a spot of light on a mirror to find a break two hundred miles out.  They had tracked invisible things too.  On the desk lay the station codex, a fault log whose margins some night clerk had illuminated in gold.  Beside each repair, in tiny gilt letters, stood the name of a splicer or grappling hand the company had never troubled to record.  Then the walls had leaned in, and Ines had fled with her thumb still on the page.  At home, under a loupe, the fleck on her glove resolved into a single letter.  She had carried off part of somebody's only memorial. 

Now she lay in the squeeze, cheek to wet limestone, the old cable under her palm like a handrail.  Her heart crashed in the quiet, louder than surf.  She did what she always did and let the fear change state.  She breathed out slowly and watched vapor turn to fog in her lamp beam.  The fog leaned inward, toward the station, and so the air was moving, and so there was a way.  Farther on, January frost furred the cable's iron armor where the draft ran coldest, and she followed that white thread through the dark.  Breath to fog, damp to frost: each change drew her a map of something she could not see. 

The iron door stood as she had left it.  Inside, mirror galvanometers sat under dust, and coils of gutta-percha sample cable hung from pegs like sleeping eels.  The silence was enormous.  It had weight, the way the sea has weight just before it breaks.  She opened the codex to the page she had wounded.  Fault at 212 miles, the entry read, and in the margin, in gold: Mended by Tam Reilly, splicer, lost with the Corvid, with a bare patch where the T belonged. 

Glue would have been a lie laid over the clerk's work.  Ines had spent three weeks learning the gilder's older trick.  She bent close and breathed on the bare patch until her breath condensed and the ancient size beneath turned tacky again, one more change of state.  With a sable brush she laid the fleck home.  It caught the lamp and held, and the name was whole. 

She could have left then.  The walls were already leaning, and her watch gave her nine minutes.  But a stranger had once stayed beside her in the dark, asking nothing, and she understood now that this was the whole debt.  So she stood at the desk and read the margins aloud, every name, splicers and stokers and the boy who greased the grapnel rope.  Her voice went out into the hush and the hush took it, the way the seabed takes a cable.  She could not make the world remember them.  She could only stand with them a while, which was different, and enough. 

Then the tide turned.  She felt it before she heard it, a shiver in the draft, and then the first breaker hit the headland and the whole hush crashed in.  Ines closed the codex, left it where it belonged, and went back into the squeeze with water licking her boots.  The rock pressed, her breath fogged, and she crawled against the draft toward the noise of the sea.  Out past the surf, on the dark floor of the channel, newer cables carried a billion voices over the bones of the old one.  She said Tam Reilly's name once more into the roar, and climbed up into the light.
