Three winters after friction left the world, Ysolde still herded eels along the tidal bluff, because eels alone had never needed grip.  The villagers below lived bolted to their floors and called the change the Slip.  They rarely saw her, only the wake of her glide across the glassy stone and the silver herd pouring after.  She kept away on purpose.  Ysolde was precognitively cursed: whenever she looked at a face, she saw how its story ended, never why.  Endings without reasons were a poor thing to carry into a market.  She had seen her brother Pell's ending a full year early, a small hand sliding from a wet rail on the first day of the Slip.  Knowing had not saved him. 

What she had left of him was a promise.  Pell had loved the eels, the way they coiled in the shallows as if writing something holy, and he had made her swear to tell him one day what it said.  The promise lingered in her like a splinter of the old world, the one thing that still held. 

Tonight the aurora hung green and rose over the bluff, and the tide was climbing the channels.  Ysolde unstoppered her jar of collapsed star dust.  It had fallen the night the Slip began, black grit from a sun that had folded in on itself, and each grain weighed as much as a grief.  Some said the dying star had taken the world's grip with it.  Ysolde only knew that the dust remembered how to stay.  Stirred into paint and brushed on a rock, it pinned that rock where she set it, though everything else on the bluff slid at a breath. 

For three winters she had been placing painted rocks in sequences, testing the eels the way Pell once tested her with riddles.  Rows of primes had bored them.  Squares had scattered them.  But when she spaced the rocks one pace, one, two, three, five, eight, each gap the sum of the two before, the herd had turned as one body and followed the curve.  That was the pattern they had been writing all along, and she meant to harness it tonight, to draw the whole shoal up the bluff and read the full sentence at last. 

Her curse had already shown her how the night ended.  She had caught her own face in a tide pool at dusk: herself at first light, hands empty, the channels bare, every eel gone.  She laid the rocks anyway, because a promise does not ask what it will cost. 

She worked on her knees, tethered to an iron bolt, gliding from mark to mark.  Thirteen paces, twenty-one, thirty-four.  The spiral opened across the stone like a shell, and under the aurora the star dust woke.  Each painted rock drank the green fire overhead and gave it back as gold, until the bluff glowed with a dawn that belonged to no sun, a light on loan. 

The eels came.  They poured up the channels with the tide, thousands of them, and wound outward over the wet stone along her spiral, rock by rock, a river of bright script.  At the last rock, on the bluff's very lip, they bunched and circled, and she understood they had stopped mid-word.  The next number was fifty-five, and fifty-five paces lay past the edge, out over open water. 

She had one painted rock left.  She could keep them here, a harnessed fortune circling in borrowed light, and walk into the village a rich woman.  Or she could finish the sentence and stand, as foreseen, with empty hands.  For the first time, a foreseen ending felt like a choice. 

Braced against her tether, she slung the rock far out into the dark.  It sank, held, and glowed gold beneath the swell.  The eels spilled over the lip after it, and beyond that last light they kept turning, wider and wider, carrying the curve on without her. 

Then she could read it.  Every turn was made of the two turns before it; nothing in the pattern stood alone, and nothing in it was ever left behind.  Her curse had only ever shown her single numbers, endings stripped of their sums. 

"It says you are still in the count, Pell," she told the water.  "It says whatever comes next is carrying you." 

The aurora thinned, and the gold on the rocks faded as the true sun came to collect what the night had lent.  Ysolde stood at first light with empty hands above bare channels, exactly as she had seen.  The vision had not shown her that she would be smiling.  She unclipped her tether and glided down toward the village, ready at last to look people in the face.
