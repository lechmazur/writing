Ilse Marrow climbed to the velvet observatory on midsummer eve with an imperial commission folded against her ribs.  The dome's inner walls were quilted in mulberry velvet, hung long ago to hush echoes and hold the cold.  In a cradle beneath the open slit lay the reason for both: a transparent violin made of ice crystal, with a small gold glow drifting in its belly like a held breath.  She was a provenance researcher, which meant she proved who things belonged to, and the Empire wanted this thing proved theirs by dawn.  Certified, the violin would go to the permafrost vault under the capital, silent forever, one more jewel in an unbroken crown.  In return, Ilse would become Keeper of the Imperial Register, the post she had wanted for twenty years. 

She opened the observatory ledger and found it ruined.  Someone had gone through the pages with a ticket punch, clipping out names, so that every line of ownership ran up to a neat round hole and stopped.  Ilse hated holes.  Her whole career was the stitching shut of gaps, because a gap in an object's story let anyone claim it, and the Empire did not like anyone. 

She poured coffee from her flask to stay sharp, and the old mulberry tree that leaned through the dome's slit said, clearly, inside her skull, You are sitting where Tamsin sat.  Ilse flinched and the cup slopped.  This was the embarrassment of her life: nine years ago a pressed nettle in a monastery herbal had stung her thumb, and ever since, by pure accident, she had been telepathic with plants.  She had never once cited them, because no register accepts the testimony of a hedge. 

But the mulberry kept talking, slow and sweet as its own dark fruit, and what it gave her was the missing chain.  A luthier named Oriel had carved the violin from river ice for Tamsin, the astronomer, and sealed a promise inside it: one midsummer it would be played beneath her stars, then given to the roots.  Oriel was conscripted before summer came, Tamsin died waiting, and the Empire inherited the dome and punched out their names.  The glow is the promise, the tree said, and it is nearly spent; it will not outlast this sunrise. 

Ilse looked down at the ledger, where her spilled coffee was drying.  Her grandmother had told fortunes this way, by reading the future in coffee stains, and Ilse had filed that with the hedges, yet her eyes read anyway.  On the left page the cup had left a perfect closed ring, brown and heavy as a vault door, with the line awaiting her signature shut inside it.  On the right, the spill had found the punched holes and run through them, page after page, branching thinner each time, until the stain was unmistakably a tree. 

She thought of pianola rolls, which are nothing but paper and holes, and sing only where they are pierced.  Perhaps destiny was perforated the same way, not a sealed sheet but a punched one, and the Empire, clipping out two lovers, had only made openings for the music. 

So she did what she was trained to do and weighed the claims, a dream against an empire.  The Empire had seals, soldiers, a vault, and her future in bronze letters.  The dream had one witness with leaves, one night left, and a prior claim.  Provenance, honestly practiced, does not ask who holds a thing; it asks where the thing was going when it was interrupted. 

Ilse lifted the violin and its bow, set that burning cold against her collarbone, and played the only tune she remembered from school, badly, under Tamsin's stars.  The sound was thin and bright and sore, the way a ripe mulberry is sweet right up against its bruise, and the velvet held every note close.  The gold glow flared, then loosened, and the violin began to weep through her warm fingers.  She caught the meltwater in her coffee cup, and it shone faintly, the promise still lit. 

Take from me, said the tree, and propagate.  With her penknife she cut seven green shoots below the node, as the mulberry instructed, and stood them in the cup before the promise could lose its glow.  By sunrise each cutting held a thread of gold in its stem. 

On the ledger's final line, Ilse wrote her finding: Made by Oriel for Tamsin; played once at midsummer; held now by seven mulberries, and by whoever plants their children.  Under Witness, for the first time in her career, she cited a tree.  Then she carried the cup down to the Empire's proctor, fingers stained purple, promotion gone, aching the exact color of the fruit.
