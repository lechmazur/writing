Evening primroses were unlatching along the jetty when Ilse set her kettle of walnut husks to steep.  Sea thrift furred the old planks pink, and the twilight smelled of tannin and salt.  She had brewed ink here for twenty years, because the covenant said the ledger must be written where the water could hear it.  The covenant was simple: for every soul the crossing kept, the ferry families would bring the archivist one small moment, and she would make it permanent in brown.  Not the storm, not the drowning, only the nearest things: a dropped glove, a joke, the hand that reached for the glove.  Her mother, archivist before her, had called this writing through proximate causes, and had believed grief was mostly a failure of detail.  Then the crossing kept her mother, and no one brought Ilse a moment, and one page of the ledger stayed bare. 

Today the bridge had opened upriver.  The last ferry had gone at noon with its flag folded, and the council had sent a kind note saying a jetty without a crossing hardly needed a ledger.  Ilse steeped the husks anyway, because she did not know what else her hands were for. 

Footsteps came down the planks, light and uneven.  It was Wren, the ferryman's granddaughter, eleven years old, holding out a fist.  "They unbolted the stern bench to sell it," she said, "and this was underneath."  A locket lay in her palm, green with salt, its chain snapped at the clasp. 

Ilse opened it with a thumbnail.  Inside was a photograph the size of a coin: a woman on this very jetty, head thrown back in laughter, hands stained dark to the wrist, and beside her a small girl stained just as dark, an overturned kettle between them.  The planks seemed to tilt.  Ilse had no picture of her mother laughing, and no memory at all of that kettle.  She wanted to clutch the thing and weep, but the covenant asked more of her than weeping, so she became what she was: gently thorough. 

"Why was it under the bench, Wren?" 

"It fell, I guess." 

"It fell because the clasp gave; see, this link is worn thin on one side, the way metal wears when someone twists it while thinking."  She saw it then, a small silver fidget at her mother's throat, forgotten for twenty years.  "And what did your grandfather say about the night she was lost?" 

Wren looked at the water.  "That she was leaning over the stern rail, searching for something, when the swell came, and he never learned what." 

So there it was, cause nested inside cause like the husk around the nut.  Her mother leaned because she believed the locket had gone into the water; she believed it because her throat was suddenly bare; her throat was bare because a link had worn through; the link wore because she touched it whenever she thought of what it held. 

Ilse eased the photograph from its frame.  On the back, in walnut ink faded to the color of weak tea, her mother had written: Ilse spilled the whole batch and we laughed until the tide turned. 

Twenty years she had thought the storm took her mother.  Now the context changed, the way a word changes when the sentence around it is finished: it had been a moment that took her, one spilled afternoon, held too dearly to let sink.  The knowledge went through Ilse like cold water and like wine.  She laughed aloud, horribly happy, and the primroses kept opening, and for a breath the jetty held three people.  She dipped her pen in the warm ink and filled the bare page with every link of it, down to the kettle. 

Wren read over her shoulder.  "Is that the last one, since the ferry is gone?" 

Ilse looked at the council's note, then at the child who had carried a small thing down a long jetty because it seemed to matter.  "My mother taught me that when the context changes, you do not close a record; you ink it again."  She turned to a fresh page and wrote the covenant new: that anyone, living, might bring one small moment to the jetty before any water asked for it, and the archivist would keep its weight.  Wren pressed a thumb in the ink and signed beneath her, a brown whorl beside a brown name. 

"Then I have one," Wren said.  "Grandpa folded the flag wrong today, on purpose, so he could do it twice." 

Ilse wrote it down.  Behind them the husks went on steeping in the dusk, darkening, patient, enough for years.  Somewhere under the planks, the tide turned.
