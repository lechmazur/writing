Maren woke on the reading-room floor with the memory still cooling inside her, and she lay rigid, afraid to crack.  It had come the way it always came, whole and sudden: another life, the same library, and the tide-cranes wheeling over the cloud sea.  She had fed them from her hands.  She had watched them dwindle to one bird, and then to none.  She was the only creature alive who had heard a tide-crane call, and she had heard it with ears long since gone to dust. 

The ledger on the lectern warned her what came next.  In the margins, in handwriting that changed with every life, her earlier selves had left the same report: woke, remembered, broke.  Brittle awakenings, one of them had written, like glass pulled too quickly from the fire.  Maren got up slowly, in pieces, and resolved to be the one who did not shatter. 

Below her the morning tide was going out.  The library clung to a peak above the weather, and twice a day the cloud sea rose through its lower stacks, beading the chained books with cold, then drained away down the mountain.  No one else had climbed here in years.  She had thought of that as peace until this morning; now it felt like the last crane's sky. 

She opened the ledger to the final entry in her old hand.  The cranes' calls were recorded there as intervals, each pitch marked above or below a fixed line, each call tagged with an hour.  Beside the last call, the one the last bird had made every day until it died, her former self had written a single translation: alone. 

Maren did not trust it.  That woman had been grieving, and grief is a poor dictionary. 

The fixed line, she remembered, was the hum.  She carried the ledger out to the summit court, where the library's walls had been raised around an ancient stone circle.  When the tide moved through the mountain's hollows, the stones answered with one low, unwavering note that she felt in her teeth.  The cranes had tuned every call against it, the way singers lean on a drone. 

At the circle's center sat the brass sundial that gave the calls their hours.  Centuries of cloud tide had honed its face until it was atomically reflective, smooth past any craftsman's skill, a mirror with no grain left to blur or flatter.  Maren looked into it and saw herself exactly: a small, tired woman under an enormous sky.  She remembered the last crane standing here, bowing to the bird in the brass.  Her former self had wept to see it. 

The gnomon's shadow crept toward the ninth line, the hour written beside the call.  Maren waited, humming under her breath until her voice locked into the stones' note and her ribs buzzed with it.  When the shadow touched the line, she sang the intervals as written: up a wide step, down a narrow one, up again, and held. 

Her voice cracked on the first try.  On the second, the call rang true against the hum, and the memory opened like a door instead of breaking like a pane. 

She knew this call.  It was not the falling cry the flock had made over its dead.  It was the rising one that fledglings gave on the day they first crossed the cloud sea without a parent's wing beside them. 

The last crane had not been lamenting to its reflection.  Each day at the ninth hour it had announced itself to the sky, and to the one honest witness the brass could offer.  It had learned to coexist with the silence where its flock had been, and with the bird in the mirror, and it had gone on flying. 

Maren's hands shook as she uncapped her pen.  She did not strike out the old word; that woman's grief had been true too, and deserved its place on the page.  Beside alone she wrote the better rendering: I carry myself.  Then, in the margin where her earlier selves had recorded their breaking, she added a new line.  Woke, remembered, sang. 

Soon the evening tide would climb the mountain and close the paths below, leaving her the only waking thing above the clouds.  Maren looked once more into the sundial, bowed to the woman in the brass, and went in to tend her library.
