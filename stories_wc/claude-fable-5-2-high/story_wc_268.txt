The library of Oss had slipped its mooring in time long ago, and only when the constellations paused between their shiftings did its doors touch the world.  A rune of drift, cut into the keystone above the Basalt Seat, kept the temple floating there, ageless, a lamplit island where no dust ever settled.  Idrun kept it.  She was the last of the titans and the least, standing no higher than a reading desk, though her kin had lain down as mountain ranges.  She wore an ink-stained apron, swept her own floors, and carried the returned books herself, yet doors seemed to open a little before she reached them.  The pilgrims who came during each pause took her for a servant's child, and she never corrected them. 

This pause, Lord Caldus Vane came with seven electors and a claim.  He opened the Roll of Crowns, where every titan sovereign had signed, and showed them his own ancestor's name written beneath Idrun's mother's.  Before the stars moved again, he would sit the Basalt Seat, and whoever sat it could steer the library to any shore and sell its memory by the page. 

Idrun knew the name was forged.  She had watched him write it three pauses ago, in lampblack, while she shelved psalters nearby and he looked straight over her head.  But in a place without time, new ink and old looked exactly alike. 

She could have spoken.  She had the blood to command those electors to their knees, small as she was.  But a truth handed down is only a decree, she thought, and a decree was all Vane had; a truth must be found to be a verdict.  Besides, his clerks scraped out any plain accusation they found. 

So she took down her last iron gall ink pellet, a black bead her mother had pressed from oak galls and rusted nails, and shaved a sliver into water.  Such ink goes on pale as breath and darkens only with age, so here it stayed ghost-gray, easy for hasty clerks to miss.  She wrote in the margins of the books the youngest elector, Maren, borrowed each evening.  In an herbal, beside the oak gall: What bites, abides.  In a treatise on chimneys: Soot sits upon the skin and never learns the bone.  In a star atlas, under the motionless Hunter: Before he walks again, bring seven witnesses to the Roll and ask it its age. 

Maren read slowly and noticed things, which was why Idrun had chosen her.  By the fifth evening she was tilting pages against the lamp; by the ninth she was watching the small sweeper with a different face. 

On the last night of the pause, Maren brought the electors to the Roll, and Vane followed, smiling, already dressed for his crowning.  "How does one ask a book its age," he said, "in a house where nothing ages?" 

Idrun set down her broom.  With the last crumb of the pellet she signed her own name on the Roll, pale and nearly invisible beside his bold black lie.  Then she climbed the Basalt Seat, not to sit, but to reach the keystone. 

She knew what it would cost.  Every shelf she had dusted, every quiet century, her whole small kingdom rode on that rune.  But a library that could be owned by a liar was already lost.  She laid her child-sized palm on the carving, and with the strength of mountains she sundered it. 

The crack was soft as a spine opening.  Time came in like a tide through a sea door, unhurried, and the library began, very gently, to keep its appointments.  Lamps guttered into verdigris.  Shelves bowed like old courtiers and let their books down in slow drifts of leaf and dust. 

On the Roll, a thousand years arrived at once.  The true names browned and bit deep into the vellum, and Idrun's faint signature bloomed dark among them, while Vane's ancestor lifted from the page as flakes of soot and blew against his coronation robe.  Seven electors watched it happen, and none of them needed telling. 

Vane looked down at last, and saw who had been sweeping around his feet. 

Idrun stepped down, and behind her the Basalt Seat split along its grain and settled into gravel, so that there was nothing left for anyone to pretend to. 

"Majesty," Maren began. 

"Idrun," said the titan, and picked up her broom, then thought better of it and left it leaning against a sinking column.  She tucked the Roll under her arm and walked the electors out through the doors as the roof came down behind them like a page being turned.  Overhead, the Hunter took his first step in an age, and the smallest titan walked beneath him into an ordinary morning.
