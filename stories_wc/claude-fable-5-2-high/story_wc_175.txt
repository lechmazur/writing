Ilse Marrow kept a ledger of things nobody else counted.  The villagers below the cloud line called her a mystic, but she thought of herself as an esoteric realist: she believed in omens only after she had measured them.  Each autumn she climbed to the grotto, a green hollow in the mountain's shoulder where a warm spring kept ferns and milkweed alive above a white floor of cloud.  She stayed six weeks and no longer, transiently rooted, like the milkweed itself, which gripped the rock all season and then let its seed go. 

Her grandmother had climbed here too, and her grandmother's mother, back when the monarchs crossed the pass in rivers.  The women had built signal towers then, slender frames of reed lashed with milkweed fiber, raised on the ledge where the valley could see them.  One tower meant the first scouts had come; three meant the sky was full, and the valley would leave its fields unburned until the orange had passed.  The towers were never meant to last.  Wind took them by winter, and that was the point, because a signal left standing after its news has passed becomes a lie.  The builders sang as they tied, a work chant in fours: knot, knot, knot, rest.  Ilse had the chant in her wrists before she had words for it. 

This year the count in her ledger was one.  A tagging station far to the north had sent word down the flyway: a single female, the last monarch anyone had found, was moving south along the old route.  Ilse did not expect to save her.  She expected only to do the job properly, which meant one tower, raised on the ledge, so the valley would know to look up. 

But the ledger troubled her.  Its oldest pages, in her great-grandmother's hand, recorded monarchs by the thousand and, in the margins, in smaller writing, the others: the gray moths that had crossed the same pass by night, unseen from the valley, signaled by no one.  The margin notes stopped forty years ago.  Nobody had built a tower for the last moth, because nobody had noticed which one it was. 

On her second morning she found a moth wing pressed in the moss beside the spring, dry and whole, the color of ash with one pale eye.  She could not say how old it was, and she did not pretend to.  She weighed it on her palm, wrote down what she could honestly observe, and decided the tower would carry it.  A signal could say two things if it was built with care. 

She cut reeds, stripped fiber, and worked through the cold afternoon, her breath and her hands keeping the old time.  Knot, knot, knot, rest.  By dusk the frame stood taller than she did, and at its crown she set the wing in a cradle of fiber, where the first light would find it.  One lashing remained, the binding that would close the cradle and finish the tower.  She left it for morning. 

Dawn came up under her, as it does at that height, the clouds below turning slowly pink like a face that has been looked at too long.  Nothing moved in the grotto but the steam off the spring.  Ilse took the last cord and began.  Knot.  Knot. 

Her fingers had made the loop for the third when a scrap of orange came over the rim of the ledge, tilting, tired, and settled on the crown of the tower.  The monarch stood beside the moth wing and opened and closed her own, slowly, drying them in the rose light. 

Ilse's hands stopped with the loop still open.  The chant hung in her wrists, three beats promised and two delivered, like a hymn that turns aside one chord before home.  She felt her face warm, absurdly, as if she had been caught at something private.  She did not move.  She counted instead, because counting was what she trusted: eleven slow wingbeats, one gray wing that did not answer, one orange pair that did. 

Then the monarch lifted, found the warm air rising off the spring, and went south over the clouds until Ilse could not separate her from the color of the morning. 

The loop was still open in her fingers.  She understood, plainly, that tying it would be a small dishonesty.  A finished tower said the passage was complete and accounted for, and it never had been; the margins proved that.  So she let the third knot go unmade and the cord hang loose, a rest where the valley would expect an ending. 

In the ledger she wrote one monarch, and beside it, in ink the same size, one moth.  Below, someone in the valley was already looking up.
