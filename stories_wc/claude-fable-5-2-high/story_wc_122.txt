Odile Marrow had taught music for thirty years, and for the last two she had taught it deaf.  The fever that took her hearing had taken something quieter along with it.  She still stood before her students, still marked their time with a steady hand, but she felt nothing when their faces lit, and nothing when they failed.  A teacher who cannot be moved cannot enthrall, and she knew it. 

So during the faint rustle of turning seasons, when the last leaves scraped downhill ahead of the first frost, she climbed to the drum maker's cavern.  In her coat pocket, folded in a handkerchief, was a broken phonograph needle.  It had snapped the night the fever broke, in the middle of her mother's favorite waltz, and that small crack was the last sound she ever heard. 

Bram Tallow, the drum maker, worked where the mountain opened into a long chamber with a still black pool at its center.  Hides cured on racks along the walls, and unfinished drums sat in rows, each skin wrapped in gray wool.  He read the question on her face and chalked an answer on his slate: Audibly muted, all of them, or they wake each other. 

She took the slate and wrote beneath it.  I am muted too, and not only in the ears. 

Then she unfolded the handkerchief and showed him the needle, and told him in careful chalk what she wanted: a drum she could feel her mother's waltz through, so that she might grieve it properly and be done with feeling nothing. 

Bram had an old phonograph of his own, kept for testing how skins answered a tune.  He set a wide frame drum across her knees, unwrapped its wool, and played the waltz she had carried up the mountain in her satchel.  The skin trembled under her palms in three-four time.  She knew every tremor before it arrived, and that was the trouble.  Her memory played the true waltz louder than the drum could, and her hands only measured how much was missing.  She felt like a clerk counting an empty till. 

She shook her head, and Bram lifted the arm from the record.  He stood a while at the edge of the pool, where the lantern and the racks and the two of them hung upside down in the black water, perfectly still. 

Then he wrote: You are listening for what you remember.  I test every drum with backward recorded songs, because a tune the ear already knows will forgive a flawed skin.  Let me give you your waltz from its other side. 

He loosened the crank, set the arm at the innermost groove, and turned the platter backward by hand, slow and even. 

What came through the drum was nothing she knew.  Notes swelled up out of their own fading and ended in the sharp place where they should have begun, like breath drawn back into a mouth.  Her memory had no map for it, so for the first time in two years her hands simply listened. 

And between the swells she found something she had never noticed when she could hear: a rest, thin as thread, running unbroken through the whole song.  A filament of hush, she thought, and the phrase was her mother's, from the days when a small girl at the piano was told that the silences are what the notes are strung on.  She had been living inside that thread for two years and calling it emptiness. 

The waltz ran backward to its first note and stopped, and the drum went quiet under her hands, and the quiet was the same as before, and it was not.  She wept, quietly, into the wool he had unwrapped.  Bram did not look away, and did not hurry her. 

When she could hold the chalk, she asked him for two things.  The first was the frame drum, with its wool, for her classroom.  The second was a small favor: she held out the broken needle and pointed to the drum's rim.  He understood, and set the steel splinter into the wood with a drop of hide glue, where her thumb would find it.  The last sound she had heard would mark the place where her listening began. 

She walked down through the first thin snow with the drum on her back.  On Monday she would seat her students in a ring, lay their hands on the skin, and play them a song from its other side, something none of them could hum along with.  She would watch their faces, and this time she would feel it when they lit. 

Behind her, in the cavern, the pool held the lantern upside down, and the wrapped drums kept their silence, and the silence kept hers.
