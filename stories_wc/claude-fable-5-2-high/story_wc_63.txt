The bob reaches the top of its swing and hangs there, a brass moon as heavy as a grown man, undecided.  In that pause Wren Ashe emerges through the stair hatch with her father's broken rake handle in her fist, and my whole life burns through me at once. 

I am the winder of the Skarn tower, and precision is the only prayer I know.  Our town clings to the high end of an obsidian ridge that the sea swallows twice a day.  At ebb the black glass emerges steaming under the sun, bright enough to scar the eye, and the rakers walk out along it to comb mussels from the fissures.  The tide returns over that slick glass without a sound, so they listen for my bell and nothing else.  Every life on the ridge leans on the same hour; that is our shared balance, and I am paid to keep it. 

Nine years ago, hurrying before a midsummer heat, I turned the rating nut beneath the bob the wrong way.  One turn, downward instead of up, lengthening the swing by a hair.  The clock lost four minutes in a week, and I, who checked everything, did not check that.  The turning bell rang four minutes late on a spring tide.  Tobin Ashe was farthest out.  The sea gave back only his rake handle, snapped a forearm's length below the grip. 

The town blamed the moon, the wind, Tobin's greed for the far beds.  Nobody blamed the tower, because nobody on the ridge can afford to doubt the tower.  I found the nut that night, corrected it by lamplight, and understood exactly what I was holding.  I could have kept silent, and the bell would have stayed holy.  I could have confessed in the square, and forty-one households would have stopped trusting the only voice that calls their people home.  Either way the balance broke: silence stole the truth from them, and one great confession would steal the bell. 

So I weighed it the way I weigh everything, in small exact measures.  I would tell one household at a time, at their own table, and ask each for nothing but to hear me and then climb the tower and check my work themselves.  After each telling I carved a line into Tobin's rake handle with the point of my smallest graver: the date, the family's name, and what they answered.  The letters are no taller than a grain of barley, because the handle is short and the debt is long. 

Pell, the first, said, "Get out."  I carved that.  The Morrows said nothing and sent their boy up next morning to watch me set the nut; I carved that.  Old Dace said, "I forgive my share, not Tobin's," and laid a lead net sinker on the bob's shoulder, so the clock would carry a little of her weight.  Others followed her, sinker after sinker, and I trimmed the rate for each one until the tower kept time by all of us together. 

No one could absolve the whole of it.  Each could only lift a sliver, and I learned to want no more than a sliver, to carry the rest up one hundred and twelve stairs twice a week without setting it down.  Nine years, forty lines, spiraling down the wood toward the splintered end.  I left the Ashe house for last, not from mercy but from cowardice, and I carved that too. 

Last night I sat at Wren's table and my mouth would not work, so I laid the handle before her with a lens and let forty small lines say it.  Now she stands in the hatch, grown, salt-eyed, her thumb resting on the blank inch I left above the break.  The bob still hangs.  Sunlight off the ridge comes through the louvers and sears the brass, the sinkers, her face.  I have measured ten thousand of these pauses and never knew one could hold so much. 

Then the bob lets go. 

"There's room for one more line," Wren says, and holds out her hand, not for mine but for the graver.  "I'm not writing forgiven.  I'm writing the date, and that I came up to check." 

She kneels by the swinging brass and begins to carve, small and exact, and below us the tide turns on time.
