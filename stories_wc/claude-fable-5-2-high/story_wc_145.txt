The falcon came back on the ninth day, and its feathers were wet.  Not frosted, not rimed, but wet, in a country where nothing had been liquid since October.  I noted this the way I note a misplaced border: without alarm, and with a pencil. 

I am the editor of the Meridian Atlas, which means I am paid to be certain on behalf of other people.  For eleven editions, five great rivers have been traced upstream across five separate plates into the same white margin, like guests who refuse to say where they came from.  I came to find where the rivers begin, because a blank is only an error that has learned to sit still. 

The outpost was all the margin offered: a survey hut of riveted iron on stilts at the edge of Lake Sennet, rusted to the color of dried tea.  The lake lay frozen to the horizon, flat and pale and extremely patient.  Nothing moved on it, and at night the ice hummed one low note, as if thinking something over.  It was the most peaceful place I had ever distrusted. 

The last surveyor, Tobin Aske, had left it tidy.  His cot was made, his kettle was scrubbed, and his falcon regarded me from her perch as though I were a clerical mistake.  She left the first morning I opened the door, and I assumed that was her review of me.  Aske himself had simply not come back, which the Survey Office described as "overdue." 

He had also left his phrasebook, a fat homemade thing, for Aske was a polyglot who collected languages the way the lake collected snow.  Each page ran in five columns, one for the people of each of my five rivers.  Exactly one corner in the whole book was dog-eared.  I had studied that page for eight evenings.  Under the heading "Asking Directions," five unrelated languages answered the question "Where does the river begin?"  Aske had translated each answer in the margin, and each time his pencil pressed harder.  Where the lake breathes, five times over.  Five peoples who shared no grammar and no gods shared one idiom.  Under the folded corner itself, in letters small enough to be shy, Aske had added three words: ask the glass.  I had taken that for a joke, and I do not enjoy other people's jokes in my source material. 

But a wet falcon is a footnote one cannot ignore.  Somewhere out on that sealed white plate there was open water, and she had bathed in it. 

That evening I filled Aske's kettle, let it boil far longer than tea requires, and stood at the lake window while the pane went gray.  The condensation did not settle evenly.  Where a fingertip had once greased the glass, the fog refused to hold, and clear lines surfaced as if the window were remembering.  Five of them, wandering in from the edges of the pane like my five rivers, meeting at a single small circle.  A singular convergence: one source for five rivers, one phrase for five tongues, one point on one window. 

I shifted, and stood on his stool, until a sixth line he had traced lay exactly over the real horizon.  Through the little circle, far out on the darkening ice, stood a thread of vapor, upright and unhurried.  The lake was breathing. 

I did not cheer, because editors do not cheer; we initial.  I took a bearing, checked it once, and did not check it twice, because I had been right the first time. 

At first light I walked out with a sounding line, the phrasebook, and the falcon, who flew ahead to herald me as she had presumably once heralded him.  It was a beautiful morning, windless and pink, and that was the worrying part.  Nothing warned me off.  The ice simply darkened by degrees, white to gray to the green of old bottles, and the hum beneath my boots rose a patient half tone.  I stopped where a careful person stops, which I suspect was some yards short of where a polyglot in love with an idiom had stopped. 

Ahead lay a round black eye of open water, steaming gently, warm from somewhere deep.  Under the clear ice around it, five dark currents slid away in five directions, toward five plates of my atlas.  The falcon circled above the steam, announcing it to no one. 

I marked the position twice, once on my chart and once in his phrasebook, beneath the fold, where I wrote: the glass answered.  The twelfth edition will show a small blue circle named Aske's Breath, where the rivers begin.  It will not show how quiet it was, or how the water seemed, very politely, to be waiting for me to come closer.
