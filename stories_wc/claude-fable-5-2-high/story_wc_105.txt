Odile Varn had spent twenty years designing hearing aids, and she had never built one that could return the sound she had actually lost.  At the lowest tide of the year she waded into her father's orchard greenhouse, which had slid into the bay when she was nine and now lay waist-deep in black water.  Seven shipwrecks ringed it on the reef, ribs up, like an audience that had never been allowed to leave.  Inside her, six weeks along, cells were dividing, a small patient arithmetic that did not yet include ears.  She wanted to be finished before it did. 

The greenhouse's green grow-lamps had once burned all winter over the apple trees, and captains steered by the glow because the charts told them to and because it had always been there.  Odile heard colors faintly, the way other people hear a refrigerator in another room, and that green had been a low steady hum under her whole childhood.  Then one January her father took the wreckers' money and switched the lamps off for seven nights.  She remembered the hulls arriving.  She did not remember the moment the hum stopped, and that hole in her memory had steered her ever since, away from promises, away from anyone who leaned on her.  Now someone was going to lean on her completely.  She needed to find the sound of trust breaking so that she would recognize it before she ever made it herself. 

A sound that old could not be recorded, so she went after it the way she fitted a client: by collecting absences.  An ear impression is only the shape of an emptiness, and she had brought a satchel of the same pink putty she pressed into canals every working day.  She packed it into the dead lamp sockets along the roof ridge.  She pressed it into the scars on the barnacled espaliers where apples had let go, into the bracket where the orchard bell had hung, and, wading out along the reef to the nearest wreck, into the davit cups that had held lifeboats nobody had time to lower.  Each plug came away holding a small exact nothing, and by dusk she had forty of them curing in the satchel like teeth. 

On the shingle above the tideline stood the wreckers' forge, where her father had worked the bellows while they melted ships' bells into anonymous ingots.  It was ashen now, its mouth full of gray drift, but the firebrick was sound, and a split ingot still lay in the cinders.  She fed it driftwood and wreck coal until it roared, and overhead, as if answering, the aurora came on.  Green.  She stood very still with the tongs in her hand, because there it was, faint as breath on glass: the hum.  Her hands shook, and she kept working.  She bedded each putty absence in damp ash, lifted it out, and poured the bell brass into the imprint it left, so that every missing thing became a small bright solid.  Strung on wire along the forge lintel, the forty castings shivered in the heat and began, very quietly, to hum in the key of the sky. 

Near midnight the aurora thinned, tore, and went out.  Odile had braced for a crack, a snap, something with an edge she could file down and study.  Instead the green note simply ceased, and the brass absences went on ringing without it, each one holding its pitch, waiting to be joined again.  That was the sound.  Trust did not break like glass; it broke like a duet when one voice stops, and the terrible part was the other voice carrying on in good faith.  Seven captains had kept steering for a light they remembered.  A girl of nine had kept listening for thirty years.  The memory she lacked had never been missing at all; it was the listening itself, and it had shaped every instrument she had ever made. 

She sat down in the warm ash and laughed once, wetly, at the battered wonder of it: a drowned orchard, a ring of ruined ships, and a chime of brass holes that worked.  At the next ebb she waded back and hung the castings in the dead lamp sockets, where wind and water would keep them sounding.  She kept one, the cast of the bell bracket, and closed her fist around it until it warmed.  Inside her the cells went on dividing, and soon some of them would fold themselves into ears.  Whatever she did next would imprint on them the way the dark had imprinted on her, so she began, out loud, in her own unsteady voice, to hum.
