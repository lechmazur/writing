Maren had worn the saffron veil since she was nine, when the Order taught her that a historian's first duty was to purify.  Records came to her stained with longing, and she burned the longing out, leaving dates, tallies, and clean dead facts.  She had been good at it, and innocent in the way that a sharp knife is innocent. 

Then, in a scorched ledger, she found one line she could not burn: the Vael did not perish, they were remembered to death.  The Order forbade any study of how the Vael ended, which was exactly why she lay awake wanting it.  So she walked eleven days into the salt hills, to the archive the Vael had sunk beneath their buried capital. 

The archive did not want visitors.  Its corridors were lined with listening stone that learned whoever entered, and once it learned the rhythm of a footstep or a breath, it folded the halls into a loop and walked the intruder in circles until she starved.  Maren saw the bones of earlier seekers just inside the threshold, each skeleton curled at the end of its own worn groove. 

The only way through was by avoiding patterns.  She took three steps, then one, then stood for a count she chose by the itch in her wrist.  She hummed, stopped, crawled, turned left twice and never twice again.  Her old teacher had once scolded her for being steadily unpredictable, a girl who could be trusted only to surprise, and now that fault was the one lantern she carried.  The stone listened, found nothing to hold, and let her pass. 

At the heart of the archive a shaft opened to the sky, and beneath it stood a pagoda of bronze no taller than she was, every tier walled with prisms.  The inscriptions said it woke only at twilight, when the light was neither one thing nor another.  She waited, shifting her weight at uneven intervals, while the sky above the shaft bruised from gold to violet.  Then the last slant of day struck the bronze, and the prisms broke it into motes that drifted out like warm pollen. 

They were not light.  They were atoms of nostalgia, the smallest grains into which a longing can be split and still ache.  One touched her cheek through the veil, and she was a Vael child smelling rain on a clay roof, so happy it hurt her teeth.  Another, and she was an old man hearing his dead wife laugh in the next room.  She wept and laughed at once, and reached for more, and that reaching was the forbidden knowledge. 

She wanted the rain again, the very same rain, and the pagoda obliged, and obliged, each return a little sweeter and a little thinner.  This was how the Vael had ended: not burned, not conquered, but kneeling before pagodas like this one, replaying one perfect hour until their bodies forgot to eat.  The listening stone had not been built to guard a secret.  The last of the Vael had raised it as a test, so that only someone who could not be caught in a loop would ever reach this light. 

And the Order, she understood, had drawn the wrong lesson from the ruin.  It had seen a people die of longing and decreed that longing was the poison, when the poison was only the repetition.  Every letter she had scoured, every lullaby she had reduced to a date, rose up in her like a tide of small murdered things.  Her innocence ended there, quietly; the knife knew, now, what it had cut. 

The rain atom hovered at her lips, offering itself a tenth time.  Maren turned her face away, which was the hardest unpredictable thing she had ever done.  She took a different mote instead, a stranger's first glimpse of the sea, felt it once, wholly, and opened her hand.  The atom rose up the shaft, clear as a bell note, and did not come back. 

So this was how to purify: not by burning the longing out, but by never asking the same sweetness to return the same way twice.  She worked until the twilight failed, choosing motes without order, a soldier's supper, a bride's wet shoes, a boy's lost dog, feeling each one once and letting it go.  Each release tore something out of her, and each wound let in air. 

When the bronze went dark she was still kneeling, sobbing, grinning, no longer anyone's clean blade.  She walked out through the listening halls in no rhythm at all, carrying the forbidden thing the Order would have to hear: that the past must be loved, and loved differently every time.  But first she would come back tomorrow at twilight, by a different road.
