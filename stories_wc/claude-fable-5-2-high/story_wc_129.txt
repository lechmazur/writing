Wren came up from the drowned marshes with a bundle of reeds on her back and no one's name in her mouth.  The old languages had gone quiet generations ago, and the world now lay beyond even their murmur, so people spoke a thin shared tongue and trusted marks more than words.  The orchard of Halm sat on the lip of a crater where a star had once fallen, and its trees were crystal, grown from seed-shards, ringing faintly when the wind crossed them.  Below, the crater floor shimmered like a held breath.  Wren wanted what the grafters gave their trees: to be cut, bound to living wood, and allowed to take.  But the orchard families nodded at her reed herons and went on by, polite as frost.  She was a marsh-reed sculptor in a place where nothing bent. 

Then she noticed the tag.  It was small, painted in silver ash on a cistern wall: a knot of seven lines crossing at one off-center point, like a web begun and abandoned.  The next morning another appeared on a pruning shed, and this one had eight lines. 

"Who paints these?" she asked a grafter named Odo, who was scowling at it with a bucket in his hand. 

"Nobody knows," he said.  "They come at night, and I have scrubbed forty." 

Wren looked at his red knuckles and said the thing a stranger should not say.  "You scrub like a man who is afraid it means something about him." 

Odo stared, but because she had said it gently and had not looked away, he set the bucket down.  "The first one appeared on my wall the week my brother went into the crater and did not climb out," he said. 

"Then help me find the hand that knew to put it there," said Wren. 

So the search began, and the search, more than any heron she had woven, opened doors.  Odo brought his niece Pell, his lost brother's daughter, who was quick on rooftops and quiet everywhere.  Pell brought the widow Sarn, who remembered which tags had been scrubbed and where.  They walked the terraces at dusk with lanterns, and Wren, who had no shared past to offer, offered her attention instead, asking each of them plainly whom they had lost and listening as if the answer were hers to carry.  She was boldly sympathetic, and it should have offended them.  Instead Sarn began to talk, and could not stop. 

Her talking cracked the pattern: every tag had a different number of lines, and the number matched the living kin of whoever dwelt behind that wall.  When someone died, a new tag appeared with one line fewer, and one of the remaining lines stretched past the knot like a pointing arm. 

"They are not insults," Wren said.  "Someone is drawing the webs of kinship, house by house, and pointing each torn thread toward a place it could be tied." 

Pell mapped the pointing lines on a slate, very fast, and they all leaned the same way, toward the crater's edge. 

The glimmering obsidian arbor stood where the orchard ended, an arch of glass fused by the star's fall, with the crater's light swimming in its black ribs.  Nothing had ever grown on it, and no one lingered there, because it was where the lost were last seen.  On its inner face, in silver ash, was the largest tag of all: hundreds of lines, every house in Halm, knotted into one web. 

Wren turned and looked at Pell's fingernails, rimmed in silver.  "You joined the hunt for yourself," she said.  "You wanted it read, not just scrubbed." 

Pell's chin shook.  "When Father went down, our house had one line fewer, and nobody said so," she whispered.  "I wanted everyone to see we are still tied." 

Odo made a sound like a branch giving way and pulled her against him. 

Wren felt the ache of standing outside a lit window, but this time she had something to bring to the door.  Ash washes off, and crystal cannot be woven, but reed can. 

For nine nights she and Pell wove the great web in marsh reed across the obsidian ribs, one strand for every living soul in Halm, while neighbors came with lanterns to name their lines.  Where a strand ended in loss, Wren tied it to another, and the crater's glow came up through the weave like light through a closing wound. 

On the tenth evening Sarn asked her to dedicate it, since the old words for blessing were gone. 

"I dedicate this arbor to every thread that holds," Wren said, "and to every one that broke and was tied again." 

Then Pell knotted in one last reed, unasked, and Wren knew it was hers.
