The courtyard of Ansel Cloister sat so high that storms happened underneath it, like arguments in a downstairs room.  Mist wandered between the flagstones, and the people who lived there saved their thin breath by never asking for anything.  Odile Marrow was their balladeer, and she was seldom heard, because her voice came out the size of a thimble.  She told herself she did not mind; she was driftingly alert, the way mist is, floating past everything and touching all of it.  So it was Odile who noticed what the carillon did between lightning and thunder. 

The carillon stood in the courtyard's middle, a cabinet of twenty-six small bells driven by a copper drum, and the pins on that drum were its code.  Old Hesper the botanist had built it and pinned every hymn herself, and Hesper had been dead three winters.  Yet whenever the clouds below flashed white, the drum shivered and rang a few notes that no hymn contained, then hushed before the thunder climbed up to them.  The neighbors called it static.  Odile, who knew a phrase when she heard one, called it a ghost in the code. 

She went looking in Hesper's shuttered workshop and found the flower press bolt plate: a square of brass with a wingnut bolt at each corner and a neat grid of holes between.  Hesper had used it twice over, to flatten gentians and as the jig for pinning her drum, for each hole matched one bell, and beside each hole she had stamped a tiny letter. 

At the next storm Odile waited by the west wall with the plate and a pot of blue limewash.  The clouds flashed; the ghost rang; she dabbed paint through the hole of each bell she heard, sliding the plate a hand's width along for every note, until the thunder arrived and the drum went still.  Storm after storm she encoded the stray notes this way, and because bare dots looked lonely she gave each one petals, until the courtyard walls broke into gardens of blue, saffron, and rust.  The prior grumbled about graffiti.  The children brought her brushes. 

One clear evening she walked the painted wall with the plate, laying it over each blossom and reading the letter stamped beside the hole that fit.  BRAM CANNOT MANAGE THE WELL STAIRS, said the gentians.  TACE EATS ALONE, said the crocuses.  THE PRIOR MISSES BEING ASKED THINGS, said a row of rusty poppies. 

Odile sat down on the cold flagstones.  Hesper had watched her neighbors as closely as her seedlings, and she had been pinning their unspoken needs into the bells, so the carillon could ask what they never would.  She had died mid-drum, and her unfinished asking had twitched in the copper ever since, waiting for lightning to lend it a hand.  It was not a haunting so much as a sentence that wanted its last word. 

One cluster remained, daubed in rust during the worst storm of autumn.  Odile set the plate over it and read, hole by hole: ODILE NEEDS LISTENING TO.  She had not known that anyone had noticed.  She had spent her life trying to sing what other people could not say, and had never once put her own wanting to a tune. 

That night the clouds below began to mutter.  Odile beat the bolt plate with a spanner until every shutter opened and the whole cloister stood blinking in the mist among the painted flowers.  Then the lightning came, huge and soundless, turning the mist to milk and the brass to gold.  The drum shivered, but Odile had already drawn breath. 

In the held silence before the thunder, she sang the wall aloud, flower by flower and need by need, in a voice that astonished her by fitting the whole courtyard.  The ghost bells rang beneath her, note for note, as though they had only ever wanted a singer.  Bram looked at his boots, and Tace looked at Bram, and the prior, asked for nothing yet, stood up very straight in case.  When she reached the last line, her own, nobody looked away. 

The thunder rolled up the mountain like applause arriving late, and by the time it faded Tace had invited Bram to supper and the prior had been asked, to his visible delight, to fetch Bram's water.  After that the carillon played only its hymns; lightning came and went, and the drum kept still.  Odile bolted the plate back onto Hesper's press over a single blue gentian, tightened the wingnuts, and went out, humming at full size, to find out who needed what next.
