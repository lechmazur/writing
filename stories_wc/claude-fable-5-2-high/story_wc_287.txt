The final petal dropped from the old rosebush at dusk, and Marit caught it in her palm before the wind could.  That was the signal, the same one her father had kept.  He had been lockmaster of the tide gate, and he had hauled this bush up four flights in a tar barrel when the lock house was sold.  When the roses finish, he used to say, the moon rides low enough to work with.  So Marit, who grew beans, thyme, and stubborn hope on a fire escape above the harbor, put the petal in her apron pocket and got to work.  Grieving the summer could wait until the mirrors were hung. 

Below the railing, the harbor lay luminous under the rising moon, every mooring and wave edge silvered, a whole basin of light that nobody was using.  Behind her, the airshaft between the tenements stayed black as a well.  It seemed wasteful to her, in the same way a dripping tap was wasteful. 

She took down the bean nets and wired up her catchers in their place: a shaving mirror, three biscuit tin lids, and a cracked hand glass that had been her mother's.  Then, from the cord around her neck, she drew the lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth.  It was a single brass blade snapped from the gauge her father had used to measure the cuts of every key to the tide gate, stepped along one edge in nine exact depths.  The rest of the gauge had gone with the lock house, but the tooth still told the truth, and a thing that told the truth ought to be used.  She slid it behind the shaving mirror as a shim, third step, and the glass tipped by a hair.  A pale coin of harbor light dropped into the shaft and wobbled on the bricks. 

That was when she heard it, or rather heard nothing where something should have been.  From the open window one floor down came a quivering hush, the held, shaking silence of a child trying not to be heard crying.  The new boy, Tobin, whose mother gutted fish on the night shift and whose room faced only the shaft. 

Marit knew that silence from the inside.  She also knew that a stranger's face at a window helps no one, and that pity shone straight at a person only makes them squint.  Light had to arrive sideways, as if by accident. 

So she worked the way her father had taught her to do fine things, with the rhythm of oars in water: reach, pull, feather, rest.  Reach for the mirror, pull the tooth one step deeper, feather the wire tight, rest and look.  Stroke by stroke the coin of light rowed down the bricks, crossed a drainpipe, and came to rest on the boy's sill. 

The hush below changed its shape. 

"Who's doing that?" a small voice asked, thick but curious. 

"The moon, mostly," Marit said, keeping back from the rail so he need not be seen.  "I only steer it." 

"Can you make it go in the corner, by the wardrobe?" 

She could not; her angles ended at his sill, and no amount of wishing would bend them.  She turned the brass tooth in her fingers, feeling the nine steps her father's thumb had worn smooth.  It was the last piece of him that still did a job, and it would do a better one down there.  A keepsake that only hung against her chest was just weight. 

She tied it to a biscuit lid with garden twine and lowered both past the rosebush, hand under hand.  "Prop the lid on your sill," she said.  "Slide the brass under it one notch at a time, slow as rowing, and watch where the light goes." 

She listened to the scrape of tin, a pause, another scrape.  She could not instill courage in him; nobody could pour that into another person like water into a pot.  But she could instill a rhythm, and a rhythm was something to hold while courage grew in its own season. 

"Got it," Tobin whispered, and then, with wonder, "There's nothing there, just my shoes." 

"There usually is nothing," Marit said, "but it is better to see it for yourself." 

Her hand went to the bare cord at her throat, and for a moment the loss ached.  Then she took the rose petal from her pocket and pressed it into the soil of the barrel, where it would be of use by spring.  Below her, the quivering hush had settled into the slow breathing of a boy keeping watch over his own small light.  Out on the bright harbor, somewhere, oars dipped and rose, and the moon kept coming.
