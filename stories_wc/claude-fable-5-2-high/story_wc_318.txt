The final grain slipped through the hourglass, and nobody in the kitchen moved to turn it.  It had been Pim's toothbrushing timer, two minutes of blue sand, and for eleven months Marguerite's husband had turned it every night out of a habit no one dared name.  Tonight Anders let it lie empty, his hands flat on the table, and their daughter Lise stared at the dead glass as if it had spoken.  Marguerite understood.  The sand had run out on pretending. 

She was a conductor by trade, though not of violins.  Every autumn she led the Parks Department Leaf Blower Symphony, twelve groundskeepers with tuned two-stroke engines, in the old pedestrian tunnel beneath the rail yard, where one note became forty.  Pim had loved that tunnel.  He used to stand in its echoing throat and laugh just to hear the laugh come back taller than he was.  He had also claimed one job as his alone: yanking the starter on her lead blower, both fists around the red pull cord handle, feet off the ground. 

Since the funeral the blower had sat in the shed, and its cord had stiffened like a tendon.  So had the house.  No one said his name; no one laughed, because laughing had been his instrument and it felt like theft to play it. 

Three days ago Marguerite had unwound the brittle cord and set it to macerate in a jar of warm linseed oil on the windowsill, the handle floating at the top like a small red buoy.  Anders had watched it soften day by day and asked nothing, which was its own kind of asking.  Now she lifted the cord out, supple again, and wiped the handle clean. 

"Coats," she said.  "We are going to the tunnel." 

They followed her because the hourglass had left them nothing else to do. 

The tunnel waited under the rail yard, a hundred yards of tiled dark that kept whatever it was given and handed it back changed.  Their footsteps multiplied until it sounded as though a crowd walked with them.  Marguerite set the blower on the damp concrete and rethreaded the cord with fingers that shook, mournfully eager, the way she felt before every downbeat and before every visit to his grave. 

"I cannot say it," she told them.  "So I am going to conduct it." 

She held the handle out to Lise.  Lise shook her head; that was Pim's job, and everyone knew it, and no one said so.  Marguerite pulled it herself. 

The engine caught with a cough, and she feathered the throttle until the nozzle gave its low, wavering drone, flat by a quarter tone, as it had always been.  Pim used to call it the sour note.  Into that off-key wind she began his lullaby, the one about the fox and the moon, but her voice broke where it always broke, so that only half of each line was sung and the rest was breath.  The blower took the fragments and carried them down the tunnel. 

The tunnel sent them back. 

Then it sent back more than it was given.  The missing halves of the lines returned on the echo, high and small and a little behind the beat, in a voice that had never once kept time. 

Anders made a sound like a man struck.  Lise gripped her mother's sleeve.  Marguerite did not stop; a conductor does not stop for the entrance she has been waiting for.  She raised her free hand and cued the dark. 

The engine stalled. 

In the ringing quiet the pull cord handle, hanging slack against the housing, lifted on its own, drew out to the length of a small boy's reach, and snapped home.  The blower roared.  And down the whole tiled length of the tunnel came a laugh, taller than any of them, delighted with itself, arriving one echo after another until the air was crowded with it. 

Lise laughed back before she could decide not to, a cracked, wet, astonished bark. 

"Pim," Anders said, the first time in eleven months, and the tunnel said it forty times, gently, as if practicing. 

They stayed until the fuel ran out. 

Walking home, none of them spoke of what had pulled the cord, and Marguerite understood that this silence was a different kind, one that could be tended rather than obeyed.  In the kitchen Lise turned the hourglass over.  Upstairs, faintly, someone small began brushing his teeth, and Anders, God help him, laughed.  Marguerite kept time on the table with one finger and let the sand run.
