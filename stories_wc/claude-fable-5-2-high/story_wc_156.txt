Tobin asked his question from the foot of the scaffold: "Are you certain that's the original blue?" 

Odalys Brin, cobalt to the elbows, saffron in her hair, felt the yes rise in her throat like a reflex.  She had built twenty years of restored murals on that yes.  Lately it tasted like old varnish, which was why she had taken this job in the Sound Wave Garden, where nothing was said only once. 

The garden lay around her in hedges clipped to sine curves, gravel raked in ripples, and concrete dishes that passed whispers across the lawn.  Its heart was the great parabolic wall she stood against, whose faded mural of a blue river she had been hired to bring back.  The groundskeeper had warned her that a sound struck at the wall's center came home changed, and could bewitch whoever was waiting for it.  Odalys had heard an invitation, not a warning. 

She wanted to exchange certainty for curiosity, and she intended to do it the way she did everything, as a game with strict rules.  Rule one: before any yes, one coin. 

So she held her answer, and in the pause she drew her great-grandfather's hobo nickel from her apron.  He had ridden freight cars with a pocketknife, carving the stern head on a five-cent piece into a grinning tramp in a bowler hat.  She set the coin on her thumbnail, winked at the tramp, and flicked it hard against the painted center. 

The ring flew out over the hedges, struck the far dish, and came back doubled.  It closed over her like warm water, and she woke. 

She was standing on the same scaffold, but the mural was wet and the hedges were only knee high.  A woman in overalls was laying the very blue across the river with a wide brush, humming.  Odalys nearly crowed, until she saw what the brush was covering: a river of green, already dry, already loved by someone. 

Rule two, she decided: when surprised, repeat the experiment.  The nickel was somehow in her hand again, so she flicked it again, and the returning ring woke her a second time. 

Now the wall was bare gray concrete, new and smelling of lime, and the garden was a field of stakes and string.  A lean young man sat in the wall's shade with a pocketknife, turning a nickel under the blade.  He rapped the half-carved coin against the concrete, listened to it come back, and laughed. 

"Never the same face twice," he said, to the coin or the wall or to her. 

His echo took her under once more, and she woke a third time into no garden at all.  There was only wind over a plain, and a river going by, and it was every color in a single minute. 

Odalys sat down in the grass and laughed until her ribs hurt.  She had spent twenty years hunting for the first layer, the true one, the one that would let her say yes.  Under every layer was someone wide awake, changing their mind.  And under them all was a river that would not hold still. 

Rule three: a game is not finished until you put the pieces back.  The nickel had not been minted yet, so she cupped her hands and called her own name at the bluffs across the water. 

The echo came back and woke her beside the young carver, who tipped an imaginary bowler.  It rang off new concrete and woke her beside the humming woman, whose blue now looked less like a cover-up than a reply.  It rang a last time off old paint, and she woke on her own scaffold with the nickel still singing in the air. 

She caught it.  The grinning tramp lay face up in her blue palm, a ruined coin and a finished portrait at once. 

Below her, Tobin had not yet shifted his weight; the whole journey had fit inside the pause between his question and her reply.  The yes was gone from her throat, and she did not miss it. 

"No," she said, and the wall said it back to her, softer, like agreement.  "I'm not certain of anything under there." 

She climbed down, pressed the nickel into his hand, and pointed him toward the center of the river.  "Ask it something," she said, "and then we will open one square inch and see who is awake down there." 

Tobin flicked the coin, and they both waited, curious, for the answer to come back.
