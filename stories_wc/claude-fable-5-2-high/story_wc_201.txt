The anesthesia wore off joint by joint, and I knew the position of every bone before I knew my own name.  That was their gift to me.  The interrogators had sharpened my proprioception until I could feel a wrist rotate half a degree, so that every stress position would be legible to me in full.  The tribunal surgeons had just removed the anchor bolts from my spine, but the sharpening lived in the nerves and would stay. 

I woke in a recovery pavilion at the edge of the Violet Stand, nine hundred miles from the drowned delta where I once taught number theory.  Beyond the shade cloth, the forest glowed.  Since the sky thinned, raw ultraviolet fell here all day, and the birches, every one a transplant from some ruined valley, fluoresced blue-white under it like bones in a lamp.  Everyone who walked the ultraviolet forest wore a pewter mask against the burn.  Everyone except the penitents, who tended the trees barefaced for as many minutes each noon as their survivors decreed.  Ultraviolet penance, the tribunal called it, and by dusk I owed them a number. 

I had asked for this duty.  Through four years of cells I had kept sane on one unsolved problem: if cruelty could be tallied, mercy must have a mathematics too, and I meant to discover it.  But an equation needs terms, and mine were missing: I could not say who had hurt me, or how often, or for how long.  They had drugged me before every session, and they had worn masks.  All I owned of those years was the thing in my fist, a pewter mask strap I had woken clutching after the final session, its buckle bent by my teeth. 

A penitent came in with water, the one the nurses said had asked for my ward.  His face was peeled raw, and the mask slung at his hip hung from one strap and a knot of twine.  He reached across me with his left hand to set down the cup, and my body braced.  Not a flinch; a structure.  Left shoulder raised eleven degrees, heels driven down, tongue flat against the palate, breath held at two-thirds: I could read the pose as precisely as a proof.  It was the shape of the chair.  My mind had been emptied, but my body had kept the minutes. 

"Stay," I said, and my voice was steadier than my hands.  "Do what I tell you." 

All afternoon I used him as a key.  I had him pour water, snap a towel, kneel, hum, and buckle a belt behind my head, and each motion summoned a different brace from me, each one a room, a posture, a duration my muscles recited without being asked.  I wrote them on the bedsheet with a charcoal stub: forty-one responses, forty-one sessions, three hundred and six hours.  He did not ask what I was counting.  When I held up the strap, he unslung his mask, and the bent buckle fit the torn rivet like a last digit. 

"Set it high," he said.  "I have been burning without a sentence for a year, and it has not come out even." 

It never would.  I ran the figures every way I knew, and the sums refused to close; no quantity of his scorched skin converted into one unbroken hour of mine.  That was when I found it, sitting in the remainder.  Mercy was not zero, which is only forgetting, and it was not the full sum, which is only the chair with the roles reversed.  Mercy was a subtraction, and a subtraction requires both terms: the whole debt, spoken aloud, and the part I chose not to collect.  That was why my body had refused to let me forget. 

"Three hundred and six hours," I told him, and made him repeat it until his voice broke.  "That is what you owe," I said.  "Here is what you will pay: forty-one minutes barefaced each noon, one for each session, for as long as it takes us to plant the north slope." 

Then I rose despite my stitched back, threaded the pewter strap through his mask, and buckled it behind his head with my own hands.  Nothing in me braced. 

At dusk we carried a birch seedling out under the last violet light, another exile with its roots in burlap.  He dug; I held the trunk plumb, feeling its lean to the fraction of a degree, the way I had been built to feel pain.  I set my bare feet in the turned soil and, for the first time since the delta, braced by choice.  I knew exactly where every part of me was.  It was here.
