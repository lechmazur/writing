The first letter arrived three days after the funeral, addressed in her father's forging hand.  "Odile," it read, "I lied for a living, and to you I lied by leaving things out."  "Everything I never said is shelved in the Library of Unspoken Words; my watch knows the way, so read it, but never trust it." 

Odile, still wearing her mourning veil, laughed once and then distrusted the laugh.  Since childhood she had dreamed of a library glowing in mist, and she had always assumed the dream was one more thing he had sold her. 

The watch was his best tool, a confidence man's watch whose second hand was cyclically reversed: sixty ticks forward, sixty ticks back, so that a mark glancing down twice would swear no time had passed.  Behind its case back she found a square of onionskin folded many times, each inner panel numbered and sealed with a dot of wax.  The outermost panel was gibberish until she noticed the watch ticking backward and tried reading every second line right to left. 

"Go to Sallow Fen at dusk," the cipher said then.  "The library flashes and tolls in the same instant, but sound is the slower messenger."  "Count the lateness of the bell; break each seal only when the delay matches its number; and document everything, because I never did." 

So she bought a ledger, which felt like a small rebellion against him, and took the evening coach to the fen.  The mist there lay thick as wool, and somewhere inside it a pale gold light pulsed like a slow heartbeat.  Her own heart answered faster, and she made herself stand still until it behaved. 

Flash; she watched the hand; the toll rolled in eight ticks later.  She wrote it down, "Dusk, coach road, delay eight," and broke the eighth seal with a thumbnail and a grin she kept under the veil.  Forward line, backward line, forward again: "Take the causeway of black stakes, and stop when the bell is six late." 

It worked, it actually worked, and she allowed herself exactly one skip before testing the next plank with her heel.  Panel by panel the cipher unfolded as the delays shrank, each seal a promise that the light was nearer. 

Then, at the fork past the drowned willow, the watch tricked her as it had tricked a hundred widows.  The hand reversed in the middle of her count, and she read a delay of two where the truth was four.  She broke the second seal early, turned left as it told her, and went into the bog to her knees. 

Cold mud is a persuasive teacher.  She hauled herself out by a stake, sat shaking, and read her ledger back like evidence against a suspect.  The entries could not lie the way the dial could: the last honest one said five, and no path falls from five to two in thirty paces.  That was his whole trade, she realized, a glance trusted over a record kept. 

From then on she counted aloud, wrote the hand's starting and ending marks, noted every reversal, and did the sums twice.  Four, then three, then a true two, where the same left turn now led onto a ridge of firm turf.  At one, the glow filled the mist like honey in milk, and she could see windows, tall and arched, every pane lit from within.  She wanted to run and instead walked, counting, until flash and toll landed together like two hands clapping. 

The last panel, unnumbered, lay open in her palm: "No delay means no distance, and the door takes only a true account."  There was a brass slot beside the door, ledger-wide.  She hesitated, because the ledger held her mistake in her own handwriting, bog and all.  Then she understood that the mistake was the truest page, and she fed the book through. 

The door opened on warm light and shelves that climbed out of sight, each spine bearing a name instead of a title.  Her dream had never shown her the inside, and she walked its aisles slowly, afraid delight might wake her.  Under her father's name stood a fat, untidy volume, and tucked inside its cover was one envelope, never posted, addressed to her in a hand that forged nothing. 

The watch in her pocket ticked forward, then back, and for once she did not care which.  All his words had been traveling toward her late, she thought, like the bell, and tonight she had closed the distance between his first letter and his second.  Odile lifted her veil, checked once that her hands were steady, and broke the seal.
