Oda Brill had kept the yew labyrinth trimmed for nineteen years, and her father had kept it for forty before her.  Tonight she stood in the top corridor of the astronomer's tower, wet to the knees, laying pieces of brass along a windowsill.  Lightning showed her the hedges below in white flashes; between flashes, the moon came through the torn clouds and did its quieter work. 

The sextant had belonged to Mrs. Quill, who gave up stars at sixty because, she said, nobody in the village ever asked her how far away anything was.  They asked how bad it was.  So she reground the mirrors, and after that you could sight a grieving person against the horizon and read their sorrow off the arc in degrees, the way you read a fever.  The baker measured eleven after her dog died and nine a month later, and nine was a thing she could carry to work.  Nobody called it a miracle, any more than they called the tide table one. 

Then Mrs. Quill's hands went, and she dropped the sextant down the tower stairs, and moved to her sister's, leaving Oda the key and a note.  "It heals in moonlight, like most honest brass, but it has healed crooked around the bent pin.  You will have to deconstruct it and let each piece mend alone." 

Oda had not wanted it for herself.  She wanted it for the yews. 

Her father had died in March, a man who spoke perhaps forty words a day and spent thirty of them on the weather.  She had worked beside him half her life and could not have said what he was like when no one was looking.  But someone had always been looking.  Two miles of yew had stood at his elbow for forty years, and Oda wanted, with a plain and stubborn hunger, to know what trees witness. 

She had begun with the dreams.  Everyone knew that if you dozed on a labyrinth bench you woke with a scrap of something that was not yours: the trees dreamed, and their dreams drifted like seed.  All summer Oda asked visitors what they had seen and marked each answer on her maintenance chart, beside the notes about blight and loose gravel.  A man humming at the east bench.  A hand freeing a wren from bird netting near the sundial.  Shears being sharpened, patiently, in forty separate dreams at forty separate turnings.  The map filled with him, but it would not tell her which scraps the trees merely remembered and which they mourned.  For that she needed a number. 

So she had climbed the tower with her smallest screwdriver, and taken the sextant apart the way she would take out a diseased hedge, down to the sound wood.  Frame, index arm, two mirrors, each laid separately in the moonlight.  She watched the crack in the horizon glass close like a cut closing on a thumb, slowly, with no fuss.  The bent pin, though, was fused into the index arm, and no tool of hers would shift it.  She set the arm in the brightest part of the sill and waited, as she had waited on slow hedges all her life. 

The thunder walked off east.  The rain thinned, then stopped, and the corridor grew so quiet she could hear the candle.  And in that silence the healing brass pushed the pin out like a splinter, and it dropped to the flagstones and rang.  One small bright note, the length of the corridor. 

Oda slid in the new pin she had filed from a hedge-shear rivet, and the arm swung true.  She raised the sextant to the window.  Far off, lightning still flickered, and by its light and the moon's she sighted the hedges one after another against the dark line of the hills. 

The east bench read four degrees.  The sundial read four.  Every sharpening place, every turning where he had hummed, read four, a low even ache spread across two miles, as if the yews missed him the way a floor misses a familiar tread. 

Then she swung the arm to the short dead end by the north gate, and it climbed to nineteen. 

She found the spot on her chart.  One dream was marked there, told to her in June by a boy on a school trip: a man's hands closing a small girl's hands around a pair of shears, and the man laughing. 

Oda had forgotten he ever laughed.  The trees had not; what they witnessed, it turned out, included her. 

She turned the sextant on her own reflection in the wet glass and read thirty-one, and wrote it on the chart beside the blight.  In a month she would measure again; tomorrow, the north gate needed trimming.
