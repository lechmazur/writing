Odile Pratt wrote Lingering Company, the county paper's column about who was haunting whom, and on weekends she evicted the tenants she had gossiped about.  Readers loved her advice because it never arrived plain.  A ghost is a letter marked return to sender, she would write, so stop licking new stamps and read the address.  This morning she had her own address to read. 

The Voss orchard would come down at seven, and in the hush beforehand the crew sat on their tailgates with cold coffee, chainsaws laid out in the wet grass like instruments before a concert nobody wanted.  Even the starlings had quit.  The pear trees stood in their rows, gray and listening. 

At the orchard's heart sat the old cider house, which for forty years had held the county sheet music archive: hymnals, harvest rounds, band parts for dances long finished, all of it crated now for a move to the city.  Maren Voss had built that archive, and Maren Voss was why Odile had come. 

Twenty years ago, Lingering Company had hinted that the choir director's celebrated Harvest Hymn was lifted from a dead pupil's notebook.  It was a good item, sly as a wink, and entirely false.  Maren lost the choir, then the town's eyes, and died among her pears with nobody singing. 

The dead stay where their roots are.  Once the trees fell, Maren would scatter like sawdust, and the apology Odile had owed for two decades would have no address at all. 

She let herself in with a borrowed key.  Inside, dust hung in the lantern light like held breath, and the shelves smelled of mouse, mildew, and old cider.  On Maren's desk, beside the hymn manuscript, stood the thing Odile had hoped for: an antique perfume bottle of clouded green glass, its silver collar black with tarnish, a last amber finger of pear-blossom scent inside.  Maren had worn it every Sunday.  Scent is where the dead keep their coats. 

Odile did not use bells or Latin.  She used copper.  From her satchel she took a spool of fine wire and wound it around the bottle, two hundred turns, until her fingertips were nicked and stinging, then clipped the ends to a salvaged telephone receiver.  A ghost cannot touch anything, but neither does a magnet touch the coil it passes through.  Something moves nearby, and the wire answers.  That was how she had always reconnected the living with their dead: through induced currents, never forced ones. 

She pulled the stopper.  Pear blossom rose into the cold, impossibly fresh, and the lantern flame leaned toward the desk. 

"Maren," Odile said, "a rumor is a seed spat over a fence, and the one who spat it never has to weed." 

The receiver gave her nothing. 

"Regret is a bell with the clapper tied," she tried. 

Silence, and outside a tailgate slammed. 

Odile knew the physics better than she knew herself.  A magnet held still induces nothing; only change makes current, and she had been holding still behind pretty sentences for twenty years.  Her mouth was dry. 

"I lied about you," she said.  "No pupil, no notebook; I made it up because the column was thin that week and you were proud and easy to envy."  "The hymn was yours, and I am sorry, and I am late." 

The scent thickened and moved, sweeping through the coil like a hand through water, and the receiver crackled alive.  It did not speak.  It hummed the opening of the Harvest Hymn, thin as wire, flat on the third note, the fourth missing entirely, as if the singer had forgotten or refused it.  Then the old glass cracked along its shoulder, the last perfume wept into the copper, and the lantern flame stood up straight. 

No forgiveness, no absolution, only a broken bar of music.  Odile had performed enough miracles to know the real ones were imperfect, chipped and off-key, and counted anyway.  She had been heard. 

At seven the first saw ripped the hush open.  Odile carried the manuscript and the cracked bottle out past the foreman, a young man who watched the first pear tree shudder and asked whether the place was really haunted. 

"Not anymore," she said.  "But if you owe somebody words, son, pay before the address burns down; interest on silence compounds." 

Friday's Lingering Company ran no gossip.  It printed a plain retraction under Maren's name and, beneath it, the Harvest Hymn exactly as the wire had sung it, with a rest where the fourth note belonged.  That Sunday, in a church that smelled faintly of pear blossom, the choir sang it for the first time in twenty years, and when they reached the rest, they held it.
