Ines Calloway climbed the moonlit ridge path with a cracked porcelain box under her good arm.  The other arm, the one the Tamsin Fire had rewritten from wrist to jaw in ropes of pale scar, swung stiff at her side.  Twenty seasons on a forest fire crew had taught her to walk uphill without wasting breath, so she said nothing to the boy behind her.  Wren was seventeen now, a seasonal recruit with her crew's patch on his sleeve, and he had asked for two things tonight: the box, and the truth about it. 

Above them the eclipse had begun, the earth's shadow taking its first slow bite of the moon.  She had chosen this night because the sky would be dancing its slow, patient dance, sun and earth and moon stepping into line, and patience was what the telling needed. 

They stopped where the path crested above the old burn.  Nine years ago this slope had been black to the horizon.  Now young lodgepole stood shoulder high in the dimming light, row on crooked row. 

"You said lightning," Wren said.  "You told the county it was lightning, and I was eight, and I let you." 

"I did," Ines said. 

She set the box on a flat stone.  It had been his grandmother's, white with blue swallows, and the heat had split it corner to corner the afternoon she found him crouched at the origin point with it in his lap and his father's lighter in the ash beside him.  Her arm had still been smoking under its wrap.  Dell Okafor, who had trained her, was already dead on the east flank. 

"I made you a bargain," she said.  "I would keep the box and the lighter and the word lightning, and you would plant this ridge with me every spring until it was green." 

"I thought planting was just something we did," he said.  "I did not know I was paying." 

She had come up here meaning to enlighten him, and saw she would have to start with herself.  She sat down beside the box, watched the shadow slide across the moon, and thought the whole thing through, the way she walked a fire line after mop up, turning over every log.  She had told herself for nine years that she lied to spare a child.  That was true, but she had also been burned and holding a friend's death with nowhere to set it down.  Blame had seemed like a language only one small boy would ever be made to speak.  She had wanted the pain translated into something anyone could read.  So she had bought his innocence with a lie, and charged him for it in seedlings. 

The moon went the color of an old bruise. 

"Open it," she said. 

Wren lifted the lid along its crack.  Inside lay the lighter, and around it nine lodgepole cones, one from every spring, each sealed tight with resin. 

"Those only open in fire," she said.  "The heat that killed this slope is the same heat that seeded it."  "I am not telling you that makes it fair."  "Dell is still dead, and my arm is still my arm." 

She took a cone in her scarred hand, struck his father's lighter, which she had kept filled, and warmed the cone over the flame, turning it with gently skilled fingers, the way she turned an injured wrist, until the scales loosened and a winged seed slid into her palm. 

"Here is what took me until tonight to see straight," she said.  "Your innocence was never mine to sell."  "I only lent it to you, and a loan comes due."  "If you need to walk into the county office and say your own name, I will stand next to you and say mine."  "But look down there first." 

He looked.  Under the copper moon the young trees held their rows, nine springs of his own small hands, taller than he was. 

"A confession is words, and words stop at the county line," Ines said.  "That down there is what you have already said, in the one language a deer or a stranger or Dell's widow can read without help."  "Say the words too, if you must."  "Just do not believe they are the bigger part." 

Wren wiped his face with his sleeve, hard, like a man angry at rain.  Then he took the seed from her palm, knelt, and pressed it into the thin soil beside the path, firming it with his thumb exactly as she had taught him. 

"Monday," he said.  "Both of us." 

Ines closed the cracked box and put it in his hands, and above the ridge the shadow began, very slowly, to let the moon go.
