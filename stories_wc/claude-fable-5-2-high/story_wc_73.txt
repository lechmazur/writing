The staircase had no bottom, no top, and no when; it had only steps, and Odile Varga had counted enough of them to stop trusting numbers that merely grew.  Walls of pale stone pressed close on either side, yet the flights ran on forever in both directions, a prison with all the room in the world. 

The rule was simple.  At each landing a walker rolled a pair of dice: the first said up or down, the second said how many flights.  Every tread sank a little under a foot and rose again somewhere else, so the walkers drove the stairs and the stairs carried the walkers, and nothing was ever lost.  Perpetual motion, the old climbers called it, with the pride of people praising their own chains. 

Odile had lost something, though.  Once, on a landing she could no longer number, a door had stood ajar in the wall, breathing out the smell of rain.  Startled, she had dropped her dice; one struck the threshold, and a corner of it skittered through the gap.  Then she had done the obedient thing: gathered them up, read the faces, and climbed four flights as told.  When she came back down, there was only stone. 

What remained was the chipped die, and the rough little crater her thumb found on it at every landing.  That was the texture of a missed opportunity: not smooth like regret, but granular, exact, the precise shape of a piece of her that had gone through without her.  She did not want to mourn it.  She wanted to capture it, to pin that roughness down so exactly that it could be used. 

So she traded her spare bootlaces to a descending peddler for chalk and began writing proofs on the risers.  First she tallied ten thousand rolls of the chipped die, the one that chose direction.  The missing corner had lightened it; it said up slightly more often than down, a bias of about one roll in ninety.  Then she proved the lemma that mattered.  The stair's endless motion required that every walker's wandering sum to nothing, each rise repaid by a fall, the books forever balanced.  A fair die guaranteed it.  Hers did not: with every roll she owed the staircase a debt it had no way to collect. 

A closed system cannot carry an imbalance forever, she wrote, pressing hard.  Therefore it is not closed, or it will not stay closed.  The strain must gather where the imbalance entered: at the seam where the chip went out.  The stairs must rupture, and the door was not a memory but a prediction. 

A stair warden read her chalk, frowned, and offered her a fresh, fair die in exchange for the damaged one.  "You'll wear yourself to nothing chasing a crack," he said. 

"Almost certainly," Odile agreed, "and it will get worse before it works, which is how I will know it is working." 

She kept the chipped die.  But she was daring the way a scale is daring, never tipping further than she could answer for.  She let the flawed die steer and the whole die measure, so that the flaw never governed more than half her fate, and at every landing she chalked the running sum so that anyone who doubted could check it. 

The drift carried her upward by slow, provable degrees.  The stairs noticed.  Treads that had always sprung back began to sag and stay sagged.  The stone hummed under her boots like a held breath, and walkers passing her muttered that the going had turned heavy.  Worse, she noted grimly, and climbed on, pleased. 

At a landing like every other landing, her thumb found the crater, and the wall beside her answered.  A hairline ran up the stone, forked, and widened with a sound like a ledger tearing.  Cold air came through the rupture, and with it the smell of rain.  Just beyond the threshold, in wet grit, lay a white crumb of bone with half a pip on it. 

Odile did not lunge.  She set the whole die on the landing for whoever came next, beside a last line of chalk: the proof holds, and the door is where you break the balance.  Then, keeping one hand on the stone until her leading foot found solid ground, she stepped through. 

She picked up the crumb and fitted it to its crater, and the roughness under her thumb was finally something she held rather than something she had missed.  It was probably just another staircase out here, she thought.  But it was raining on this one, and she had already proved that anything endless could be made to open.
