The pigeon had followed Odile Maren home from the rope line, and now it sat on the rim of the courtyard well, watching her with one amber eye.  Dusk was settling in layers: first the swifts went quiet, then the fig leaves, then the light itself.  Odile set her rain gauge on the bench and did not hurry.  For nineteen years she had been the storm recorder of Saltmere, and hurry had never improved a measurement.  She logged pressure at dawn, noon, and dusk, wind by the vane on the chapel, rainfall to the tenth of an inch, and she logged them alone, with nobody to check her sums.  That was the point of it.  A record kept honestly where no one could see was the only kind of honesty she fully trusted, and she tended it the way other people tended a garden. 

The bird wore a message tube on its left leg, a sliver of goose quill sealed with green wax.  It had flown out over the rope that afternoon while she stood asking the warden's guard about drainage.  She was credibly curious, which is a rare protection; a woman with a rain gauge may ask anything about puddles, and the guard had answered her yawning.  Fever in the Lower Steps, he said, eleven days sealed, nobody in or out by order of Warden Brask.  No physician's seal hung on the rope, only the warden's.  She had noted that the way she noted a backing wind, without opinion.  Then the pigeon had trailed her through four streets, roof to roof, as if the brass gauge under her arm were something it knew. 

Walking straight at a bird only teaches it distance.  So Odile kept her back to the wall and moved sideways along it, one flagstone at a time, looking at the fig tree, until her hand was simply beside the well and the pigeon simply stepped onto it.  The wax gave under her thumbnail.  Inside the tube was a curl of paper bearing no words at all, only eleven small figures in a careful hand. 

She carried it to the table under the lamp bracket and opened her ledger beside it.  Read head-on, as a cipher, the figures refused her.  They were not dates, not a count of the dead, not coordinates.  She did not push.  Instead she let her eye drift across the ledger the unaccustomed way, not down a single day's column but sideways along the rainfall row, day after day, the way she had crossed the courtyard.  Eleven days back lay the equinox gale: two inches and four tenths, the heaviest fall in a decade.  The first figure on the slip was 2.4.  The next ten matched hers within a tenth, drizzle for drizzle, dry day for dry day. 

Odile sat very still while the courtyard darkened another shade.  Nothing on the paper said so, yet she could intuit the rest as surely as she could feel a front arriving in her knees.  Someone inside the rope kept a gauge and read it every day, steadily, in a neat unshaking hand.  Fevered districts do not produce eleven days of patient decimals.  And the seal on the rope had gone up the morning after the gale, the morning the seawall Brask had been paid to mend would have let the harbor into every cellar of the Lower Steps.  The quarantine was no physician's work.  It was a rogue zone, a rope drawn around evidence, with healthy people shut inside to keep a flood from being seen.  Whoever sent the bird had not begged or accused.  They had sent measurements, trusting that the one person in Saltmere who would recognize them would also know what they were worth. 

The pigeon drank from the well's dripping bucket.  Odile understood that she could close the ledger and remain exactly as safe as she had been at noon.  No one would ever audit the omission.  But she had not spent nineteen solitary years raising a conscience by hand in order to starve it on the one evening it was asked to bear weight. 

She dipped her pen and wrote in the remarks row, in her ordinary script: Lower Steps sealed eleven days under warden's mark only; rainfall there independently recorded and in agreement; no evidence of fever observed.  Then she copied the entry twice, once for the provincial physician and once, very small, for the tube.  Beneath the small copy she added her own eleven figures, so that whoever unrolled it would know they had been counted alongside.  She sealed the quill with a drop of candle wax and released the bird, which circled once and turned for the Lower Steps.  Then she read the dusk pressure, steady, and wrote it down.
