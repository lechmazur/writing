Before the energy beings arrived, Oda Venn kept the ripple-mark maps of the harmonic caves the way her mother had, with wax, slate, and patience.  Every floor in those caves was ridged by sound, fine sand combed into ripples wherever a note had stood long enough to settle.  Her maps told the villagers which galleries hummed sweetly, which ones cracked teeth, and which could be slept in.  Then the beings came down through the rock like weather, and no gallery could be slept in at all. 

They were not cruel.  They were gently luminous, drifting ribbons the color of milk held to a candle, and they sang because singing was how they moved.  But the caves answered every note with ten, and the ten with a hundred, until the whole mountain rang like an anvil struck without rest.  The sand ripples she had mapped for thirty years were erased and redrawn every hour.  Children stopped speaking; old Harl stuffed his ears with tallow and still wept. 

Oda did what a mapkeeper does when the map fails: she went looking for the one thing it had never needed to show, the quiet in the storm.  Her mother had called it a bloom of hush.  Somewhere in any ringing space, she had said, the waves meet and cancel, and silence opens there like a flower, small and exact.  Sand could no longer hold a pattern long enough to find one.  Ice could. 

So Oda climbed to the Cold Gallery, where the meltwater pools froze a hand thick, and she knelt on the largest pool with a lamp and a slate.  The singing had been working on the ice for weeks.  Cracks ran through it in white feathers and long forked seams, and she read them the way she read ripples, as the handwriting of force.  Where the sound beat hardest, the ice was shattered to frost.  Where it beat less, the cracks lengthened and thinned, and every one of them bent, slightly, in the same direction. 

She copied them for three days, pool after pool, her knees bruised and her ears bleeding a little, and laid the slates side by side.  The cracks pointed like compass needles toward a low side chamber the old maps marked only as Dull.  In its frozen pool she found a circle of ice no wider than a bowl, clear as glass, without a single flaw.  She put her head down over it, and for the first time in a season she heard her own breath. 

It was a bud, not a bloom; one head could fit, not a village.  But a mapkeeper owns one tool that is not a map.  The echo chamber stone had sat on her shelf since her grandmother's time, a hollow geode the size of a loaf, used for testing galleries.  Whatever sound entered its mouth came back out a half-beat late and turned inside out, trough for crest.  Set in a loud place it only made a muddle.  Set exactly at a node, her mother had claimed, it could conjure a hush wide enough to live in. 

Oda carried it up in a sling and placed it on the clear circle, and nothing happened.  She turned it a finger's width.  Nothing.  She did not curse; she watched the ice, because the ice would tell her before her ruined ears could.  Turn, wait, look; turn, wait, look, the slow work of a smith bringing a bowl round under the hammer. 

On the fortieth turning, the newest crack at the pool's edge stopped growing.  The quiet came outward from the stone in a slow ring, then another, petal over petal, until it touched the walls.  It was not the dead silence of a sealed tomb.  The mountain still rang beyond the doorway; she could feel it in her teeth, the storm leaning on the hush from every side and holding it in shape. 

One of the beings drifted in, as they drifted everywhere.  Its song met the stone, came back inverted, and went out like a wick.  Oda held her breath, afraid she had hurt it.  It only slowed, dimmed to the glow of an ember under ash, and hung above the pool as if it, too, had been tired for a long time. 

She brought the villagers up by lamplight, six at a time, children first.  Old Harl dug the tallow from his ears and said his own name aloud, just to hear it.  They slept in rows around the stone while the being kept its pale watch overhead.  Oda stayed awake long enough to draw the chamber on a clean slate.  She scratched out Dull, wrote Bloom, and marked around it, carefully, every crack that had shown her the way.
