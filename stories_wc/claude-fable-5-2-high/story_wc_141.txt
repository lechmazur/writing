Ines Calloway began to close her grandmother's logbook, and the pages started their long, papery whisper.  Everything that mattered would have to happen before the covers met; the last entry said so. 

She had come to the abandoned observatory on Gannet Cliff to bust one more myth, which was her trade and her favorite gloomy hobby.  Weeping statues were condensation, ghost lights were swamp gas, and Ines announced each small death of wonder on her radio hour with the brightness of a weather girl forecasting hail. 

This myth, though, was family property.  Villagers swore that Odalys Calloway, the last astronomer here, had kept an ephemeral compass, one that existed for a few seconds and pointed at the answer to the universal riddle.  The riddle was still chalked inside the rusted dome in Gran's cheerful capitals: EVERYTHING LEAVES, SO WHICH WAY IS HOME?  She called it universal because, she said, every star and every grandmother eventually asked it. 

Gran had asked it aloud in the hospice, three winters ago, and Ines had answered.  What she had answered, she no longer honestly knew.  Ines reimagined past conversations the way other people bit their nails, and she had rewritten that one a thousand times.  In some drafts she said something wise; in most she said, "Statistically, Gran, nowhere," and watched a dying woman turn her face to the wall.  The drafts had eaten the original, which was a poor state of evidence for a professional skeptic. 

So she had climbed the cliff path at dusk, whistling a hymn in the wrong key, and found the logbook on the transit desk beside two other things.  One was the cello Gran had been building between comets, half-finished forever: unvarnished, two strings, its scroll still a square block of maple.  The other was a seashell, a limpet painted by a child's hand with a red arrow and a lopsided N.  Ines recognized the hand as her own, age six. 

The last entry was addressed to her.  "Lay the cello on its back, shell on its belly, and retell me our last talk, but not in words, Inesita, because you will only improve them."  "Hum it into the wood; the compass lasts as long as a book takes to shut, so shut this one slowly." 

Which is why Ines now bent over the desk, one hand easing the heavy cover down, her mouth near the cello's f-hole, feeling ridiculous in a pleasantly doomed way.  The pages whispered, and she hummed. 

A hum cannot be edited into wisdom; it holds only the shape of a talk, its rises and falls.  She hummed Gran's question, a thin climbing phrase, and the raw spruce took it and gave it back deeper, the two strings shivering in sympathy.  She hummed her own answer, and her throat, which had attended none of her revisions, produced the flat, droll drone she used for punch lines.  Then, before she could think, it produced what came next: a bright, stuttering, upward tumble. 

Gran, laughing. 

Gran had not turned to the wall; she had wheezed with delight, as she always did when Ines was dreadful, because dreadful was their oldest shared music. 

On the trembling belly of the cello, the painted shell began to skate.  It turned a slow half circle, its red arrow swinging past the dome slit, past the sea, past the chalked riddle, and stopped.  It pointed at her mouth. 

The myth buster in her, loyal as a terrier, reported at once: vibrating wood herds loose objects along its quiet lines, a thick daub of paint weights the arrow, and Gran knew exactly where a hummer would have to bend.  Busted, then. 

And yet the trick was the answer, built by a woman who never lied to her, only arranged the truth where it could be found.  Everything leaves, so home is whoever is still retelling you, in a voice too plain to flatter.  The compass could only ever be ephemeral, because it lasted exactly as long as someone kept humming. 

Ines ran out of breath; the hum thinned, the strings stilled, and the shell became a limpet with a child's scribble on it.  The last page settled, and the cover met the boards with a soft clap, like one person applauding politely at a funeral. 

She laughed, wetly, in the cold dark, with the sea grinding below.  On Thursday's radio hour she would announce, brightly, that the compass of Gannet Cliff was sympathetic resonance and a daub of paint, and that it worked.  Then she pocketed the shell, tucked the unfinished cello under her arm, and went to learn how one carves a scroll.
