The boy stopped counting at seven, and Mareta felt his fingers go slack around hers.  The anesthetic had him now.  Dr. Osei cut the old cast from Tavi's badly set arm, and the saw's whine carried across the atrium of the ruined customs house, where the island's only field hospital sheltered under a shattered glass dome.  Twilight came down through the broken panes, and with it the hill mist, settling on cots and palm fronds and the cracked fountain where one stubborn lotus had opened in the rainwater. 

On either side of that fountain sat the two people who had kept the war alive for nine years.  Elder Kaho of the reef villages was Tavi's grandfather; Mother Ilse of the hill clans was his grandmother.  They had come under truce for the surgery, and they had not looked at each other once. 

Mareta had translated between them through four failed ceasefires, the war's one pacific voice.  She spoke Reef and Hill and the trade tongue, and she was tired of being a clean pipe for poison, of carrying ultimatums so faithfully that her own brother had died under one she delivered word for word.  She wanted, just once, for her mouth to graft something living onto all that scar. 

Dr. Osei handed her the two halves of the cast.  "He made me promise you would get it," the surgeon said, "and he was very firm for a person of eight." 

Mareta already carried his other gift in her pocket: a fragment of brain coral the size of a plum, blasted off the home reef by the same shell that broke his arm.  He had pressed it into her palm before the needle.  "It is the reef's thinking," he had said, with that shrewdly innocent look of his, the look of a child who asks why the sky is blue and already suspects the adults do not know.  "Read my arm to them while I sleep, Auntie, because they cannot shout when I am sleeping." 

She carried the cast to the lantern by the fountain and unknotted the sling still tangled through it.  For six weeks Tavi had walked the ward with a marker, asking every child the same question: what scares you at night?  The answers crowded the plaster in two alphabets, Reef hooks and Hill strokes, blurred by sweat and mist until they bled into one another.  It was a cipher only someone fluent in both griefs could break.  Mareta tilted the plaster, guessed at drowned vowels, matched a Hill stroke to the ghost of its ink on the facing half.  Then she understood what the boy had built, and what he was asking her to do with it. 

"Your grandson left a message," she told the elders, first in Reef, then in Hill.  She read each fear aloud in the language its writer had not used. 

"I am afraid the men will come back." 

"I am afraid I will forget my father's face." 

"I am afraid of the quiet, because the quiet is when it starts." 

Kaho leaned forward.  "Which of ours wrote that one, about the quiet?" 

Mareta carried the question across, and Ilse bristled: "That one is ours, that is how hill nights go." 

Here Mareta borrowed the boy's wide eyes and let the mist take the blame.  "The ink has run together," she said, in both tongues, "and I can no longer tell whose is whose."  It was the first unfaithful translation of her life, and the truest. 

The two elders stared at the plaster, each hunting for a fear that belonged only to the enemy, and neither found one.  Something that had been pulled tight for nine years began, strand by strand, to unknot. 

Mareta set the coral on the fountain's rim between them.  "He says it is the reef's thinking," she said, "and the fishermen say a broken piece, tied back onto the wound, will grow." 

Kaho turned it over in his scarred hands.  "It needs clean water and no shelling," he said slowly, "for a year at least." 

Ilse was silent a long while, mist beading in her gray hair.  "Then let it have a year," she said, "and let the boy tie it down himself, with both of us holding the boat." 

Mareta translated that faithfully, every word, and her voice shook only a little. 

It was not peace.  It was a year, a fragment, a promise small enough for a child to hold; but behind the screen Tavi slept on, his arm being set straight this time, and in the fountain the lotus kept its one pale flower open against the dark.  Mareta ached with it, the way a mended bone aches before rain, and for once she did not mind.
