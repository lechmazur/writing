Ilsa Venn went down into Echo Gorge while the sun was rising somewhere she could not see.  The walls stood so high that dawn arrived here only as a rumor, a thread of gold along the upper rock and a hush in which even the river seemed to hold its breath.  That hush was the only hour the lost would speak. 

She was the gorge's collector of lost souls, as Oro had been before her, and she carried what he had carried: a lidded reed basket, a harp, and a crocodile's tooth.  The harp was wrong, and had been since the day a river crocodile mistook it for something worth eating.  The bite had twisted the frame so that no string sat true against its neighbor, and the animal had left one tooth in the wood by way of apology.  Oro had pried the tooth out, laughed until he wheezed, and used it as a pick ever after.  He never mended the harp.  True notes, he said, made the dead stand up straight and lie; crooked ones made them forget to be ashamed. 

Ilsa settled on a wet ledge and drew the tooth across the strings.  The chord limped out, sour at one corner, and the gorge handed it back changed, the way it changed everything.  Inside the echo, a woman's voice was speaking. 

"I watered the wine at my sister's wedding," it said, "and let her blame the vintner for thirty years." 

Ilsa plucked the worst string on the harp, the one that honked like a goose.  A startled laugh came off the rock, then a second, looser one, and something lighter than a held breath drifted into the open basket. 

This was the whole of Oro's craft, and now hers: confession opened the wound, but laughter was what let it close clean.  Solemnity alone, she had learned, only taught a grief to sit politely and never leave. 

But the soul she wanted was not the wine-waterer.  Oro had died in the first frost, and for four dawns his voice had flickered ahead of her through the echoes, always one bend deeper, always falling silent when she played.  A collector who would not be collected. 

She shouldered the harp and pursued him. 

The path past the third bend was a seam of slick stone no wider than her foot, with the river loud and white a long way below.  Ilsa crossed it the way she did most things, without hurry and without remark.  She had never thought of herself as brave; she was simply not afraid, as some people are simply left-handed, and it had never seemed worth mentioning.  Every few steps she struck a note and listened for which wall flinched. 

The gorge ended in a blind pool under an overhang, where there was nowhere deeper to go.  She sat down, set the tooth to the strings, and played the burial song exactly as badly as the harp required. 

The echo came back in an old man's voice, cracked and close.  "You should not carry me up, girl." 

"Then tell me why," Ilsa said. 

"I played this song at your mother's grave, the week after the crocodile," Oro said.  "The low string honked in the middle of the blessing, and I laughed out loud over her, with you standing there nine years old and hollow-eyed.  I built a whole craft afterward to excuse it, and I have never once believed my own excuse." 

Ilsa's hands went still on the strings.  She remembered a week of stone in her chest, and then one ridiculous sound, and the old harper's helpless bark of laughter. 

"I laughed too," she said.  "It was the first breath I took after she died.  I followed you home that day because of it, Oro, and I have followed you ever since." 

The pool was quiet for a long moment.  Then she plucked the goose string, once, deliberately. 

His laugh broke off the overhang and multiplied down the gorge, wheezing, enormous, a dozen Oros laughing from a dozen walls, and Ilsa laughed with them until her eyes ran.  When the echoes thinned, the basket had gained the weight of one held breath. 

She closed the lid gently and began the long climb.  At the rim the hidden sunrise was hidden no longer; it lay across the plateau like spilled honey, and it hurt to look at, and she looked.  She lifted the lid and felt the basket lighten, soul by soul, the last one slowest.  Then she sat in the new light with the crocodile's tooth warm in her palm, missing him, and played one crooked note for the aching pleasure of hearing nothing answer.
