The rain quit at dusk, and the old sea wall began to whisper as it always did, water threading down through its weep holes toward the harbor.  Ine sat on the parapet with the big seine across her knees, mending by feel.  She was the town's net-mender now, and privately its philosopher, though nobody had asked for one.  A net, she liked to think, is mostly holes; what catches the fish is the part nobody looks at.  Old Sabu had taught her that, along with every knot she knew, before his heart stopped in the spring.  He left her his needles, his stool, and a bamboo tea whisk with a note tied to it: Read the big net by the moon.  She had assumed the whisk was sentiment, since he had frothed tea on this wall for every returning crew for forty years.  The note she had not understood at all. 

But the seine troubled her fingers.  Scattered through its mesh were doubled knots where no strain required them, hours of extra work that strengthened nothing.  Sabu had never wasted a hand's length of twine in his life.  If he had spent that effort, she wanted to know what it had bought, because work that hides itself deserved at least one witness. 

Tonight the moon came out full over the breakwater, so she hung the wet seine along the drying poles and stepped back.  It was firefly season, and the insects drifted up from the wet reeds and settled in the mesh until the whole net glittered, scintillatingly somber, like a funeral held by stars.  For a while she could read nothing in it.  Then she noticed that the fireflies wandered and the moonlight did not.  The single knots had already shed their rain, but each doubled knot still cupped a bead of water, and as the moon climbed, its light walked across the net in a slow bright path, kindling those beads one after another. 

She followed the path with her eyes, then lost her place among the blinking insects.  On an impulse she took the whisk from her apron and drew its tines along the row.  The fireflies lifted away; the tines slid over single knots and snagged, with a tiny click, on every doubled one.  Of course: he had gauged this mesh against the whisk in the first place.  Some sat high in the mesh, some low: click high, click low, a pause, three climbing.  Ine's lips shaped the intervals before her mind caught up, and she heard herself whistle. 

It was Sabu's tune, the wandering, unfinished-sounding thing he whistled from this parapet on thick nights, which the whole town took for an old man's habit.  Only it was not a habit; it was a chart.  She knew the reef channel as every harbor child did, and the tune fit it bend for bend: rising phrases for the northward turns, falling for the southward, pauses for the straight reaches.  For forty years the helmsmen had steered home on a whistle and praised their own instincts.  And he had knotted the tune into the town's net so it would outlast his breath. 

She thought of the two hulls scraped on the reef since spring, and of the mist now rising off the rain-cooled water, swallowing the lamps of the night fleet.  Ine was not a person who raised her voice; she apologized to knots when she cut them.  Nevertheless she smoothed her apron, climbed onto the parapet's wet lip where the harbor master forbade anyone to stand, and whistled the dark sea a set of directions.  She read as she went, following the moon's path across the net, bead by lit bead, and when she reached the end she began again.  Her mouth went dry and her ankles shook.  Below her the wall kept up its whispering, as if prompting. 

Near midnight a lamp swam out of the mist exactly where the channel opened, then another, then seven in a line.  By the time the crews climbed the harbor steps she was seated again, whisking tea in Sabu's chipped bowls. 

"Strange," said the eldest helmsman, accepting one.  "I could have sworn I heard the old man out there." 

"You did," Ine said, eyes lowered, and turned to pour for the next man before he could ask what she meant. 

Later, alone, she ran her thumb over one doubled knot and thanked it out loud, which felt both foolish and correct.  Those knots would fray in time, and she would retie each one twice, and nobody would notice.  The fireflies drifted back to the net, and Ine, philosopher of holes, whistled them the first three notes.
