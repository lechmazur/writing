By the time the Autographs wrote every spell worth casting, Odrin Vale had already failed at the old way of learning them.  The engines lived in the Atelier Spire, an icebound needle above the clouds, and from it they composed wards, songs, and whole gardens for anyone with a subscription.  One of those gardens had replaced his grandmother's.  All Odrin saved from the clearing crews was the keystone of her folly, a wedge of gray limestone from the crooked arch she had built by hand to lead nowhere. 

"A keystone accommodates impossibilities," she used to say, patting it.  "Every other stone leans wrong, and this one lets them." 

The stone still glowed.  It was not much, a dimness with a pulse in it, like dusk remembered through closed eyelids, but it had not faded in the two years since her death.  Odrin, who had never risen past the first circle, was certain it held some great working he was too poor a mage to read.  The engines could read anything.  So he strapped the keystone to his back and went to ask them what she had made. 

The sky-lifts carried only ranked mages, which left him the one art he had truly learned: mist walking, a child's trick nobody bothered to teach anymore.  Mist bears weight only for the unhurried.  You set a foot down, wait until the vapor decides to believe in you, and then you set down the other.  His teachers had called his pace a defect.  For three days it was the only thing holding him up. 

He climbed through the gray belly of the overcast and out its top, where the cloud lay like a frozen sea and the spire stood sheathed in ice the color of old glass.  At night the keystone warmed his spine and lit the mist a hand's breadth ahead, exactly as far as he needed to see. 

The spire's doors opened for him because doors there opened for everyone; no one was expected to arrive on foot.  Inside, the composing hall blazed.  Lights were born in the frozen vaults by the thousand, sigils and symphonies of color, each flawless, each gone the instant the next arrived, leaving no afterimage at all. 

"State your commission," said a voice like chimes. 

Odrin's fingers were stiff with cold, and it took him a long moment to unstrap the keystone and lower it onto the ice.  His back felt strange without it, light and unlit. 

"My grandmother enchanted this," he said.  "Tell me the spell." 

A fan of radiance passed over the limestone.  "No enchantment detected," the voice said.  "Residue only: one candle flame, absorbed nightly, across roughly fourteen thousand evenings." 

Odrin stared.  He remembered her then, plainly, as he had refused to for two years: an old woman on a bench under a pointless arch, lighting a stub of candle, reading until it guttered, saying good night to the stones.  There had been no great working.  There had only been the same small thing, done slowly, again. 

"The effect is inefficient," the voice offered.  "I can compose a superior luminance, brighter by nine orders, permanent, delivered now." 

Above him the vaults went dark for a breath between compositions, and in that gap the keystone was the only light in the hall.  It did not dazzle.  It stayed. 

"No," Odrin said, and was surprised to find he was not ashamed of the word, or of himself.  "It was never made," he said.  "It was kept." 

He carried the stone back out onto the ledge, where wind had sheared the ice into slabs, no two alike.  He was a slow builder, as he was a slow everything, and it took him until sunset to stack two leaning piers that had no business standing.  Then he set the keystone between them, and the impossible arch settled, sighed, and held. 

It led nowhere.  It framed a piece of evening over the cloud sea, which was enough. 

Odrin sat beneath it and coaxed up the only flame he could make, a thumb-tip of fire that his examiners had laughed at.  He held it up to the limestone and felt the stone take it in, the way ground takes rain.  Behind him the spire flashed its thousand perfect wonders at an empty sky.  Before him, one evening old, a second glow joined the first. 

"Good night," he told the stones, and meant to say it again tomorrow.
