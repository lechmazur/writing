The leash broke at the ferry landing, a small frayed sound under the gulls, and the dog was gone up the ridgeway before Pia could close her hand.  Fog was already coming in off the water, veiling the long stone spine that ran from the landing to the far headland.  She stood holding half a rope and did not call out, because she was a child who had never once raised her voice. 

Nell and Osric, who had guided the ferry and its walkers for forty years, tied off the boat and came to stand on either side of her. 

"He went straight up the spine," Osric said.  "So we will not." 

Nell took Pia's ticket from her cold fingers and turned it to the last of the light.  It had one star in it already, from the crossing, punched clean by the brass ticket punch that hung at Nell's belt, which no hand but hers had ever worked. 

"Five spaces left," she said.  "A frightened thing runs from whoever chases it, so we traverse the ridge the slow way, by the sheep trods that loop down to the shingle and up again.  One star for every turn." 

"What happens when it is full?" Pia whispered. 

"Then you will be standing where your courage has been waiting," Nell said.  "The kind you have never used does not spoil; it only sits somewhere ahead of you, like a boat left on a far beach.  Finding that place for people is the better half of our trade." 

They went in under the fog.  The first trod slid seaward through thrift and wet heather, and the tide below them said hush, and hush, and took its time.  Osric hummed the old landing song, the one that brought boats in, low and even as an oar stroke.  At the turn Nell stopped, and the punch clicked, and a second star opened in the ticket. 

Pia wanted to run straight up the stones and said so twice.  Twice Nell answered, without any hurry, "The tide never runs at the shore, little one, and it always arrives." 

So they went round by the marsh side, where curlews complained, and round by the sea side, where the foam showed pale as milk, and each bend cost a click and earned a star.  Between the third star and the fourth, Pia noticed she had stopped watching her own feet. 

At the cairn, with five stars punched, the trods ended.  Ahead lay the Needle, a neck of rock no wider than a doorstep, and beyond it, faint in the grey, a dog was crying. 

Nell looked at the Needle for a long moment.  In forty years she had led every walker across it herself, her hand on their sleeve, and had called that wisdom.  But her knees were old now, the rock was slick, and a dog would not come to a stranger's lantern.  Osric met her eyes and said nothing, which was how he said the hardest things. 

"We are stopping here," Nell told Pia.  "Osric and I will keep a twilight vigil at this cairn, with the lamp lit and the landing song going, and we will not stir until you are back."  Then she did a thing she had never done: she unhooked the ticket punch and folded it, with the ticket, into the child's hands.  "The last star is not mine to give." 

Pia went out along the Needle on her hands and knees, the brass warm against her chest.  Behind her the song went on, gently persistent, the same four notes laid down like stepping stones. 

On the far side the fog was thick as wool, and the crying had stopped.  She knew what was wanted of her, and that no trod went around it.  She set the ticket in the jaws of the punch and squeezed, and the sixth star fell out into the dark.  Then Pia, who had never raised her voice, filled her whole small chest and shouted, "Barley!" 

It rang along the whole veiled ridgeway.  Something wet and shaking came out of the fog and pressed itself against her knees. 

At the cairn, Nell heard the shout and let out a breath she had been holding, it seemed, for forty years.  Waiting had been the harder crossing, and she had made it. 

"There it was," Osric said softly, and she did not ask whose courage he meant. 

They kept the lamp high and the song low until a girl and a dog came back across the Needle, slowly, the way the tide comes home.  Pia held up the ticket, and the lamplight came through it in six small stars.
