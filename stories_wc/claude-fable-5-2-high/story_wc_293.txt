The hitchhiker was gone before Ilka Varga's handbrake finished clicking.  No door had opened; the passenger seat simply held less.  What it held instead was a flake of glass the size of her thumbnail, smelling of ether, like him.  She carried it into the noon glare of the mountain scenic pullout, where the Kestrel Gorge Bridge hung below the guardrail, its north tower bandaged in white scaffold wrap for restoration. 

Ilka was the valley's bridge-song fiddler.  Every span here had a tune that called its builders' names, and each year she played every bridge its own song so the steel would remember who raised it.  The Kestrel song had one silent bar.  It belonged to her great-grandfather Tobiah, the rigger blamed when the first span fell in 1911, whose name the county ordered never to be carved, sung, or spoken. 

Last spring the county had finally granted amnesty, of a kind.  The kiosk at the pullout showed it: the 1911 crew portrait, digitized from a newspaper, with one face left as nine gray squares and a caption reading PARDONED, IDENTITY UNVERIFIED.  A pixelated amnesty: forgiveness too coarse to hold a man.  The photographer's glass plates had burned with the courthouse archive, the clerk said, so nothing could ever be proved.  Ashes, and one more never.  For twenty years she had bowed around that silent bar, trying to sculpt some possibility from the ashes of never. 

Now she lifted the flake to the sun and stopped breathing.  In the amber emulsion, ghost-small, two fingers gripped chalk beside five chalked letters on a girder: NEVER. 

The old man had thumbed her down at mile nine, black-fingered, and asked only why a fiddler played to bridges.  She had told him everything, and he had answered twice.  "I poured wet-plate collodion long after the trade went dry," he said, and later, "A man walls up what he cannot bear to burn."  She had taken that for grief. 

Wind came up the gorge, and Ilka, who set her tempos by whatever moved, by dancers' heels and cable sway and the shiver of a loaded deck, watched the wrap.  On the tower's sheltered face it lay flat wherever stone stood behind it and fluttered wherever the wind found a way through.  Arch, arch, lattice window: three flutters, matching the three openings on the elevation drawing etched beside the kiosk.  Then a fourth, low on the anchor pier, where the drawing promised solid masonry.  There a square of wrap pulsed in and out, slow as a sleeper's chest, as if the pier were breathing through bad mortar.  The whole white sheet was a map, and it had one landmark the county did not. 

A restoration engineer named Dunmore sat on a tailgate nearby with a sandwich and a radio.  Ilka went to her and began to recount it, badly and out of order: the silent bar, the vanished passenger, the flake, the fourth flutter.  Dunmore's face said ghost stories were not in her contract.  But she was an engineer, so she counted the flutters, checked the drawing, and recounted them.  "Four," she admitted, and raised the radio. 

Far below, a figure in an orange vest unlaced the pulsing panel and ducked behind it.  The radio hissed for a long minute.  "Bricked niche, mortar's gone to sand," the voice said.  "There's a tin box, and a glass picture in it, a whole crew, and one fellow up front chalking a beam." 

"What did he write?" Ilka asked, though her voice barely carried. 

"It says LOAD THIS SPAN, signed T. VARGA, but the first word is chipped clean off." 

Ilka opened her hand.  The first word lay in her palm, throwing a small amber coin of light onto her wrist.  He had not caused the fall; he had chalked a warning across the steel, the company had loaded the span anyway, and a man who could not bear to burn the proof had walled it inside the bridge.  All these years her family had carried the county's never, and Tobiah's own had been waiting inside the stone, meaning the opposite. 

She laughed, a cracked, bright sound she did not recognize.  Dunmore was staring at the empty passenger seat of Ilka's truck as if it might explain itself.  On the kiosk the nine gray squares still stood where a face should be, but they looked less like a verdict now than like a picture still loading. 

Ilka took her fiddle from its case, faced the bridge, and played the Kestrel song through to the silent bar.  This time she did not bow around it.  She sang his name into the gorge, and below her the white wrap lifted, all of it at once, like something applauding.
