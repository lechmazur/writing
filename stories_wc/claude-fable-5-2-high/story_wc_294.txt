The circus train had sat on the loop spur behind Harlan Gap since before Emil Varga was born, its painted lions fading to the color of dusk.  His father had waited in these cars between jobs, a highway robber of the modern kind who stopped courier vans on Route 9 with a flare and a polite voice.  He had marked the place as his: three red slashes on every milepost from the highway to the spur, three more on each car door, a warning other thieves understood.  He had also brought Emil one banknote from each job, pesos and dinars and lempiras, and let the boy believe they were souvenirs of travel.  Emil learned the truth at fourteen, when the police followed those red slashes like a signed map straight to the lion car. 

The evening his startup closed its seed round, he drove out with the album of foreign paper money and shelved it in that same car.  Passage would let workers wire wages home across borders, cleanly, legally, the opposite of everything his father had done.  He walked the spur once before leaving and found, without surprise, that it curved through the birches and delivered him back to where he had started.  Why did some paths do that?  He promised the faded lions he would find out, then drove back to the city to prove his own road ran straight. 

For three years it seemed to.  Passage opened in nineteen countries, and at each launch Emil bought one crisp note of the new currency and carried it out to the album at sundown.  Investors praised his calm, which was real but not natural; he had polished it the way his father polished his, by handling it daily until it shone and gave nothing away.  What was his alone was the noticing underneath, a stillness that caught the tremor in a handshake or the fee buried in a footnote.  He noticed the fees.  The board raised them a little each quarter, a toll here, a spread there, and he signed, calmly, telling himself a toll was not a flare on a dark road.  Then a regulator in Manila did the arithmetic, the partner banks withdrew, and Passage came apart in eleven days. 

On the last evening before the accounts froze, Emil sat in the lion car turning the album's pages, and heard a car stop on the gravel.  Dena Okafor, his cofounder, climbed in with her laptop already open.  "There is four million in customer float sitting in transit," she said.  "Tomorrow the lawyers eat it.  Tonight, with your key and mine, it goes somewhere we can reach." 

He watched her thumb worry the edge of the trackpad, saw she had not slept, saw she wanted him to say no and feared he would not.  Argument would only harden her, so he closed the album and said, "Walk with me first." 

He led her along the train in the amber light and stopped at each door to touch the three red slashes.  "My father's," he said.  "They told every other thief this ground was taken.  They also told the police exactly whose it was, milepost by milepost, all the way from Route 9.  A claim is a confession, Dena.  Our two keys on that transfer are three red slashes on a door." 

They had followed the rails as he spoke, and now the curve closed and the lion car stood in front of them again.  Dena laughed, tiredly, at being returned.  Emil did not laugh, because he finally saw it.  The spur was not cursed; it circled because a ringmaster had once wanted his train always pointed home, and no one since had thrown the rusted switch at its mouth.  A path circles back when you keep marking it as yours.  He had fled his father's highway and still collected one note from every border, still taken a little from each traveler, still signed his name to the ground. 

Dena shut the laptop, then opened it again with a different face.  They used both keys the other way, tagging every stranded transfer with its sender's name so the trustees would have to send it home. 

Afterward Emil took a stone and scored a line through the slashes on the lion car door, not erasing them, only canceling the claim.  He left the album on its shelf, no longer trophies, only paper from places people had been trying to reach.  Then he and Dena leaned together on the rusted switch until it groaned over, and the rails, for the first time in fifty years, pointed out toward the main line.  Night had gathered the lions and the birches into one soft color, and his calm, no longer polished, was simply quiet.
