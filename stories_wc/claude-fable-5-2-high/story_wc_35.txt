Odalys Venn woke at the close of a dream she had never spoken aloud, and rain was hammering the roof of Fort Kessarin.  In the dream her mother always knelt in the coral pavilion, bare palms on the ice, and always the ice took her.  Odalys pulled on her kidskin gloves before her feet touched the floor.  Feeling passed through her skin like fever through a barracks, in both directions, and a cook cannot afford to season the bread with her own dread. 

By dawn the rain had stopped, the first in eleven years, and the fortress stood dark and sweating like a clay pot fresh from the kiln.  She drew a cup from the kitchen well and tasted it.  Her tongue had always found memories in whatever people handled, and this water had been handled by everyone.  It tasted of Sergeant Brandt's burned village, of a sentry asleep at his post, of forty years of garrison shame gone thin and cold.  The pavilion was thawing. 

Her work, like her mother's, was to sandpaper rough memories.  A soldier kneaded a loaf; she tasted it, found the splintered night inside him, took his wrist bare-handed, and worked the memory smooth enough to carry.  The grit that came off was remorse, and it had to go somewhere.  It went into the ice of a pavilion the sea people had carved from coral when the desert was a reef, and the cold of all that stored remorse kept the place frozen beneath the dunes.  Now warm rain had gone down after it.  If the melt reached every well, every soldier who drank would feel everything at once, and soldiers who feel everything at once walk off walls. 

She chained the well, told the garrison to drink from the rain barrels, and went out the postern gate with a lantern and a shovel.  The storm had moved the dunes and buried the door she knew.  But the storm had also left pools, and her mother had taught her to read them.  Rainwater on that sand settled the way a tide once settled on the reef below, so the pools were tidal pools in everything but salt.  She walked their formations slowly, analyzing each one: shallow crescents meant solid coral shelf, a string of small round basins meant the old colonnade, and water that would not hold meant a hollow underneath.  At the end of the colonnade one pool was draining in a slow spiral, rimmed with frost.  She dug there until the shovel rang on coral. 

Inside, the pavilion was pink and white and weeping.  Ice still sheathed the branching pillars, but it had gone glassy, and meltwater ran in threads toward the cracks that fed the wells.  There was no cold left in the world to freeze it again. 

Her mother had met a smaller thaw by baring her hands and drawing the whole store into herself, and it had subsumed her in an afternoon; Odalys, twelve, had watched from the steps and never told anyone.  That was the dream.  It was the one rough memory she had never sanded, because sanding took a second pair of hands and she kept hers gloved. 

She stood with her fingers on the glove buttons for a long moment.  Then she thought like a cook instead of a daughter.  No one swallows a goat whole; you portion it, and a table subsumes what would choke one mouth. 

She dammed the cracks with her apron and wet sand, climbed out, and came back with Sergeant Brandt and every kettle in the kitchen.  By noon the melt was a broth, thickened with barley, salted hard, with onions to give the grief something to stand on.  She called the garrison to the long tables in the wet courtyard and told them plainly what was in the bowls. 

"It is yours," she said, "and it will come up the wells tonight whether you eat it or not." 

Then she took off her gloves, set her bare hand in Brandt's, and had every soldier at the tables take the hand beside them.  They ate one-handed, and what each spoonful woke ran down the chain of palms and came back divided by two hundred.  Soldiers wept into their barley; nobody walked off a wall. 

Through Brandt's grip she felt her own dream going around the tables, her mother kneeling, the ice, the steps, and coming back with its edges taken down. 

"My mother died down there," she said aloud, for the first time, to no one and everyone. 

Brandt's hand tightened, hard and cracked as old glaze, and held.  Below the drying dunes the pavilion stood empty, only coral now, and the dream did not come again.
