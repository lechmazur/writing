The station stood on four legs at the edge of the dead reef, and on calm days it seemed to drift while going nowhere.  Ines Maru had kept it for eleven years, ever since the fossil fuel ban turned the last tankers to scrap and sent the supply boat out under sail.  Her work was simple: read the float gauge in its steel well, change the paper on the recording drum, and send the numbers north.  The numbers said the sea had nearly stopped rising.  That was the news everyone wanted, and it was not the question she had come to ask. 

At nine she had floated above this same reef and watched it turn white in a single hot season, and at nineteen she had told a hearing what that looked like.  People still called her the witness, as if seeing were a thing you finished. 

What she had not finished was pinned above the drum: a page torn from a school atlas, its inner edge ragged.  It showed her home island, the reef flat, and a dotted line running across the flat to Motu Iti, the islet where her family buried its dead.  Her grandmother, Tia, had ripped it out the year the new edition arrived without the line.  "They walked out once, found water, and erased us," Tia had said.  "Keep this, but remember the page is only half the map."  She never said what the other half was, and then she died, and nobody walked to Motu Iti again. 

Everyone had an answer for why paths disappear: the sea took it, like it took everything.  But Ines read the sea for a living, and her own gauge said the water over that flat stood barely a hand higher than in Tia's girlhood.  A hand of water does not drown a road.  So she stayed, year after year, wanting to know where a path goes when it goes. 

Each dawn, before lifting the well cover, she tapped the steel pipe with her knuckles, the way Tia had tapped every table, gunwale, and doorframe she ever leaned on.  It was a long pattern, clusters of quick knocks parted by rests, and Ines had absorbed it as a child the way one absorbs a lullaby.  She had always taken it for an old woman's habit, a way of keeping the hands company.  The pipe rang, the float bobbed, the day began. 

That season the office up north asked for the whole record, every chart since the station was built, to show what the ban had bought.  It took her three evenings to assemble forty years of paper across the floor, one sheet for each year, edge to edge, like planks.  She inked each year's lowest ebb in red, and when she stood back, the marks made a slow swell, sinking deep twice in forty years.  She knelt and counted the sheets between the two troughs, knocking the floor once for each.  Eighteen knocks, and then her hand rested on the deep red mark, and her knuckles knew the phrase before she did.  It was the opening of the lullaby. 

Tia had not been keeping her hands company; she had been keeping a calendar, handed down knuckle to knuckle from people who owned no paper.  The knocks were the years the flat stayed under, and the rests were the years the moon drew the ebb down far enough to bare the road.  The path had never been taken.  It was eternal the way an eclipse is eternal, periodically, and it vanished from the atlas only because the surveyors came in a knocking year and nobody they trusted was counting.  Paths disappeared, she understood, when the rhythm that held them went quiet. 

She counted forward from the last trough, seven sheets of a stranger's handwriting and eleven of her own.  Eighteen; the year she was kneeling in was a rest. 

On the lowest ebb of the next new moon she rowed to the island's southern point before dawn, the torn page folded against her ribs.  The water drew back like a held breath, and the dotted line rose out of it, a spine of pale rock, wet and exactly where the paper promised.  She walked it slowly, a witness again.  Halfway across, in a pool beside the rock, something the color of weak tea had fastened itself to the white rubble: a knob of living coral no bigger than her thumb.  She did not trust it yet, but she crouched and looked for a long time, because looking was her trade.  Then she walked on toward the graves, knocking the count against her thigh so that her hands would have it ready for whoever came next.
