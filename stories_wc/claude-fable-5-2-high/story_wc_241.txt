Three days after the heart attack, my father pushed his oxygen mask aside and handed me two things: his transit pass and a stub of carpenter's pencil. 

"Green Line, car four," he whispered. 

"Read the seats, Ines." 

I am a professor of probability and the daughter of a compulsive gambler, which means I have spent my life proving, with tenure, that he was wrong.  My colleagues call me theoretically rebellious: on paper I attack every orthodoxy of chance, but I have never jaywalked, never bought a raffle ticket, never once written in a book.  Order was the only revolt available to a girl whose college fund ran sixth at Belmont. 

Still, I went, because I tell my students that a responsible mind widens its view before it narrows its verdict, and I had never done that for him. 

It took four trains to find the first one, on the back of an orange seat, in his thick gray graphite: "7:2 she never comes."  I knew that blunt hand from a thousand racing forms.  All afternoon I traversed the city inside its wet green artery, changing trains, collecting his scrawls the way I collect data, without mercy.  One seat said, "Quit today, again."  Another said, "The surest thing I ever lost was you."  They were contradictions, but lucid ones, each as clean as a proof with the last line torn off. 

Then, near the river, under a window streaming with rain: "519 to 2, Alder, Thursdays."  No horse in history has gone off at 519 to 2.  I stared until the numbers stopped being odds and became an address I knew by heart, because 519.2 is where libraries shelve probability, and where they shelve me. 

The Alder Street branch stood rainwashed in the long midsummer light, its lawn gone entirely to clover.  He used to park me there on race days, a child hunting four-leaf luck in the grass while he hunted his across the river.  I had not been inside in thirty years. 

The storm had taken the power, so the librarian was working by a candle lantern, an old brass one with a mica pane gone amber from heat. 

"You want Tibor's shelf," she said, when I asked for 519.2.  "He leaves that lantern here so he can keep reading when the lights fail, which in this building is often." 

I knew the lantern: it had been my mother's, and he had pawned it the winter I turned nine.  Somewhere, some year, he had bought it back and told no one. 

She let me carry it down the aisle, rain ticking on the skylight, the flame making a small gold weather of its own.  My book was there, Chance Without Ruin, its spine cracked by honest use.  Pressed between its pages were clovers from the lawn, forty-one of them, every one plain and three-leaved. 

"One for each Thursday," the librarian said.  "He says he sits here so he cannot be at the rail, and he says he is finished looking for the lucky kind." 

Forty-one Thursdays did not erase the other six days of the week, and I knew from his nurse that a betting slip had been in his pocket when he fell.  He was quitting and he was not quitting; he had robbed me and he had been reading me, slowly, with a pencil, by candle.  Both were true at full strength, and neither cancelled the other.  My whole discipline is built to collapse such things into one number, and I held up the lantern and declined. 

That was when the light caught the scratches on the mica pane.  With a nail, in letters the flame threw large across my own chapter on gambler's ruin, he had written: "I bet nothing here and won." 

I sat on the floor of the aisle and cried the way rain falls at midsummer, warmly, with the sun still up. 

Widening a perspective responsibly, I decided, does not mean forgiving the arithmetic.  It means refusing to let the arithmetic be the only witness.  In autumn I would teach his scrawls beside Pascal, with his permission, so my students could see that a gambler is not a man with wrong numbers but a man holding two true things at once. 

I left the clovers where they lay and returned the lantern to the desk, because he would need it some Thursday. 

On the train home, in car four, I did the first truly lawless thing of my life.  On the back of a clean orange seat I wrote, with his pencil, "Evens, she comes Thursday."  It promised attendance, not absolution, and outside the window the rain kept falling through the light that would not set.
