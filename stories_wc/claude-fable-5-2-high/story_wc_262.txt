Rain leaned on the roof of the Aldine Palace like a crowd that had agreed to whisper.  Marisol Quade climbed the iron stairs to the projection booth with her crystal ball wrapped in a scarf against her hip.  Far below, in the gilded dark, old Tobias sat at the Wurlitzer, speaking lines of a song over chords that would not hold them.  He had found the lyrics in the dead projectionist's locker, a page of words with no notes, and all week they had wandered the organ pipes searching for their tune. 

"Emil kept everything," Tobias had told her at her fortune table in the arcade.  "The melody is up there somewhere, and the wreckers come Monday." 

She had not come for his melody.  Since Dov died in April she had carried a love with no address, heavy as a reel, and her crystal showed her nothing but weather.  Emil Varga, people said, had loved someone for forty years without spending a word of it.  If unused love was stored anywhere, a man that careful would have built the shelf. 

The booth smelled of carbon and machine oil.  Two projectors crouched at their ports like patient animals, and the pedal notes from below came up through the floor into her shoes.  Marisol did what she always did in a strange room: she closed her eyes and rebuilt it.  She had thought in floor plans since childhood, turning houses in her head like dice, and lately grief was a building whose rooms she kept measuring.  The corridor outside had been eleven paces long.  The booth was nine.  Two paces of the Aldine were missing, and they were missing behind the rewind bench, where the plaster was a shade newer than the rest. 

She dragged the bench aside and found a low door, and behind it a single film can with DO NOT SCREEN grease-penciled across the lid.  Inside lay a film strip of clear leader, coiled tight, cold as a coin.  Something had been scratched into every frame, too small to read. 

Cold things keep their secrets; her grandmother had taught her that an object must be warmed to the temperature of the hand that last loved it.  So Marisol sat on the floor, slid the coil inside her coat against her breastbone, and let it incubate there while the storm muttered and Tobias tried the second verse again.  When the strip was as warm as skin, she drew it through her fingers and began the psychometry reading. 

It came the way it always came, as a place.  She was in this booth on a winter night, inside a thin man's stooped shoulders, a scribing needle in his hand and a pain like a closed fist under his ribs.  He knew what the doctors knew.  He was writing one word to a frame, and through the port, down the long slope of dark, he was watching a silver head bent over the organ.  And he was humming. 

Marisol opened her eyes, unwrapped the crystal, and held it over the strip like a lens.  For the first time since April the glass showed her something besides rain.  The scratches swelled into handwriting: Dear Tobias, goodbye again.  There were dozens of goodbye letters, one for each night of that last winter, each dated, each ending before it asked for anything.  I wrote you words for a song, the last one said, but the tune I only ever trusted to the dark. 

She had expected a vault.  But what she held was not in storage; it had gone on living in two missing paces of wall, and it had quickened against her chest like something about to hatch.  Unused love was not kept anywhere.  It incubated, in whatever warm dark would have it, until someone threaded it into the light. 

Marisol went to the port and looked down the long slope at the silver head.  Tobias was speaking the first line again, stranded.  She hummed what Emil had hummed, low, hardly louder than the rain. 

The organ stopped.  Then one pipe answered, and another, and the forgotten words stepped onto the melody like passengers onto a late train, and the whole empty palace shook very softly. 

She would carry the can down to him when the song was finished, and let him read with her crystal what had been aimed at him for forty years.  First she found a foot of blank leader on the rewind bench and Emil's needle in its drawer.  Dear Dov, she scratched, one word to a frame, while thunder turned over in its sleep.  Goodbye, and here is all I was saving.
