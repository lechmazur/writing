The last tour group climbed toward daylight, and Ines Varga waited at her easel until the last echo of their laughter faded among the crystals.  In the Selenite Gallery, ninety meters underground, laughter did not die quickly; it rang from spar to spar, thinning, like a bell someone was carrying away.  Then there was only the drip of water and the gray dust on her hands. 

She had drawn forty-one tourists that day, ten dollars a face, and her fingers were black to the second knuckle with charcoal.  She remembered all forty-one, as she remembered every face she had ever sketched, every phone number, every date.  Her memory kept everything and forgave nothing. 

The last sitter had been a girl of about six, who studied her finished portrait and asked, "Why is everybody you draw the color of smoke?"  Ines had laughed with the parents.  Now the question stayed behind in the cave with her, the way the cold did. 

Eleven years ago she had painted in oils, and people had driven from the city to see what a fifteen-year-old could do with a face.  Then her mother got sick in the middle of sitting for a portrait and was dead by spring.  Ines shut her paints and the unfinished panel in a tin cashbox, snapped a combination lock through the hasp, and announced that she would forget the numbers.  Her father, who guided tours in this cave and knew his daughter, only said, "You have never forgotten anything in your life." 

He was right.  Fourteen, thirty-two, six: the numbers had sat in her like a swallowed stone ever since.  He took the box away and told her where it was only once, last winter, in the hospital.  "Behind my northern lights," he said, "for when you are finished pretending." 

His northern lights hung at the back of the gallery, the only painting he ever made.  It was clumsy and enormous, an aurora over the valley, painted so that people standing under all that rock could remember there was a sky.  Ines had walked past it every working day for a year without touching the frame. 

Tonight she carried her lantern to it, because a child had asked a question and her hands would not stop moving at her sides, shaping strokes on nothing.  They had always done that.  She had hoped charcoal would quiet them, as a hungry person hopes crusts will do. 

The frame came off its hooks more easily than she deserved.  Behind it, a niche had been chiseled into the calcite, and in the niche sat the cashbox, furred with pale mineral bloom.  She set the lantern down and reached for the dial, the numbers already rising in her wrist. 

The lock came apart in her hand. 

Its shackle had been sawn clean through long ago, the cut gone brown with rust, and the broken thing had only been hung back in the hasp to look whole.  Ines stood holding the broken combination lock while her eyes blurred.  For eleven years she had told herself the box was sealed, and that her perfect memory was the only key, a key she nobly refused to turn.  Her father had taken even that excuse away.  Nothing had ever stood between her and the calling except her. 

She lifted the lid.  The tubes of paint inside were soft and new, with a receipt dated last autumn; he had been replacing them every year, as other men bring flowers to a grave.  Beneath them lay the panel.  Her mother looked up from it at forty-four, finished to the last eyelash, except for a patch of bare primer where her mouth should be.  Ines had been saving the mouth for the day her mother felt well enough to laugh. 

She could not rectify the dying.  She could rectify this, because she alone on earth still held that laugh entire: the chipped tooth, the left corner lifting first.  The memory she had cursed as a jailer had been keeping it safe for her. 

She propped the panel against a selenite blade, squeezed out rose madder, and the smell of linseed opened something in her chest like a cork leaving a bottle.  The lantern struck the crystals, and they threw green and violet ribbons across the ceiling, an aurora with no sky.  She wept as she mixed the color, and did not stop mixing.  Her charcoal fingers left gray prints on the brush handle, the old life signing off on the new. 

Then, in the silence where the laughter had been, she began to paint her mother's laugh.
