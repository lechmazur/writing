The Tidal Arbor hung above the clouds like a rusted trellis, its iron ribs draped in sky-vine, built to refuge airships from the vapor tide that rose twice daily through its lattice. 

Corwin Hale, the wistful station pianist, had played the waiting-hall upright there for thirty years, and this afternoon he had played his farewell to forty empty benches. 

The Company was closing the station because of the compasses. 

Whenever the tide came in, every needle aboard every ship began to flicker, shivering between a dozen false norths, and pilots had grown tired of docking by prayer. 

One year ago tonight, his wife Odile, the station projectionist, had flown a supply skiff into that flickering and never flown out. 

"The last ferry will circle until the horizon invites night," said Bree, the stationmaster, using the old dock phrase for dusk, "and then she turns back for good." 

Corwin packed little: sheet music, Odile's scarf, and the anniversary clock she had given him, its brass pendulum frozen under a glass dome. 

It was the kind wound once a year, and its spring had snapped the night she vanished; he had never had the heart to open it. 

Now his hands, steadier on keys than on grief, fumbled the broken thing into its crate, and the base plate dropped off. 

A folded note fell out, tucked where he would have found it on winding day. 

"Reel nine," Odile had written, "past where the lovers stop dancing." 

"Count it like our waltz." 

He was still staring when the radio spat static, then the ferry pilot's voice. 

"Tide is in early and my compasses are flickering." 

"I cannot find your docks, and I have twenty minutes of light." 

Bree swore softly, because a ferry groping blind toward iron ribs was worse than a ferry turning home. 

Corwin went to the projection booth instead of the window. 

Reel nine was the last of a black-and-white film he had accompanied a hundred times, and he unspooled the reel's tail across the light table with a musician's patience. 

Past the end title, where no projectionist ever lets a film run, Odile had spliced hidden sections: clear frames broken by black, long, short, short, three beats to the bar. 

Pencilled on the leader were her sums: flash intervals, lamp strength, the density of tide vapor. 

She had been building a beacon that needed no north, and she had flown out before she could test it. 

Corwin felt the old longing rise, and he drew it tight the way a tuner draws a wire, until it could ring instead of sag. 

Fate had already printed tonight's program: a dark station, a ferry turned away, a widower with a crate. 

He meant to surprise fate with an unplanned encore. 

He was creative the way accompanists must be, strategically, giving every instrument within reach a job. 

The projector went onto a luggage cart and out to the terrace, its lens aimed down the docking channel. 

But the tide bred its own lightning, random flickers a frightened pilot might chase, so the beacon needed a voice to vouch for it. 

They shoved the piano to the open doors, and Bree propped the radio handset on the music stand. 

"Tell her to steer only for the light that keeps time with this," Corwin said. 

He started the reel and began the waltz he and Odile had danced to on this terrace. 

Light pulsed into the vapor, long, short, short, and his left hand landed on every long. 

Out in the grey, lightning lied like one more compass, but only one light kept the waltz. 

"I have it," the pilot said, wonder cracking her voice. 

"Coming in on the downbeat." 

The ferry's lamps swam up out of the tide as the sun's rim touched the cloud line, and her hull kissed the dock while the horizon was still only inviting. 

Corwin played the last bar anyway, for an audience of two and one absence. 

The pilot climbed out pale and said no station had ever refuged her through a tide without a working needle. 

"Then tell the Company the Arbor stays open," Corwin said to Bree, "and I will run the reel at every tide." 

Bree looked at his crate, and he unpacked it himself. 

That night he took a length of fine treble wire from the piano's spares and hung the clock's pendulum from it. 

The brass spheres began to turn, slow as a waltz, counting toward next year. 

Somewhere in the tide a skiff might still be drifting behind a flickering needle; he did not let himself believe it, and he did not let himself stop. 

The beacon pulsed out over the clouds, long, short, short, with steel under it.
