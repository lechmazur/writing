The telephone clicked dead, and the dome repeated the click twice, as if checking her hearing.  Odette Vale, sole heiress to the Vale Meridian and all its rivers of newsprint, set the receiver down.  The journalist, young Fenn from her own city desk, had wanted only a quote for tomorrow's front page.  Vale's Comet was returning tonight, and the paper meant to salute Grandfather Ambrose, who had discovered it alone from this very peak on the ninth of March.  She had promised him something stirring within the hour. 

Instead she stood listening to her own breath come back from the copper ceiling.  Below the catwalk windows the clouds lay flat as an unprinted sheet, and the world that read her family's headlines was somewhere underneath it. 

Odette noticed things; it was her one devotion.  At eleven she had noticed that Grandfather flinched whenever a guest toasted the ninth of March, and she had said nothing, and that silence had circled back to her every year since.  "Regret keeps an orbit, Dettie," he used to say.  "It sails out where it is cold enough to forget, then swings home brighter than before." 

She wanted the truth under the toast before she lent it her name.  So she hauled the discovery log from its cabinet and opened it on the chart table.  Something small rolled out of the gutter of the book: a rosewood pen nib, whittled by hand, its tip worn round as a thumb.  Grandfather had carved one each autumn, because steel nibs bit frozen fingers at this altitude, and he wrote with it until the snows left.  It had been marking the page for the ninth of March. 

"Now, Grandfather," Odette told the dome, "we shall do this the way you taught me geometry." 

"Geometry," the dome agreed. 

"Assume the comfortable thing is true, and walk it forward until it trips."  She seated the nib in a holder, bowing slightly, as one does to a witness.  "Suppose Ambrose Vale wrote the entry of the ninth on the night of the ninth."  "Then he wrote it with that winter's nib, this one, already five months blunt." 

She dipped it and drew the date in the margin; the stroke came out fat and furred, exactly like his entries for the eighth and the tenth.  But the famous entry between them, "New comet near Capella, sighted by A.V. alone," ran fine as spider silk. 

"A nib does not grow young for one evening," she said.  "Contradiction; therefore the ninth was written later, with a fresh nib, after the comet had a name worth owning." 

The dome said "owning" and let it hang. 

Her hands were not quite steady as she turned the leaf.  Under the eleventh, in thin steel script that was not his, someone had written: "H. Pell finds faint tailed object near Capella; Mr. Vale confirms at the large glass."  Henrietta Pell: the woman hired to compute orbits, whose name Odette had met exactly once, on a salary ledger that stopped that same spring. 

Grandfather had not burned her line.  He had left it one leaf from his lie and tucked the worn nib beside them, a confession addressed to whoever looked closely enough. 

Odette laughed once, wetly, and the observatory laughed with her.  "You old coward," she said, "you made me do the proof." 

Then she rang the city desk, because a truth found above the clouds was no use until it came down. 

"Fenn, stop the front page." 

"Miss Vale, the plates are cast, and the presses roll at four." 

"Then I intervene before four, and here is your quote: the comet was first seen by Henrietta Pell on the eleventh of March, and this newspaper has been wrong for sixty years." 

A long pause crackled down the mountain.  "Ma'am, that is your own name you are taking off the sky." 

"No, I am letting a regret finish its orbit; run it above the fold, Fenn, since bad news should at least have good posture." 

He was writing before she hung up; she could hear the pencil. 

Odette cranked the shutter open, and cold starlight poured into the dome like ink into a well.  In the east, just clearing the white floor of the clouds, a faint tailed object was coming home.  She took up the blunt rosewood nib and, under tonight's date, wrote in a fat, furred, honest line: "Pell's Comet recovered; O. Vale confirms." 

"Henrietta Pell," she said aloud, to hear how it would sound in the world. 

The dome, devoted pupil, said it back.
