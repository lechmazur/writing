On the floating isle of Sennet, a shadow detaches when its owner dies, and for seven nights it wanders, dreaming the dead one's last dream.  Six nights after Tavi's shadow detached, Marrow the lantern maker watched it drift between the tea terraces he had pruned for thirty years.  It touched each bush the way he used to, checking for frost.  Her grief sat in her like swallowed glass, bright and cutting.  Her mother had taught her that grief cannot be burned or buried, only folded: put it on paper, crease it into cranes, and let the wind have them.  But Marrow's grief had no shape to put on paper.  It needed his last dream. 

Dream recording was her trade.  A raw stone, polished beside a sleeper, took the dream into its facets stroke by stroke, and her lanterns shone it back out.  She had recorded brides and children, never a shadow.  Shadows dreamed only in the undercroft of the glass basilica that crowned the isle, and the wardens allowed no flame down there, because light unmakes the dead early. 

So she had spent five nights building a lantern that lied.  Its panes were smoked with tea ash and mirrored inside, so every scrap of glow fell inward, onto one uncut moonstone.  She set a kettle on top, tucked a roll of rice paper in her sash, and carried it all uphill at dusk like a tea warmer. 

The warden at the door sniffed the steam.  "Tavi's last picking," Marrow said, and poured him a cup.  He drank, wept a little, and waved her through without lifting the kettle. 

Inside, the basilica held the sunset in every wall, and its one stair coiled toward the dome.  The builders had been cruel or wise: the stair descended while ascending.  Marrow climbed, her knees insisting on up, while the fire in the glass sank past her and the air turned cellar cold.  At the top step she stood at the bottom of everything, among the pooled shadows of the week's dead.  She knew his by the stoop of its shoulders. 

She knelt, wet her cloth with cold tea and ash, slid her hand through the lantern's leather cuff, and began to polish the moonstone.  Through the smoked pane she watched the first facet clear: the lower terrace in morning mist.  She had braced for his dying: the fever, the rattling cot.  Instead each facet gave her the day they met, when a young pruner tried to impress the lantern maker's daughter by folding a crane from a tea wrapper and made a trodden frog.  In the stone she laughed at him, and he looked at her as if she were the only lit window on the isle.  It was the loveliest thing she had ever recorded, and it flayed her. 

She polished through the night until her fingertips split and stained the cloth.  Then she noticed the cost.  With every facet finished, Tavi's shadow thinned, its dream drawn out of it like thread from a hem.  One facet remained rough.  If she stopped, she could keep the stooped shape beside her until the seventh dawn took it anyway, and own a dream with a hole in it.  If she finished, she would be the one who ended him. 

Her hand hovered.  The shadow reached out and touched the lantern the way it had touched each tea bush, checking for frost. 

Marrow polished the last facet.  In it, the young pruner handed her the ruined crane, and she kept it.  Then the floor beside her was only floor. 

She walked down the stair and so rose into the dome as dawn struck the glass.  There she threw open the lying panes, set the lantern where the whole basilica could pour sunrise through it, and unrolled her paper.  Light went through the moonstone and came out as mist, a terrace, a boy's clumsy hands, and where it fell it transformed each blank sheet into a piece of that morning.  It hurt to look at.  She looked anyway, and folded.  Her split fingers made trodden frogs at first, and she laughed, and the laugh cracked into the weeping she had not managed in seven days.  Crease by crease the swallowed glass came out of her and into wings. 

By noon she stood at the garden's edge, where the tea bushes leaned over nothing, with ninety cranes in her apron.  She let the wind have them.  The isle was rising on the noon warmth, so the cranes fell even as they climbed, descending while ascending, the way she had gone to find him. 

She kept the worst one.  She hung it in an honest lantern over his terrace, where each evening it threw a small, crooked shadow that never detached.
