Beneath the rotting pier at Cutler's Reach, the pirate market opened the way a held breath opens, slowly and without sound.  Lanterns hung from barnacled pilings, and the tide slapped the planks underfoot like a patient hand.  Junie Tarver, a moonshine runner's daughter, knew the law here better than her prayers: a truce of hush, from lamplighting until sunrise.  No voice above a whisper, no names, no old grievances, no blades.  Smugglers who would drown each other by daylight traded here in nods and chalk marks, and the quiet felt like a quilt with something sharp sewn inside. 

She set her crate of jugs on a trestle and checked the thing hidden in the straw: a secondhand dictating machine, its small horn aimed outward, a fresh wax cylinder waiting.  Three years ago, someone under this pier had sold her father to the revenue men.  "I never heard it coming, June-bug," he told her through the jail screen, and that sentence had lodged in her like a fishbone.  So she had come to capture the sound of trust breaking, to press it into wax and learn it by heart, so no Tarver would ever miss it again. 

She found the betrayer before midnight, without asking a single question.  Dell Pruitt, her father's old partner, sat on a keg near the pier's seaward end, and from his vest hung an antique watch fob, a brass anchor worn smooth as a river stone.  Her father had worn that fob every night of her childhood.  On his last run he had handed it to Pruitt as a pledge, the way runners did: hold this until I am back.  A man who had expected his partner home would not wear the pledge like winnings. 

Beside Pruitt's boot sat a shuttered lantern with red glass, and beyond the bar, where the dark was thickest, Junie could feel a cutter waiting the way you feel a dog watching you eat.  She understood the arrangement as if he had whispered it to her.  When the loaded boats fired their engines to leave, he would bare the red light, and the channel would close like a purse. 

She could have screamed his name, but she was curiously steadfast about the hush, even now, even toward him.  The truce had rocked her to sleep in the bilge of her father's boat; she would not be the one to break it.  Besides, her father had taught her a quieter way to thwart a listening man.  "Start her three times and leave on the fourth," he used to say, "and whoever is waiting on you wears himself out guessing." 

An hour before dawn, she slid her crate to the trestle beside his keg, set the cylinder turning, climbed down into the Patience, and cranked the engine.  It roared under the pier like a sin confessed, and she killed it.  Pruitt's hand had already jumped to the lantern; a blink of red escaped before he slapped the shutter closed.  A few heads turned, then turned back, because the hush forgives one oddness. 

She cranked again, let the engine cough and catch, and killed it again.  Red winked a second time, longer, and now a fishwife and two rum brokers were watching his boot instead of their scales. 

The third false start did the work.  Pruitt, sweating, held the shutter wide, and out past the bar a heavy government engine grumbled awake in answer, plain as a cough in church.  Then Junie's engine died, and the cutter's kept running, alone, with nothing to chase. 

Nobody shouted.  That was the unsettling mercy of the place: a hundred people learned they had been sold, and not one voice rose above a whisper.  Lamps went out in order, stall by stall, and boats slid away on oars through a marsh cut where the cutter drew too much water to follow. 

Junie climbed back up to her crate.  Pruitt stood beside it with the red lantern dark at his feet, looking at her the way her father had looked through the screen.  He did not plead, and she did not accuse; the truce held until sunrise, and sunrise was only a gray seam yet.  He unhooked the watch fob from his vest and laid it on the crate lid, and the clasp made one small click against the wood. 

She had expected a crack like river ice, and instead trust broke with the sound of a button coming undone.  The needle had caught it, and caught the long hush around it, a hundred strangers keeping faith anyway. 

When the sun cleared the marsh, the pier was only rot and gulls.  Junie pocketed the fob, cranked the Patience a fourth time, and left for real, unsure which sound her father should hear first.
