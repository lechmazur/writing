The ice on the reservoir was letting go in long groans when Maren unlocked the control room of the dead dam.  Nobody had steered water from here in nineteen years, not since her father, the last operator, abandoned his post without a word.  His logbook still lay on the main console, shut on a cracked leather bookmark, and she had read every page except the one it kept.  He had run this room on a single rule: watch the gauges and do not ask. 

Maren, a glassblower by trade and a brooder by temperament, had spent six thaws turning that rule inside out.  Her small furnace squatted where the turbine readouts used to glow, and over each dead switch on the panels sat a glass dome she had blown, sheltering a folded slip of paper.  Every slip held a question some child from the valley had been afraid to ask anywhere else.  Why does Grandpa forget me?  Is the dam going to break?  What if I am not smart?  Under glass, a question could not be laughed at, corrected, or blown away; it could simply sit in the light until someone was ready to think about it. 

The children came up when the road thawed, and this year they brought a new boy, Tobin, who stood by the door like a coat on a hook.  Maren knew that posture; she had worn it in this very room.  So she did what she always did to make the air safe: she picked up a broom and became ridiculous. 

"Behold," she announced, in the voice of a storm warning, "the sweeper approaches the eastern console, where dust has ruled unchallenged since autumn."  The regulars groaned happily.  "She hesitates, for the dust is cunning, but lo, the dustpan arrives, loyal and slightly bent!" 

She narrated the filling of the kettle as a river crossing and the lighting of the furnace as the waking of a small, bad-tempered sun.  It was a trick she had worked out carefully: if sweeping could be an epic, then no question was too small to deserve a hearing, and nobody who laughed here was laughing at you.  Tobin's shoulders came down an inch. 

Outside, a slab of ice broke with a boom that rattled the dead dials, and meltwater ticked through the roof into her quenching bucket.  She gathered molten glass on the pipe and began a new dome, narrating each breath as a diplomatic negotiation with fire.  Tobin drifted closer, past the domes, reading the slips he could see, until he reached the logbook. 

"This one has no glass," he said.  "Whose question is in here?" 

The heroic voice died in her throat.  For six years she had built shelters for everyone's wondering but her own, and a boy had found the gap in under an hour.  She could deflect; she had a fine speech ready about the Perilous Kettle.  But the room's whole promise was that asking would be met, and a promise with one locked drawer in it was her father's rule wearing a friendlier hat. 

"Mine, I think," she said plainly, and set the pipe on its rest, abandoning the dome to sag.  "I have been afraid to look." 

"We could look together," Tobin offered, as if it were the most ordinary thing, which in this room it was. 

She opened the log at the bookmark, and the old leather cracked a little more under her thumb.  Her father's square handwriting recorded the ice going out, the spillway holding, and then: Maren asked today why the water sounds angry.  Told her to hush.  I did not know the answer, and I do not know why not knowing frightens me so-- 

The sentence stopped there, nineteen years unfinished.  He had not left because he had no questions; he had left because he had one and no safe place to set it down.  Something in Maren's chest shifted like the reservoir, a hard white plate cracking loose, with dark, moving water underneath.  It hurt, and it was also, absurdly, a relief. 

"He was scared," Tobin said, surprised.  "Grown-ups get scared of not knowing?" 

"Constantly," Maren said, and wrote his question on a slip, because it was a good one. 

Then she reheated the slumped glass and, with Tobin turning the pipe, blew the widest dome she had ever made, lopsided and bright.  While it cooled in the annealer, they left the logbook open, the bookmark laid beneath her father's unfinished line, waiting for its roof.  Outside, the ice went on breaking, loudly, into something that could move. 

"And so," Maren told the room, picking up the broom, "the sweeper returns, somewhat thawed, to the matter of the dust."
