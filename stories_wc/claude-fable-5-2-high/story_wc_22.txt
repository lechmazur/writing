After the reef died, the sea went quiet enough for Garrow to hear the birds thinking.  He stood at the end of the old landing jetty at twilight, a single line of stone ruled across an ocean that carried no other mark.  Sea thrift bloomed pink in every crack under his boots, rooted in years of gull droppings, and that small trespass of flowers was why he had come.  Behind him his black coach waited with its two old mares, and behind the coach lay the Strand, a mile of salted gray sand where nothing had grown in thirty years. 

Garrow had been the coachman of Gloom Hill for all of them.  He had driven the hill's sons down to that sand, six to a bench, to fight the shore clans for the reef and its fish, and he had driven home with the benches empty.  Now the reef lay bleached and crumbling, there was nothing left to kill for, and both peoples had turned their backs on the place.  Garrow could not. 

On every run since, he had gathered seed pods from the roadsides: lupine rattles, poppy urns, milkweed purses, the winged keys of hill maple.  He kept the dried collection in a boot box under the driver's seat, where it shook like small bones on bad roads, and tonight the box rode under his arm.  Twice he had sown the Strand, and twice the salt had eaten every seed.  So he had come to ask the dead how they once kept it green. 

His grandmother had sung of a Concord, a peace cut in stone between hill and shore before either had kings, set at the jetty's foot and swallowed when the reef grew over it.  Forgetting it, she said, was how the war began. 

A curlew called from the flats, two rising notes and a fall.  Garrow knew that call the way he knew a loose wheel: the water had reached its lowest and would hold a quarter hour.  He climbed down the weedy steps and waded in to his knees. 

The stone lay where the dead coral had sloughed off it, a slab as long as his coach, lettered in two columns.  The left was Old Hill, the tongue of his grandmother's road prayers, dead two centuries and alive only in his mouth.  The right was Shore Speech, which had died with the last clan widows and which no book had ever held.  But the shore people had built their words from bird calls, naming the tide for the curlew and the rain for the tern, and the last widow he carried inland had whistled each word to its bird for him.  Thirty years of dawns on a driver's box had taught him the rest. 

He read the hill column aloud, then whistled the shore column into the darkening air, and the two halves agreed.  Let the Strand belong to neither and feed both.  Let the reef that fed us living feed us dead.  Where the white birds quarrel, gather; when the tern cries thrice, give. 

Garrow stood in the cold water and understood what he had lacked.  The old peoples had sweetened their salted ground with crumbled coral and fed it with guano from the roosts, and now a whole reef of lime lay in white drifts along the tideline, free to anyone who would stoop. 

The curlew called again, falling first, and he climbed out ahead of the returning sea.  On the north rocks the gannets were quarreling over their ledges, and he followed the noise.  He crushed bleached coral under the coach wheels until it ran like flour, and filled the mares' feed sacks with it and with guano scraped from the ledges. 

Then he walked out onto the Strand in the last light and began to fertilize it, one man under the whole sky, casting a strip no wider than his axles.  He could not cover a battlefield, but he could draw a line across it, from the jetty toward the hill road, the way the jetty drew its line across the sea. 

Overhead a tern cried three times: rain before morning.  Garrow opened the box and gave, cracking each pod with his thumb and pressing the seeds into the whitened sand where boys he had delivered still lay. 

He did not stay to guard them.  He had spent his life carrying people to that sand and leaving them to orders; the stone said only let, in both dead tongues, and letting was the one thing the Strand had never been given.  He climbed to his box and turned the mares toward Gloom Hill, and behind him the jetty kept its thin row of flowers above the dark water, waiting to be joined.
