The whale beached at dawn, and by dusk the whole harbor had gone quiet around it.  Odile Farrow, lantern lighter for the nine docks of Brinemouth, knew why it had come ashore.  The pockets had moved. 

Brinemouth sat on a coast riddled with pockets of gravity, invisible wells where the world pulled harder or forgot to pull at all.  Their strength fluctuated mysteriously, swelling and thinning by no tide table anyone had managed to write.  A lighter learned them or lost her flames, because fire leaned toward a heavy pocket the way grass leans from wind.  So every evening Odile drank one cup of coffee on the harbor wall and read the grounds.  Grounds were fine enough to feel every pull, and they settled into ridges and rings that mapped the night's wells better than any instrument. 

Tonight they had not made rings.  They had drawn a single dark spiral, tight as a snail shell, and its tail pointed inland at the abandoned saltworks.  Something there was pulling hard enough to drag a whale off its road. 

Odile had her own reason to follow a pull like that.  In her coat she carried a slim book of pressed wildflowers, sea thrift and campion and harebell, each one labeled in her handwriting.  She did not remember picking a single stem.  Beside her own script ran a second hand, small and slanted, and she did not remember that either.  The receipt folded in the back cover said only that the Harbor Clinic had deleted one person from her, at her request, four years ago. 

The clinicians claimed deleted memories went nowhere.  Odile had tended flames too long to believe that anything simply went out; things burned down into something, and the something had weight.  For four years she had wanted to find where deleted memories go, and for four years the grounds had shown her only weather. 

She lit her nine docks first, because the boats still needed to come home.  Every flame bent toward the saltworks like a row of bowed heads.  Then she took her pole lantern and a soot scraper and walked inland while the town stood on the beach passing buckets in silence. 

The saltworks had been shut since the brine pans cracked, but the growing chamber beneath it had never stopped working.  She felt the pocket on the stairs, a slow hand pressing her shoulders, and each step down cost more than the last.  At the bottom her lantern showed a vaulted room where salt crystals had been seeded on hanging threads and left to grow in the dark.  They had grown.  Cubes the size of fists crowded the walls, columns joined ceiling to floor, and everything gleamed without a sound, as if light itself were holding its breath. 

Her flame did not lean here.  It pointed straight down. 

She understood then that whatever the clinic lifted out of people sank to the heaviest place on the coast, and made it heavier still.  A town's worth of forgetting had finally pulled a whale ashore. 

She knelt where the flame aimed and scraped through crust the color of milk, then through older salt as clear as ice.  Each crystal she lifted to the lantern threw a small moving picture on the wall: a stranger's wedding, a capsized skiff, a dog.  She set each one back gently, because they were not hers to keep. 

Four years down, her scraper rang against a crystal that showed a headland pink with sea thrift and a small hand closing a slim book on a harebell.  Odile exhumed it with her bare fingers, the way one lifts a sleeping thing.  The picture widened as she turned it.  A girl of six, laughing, writing flower names beside her mother's in a slanted scrawl; then a fever ward; then a small lantern Odile had not been able to light. 

The weight of it came into her arms all at once, exactly the weight of a sleeping child, and Odile sat down hard on the salt.  So this was what she had paid to put down.  She could bury it again and climb the stairs light. 

Instead she opened the book to the harebell, touched the slanted letters, and said the name the crystal had given back: Wren.  The chamber took the word and kept it, gleaming. 

She wrapped the crystal in her scarf and carried it up, every step heavy, every step hers.  On the ninth dock she set it inside the last lantern, against the seaward glass, where the light would pass through a pink headland before it reached the water.  For the first time that night, a flame stood upright.  Down on the sand the tide came in under the whale, and the town, without a word, began to push.
