Odile Marsh made jewelry for the bereaved: brooches of braided hair, lockets of jet, rings engraved with dates no one wanted to remember.  For twenty years she had believed the work was a kindness.  Then Tobias died, and she learned that her brooches only taught grief to hold still.  By the winter after, she had sold her gravers, pawned her wedding band for coal, and taken to sleeping on the workshop floor among unanswered commissions.  That floor was the bottom, and it was from the floor that she finally saw the tube.  It was wedged beneath Tobias's old tattooing bench, where only someone lying down could have found it.  Inside was a tattoo stencil on vellum, his last, never inked onto anyone's skin.  He had drawn a peony gone to ruin, its stem bent, most of its petals missing, the bare places pricked with tiny holes as if he had lost patience with the needle.  Rolled inside the vellum was a paper crane.  Odile sat up. 

When they were courting, Tobias had been too shy to ask her anywhere aloud, so he had left origami figures on her windowsill, a fox, a moth, a boat, each with coordinates written inside.  She would unfold them, consult her father's atlas, and find him waiting at a bridge or a bakery, pretending to be surprised.  Her fingers remembered the creases before her mind did.  Inside the crane were two sets of numbers in his cramped hand. 

The first pair led her, the next evening, up Carrow Hill to the old observatory.  She had passed it all her life without entering.  Every clock in its hall had been stopped at a different hour, and the keeper, a stooped woman named Wren, did not apologize for them.  "The light up there left home before your grandmother was born," Wren said.  "Nothing arrives here on time, so we stopped pretending to keep any."  She looked at the crane in Odile's hand and nodded, as if a long-expected parcel had come.  "The tattooist said you would be late, and that I was not to mind." 

The dome had no shutters.  Instead, heavy velvet drapes hung across the slit in the roof, worked by a rope as thick as Odile's wrist.  "Curtains of intention," Wren said, resting her hand on the rope without pulling.  "They do not open for wandering eyes; you must tell me what you have come to see." 

Odile had not said what she wanted in a year.  Wanting had seemed a disloyalty, a thing the living did.  She unrolled the vellum, looked at the ruined flower, and felt the words come up slowly, like water in a well.  "I have come to see what he meant," she said, "and whether it can be finished." 

Wren pulled.  The curtains parted on a strip of cold sky, and Wren swung the brass telescope to the second pair of numbers, which were not a place on any atlas but a place overhead.  Below the eyepiece waited a glass plate in a frame cut to the exact size of the stencil.  Odile laid the vellum down and watched the stars illuminate it from behind.  Each pinprick caught a point of old light, and the points gathered where the petals were missing, until the withered peony stood on the plate in full, trembling bloom.  He had not drawn a dying flower; he had drawn the half that needed her to carry it somewhere and look. 

She did not weep loudly.  She stood with her palm near the glass, not touching, the way she used to stand near him while he worked. 

"It is not a real flower," Wren said gently, in case she had forgotten. 

"No," Odile said, "it is genuinely artificial, like everything I ever made, and I think that is why it is true."  A hair brooch never claimed to be the person; it only claimed that someone had chosen, with their hands, to keep loving.  She had forgotten the choosing. 

In spring she bought back her gravers one at a time.  She answered every commission on that floor, but the pieces she sent were strange: discs of jet carved with half a flower and pierced where the rest should be.  A week after each delivery, she walked out before dawn and left a paper crane on a mourner's windowsill, coordinates folded inside.  She could not make anyone climb the hill, or say aloud what they had come to see.  But some nights, from her workshop window, she saw the dome's dark seam open, and knew that somewhere under it, something withered was learning to bloom again.
