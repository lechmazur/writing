Maren kept the sleeping tablet under her tongue until the night nurse turned away, then spat it into her sleeve.  It was the only kind of rebellion she had ever managed: small, silent, and logged on her chart as compliance.  For eleven months she had been the Institute's guinea pig, one half of an experimental treatment that braided two damaged memories together so each could hold up the other.  Her half had healed.  Tomas, the other half, lay three floors up with his rhythms flattening on a monitor, and in the morning the doctors would cut the entanglement before his dying could spread into her.  They called it mercy.  She called it losing the lagoon. 

Neither of them had ever seen it awake.  It had surfaced in the overlap, where her mother's brass kettle met his grandfather's fishing boat: a lagoon of gold water under starlight, auric and unhurried, pulsing as if something beneath it kept time.  They dreamed it on the same nights and compared notes at breakfast like children.  Tomas, a drummer before the accident, swore the lagoon had a beat, and that if they ever found it, the dream would stop being a symptom and start being a place.  He had spent months drumming on everything, on bed rails, meal trays, her knuckles, hunting for it.  Now his hands were still, and the hunt was hers. 

She slipped barefoot through the service door into the sound wave garden.  The Institute had built it for therapy: terraces of tuned reeds, steel plates dusted with sand, and at the center a shallow basin whose water was seeded with gold mica so that patients could watch sound take shape.  Overhead the aurora hung in long green veils, grieving slowly across the northern sky.  Its light made the garden look like a place already being remembered. 

Maren began the way he would have.  She drummed on the gate latch, and the reeds shivered a thin chord back.  She drummed on the handrail, the bench slats, the lamp post, her own sternum, and each surface answered through the garden in ripples of sand and light.  None of it was right.  The patterns on the plates stayed geometric and cold, hospital shapes, nothing like water that kept time. 

She climbed onto the rim of the basin to perch there, knees drawn up, exactly as she sat at the lagoon's edge in every dream.  From there she stopped trying to remember the beat with her own mind.  She reached instead for his: the borrowed ache in wrists that had never held sticks, the smell of diesel and nets, a boy tapping a gunwale while his grandfather hummed.  Her fingers found the stone rim and moved without her.  Three slow strokes, a held breath, two quick. 

The mica lifted from the bottom of the basin and turned.  Gold spread across the water in soft rings, unhurried, pulsing, until the stars and the green veils lay reflected in it and the basin was no longer a basin.  An auric lagoon under starlight, small enough to drown a hand in, real enough to wet her feet. 

She kept the rhythm going until her fingertips split, afraid of what would come when she stopped.  Then her hands simply rested. 

Tomas had a name for the silence after a last beat, before the room decides what it has heard: the hush of the universe inhaling.  In that hush the reeds went still, the aurora seemed to pause mid-fold, and three floors up a monitor drew its line straight. 

She knew, because a memory arrived that was not hers and never could have been.  It was the lagoon seen from the far shore: gold water, cold stars, and across it a thin woman perched on the edge, drumming with bleeding hands, looking nothing like a patient.  He had found the place before he left, and he had found her in it. 

Maren wept without sound while the gold settled grain by grain and the sky went on mourning in green. 

At dawn the doctors came with consent forms and gentle voices, explaining that severance was now only a formality, a tidying of loose threads.  She listened politely, as she always had.  Then she handed back the pen unsigned, and on the arm of the chair her fingers began, very softly, three slow strokes, a held breath, two quick.
