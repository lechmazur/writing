The drawbridge rose at dusk to let the trawlers out, and Maren Holt stayed on the wrong side of it on purpose.  Until the tide turned at midnight, no councilman could cross the channel to hurry her, and the drowned pavilion was hers.  It stood in the lagoon where the old promenade had been, sunk to its railings since the seawall was moved inland forty years ago.  Only the roof and its latticed frieze showed above the water, a lacework that held its pattern by what had been cut away. 

The council wanted one word from her: condemned.  Maren was known along the coast as the surveyor who never redrew a line, and they trusted her to give it without sentiment.  They mistook her stillness for an absence of feeling.  In truth she felt everything, the way a tide gauge feels the moon, and simply declined to tremble where anyone could see. 

She pulled on her waders, hung the lantern from her pole, and walked down the submerged steps until the cold closed around her chest.  Inside, the dance floor lay under four feet of black water, and her light went down through it like a slow coal, gilding the footings of the twelve columns.  She worked by touch, pressing her palm to each repair the way her father had taught her to read a wall.  The patches were many, laid by different hands in different decades, and at the seventh column one of them rang hollow under her hammer. 

She set her chisel and had to wrest the thing loose, mortar giving up its grip a grain at a time: a corked glass vial, and in it a note.  She read it by the lantern, standing in the sea.  "Mended this footing, March 1911, instead of building the violins I meant to build," it said, signed Tobias Arkwright, mason. 

Maren stood very still, which was how she burned.  Then she went back to every patch she had already passed, and the hollow places answered her one by one.  A plasterer in 1934 had wanted to see Lisbon.  A girl who mixed mortar during the war had planned to study the stars and married instead.  The last repair, from the year the seawall moved, said only, "I was going to dance here with her."  Nine vials, nine things that never happened, each one sealed into the place where the stone had failed. 

She understood then what she had been measuring all evening.  The pavilion did not stand on granite alone; it stood on absences interlaced like its own frieze, each surrendered dream braced against the next, the gaps carrying the load. 

Her father had left a gap of that kind.  He had surveyed this same shore for thirty years with a brass traveling inkwell screwed shut in his coat, meaning someday to write the history of everything the sea had taken, and he had died with the book unbegun.  The inkwell rode in her own coat now, an antique she filled every Monday and used only for figures. 

She waded out, sat on the top step above the waterline, and unscrewed the inkwell's cap.  First she copied all nine notes into her field book, exactly, with the column numbers and dates, as carefully as she had ever logged an elevation.  This was a survey too, she thought, and nobody had ever been appointed to it.  Somebody ought to be the archeologist of abandoned dreams, digging where people had buried what they could not carry, and she found she wanted the post more than she had wanted anything in years. 

Then she wrote her report, and it was not one word.  The footings were sound, she stated, because generations had kept them sound; the structure required one further repair at the seventh column and could be raised onto new piers when the council found its nerve.  The council would argue, and she would not redraw the line. 

Before the tide turned she resealed each vial and returned it to its hollow, packing fresh cement over them with her own trowel.  Arkwright's hollow she left open a moment longer.  On a leaf torn from the field book she wrote, in her father's ink, "He was going to write the book of what the sea took, and I am going to write it for him, beginning here."  She rolled it into a spare sample vial, sealed it with candle wax, and set it into the seventh footing beside his, the first note there that was a promise rather than a farewell.  She troweled the cement smooth over both. 

At midnight the drawbridge came down, and Maren crossed it wet to the collar, face composed, the inkwell warm against her ribs like a banked fire.
