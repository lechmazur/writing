The package arrived on the morning Bettina Crake meant to post her resignation from the dead.  It bore no return address, only a call number inked on brown paper: 387.1, Reference.  Inside, wrapped in a page of tide tables, lay a wedge of ribbed glass as long as her forearm, one prism from a lighthouse lens.  She knew the lens it belonged to.  Everyone in Upper Harrow did. 

Forty years ago the seawall failed and the lower town went under in a night, taking three hundred and twelve people and the public library where the old lighthouse lens stood on display.  Ever since, on moonless nights, a cold light stammered up through the harbor: three short, three long, three short.  Nobody answered distress signals from the drowned.  They closed their curtains and, eventually, forgot there had been a library at all. 

Bettina could not forget, because she had buried every one of them.  Three hundred and twelve coffins weighted with sandbags, and in her ledger, under Location of Remains, the same word every time: sea.  The sea was not an address.  An undertaker's whole trade was giving rest a permanent address, a place the living could walk to, and she had failed at it so thoroughly that the dead themselves kept complaining. 

She was tired enough to quit.  She had been tired enough for years, but she had never managed it; each morning she simply failed to stop, the way a clock fails to.  Her hands just went on without consulting her. 

They went on now, setting the letter aside and reading the tide tables the prism had come wrapped in.  One slack water was circled: tonight, eleven minutes long.  The hand was a librarian's, small and exact. 

She borrowed a salvage tank from the Pell boy and rowed out over the drowned rooftops with the prism in her lap.  Below her, the light was already stammering. 

The water closed over her like a held breath.  Lower Harrow lay perfectly still, streets furred with silt, doors standing open, yet the stillness felt hunted, a quiet with the tide's return running close behind it.  She followed the pulse down the high street and through the library's fallen doors.  The stacks still stood, their books swollen into single grey loaves.  In the reading room the roof had come down around the lens, cradling it in a cage of beams no salvage diver would have squeezed through. 

Bettina had spent her life occupying impossible spaces: the inch between a widow and a casket, the doorway of a house that wanted no one in it, the silence after a name.  She unbuckled the tank, pushed it ahead of her, and threaded herself in sideways after it. 

Up close, the lens was a glass beehive taller than she was, and one slot in its ribs gaped empty.  There was no lamp inside.  The light lived in the gap itself, and when she looked into it she saw why it stammered: they were all in there, three hundred and twelve of them crowded into a space the width of her palm, taking turns at the opening.  A broken lens could not house them, only leak them, flash by flash.  Whoever sent the prism wanted the light not snuffed but revitalized, made whole enough to live in. 

She set the prism to the slot.  It would not seat; forty years of salt had crusted the brass.  Her gauge slid toward red, and the silt began to lift and lean as the tide turned and came looking for her.  A sensible woman would have left.  Bettina did not decide to stay; she only noticed, distantly, that her hands were scraping the channel clean with the coffin key from her belt and had not asked permission. 

The prism slid home with a click she felt in her teeth. 

The stammering stopped.  The light did not go out.  Mended, the lens gathered it, steadied it, and swept it slowly around the reading room, one calm bar of brightness passing along shelf after shelf.  And in the gaps between the ruined books, in spaces too narrow for anything living, the dead were settling, one to a place, like volumes returned. 

She threaded herself out and rose on the last of her air, the tide snapping at her heels.  From the boat she watched the beam wheel under the harbor, unhurried, asking for nothing. 

By dawn she had struck out sea three hundred and twelve times and written, in her careful hand, Lower Harrow Public Library, Reference.  The letter stayed on the desk, unposted, and at nine she opened the parlor, having once again failed to stop. 

That night, for the first time in forty years, Upper Harrow left its curtains open.
