Odile Vermeer climbed the hundred and nine stairs of the Zevenster clock tower with a folder of minutes under one arm and an iron pinecone in her coat pocket.  The pinecone was a cuckoo clock weight, the last piece of her grandmother's clock, and she carried it the way other people carry worry stones.  By day she was librarian to the Two Banks Choir, the woman who knew which altos needed large print and which basses needed forgiving.  Tonight she was only supposed to take notes. 

The grain elevator talks had been moved to the tower because it stood on the river island, belonging to neither bank, under an astronomical clock that had not moved in eleven years.  Around the oak table in the dial room sat the Flemish cooperative from the east bank and the Walloon cooperative from the west, twelve farmers with folded arms.  The east bank had a sound elevator, its bucket belt still able to lift a harvest skyward, but its silo was cracked and weeping damp.  The west bank had a dry, magnificent silo and an elevator whose motor had died the same winter as Henri Hardy, the only man who understood it.  Each side wanted money to rehabilitate its own ruin, and the province would fund only one. 

For five hours the interpreters had ferried sentences across the table like coal, and every sentence arrived colder than it left.  Odile wrote it all down, and listened underneath the words, as she listened to a choir.  She heard that Pieter Daems was not angry about concrete; he was frightened of being the chairman who lost his father's silo.  She heard that Henri's widow, Lucienne, said "notre silo" because "notre" was the last place her husband still lived. 

Near midnight Pieter stood, Lucienne stood, chairs barked against the floorboards, and the interpreters capped their pens.  Through the tall window the moon poured in, that old borrower of starlight, and silvered the dead brass planets of the clock.  She had spent her life gathering separate parts into one score; she could not file minutes that recorded two silos and no bridge.  Odile felt the pinecone heavy against her hip, and decided. 

"Sit down, alstublieft, asseyez-vous," she said, in the voice she used on tenors, and astonishingly they sat.  She waved the interpreters quiet.  "Tonight nobody gets a whole sentence in their own tongue," she said.  "You will each have to lean on a neighbor to finish understanding me." 

Then she spoke as she had sung since childhood, interspersing languages like alternating voices in a canon.  "Een lift zonder silo," she told the east bank, then swung west, "est un escalier vers nulle part."  Pieter frowned at the half he was missing, and it was Lucienne, grudgingly, who gave it to him in kitchen Dutch: a staircase to nowhere.  "Un silo sans ascenseur," Odile went on, "is een graf voor de wind."  This time Pieter translated for the west bank without being asked, a tomb for the wind, and someone laughed. 

She took the pinecone from her pocket and crossed to the clock, where the small chain that drove the moon dial hung slack, its weight lost in the years when neither bank would pay a keeper.  "My grandmother's cuckoo clock had two weights," she said, hooking the iron cone onto the chain.  "One turned the hands, one gave the bird its voice, and when the family split the clock, each side got a weight and nobody got the time."  She let go.  The pinecone sank a finger's width, the chain bit its sprocket, and with a dry cough the painted moon shuddered forward eleven years late. 

"Here is what I will write in the minutes, unless someone stops me," Odile said gently, and did not pause for anyone to stop her.  "One application, not two: rehabilitate the eastern elevator, keep the western silo, and run a conveyor bridge across the river between them, een brug, un pont."  "Pieter, your father's silo will not be lost; its steel hoops will become the ribs of the bridge."  "Lucienne, Henri's silo will stay full, and notre will finally mean all of us." 

Pieter looked at the moving moon, then across the table.  "Notre pont," he said, in terrible French.  "Onze brug," Lucienne answered, in worse Dutch, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. 

Odile wrote the accord herself, one clause in each language by turns, so that neither half could be read without the other.  Twelve farmers signed it under the hush of borrowed starlight while the little iron pinecone went on falling, patiently, so that a moon could rise.  Odile went down the hundred and nine stairs humming, choosing an anthem for the first grain to cross.
