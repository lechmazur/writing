Three years after thoughts became taxable, Dario Quill had learned to think in beans.  The Levy's meter, clipped behind every citizen's ear, billed each worded thought by the syllable, and the university had dismissed him for arrears no lecturer's salary could cover.  So he took his grin and his grammar elsewhere.  He charmed allotment wardens across the city into giving him plots, joining one community garden after another, and in each he planted sentences.  Marigolds were verbs, pole beans were nouns, and a bare furrow meant not.  A meter could not bill what a person merely noticed from a tram window, because noticing a row of flowers was not yet a thought in words. 

The club that read those rows called itself the Furrow, and it met where no meter worked: the greenhouse of the late entomologist Dr. Adaeze Okafor.  Her cicadas still bred in the root beds, and their drone could warp any meter's needle into useless static.  Among her pinned moths Dario had found a gourd rattle she once shook to call her beetles to feed.  Its seeds became the club's only pronoun.  A gourd seed, planted at the end of a sentence, meant I, the mark of someone willing to own what the garden said.  Each member carried one, and the rule was simple: stand guard over your own spark of defiance, because nobody can plant it for you. 

Dario had written that rule and never once obeyed it.  His seed had ridden in his waistcoat pocket for two years while he winked other people into risk. 

Tonight everything arrived at once.  Across forty gardens the marigolds were opening on schedule, finishing a sentence the whole city could read from its trams: OUR THOUGHTS ARE NOT YOURS TO COUNT.  A heat wave was pressing on the greenhouse until the old panes ticked in their frames.  Okafor's seventeen-year brood had chosen the same evening to climb out of the soil by the thousands.  And Pim, the youngest member, burst through the door with soil to her elbows. 

"Assessors," she gasped.  "They traced the rows here, and nobody has signed the sentence." 

Dario knew what she meant.  Without a gourd seed at its end, the message was only weather, a rumor of flowers that the Levy could dismiss as coincidence and quietly mow.  The final bed, the one just inside the greenhouse door, was his. 

Assessor Brandt entered with two clerks, brushing cicadas from her gray lapels.  Her reader, aimed at Dario's ear, showed a needle swinging like a drunk. 

"Mr. Quill," she said over the rising scream of the hatch, "what exactly are you thinking?" 

He gave her his best smile, the one that had talked wardens out of forty plots.  "I will tell you all of it, madam, and you may write it down.  I am thinking what forty gardens are thinking, and not one word of it is in my head." 

It was the plain truth, which was why it sounded like a riddle. 

Brandt did not smile back.  "Give me the key to your flower code and name its members, and your arrears vanish tonight." 

A pane cracked overhead.  Pim was watching him, and so, he felt, was every member he had ever charmed into a garden at dawn.  For two years he had mistaken hiding his spark for guarding it.  A thing kept in a pocket is not defended, only delayed. 

"There is no key," he said, "only a seed." 

He walked past her through the storm of wings, knelt at the last bed, and pressed the gourd seed into the warm soil with his thumb. 

The hatch reached its full pitch.  Every meter in the room, including the one behind Brandt's own ear, spun and stuck. 

"What did you just do?" she demanded. 

"I signed it," Dario said.  "Bill me for the seed, if you can find its syllables." 

She could not, and they both knew it.  Brandt stood a long moment among the wings, looking through the cracked glass at the marigolds burning down the hill, row after row.  In the static, for the first time in three years, nothing she thought was being counted, and her lips moved as if she were sounding something out.  Then she gathered her clerks and left without writing a line. 

Pim let out a breath.  "They will come back when the brood dies down." 

"They will," Dario agreed, and settled himself on an overturned pot beside the bed.  "So I will be here." 

Around him the cicadas screamed, the glass ticked, and forty gardens finished their sentence, while one charming, tardy scholar kept watch over a single word that was finally his.
