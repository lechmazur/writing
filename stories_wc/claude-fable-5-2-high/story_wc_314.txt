The waterfall had been falling since before anyone named colors, yet every evening it invented them again.  Behind its kaleidoscopic curtain, where broken light slid green and violet over the moss, lay the orchard that no map admitted.  Its apple trees were older than the kingdom downstream, and they were in bud. 

Ilsabet Marrow, auburn hair knotted back with twine, sat at her bench beneath the eldest tree and glared at a column of figures.  Seven years of alchemy had brought her here: six stages finished, sealed, and logged, and one remaining.  At dawn the work would undergo its final transformation, or it would spoil, and everything depended on a single instant.  In that instant she must flame the frozen dew crystal. 

It lay in its nest of cold salt, a bead of ice no bigger than a lentil, gathered from an apple blossom seven winters ago.  Her ledger called it reagent nine, of adequate purity.  Her ledger did not mention that the blossom was the last thing her teacher, Oda, had touched, or that Ilsabet had repacked the salt every night since, by hand, when a weekly check would have served. 

Oda had worked by feel.  She tasted solutions, hummed at crucibles, and was right so often that it had driven her pupil half mad.  "The work finishes when your head and your hands agree," she used to say, and Ilsabet, who trusted only what she could weigh, had spent seven years trying to prove the old woman right by arithmetic. 

The arithmetic refused.  Forty calculations gave forty different moments for the flame, and Oda's own notes offered only a riddle: flame it when the sky turns its page.  Ilsabet suspected, in a place she did not examine, that her sums kept failing because she did not want them to succeed.  To finish the work was to burn the only keepsake she had ever allowed herself. 

Dusk came through the waterfall in bands of amber, and with it came the starlings.  They poured over the cliff's lip in their thousands, as they had every spring since the trees were seeds, and unrolled above the orchard like smoke deciding what to become.  Out of habit, Ilsabet reached for her slate to count them.  She lost the count at once, started over, lost it again, and then, for the first time in years, simply looked. 

The flock swelled, thinned, folded, and gathered.  Dissolve, separate, join, fix: the birds were performing her whole work across the sky, and no bird among them knew the recipe. 

Something brushed the back of her neck, light as a blossom falling.  She knew it from childhood, from the first time she watched salt bloom out of clear water: the touch of wonder, which she had long ago filed under distractions.  Now it steadied her eye instead of clouding it.  She saw that each starling was doing something almost stupidly logical, matching the few neighbors nearest its wing, and that the great shape was only that small rule, felt all at once.  Reasoning and feeling were not rivals, then; they were one motion seen at two sizes.  And before the flock dropped to roost, a dark ripple ran through it from edge to edge, the whole sky turning like a page. 

She worked through the night, but differently.  The starlings turned when the air itself turned, cooling at dusk, warming at dawn, on the very breath that makes and unmakes dew.  She measured the ripple's crossing in heartbeats, set it beside her forty failed answers, and saw them at last as forty neighbors of one true moment, which the flock itself would announce. 

Near dawn she did something her ledger would never record.  She lifted the crystal from its salt, held it for one breath on her bare palm, where it burned with cold, and said Oda's name aloud.  "I kept it because I loved you," she told the eldest tree, "not because it was adequately pure."  The admission weakened nothing; her hands were steadier for it. 

She set the crystal on the crown of the sealed vessel, lit her taper, and waited.  Grey light entered the waterfall and came out the far side in every color it had ever been.  The starlings rose from the ancient boughs with a sound like one long breath, shaking the first buds open as they went.  They swelled, thinned, folded, gathered. 

Ilsabet did not count.  She knew the number and she felt the number, and for once they were the same number.  The dark ripple started at the flock's eastern edge, the sky began to turn its page, and wonder touched the back of her neck like a hand she remembered.  Head and hands agreeing, she brought the flame down.
