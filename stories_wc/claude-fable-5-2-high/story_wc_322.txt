The Gallery of Honest Deceptions closed at six, and by seven Odile Marsh had the last room to herself.  Behind her, the corridor that seemed to lengthen as you walked it sat quietly at its true eleven meters.  Every trick in the building came with a brass plaque explaining it, and visitors gasped anyway.  That was why she had accepted the commission: she trusted only rational enchantment, the kind of wonder that survives its own diagram. 

The catalog called her a fever dream architect, which was accurate and not a metaphor.  She built, to measured scale, the rooms that illness had once shown her.  This one came from the winter she was seven, when scarlet fever turned her bedroom ceiling into a loom and her mother's hand on her forehead into the single line holding her to the world. 

Her mother had been dead since March.  Since March, the memory had begun to drift.  Some mornings the hand was cool and dry; some mornings it was damp, or the left instead of the right, or smelled of beeswax when it should have smelled of leather.  Odile noted each revision in a ledger, calmly, the way one logs a slow leak.  She wanted to know what that touch had actually felt like, what connection was made of, before her own mind finished rewriting it. 

So she strung the room with sinew thread.  Her mother, a saddler, had stitched with it for forty years: split deer tendon, waxed, the color of weak tea.  Odile had inherited nine spools and one useful fact, that sinew goes on damp and tightens as it dries.  For three weeks she had worked by weaving patterns, two veils of thread hung a hand's width apart from floor to ceiling, each strand set at an angle she had computed twice.  Slack, the veils produced only a gray shimmer.  Taut, and seen from a brass disc set in the floor, their overlapping lines would interfere and assemble a thing that was not there. 

The night guard, Teodor, stopped in the doorway with his thermos. 

"Still wet?" he asked. 

"Drying," Odile said.  "The last strands should reach tension at about nine." 

"And then?" 

"And then you will see a hand that does not exist, and I will be able to tell you exactly why." 

He considered that, nodded as if it were a weather report, and stayed. 

They waited under the humming lights.  The sinew ticked faintly as it shrank, like a house settling, and the gray shimmer began to crawl.  At four minutes to nine, Odile stepped onto the disc. 

She had expected the image to surface gradually, like a photograph in its bath.  Instead she watched it erupt: the flat haze buckled, gained depth all at once, and a hand the size of a door pushed out of the air toward her, palm down, fingers slightly spread.  It was realistically imaginary, wrong in no detail and present in none.  It had knuckles, a wedding band, and a pale needle scar across the thumb, all of it made from the gaps between threads. 

Odile had woven no scar.  She searched the memory and found no scar there either, and then, as she looked, she found that there had always been one. 

She stepped aside, and Teodor took her place on the disc. 

"That is my grandmother's hand," he said mildly.  "She burned that thumb on a stove." 

"The eye is given gaps and the mind fills them," Odile said.  "It will be on the plaque."  Her voice was steady, and the explanation was true, and the hand went on reaching for both of them. 

She walked forward until the image dissolved into plain string, and laid her palm against the first veil.  The threads were not soft.  They were waxy and hard and they hummed, each one pulling evenly against the two points that held it, giving a little under her hand and then quietly insisting.  So this was the texture: not skin, not warmth, but tension, a line that existed only while both ends refused to let go.  One end of hers was now anchored in a memory that would not hold still, which meant the line would keep adjusting, tightening and easing with the weather like sinew itself.  That was not a leak.  That was a thing still under load. 

Tomorrow the hand would be cool, or damp, or scarred, or someone else's grandmother's, and she would not write it down. 

"Does it bother you," Teodor asked, "that it is not there?" 

Odile pressed the humming threads once more and felt them press back. 

"It is pulling," she said, "so something is."
