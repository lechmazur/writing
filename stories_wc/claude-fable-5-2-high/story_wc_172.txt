Ilse Varga walked onto the salt flat with a shovel, a radio, and three years of other people's thirst behind her.  Far beyond the mountains, thunder grumbled once and stopped, as if waiting to hear what she would do. 

She had made this drought the way she made everything, with contracts.  She bought the three upstream dams, then the seeding planes that wrung the clouds dry over the far side of the range, and the rivers of the basin shrank to stains.  Then she paid for the water trucks, the clinics, and the school with her name over the door.  She did not think of this as guilt or as generosity; it was a ledger, and she kept it balanced. 

The salt herons had gone extinct forty years ago, and their last nesting island lay somewhere under the brackish lake that used to cover this flat.  No sonar had found it, but her father, the last warden of the lake, used to say the birds nested where the three rivers braided together.  So she dried the rivers and let the mud tell her.  Clay laid down over the old buried channels cracked in long straight seams, while the ground around it shattered into ordinary tiles, and from the air the seams drew the map her surveyors could not.  Three dark lines ran down the dead riverbeds, crossed the white flat, and met. 

"They meet here," said Dessa, her driver, who came from one of the emptied villages.  She scuffed the salt with her boot where the seams converged on a low swelling in the crust.  "My mother says you are a saint, because of the clinic." 

"Your mother's well is dry because of me," Ilse said, and drove the shovel in.  "I closed the rivers to read their beds." 

Dessa stood very still.  "Why would you tell me that?" 

"Because it is true, and because lying to you would cost me nothing, which is a poor reason to do it."  Ilse dug.  "People recover from thirst if someone pays for the trucks, but a bird that is gone leaves only an echo, and echoes fade unless somebody keeps them." 

That was the speech she gave donors about her vault of skins, feathers, and wax cylinder recordings.  She never told them about the brass whistle in her breast pocket, the one her father had filed by hand until it matched the heron's call, or that she had not been able to blow it since his funeral. 

The radio hissed.  "Storm is over the range and too big to seed away," her engineer said.  "The dams can still hold all of it, if you want the basin kept dry." 

"Wait," she said. 

Thunder rolled again, nearer, and then came the lull, a silence so wide she could hear salt ticking as it cooled.  In that silence the shovel rang on something that was not stone.  She knelt and cleared the salt with her fingers.  The egg lay in a cup of petrified reeds, pale green under its crust, whole, and as light as a held breath.  Nothing would ever hatch from it.  She had known that, and had planned a glass case for it, a climate, a plaque.  She had not planned for her hands to shake. 

Before she could reason herself out of it, she took out the whistle and blew.  The call went out across the flat, two falling notes, struck the dry bluffs of the old shoreline, and came back thinner, from every side, like a colony answering. 

Dessa's hand went to her mouth.  "My grandmother used to make that sound," she said.  "At dusk, to call us home." 

Ilse listened to the echo die and understood, with the cold clarity she brought to any bad investment, that she had been collecting the wrong thing.  An echo locked in a vault was only a recording; here it had bluffs to answer it and people who still carried the tune.  But those people could not stay beside a dead lake. 

She set the egg back in its reeds, then, because she was who she was, lifted it out again and wrapped it in her scarf.  The whistle she pressed into Dessa's palm.  "Teach it to the children at my school," she said.  "I am not sorry, so do not ask me to be." 

Then she lifted the radio.  "Open every gate and ground the planes for good," she said.  "Release all of it." 

The thunder returned as they walked to the truck, and behind them the first rain in three years fell into the cracks, slowly erasing the map and filling the place where it had led.  Dessa did not forgive her, but she kept the whistle.
