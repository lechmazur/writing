On the night the clocks fell back, Ines closed her tattoo parlor early and drove to her dead mother's Laboratory of Lost Sounds.  All day she had inked constellations onto strangers, Orion across a shoulder, the Pleiades behind an ear, and all day her hands had shaken.  Nineteen years ago tonight, her baby brother Tomas had stopped breathing while she babysat with headphones on.  The night had given her an extra hour, and she had spent it not listening. 

The laboratory occupied an old observatory on the ridge, its dome rusted open to the sky.  Inside, shelves held her mother's rescued noises on labeled reels: rotary dials, a drowned village's church bell, the last song of an extinct honeyeater.  On the central bench sat the final project, a gray box whose firmware translated baby cries into plain words on a paper strip.  Her mother had trained it on tapes of Tomas. 

Since her mother's funeral in spring, the box had been waking by itself.  Unplugged, wiped, even reflashed, it still threw one thin cry through every speaker in the building, wandering from horn to horn, and printed a single broken syllable.  Ines had come to kill it or to hear it out; she had not decided which.  Either way, she wanted the old dissonance in her chest to finally go quiet. 

Searching for a screwdriver, she pulled a drawer from her mother's antique apothecary cabinet, and its false bottom came loose in her hand.  Beneath lay a bundle of handwritten letters, each addressed to her, none ever mailed.  She read them under the open dome while the first slow meteors crossed overhead without a sound. 

"A ghost is only a signal with no ground," her mother had written.  "His voice drifts through my machines because I never gave it anywhere to rest."  "The firmware keeps two clocks, mine and the one from his last night, and they only agree during the repeated hour, when time folds in on itself."  "Then, and only then, the whole cry can be translated."  "Tether the board to the amber in this drawer." 

The last letter was shorter, the handwriting unsteady.  "I was too much of a coward to do it, because I was afraid of what he would say about me, the mother who left a sick baby with a child."  "I let you carry the blame because I could not lift it."  "Forgive me, or do not, but listen to him." 

Ines sat very still.  For nineteen years she had believed the guilt was hers alone, a single wrong note; now she heard it as two notes, hers and her mother's, grinding against each other. 

The amber lay wrapped in flannel at the back of the drawer, a honey-colored lump the size of a thumb, with a single midge suspended inside.  She remembered it hanging above the crib, and her mother saying it was the oldest recording on earth, one wingbeat held for forty million years.  Tomas used to stare at it while Ines dotted stars on his feet with a washable pen, her first constellations. 

At one o'clock, she wound copper wire around the amber and soldered the other end to the board's bare ground pin.  Her hands, steady now, moved the way they did over skin.  The hour ran out, and then the clocks slid back and the same hour began again. 

The cry came, but this time it did not wander.  It rose from the amber itself, small and whole, and the midge seemed to tremble in its gold.  Ines braced for the word she had imagined every night since she was fifteen: help. 

The paper strip crept out.  MORE STARS, it read.  Then, after a pause: AGAIN. 

The timestamp placed the cry on an afternoon three days before he died, an afternoon when she had heard him and come running, pen in hand.  Beneath it the firmware printed the log of his last night: one folded hour of soft breathing, then nothing, no cry at all.  There had been nothing to hear.  He had not been calling for rescue all these years; he had been asking her to keep drawing. 

Ines wept without noise, the way the meteors fell.  Then she fetched her machine from the car, set the amber on the bench beside her, and inked seven small stars along her own wrist, the pattern she had once dotted on his heel.  She returned her mother's letters to the drawer gently, which was the only forgiveness she had ready. 

When the second hour ended, the box powered down on its own and stayed dark.  Above the open dome, one last meteor drew a pale line across the sky, and the laboratory, for once, had no sound left to save.
