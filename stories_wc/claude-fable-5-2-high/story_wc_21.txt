Three weeks after the letters were found, the airship library Open Stacks hung above the barrier dunes like a held breath, and I had not truly slept since.  I am Imara Dell, civil-rights archivist, keeper of what no county courthouse would shelve, which is why our reading rooms ride a zeppelin.  The letters came from Sheriff Pruitt's attic: eleven notes in Ruth Calloway's hand, each naming a beach where the 1961 wade-ins would gather.  Ruth Calloway, who led those wade-ins, whose portrait hangs in our gondola.  Below us the town split along the old line, half crying informant, half crying forgery, and both demanding that I rule.  I do not rule; I keep. 

Then a hymnal in the Calloway donation gave up a diary sewn into its spine, every page crowded with tiny drawings instead of words.  The moment my bare fingers touched it, the summer of 1961 closed over me like a wave.  It has not let me go.  Every blink since has dropped me on that white sand at noon, voiceless, shadowless, a witness nailed to a past I cannot alter or leave.  The heat there is a brand; I wake in the stacks with my collar soaked and salt on my lips. 

But the trap taught me.  Each noon I watched Ruth walk the tide line, laying driftwood at angles, setting whelk shells in rows, pressing green bottle glass into the wet sand.  Phones were tapped and mail was opened, so she wrote to her swimmers in wreckage, and the sea erased each message by dusk.  The diary's drawings were those arrangements, copied small.  Vision by burning vision I matched stick to syllable, shell to number, and the cipher opened. 

The letters were real, and they were lies: Ruth sent the sheriff's deputies to empty beaches while her people gathered elsewhere.  Elsewhere was the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes, a tidal cavern of black, glass-still water where she taught children barred from every pool to swim.  Yet one entry scorched my hands.  To keep Pruitt believing, she had once told him the truth, and on August ninth his deputies found sixteen-year-old Leon Hask in the surf and broke his spine.  Protector and betrayer, in the same handwriting.  That was the truth I wanted, the kind that lives only under tension, like a rope between two posts, and I would not cut it to please either side. 

So I called a parley aboard the Open Stacks, neutral air above disputed ground.  Delphine Calloway climbed the mooring ladder wanting the letters burned; Wade Pruitt followed, wanting them framed in the courthouse as proof the movement sold its own.  They sat across the long reading table while the envelope hummed with wind and the shelves creaked in their gimbals.  I gave them no verdict.  Instead I emptied a crate I had gathered from the beach below, and arranged driftwood, whelks, and green glass on the oak exactly as Ruth had on the sand.  I taught them the alphabet, then set the diary between them and let them read her themselves. 

Delphine reached the decoy letters first and wept with relief.  Wade reached August ninth and said, almost gently, "So she did give one up."  "To your grandfather," Delphine said, "who broke him."  Neither could strike the other without striking themselves, and in that silence the bargaining began in earnest. 

The last page held no entry, only a map of the dunes and a single shell drawn above a waterline.  We descended together in the cargo basket at low tide and crawled through a slot in the coquina into the lagoon's cold dark.  My lantern slid across water black as volcanic glass, and there, on a ledge the sea had never reached, lay Ruth's final message, undisturbed for sixty years.  Driftwood, whelks, green glass.  Delphine and Wade knelt and spelled it out together, their voices overlapping: "I chose, and Leon paid; hide nothing of me." 

I blinked, braced for the noon glare, and saw only the cavern, the lantern, two living faces.  The summer had released me, because at last someone besides the dead and me knew all of it. 

Back aloft, the terms were set down and signed: letters and diary shelved in one box, displayed side by side, with Leon Hask's name above both.  I catalogued it myself, hands steady, lips still tasting of salt.  The rope holds between its two posts, and the Open Stacks sails on, carrying the weight.
