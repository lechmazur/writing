After the lake froze over, Maren climbed the basalt sanctuary each dusk to cut the moon's rising into stone.  She was the horizon mapper, as her mother had been, and vigilance was the whole of the craft: one notch on one column for every moonrise, eighteen winters of them now, climbing the black hexagons like frost. 

Below her, the Assessors' viaduct crossed the narrows on lodestone piers.  Its pull held the iron-keeled tithe sledges to the rail so that nothing fell and nothing left, and forty yoked ox teams from the lake villages stood on it all winter, hauling grain toward a far shore that never sent anything back. 

Tov found her at the columns with rain still dripping from his hood.  He was sixteen and carried an axe he had no use for. 

"It rained," he said. 

"I heard it."  She had also heard what came after: the viaduct beginning to hum.  Wet lodestone gripped twice as hard.  After rain, a guard in mail could not lift his boots from the span, so the garrison kept to its tower and trusted the magnet to do its watching, since every yoke pin on the viaduct was iron and would not draw until the stone dried. 

"Then tonight," Tov said. 

"You said tonight three times this winter." 

"And three times you said wait."  His knuckles whitened on the axe.  "My father died yoked beside his own oxen, Maren, and you scratch at rocks." 

She did not answer with words.  She took his wrist and laid his hand on the oldest column, where the first notches were gouged deep and crooked, nearly slashes. 

"I cut those the winter the Assessors hanged my mother for burning one sledge," she said.  "I wanted fire too.  Every night I wanted it, I came up here and cut the moon instead, until the wanting had a shape I could use." 

His fingers moved up the stone.  Higher, the notches grew shallow and even, patience sculpted out of impatience one stroke at a time. 

"The marks are a map," she said.  "The moon wanders along the horizon, and once in eighteen winters it takes its lowest road, sliding behind the south ridge so that the cliff's shadow covers the viaduct from dusk to dawn.  This winter it walks that road one night each month.  Three of those nights were dry, with guards on the span.  Tonight is the fourth, and it rained." 

Tov stared at her.  "But the pins will not draw." 

"Iron pins will not."  From her coat she took a peg of bog oak, blackened with soot to pass for iron.  "The drovers have been trading them in since my mother died, one pin each moon, whenever a team came home for shoeing.  The last went in at midwinter." 

The boy looked at the axe in his hand as if someone else had put it there, and set it down against the column. 

They went down at full dark.  The shadow lay exactly where eighteen winters of notches said it would, a black sleeve drawn over the viaduct, while beyond it the ice shone blue under the low moon.  They wore no metal, not a buckle.  The wet span hummed in her teeth, and the oxen stood steaming in their rows, forty teams rolling their eyes at the smell of open ice.  Drovers rose from beneath the sledges where they slept, and no one spoke. 

Maren put her shoulder to the first great neck, softly purposeful, the way she set a chisel, and drew the oak pin.  It slid free like a held breath let go.  The yoke lifted.  The ox shuddered through its whole body, and she felt everything in it that wanted to bolt, and everything in herself that wanted to bolt with it, bellowing, across the lake. 

"Slowly," she breathed, to the animal and to Tov and to herself.  "Running is loud, and walking gets there." 

Tov unyoked the next team with shaking hands, then the next with steadier ones.  Down the line the pins came out, quiet as snowfall, and the herds went down the ramp onto the ice at a walk, the villagers falling in beside them with bundles and children, bound for the passes Maren had mapped beyond the north horizon.  Behind them the sledges stayed where the Assessors' own magnet clamped them, forty loads of grain that no one was left to haul. 

Maren climbed the sanctuary once more before she followed.  Tov came with her and asked for the chisel.  He cut the night's notch himself, shallow and even, and their patient revolution, eighteen winters long, made no sound louder than hooves walking free across the ice.
