The tremors began at dusk, and by moonrise the Halvard Lunar Observatory was humming like a struck bowl above its floor of clouds.  Ilse Marrow climbed the last switchback with her tool roll on her back, seventy-one years old and summoned, as always, when a machine forgot how to hold light.  Dr. Wren Adeyo met her at the dome door, pale under the red lamps. 

"In four hours a fragment of comet strikes the dark limb of the moon," Wren said.  "The dust will leave at escape velocity, climb into sunlight, and shine for maybe ninety seconds before it thins to nothing."  "It never falls back, Ilse, and nobody has ever photographed one." 

The floor shivered, and somewhere a rack of plates chattered like teeth. 

"And you cannot photograph it either," Ilse said, "because your mountain will not sit still." 

Wren led her to the worse news: the drive clock, the brass heart that turned the great telescope in step with the sky.  The first shock had sheared its escapement, and now its single long hand whirled around the dial, a clock hand that would not stop spinning no matter who cursed it. 

Ilse knew the maker's mark without reading it.  Tomas had built this clock the year they married, and she had kept climbing to this dome for the nineteen years since his death partly to hear it tick.  She had become a camera repairer for the same reason other people pray: to harness the ephemeral, to build small dark rooms where a leaving thing might be persuaded to stay. 

She set two fingers on the clock case and counted the hand's revolutions against her own pulse, then against her watch.  "Three turns a second," she said slowly.  "His governor still holds, so the speed is wrong but changelessly constant, and a constant thing can be used." 

"Used for what?" Wren asked. 

"One long exposure will smear with every tremor, so we will not take one," Ilse said.  "We restructure the camera around the broken clock, and let that hand become the shutter." 

For three hours she worked while the foundations shook, pulling the old leaf shutter, cutting a slotted disc from blackened tin, and coupling it by a salvaged shaft to the runaway hand.  Each pass of the slot would give the plate a blink too brief for any tremor to blur, three blinks a second, stacking the same faint light again and again. 

"But the blinks must land in the same place, so the plate stays covered until the rock is truly quiet," she said. 

Wren gestured at the seismograph, whose needle had scribbled itself off the paper.  "Nothing here can tell us that." 

Ilse opened the door to the courtyard, where the founders' ginkgo stood gold in the moonlight, the only tree above the clouds.  "My teacher tuned shutters by ear," she said, "and she taught me that a room is still enough for fine work only when you can hear a leaf land." 

So they waited in the dark, Wren at the eyepiece, Ilse at the plate cover with her eyes closed.  The mountain groaned, the tin disc purred, and the dry ginkgo leaves rattled together like coins. 

"Impact," Wren whispered, and then, cracking, "It's rising, Ilse, it's lit, please." 

Ilse did not move.  The rattling thinned, the groan sank below hearing, and into that hush came a sound smaller than breath: one leaf letting go, turning, touching stone. 

By the sound of a leaf falling she pulled the cover, and Tomas's hand, spinning, fed the plate its blinks of moon.  She counted eleven seconds before the leaves rattled again, and she closed it.  Twice more a leaf fell, and twice more she opened the dark to the light. 

Then Wren said, very quietly, "It's gone." 

In the darkroom the image came up through the developer like something surfacing to say goodbye: the moon's old scarred face, and off its dark edge a pale fan of dust, sharp as frost, already on its way to everywhere.  Wren wept openly. 

Ilse held the wet plate to the safelight and felt the ache she had carried up this mountain for nineteen years turn over and show its bright side.  She had not kept the dust; the dust was gone, as he was gone, past any pull that could call it back.  What she held was only proof that it had been lit on its way out, and that someone had been listening. 

"I can rebuild the escapement tomorrow," Wren offered. 

Ilse looked at the hand, still whirling, faithful to nothing but its own leaving.  "Tomorrow," she agreed, "but let it run tonight." 

Outside, the mountain settled, and one more leaf came down, and she heard it.
