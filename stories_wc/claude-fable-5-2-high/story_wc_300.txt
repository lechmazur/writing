Odile Farrow had inspected doomsday bunkers for nineteen years, and she knew the sound a sealed door makes when nobody means to open it again.  Bunker Nineteen had no such door yet.  It lay at the bottom of the crystal caves under Wren Point, where every footstep came back to her softened, as if the stone were thinking it over.  Tobias Wren, a pearl farmer, had spent eleven years fitting a shelter into the deepest chamber, and he had died in the spring without ever sleeping in it.  His daughter Ines waited at the cave mouth with her arms crossed. 

"Condemn it," she had said.  "I want it filled with concrete before I ever have to see it." 

Odile had only nodded, because she never argued with a client above ground.  Her satchel argued for her.  Besides the two rubber stamps, it held what she took from every shelter she closed: a list of baby names, a packet of unplanted tomato seeds, a wedding invitation addressed and never mailed.  People carried their hopes underground and abandoned them there, and Odile acquired them, one by one, the way other inspectors acquired coughs.  She had started the year her mother bolted the cellar against a war that never arrived and went on living behind the bolt long after.  Odile had grown up talking to a door.  Since then she had wanted one thing from her work, which was not to certify walls but to dissolve the ones that stood between hearts, and each orphaned hope she managed to return was a brick pulled loose. 

Wren's shelter was sound.  The air scrubbers hummed, the cistern was sweet, and the crystal walls stood gray and dull in the glare of her lamp.  There were two cots.  On the second lay a conch shell and, in its lip, a single cultivated pearl. 

She lifted the shell to check for mold and felt scratches under her thumb.  Words had been etched with a needle along the inside of the spiral, winding inward, so that she had to turn the shell like a slow wheel to read them. 

Ines, I told you this place was for the end of the world.  It was for the end of ours, the night you left and I let you.  I seeded this pearl that week.  A cultivated pearl is only a hurt I put inside an oyster on purpose, and the creature wraps it in light because it cannot wrap it in words.  I am the same kind of animal.  I meant to crown the tall stone with it and bring you down to see, but I stood at your gate eleven times and hesitated, and a hesitation can last a man's whole life. 

The spiral narrowed until the letters were small as sand.  The last line read: Put out the lamp. 

Odile looked at the tall stone, a column of crystal rising from the center of the floor, its tip worn into a shallow cup by a century of dripping.  Her hand already held the stamp that said CONDEMNED, because that was what the client had paid for, and it hung a finger's width above the form on the cot. 

She hesitated. 

It lasted one heartbeat, and inside that heartbeat she heard her mother's bolt slide home, and felt the whole weight of her satchel, all those hopes she had gathered too late to hand back.  This one was not late.  Its owner was standing sixty yards up the passage with her arms crossed. 

Odile set the stamp down.  She carried the pearl to the column and crowned it, settling the small moon into the cup the water had made, and then she put out the lamp. 

For a moment there was nothing.  Then the dark began to give back what it had been lent.  The crystals had been drinking her lamp the whole time, as they had drunk Wren's, and now they released it, a pale green breath rising in the walls, the ceiling, the column, until the pearl at its crown gathered every thread of it and shone.  The radiance had been there all along, latent, waiting only for someone to stop glaring at it. 

"Ines," Odile called, and the caves passed the name upward from chamber to chamber, gentler each time.  "Before I stamp anything, there is something here addressed to you." 

Footsteps came down slowly and stopped at the threshold.  Odile held out the shell and said nothing more, and in the green glow she watched it begin to turn in another woman's hands. 

She climbed toward daylight with the form unstamped.  On the line for findings she wrote: Structure sound; walls no longer required.  Her satchel, for once, was no heavier than when she had come.
