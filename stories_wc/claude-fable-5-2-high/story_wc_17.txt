After the climate shifted and the rains moved north to stay, ivy swallowed the city of Harrow faster than anyone could draw it.  Green sealed every window, so no one could tell by looking which buildings were still sound inside.  That was why the Salvage Guild kept corridor echo cartographers, and why Ines Varro walked dead hallways with a pair of clapping boards, charting what came back.  A crisp return meant solid plaster; a smeared one meant rot; and a corridor fully charted was a corridor cleared for stripping. 

Her grandfather Orrin had invented the craft.  His Atlas of Returns covered four hundred buildings and lacked one page: the ivy-veiled passage that joined the two wings of his own house, where the roof glass had fallen years ago and the vines hung down like a second ceiling.  He died with that page blank, and the Guild called it the old man's one failure.  Then they handed the failure to Ines, along with his boards, his name, and a deadline before the dry-month crews rolled out. 

She had the ear for it, everyone said.  It was truer than they knew: Ines was so sensitively perceptive that she could hear a door's absence, the faint give in a return where a rug had once lain, the difference between a room left and a room fled.  As a child she had asked Orrin what walls dream about, and he had said, gravely, that he was still finding out.  She had become a cartographer to discover the answer herself, and had found only ledgers of plaster and rot. 

On her first night in the house, she knelt to light the study fireplace and found it already full.  Diaries, six of them, half-burned, their spines curled like dead leaves; the damp that ruled the new climate had smothered the flames before they finished.  She read what the fire had spared. 

The early pages were all method, but the later ones kept circling the passage.  "Clapped at the west end today," Orrin had written.  "The return came back eleven paces short, the way it did when Maren stood at the east door calling us to supper."  A scorched gap, then: "Walls do not dream of being walls."  And on the last legible page: "If I chart it, they will strip it, so let them say I failed; I am too much a coward to tell the Guild no, and too much a husband to tell them yes." 

Ines sat back on her heels.  The blank page had never been a failure; it was a refusal he had been ashamed of, and now the refusing had fallen to her. 

Pressed inside the sixth diary's back cover, protected by the thick boards, was a film negative strip, four frames, brittle at one corner. 

At dawn she carried it into the passage.  Ivy draped the broken roof and both walls, and the light came through it green and trembling, as if the corridor lay underwater.  She held the strip up to that light and glimpsed the passage as it had been: bare walls gone black, glass roof gone black, and at the east door a woman rendered in reverse, her dark hair blazing white, one hand raised.  A negative, Ines thought, is only an echo made of light: everything returned, everything reversed. 

She walked to the west end, where Orrin must have stood, lifted his boards, and clapped once. 

The return came back eleven paces short. 

She did not flinch from it, as he had; she listened the way only she could, and the shortness opened into detail.  The floor at the east door was worn into a shallow dish by forty years of one woman's waiting feet.  The frame bowed where a shoulder had leaned, and the ivy, rooting in the cracks that leaning had opened, had grown thickest exactly there, a soft green body that swallowed sound.  So this was what walls dream about: not themselves, but the shapes of whoever leaned on them. 

Orrin had heard it and hidden; the question he left her was not how to finish his atlas but whether to carry his silence. 

Ines opened the Atlas of Returns to its blank page and charted the passage truly, every pace, the dish in the floor, the bowed frame, the short return.  In the Guild's condition column, where the choices were sound or rotten, she wrote a third word in ink: occupied.  Then she signed her own name beneath it, not his, because the crews did not strip occupied houses and she meant to make it true. 

She tucked the negative into the atlas, walked the passage to the east door, and stood in the worn place, giving the walls something new to dream.
