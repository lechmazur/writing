Maren baited no hooks on the night the patterns broke themselves.  It came each autumn: tides skipped a beat, geese unraveled their arrows, and the constellations climbed down from their shapes and walked in their sleep.  The children walked with them.  Three nights running, Maren had found her daughter Wren barefoot at the cliff edge, eyes shut, humming half a tune toward the sea.  Tonight Wren slept tied to the bedpost with fishing line, and Maren went up to the sound wave garden to catch the only thing that could keep her there. 

The garden grew on the terraces above the village, where the goddess Ilu had once sung the stars to sleep.  Her lullaby had soaked into the ground, and sound had sprouted from it ever since: hedges of standing hum, bell-vines, reeds that whistled back whatever the wind said.  Somewhere among those rows, people swore, her echo still swam. 

Maren had fished harder waters.  She carried a reel of gut line, a rail spike, and her one odd gift.  The spike came from the dead railway below the cliffs, iron that had carried so much thunder it had gone deaf.  In a garden where every stone sang along, it was the only anchor that would not answer back. 

The gift was this: Maren transliterated emotions.  She did not translate them, did not say what they meant; she only spelled them, feeling by feeling, into the knot-alphabet of her line, the way a scribe copies a tongue she cannot speak.  It kept her cool.  A woman who spells her terror in half hitches has no hands left to shake. 

Overhead the Heron let go of the sky.  Its seven stars came down slack-jointed and blind, and began to zigzag through the humming rows, snapping bell-vines, trailing cold light like wet feathers.  Maren followed, zigzagging when it did, never running, playing out line behind her.  The hedges were breaking too, their steady waves tearing themselves into howls and hiccups, and she stepped through the gaps as she would through surf. 

First she tried the old baits.  She hummed the tune Wren hummed; the garden swallowed it and sang it back a hundredfold, mocking.  She called the goddess by name; the reeds called louder.  No echo could be heard, let alone hooked, in a place that never shut its mouth. 

So Maren did what a fisher does when the water tells her nothing: she read the fish.  She walked close beside the sleepwalking Heron, close enough to feel what poured off it, and her fingers went to work.  Knot, knot, loop, a long bare stretch of line, knot. 

She had expected to spell fear, or grief.  When she ran the line back through her fingers, it spelled neither.  It spelled a breath held in, the lean of a child waiting for the next line of a song. 

The stars were not lost.  They were listening.  Ilu had stopped mid-lullaby, on the rest before the final phrase, and every sleeper she had ever hushed still hung in that pause, walking toward where the ending ought to be.  Wren hummed half a tune because half was all anyone had been given. 

The echo was not hiding in the noise.  It lived in the gap, and a gap must be offered. 

Maren found the node of the garden, the one still spot where the broken waves cancelled, and drove the rail spike into it with a rock.  Deaf iron bit deaf earth.  She made her line fast to it, wound the free end twice around her wrist, and sang the half tune Wren knew, low, with her teeth in it.  Then, at the place where the song ran out, she did not stop.  She kept her mouth open and voiced the silence, shaped the rest with lips and breath and the whole stillness of her body, held it the way she held a slack line over deep water. 

The garden, baffled, had nothing to throw back.  Row by row it hushed. 

Something struck. 

The line went taut enough to cut, and a voice older than tides poured down it, wild and tender, finishing the phrase.  Maren bled a little and did not let go.  She let the echo run, as you let any great fish run, straight down the knots into the spike, and the deaf iron rang. 

The Heron stopped, sighed like a tide going out, and climbed home star by star. 

Maren pulled the spike, still humming, and carried it down the hill.  She cut Wren loose and hammered the iron into the bedpost instead.  Hush, said the spike, with a goddess's teeth in it, and the child slept through.  Above the roof, the stars held their shapes until morning.
