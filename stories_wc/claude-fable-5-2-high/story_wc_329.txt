Ilse played violin on the Ninth Sky-Bridge, where the commuters of Cadence crossed in step with the pistons below.  Her tone was resonantly soft, a sound that carried without pushing, and for eleven years it had carried to nobody.  Tin coins fell into her case on the beat; no one ever broke stride to listen.  Far under the bridge, walled in brass, lay the only thing in the city that did not tick: the Orchard.  The Guild had enchanted it centuries ago to blossom and fruit forever, then sealed it as a specimen, and set living statues to walk its rows.  Ilse had watched those pale wardens from above for years, and she had made a plan the way she made a melody, one stubborn note at a time.  She would uproot a single sapling, carry it up, and plant it where ten thousand people a day would have to walk around something alive.  Beauty, she believed, only needed to get in the way. 

On the night of the boiler festival, when the pistons ran loud, she slid down a maintenance cable with her violin on her back and a trowel in her belt.  The Orchard smelled of rain that had never fallen.  Pears hung gold and perfect and slightly dusty, like ornaments nobody had the heart to take down.  The statues, everyone said, were deaf to footsteps and blind in the dark; they hunted by heartbeat, turning toward any pulse that raced.  So Ilse breathed the way she breathed before a long slow bow, and kept her heart at an adagio. 

She found her sapling at the end of a row, a pear whip no taller than her bow.  Clinging to its bark was a locust shell, amber and weightless, split clean down the back.  She knew what it was: the husk of something that had climbed out of itself and flown.  She did not yet understand it, and everything that followed happened in the gap between knowing and understanding. 

She cupped the shell in one hand and dug with the other, and the trowel struck a stone, and her heart jumped.  Marble footsteps turned. 

The warden that came down the row had a gardener's apron carved at its waist and moss in the folds of its sleeves.  It laid one cold palm flat against her breastbone, reading her.  And through that palm, faint as a clock in another room, Ilse felt something answer: a beat, slow and buried, inside the stone.  The statues had not been made.  They had been gardeners, enchanted along with their trees, sealed as specimens too. 

It tapped a finger on her chest, twice, a question in her own rhythm.  Words would mean nothing to it, so she answered in the only language it kept.  She set the locust shell in its free hand, rested the scroll of her violin against its marble ribs, and began to play its heartbeat back to it, resonantly soft, each note a held pulse.  Then, a little at a time, she quickened it.  She played the rhythm of a thing pushing against its own back, the stutter of a seam giving way, and her own heart rose to carry the tempo so the palm on her chest could feel that she meant it.  Inside the marble, the buried beat hesitated, then followed. 

The warden closed its fingers over the shell without crushing it.  A crack ran down its spine with a sound like ice on a pond, and stopped.  No one stepped out; centuries do not open in a night.  But the statue set the shell on its own shoulder, knelt, slid its stone hands into the soil more gently than any trowel, and lifted the sapling free with every root unbroken. 

Then Ilse understood.  She had come to steal beauty out of a cage, as if it were a coin.  But nothing living is carried out of its shell; it has to be called, in a rhythm it remembers, until it breaks free on its own. 

By spring the sapling stood in an oil drum on the Ninth Sky-Bridge, sooty and crooked, putting out leaves the size of thumbnails.  Most commuters stepped around it without looking.  Ilse no longer played against the pistons; she guessed at the heartbeat of whoever was passing and played that instead, a shade freer than the march.  One morning a boiler clerk missed a step, stopped, and touched a leaf.  It was one clerk, in a city of millions.  But at night, with her hand on the bridge cable, Ilse could feel a slow beat climbing up from the Orchard, a little faster each week, and she tapped her answer back.
