At twilight the fort of Halewick glowed, as it always did, like a lantern nobody was allowed to open.  Its walls were plastered with glow-lime that drank the day and breathed it back at dusk, a pale green shine that lit every face and warmed no soup.  Real fire lived in one place only: the Cradle of Sparks, a bronze basin of founding embers in the keep vault, from which the Commandant sold flame by the taper. 

Maren Tolk, lock sentinel, had guarded that vault for eleven winters.  She was vigilant by trade and by temperament; she knew every hinge in Halewick by its complaint, and she knew, from her own ledger, which families had gone dark in which January.  The ledger was the trouble.  A sentinel who writes down enough cold hearths eventually starts counting something other than keys. 

She could have opened the Cradle herself any night, of course.  But one open door makes one hanged sentinel, and Maren wanted to spark a silent revolution, which is harder to hang: a whole fort that simply knew the combination. 

So in spring she had volunteered, very dutifully, to repaint the courtyard sundial, whose gnomon had gone missing years ago.  She painted six hour lines, every one at the wrong angle, and stopped.  A half-painted sundial that tells no time is an itch, and Halewick scratched: laundresses argued over it, lamp boys laid bets, and the Commandant's clerk inspected it twice and ruled it bad art.  It was meant to intrigue, not to inform, because nobody can be hanged for wondering.  She planned to supply the missing piece at midsummer noon, with the sun high and the Commandant away hunting. 

Then, on an ordinary autumn dusk, every purchased flame in Halewick went out at once.  Tapers, braziers, the kitchen ranges, the Commandant's own chandelier: gone, without wind, without reason.  Only the walls kept glowing, and, behind Maren's lock, the Cradle itself.  The Commandant shouted from a black window that flame would be resold at dawn, at double price, and the whole shivering fort drifted into the courtyard, because the courtyard was where the light was. 

Maren took stock: no sun, no noon, no midsummer, but a crowd, a grievance, and guards who could not see their own boots.  She was calmly inventive in the way of people who mend locks: she did not need the right tool, only a tool.  From her desk she took the bronze quill holder, which had begun life as the spare hub of the vault wheel, six-petaled rose and all.  Everyone in Halewick knew that rose; they had stared at it for years while queuing to buy fire. 

She walked out with her ledger, announcing to no one in particular that regulations required her to record the hour of the outage.  To free her hands, she set the quill holder in the sundial's empty socket.  It fit, which was not luck.  The western wall, fat with a whole afternoon of sun, glowed strongly enough to throw a shadow, and Maren tilted the single quill, as one adjusts a hat, until its thin dark line lay exactly along one painted stroke.  Then she studied her ledger at great length, with her back to the dial. 

The hush behind her changed flavor.  A lamp boy saw it first: the bronze rose in the middle, the six strokes around it, the shadow pointing like a finger.  "That's no clock," he breathed, "that's the wheel."  A laundress pinched him quiet, but her eyes were already walking the dial sunwise from the shadow, stroke to stroke, six wrong angles that were six right stops.  Nobody said anything else, and by full dark everybody knew. 

Maren reclaimed her quill holder and returned to the vault corridor, where she spent the small hours vigilantly inspecting the far wall.  Behind her, at polite intervals, the wheel clicked through its six stops, a pot lid rattled, and the wheel clicked shut again.  She counted forty-one households and wrote down none. 

At dawn the Commandant came down to open his market and found the vault locked, the sentinel at her post, and the Cradle of Sparks brim-full, since an ember shared is not an ember spent.  He also found, through every arrow slit, the chimneys of Halewick smoking like a town with nothing left to buy.  There was no one to arrest; no door had been forced, no word had been passed, and one cannot put a sundial in irons. 

The blackout was never explained.  Some blamed a draft and some a judgment, and Maren Tolk, asked for her professional opinion, said that she only looked after locks.  Then she went out, in honest daylight, to paint the other six hours where they belonged.
