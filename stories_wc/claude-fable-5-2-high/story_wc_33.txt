The cobalt arcade held its breath at twilight, the way it always did before the day's lone mail drop.  Tavin Rusk stood under the ninth arch with his ledger open, the only mailman in the Seam, the disputed strip two governments claimed and neither served.  Blue-glazed tiles ran the length of the old market hall, from the Seam's side to the far side, and halfway down, where the line was drawn, the border hovered.  It was made of drones, forty of them, hanging in the dusk like a row of patient wasps.  Nobody spoke near them.  The hush in the arcade was thin as old glass, and everyone walked as if it might crack. 

Ines sat on the step beside him, nine years old, counting the drones under her breath.  He had agreed to foster her for a month, three years ago, when her mother crossed to find work the night before the line closed.  Her mother had promised a letter every week.  None had come, and Tavin, who had promised the girl they would, had spent three years looking for wherever it was that broken promises went to heal. 

He had looked the only way a mailman could: by linking absences.  His ledger recorded what failed to arrive.  Blank line after blank line, he had noticed that the missing letters all belonged to households whose kin had left that same night, and that censors only steal mail that says something useful.  Then he had found the other absence, the one in the sky.  Each twilight, when the courier drone came through with the sack, the patrol parted to let it pass, and for ninety seconds the middle arch stood unguarded.  Laid side by side, the blanks made a sentence: the people whose words never arrived were waiting at the far end, and their letters had named the hour.  But ninety seconds would not carry anyone down two hundred yards of tile. 

The courier drone came whining out of the dusk.  The patrol slid apart.  Tavin touched his cap to the machine, as he did every evening, because the post deserved courtesy even when its masters did not.  The sack dropped, the drone withdrew, and the line closed behind it like a zipper. 

Inside were eleven letters, a seed catalog, and a parcel stamped BAROMETER, DAMAGED IN TRANSIT, addressed to Ines Varga in care of the mailman.  It hummed against his palms.  Regulations required him to inspect damaged goods, so he did, carefully, in full view of forty lenses.  Under the sawdust lay a stoppered bottle, and inside the bottle a thunderstorm turned over in its sleep, bruise-dark, stitched with small white lightning.  A slip of paper was wound around the neck.  Ninety seconds is too short, it said, in a hand Ines had kept under her pillow for three years.  This is an hour. 

Her mother had pieced together the same blanks from the other side and smuggled weather through the one drone the border trusted.  Tavin's hands shook.  He was not a man who broke rules; he was a man who read them closely.  He wrote the parcel into the ledger, signed the manifest in both governments' columns, and turned to the hovering line. 

"With respect," he told the drones quietly, "the addressee opens her own mail." 

He set the bottle in the girl's lap.  Ines looked at the note, then at him, and did not ask a single question.  She pulled the stopper. 

The hush broke the way glass breaks, all at once.  Cloud poured up through the arches and out over the roofs, thunder rolled down the blue tiles, and rain came in sheets so thick the far end vanished.  Lightning walked along the patrol, and one by one the drones folded their rotors and sank to the floor to wait it out, as their own manuals instructed. 

For a long minute there was only water.  Then shapes came down the arcade from the far end, slow at first, stepping around the grounded machines: the bell-founder's brother, the widow Oster's two sons, strangers carrying children.  Every one of them matched a blank line in his ledger.  Last came a woman with wet hair and a barometer-maker's burned fingers, and Ines was running before Tavin could say her name. 

He stayed where he was, under the ninth arch, and let the rain soak his cap.  He had searched three years for the place where broken promises heal, and it had never been a place.  It was a gap, held open long enough for someone to walk through.  When the thunder thinned, he opened the ledger and, beside each empty line, wrote the same two words in his neatest hand: delivered, late.  The hush that settled afterward was not afraid of breaking.
