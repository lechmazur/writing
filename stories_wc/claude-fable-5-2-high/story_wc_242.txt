Wren Sato worked the last switchboard in the city, bolted to the wall of a subway utility tunnel beneath the Sixth Avenue line.  Her shift ran between the hum of streetlamps: from the dusk buzz when they flickered on overhead to the dawn buzz when they quit.  She heard both through the ventilation grate, and in the hours between, the only people she met were voices on copper.  The city kept forty-one phone booths alive for emergencies, and Wren, their underground operator, patched every call by hand. 

She had chosen the job for its distance.  Estrangement, for Wren, was not an accident but a garden, something she had cultivated for nine years with missed holidays and unanswered letters, ever since her father's funeral.  That day she and her brother Tobias had fought over the glass harmonica their father had built and played every night of their childhood.  Tobias kept the instrument; Wren, out of spite, slid one glass disc off its spindle and walked out with it under her coat.  It was the high G, the note that finished their father's lullaby. 

The disc hung now from a hook above the switchboard, and Wren lived melodically haunted, the lullaby looping through her skull all night and breaking off one note short, as if the tune refused her the ending she had stolen. 

Maybe that was why she had started meddling.  People used booths at night for endings: a last call to a wife, a resignation left on a machine, a goodbye rehearsed on the walk over.  Wren could hear an ending in the way a caller breathed before giving the number.  So sometimes she pulled the plug from the jack they asked for and pushed it into another booth entirely, where someone else stood holding a receiver and a grief.  A widower on Canal Street got a night nurse on Delancey instead of his dead wife's voicemail, and they talked until the lamps hummed off.  They still called each other every Thursday, always asking Wren by name for the wrong number. 

She kept a ledger of these wrong number friendships, thirty-seven so far, and on its cover she had written her whole philosophy: to teach endings how to begin.  She was careful never to read that sentence as if it were addressed to her. 

On the ninth anniversary of the funeral, an hour before dawn, the light for the Mercer Street booth came on. 

"Operator," Wren said. 

The caller breathed like an ending, then recited a number she had not heard in nine years: her father's house, disconnected since the sale. 

"That line is dead, sir," she said, and her own voice sounded strange to her. 

"I know," the man said.  "I call it every year just to hear it not ring, but I am selling his old instrument tomorrow, so this is the last time." 

Then, while he waited, he began to hum, and Wren knew her brother.  It was the lullaby, and it stopped exactly where hers always stopped, one note short, because the instrument he had played it on for nine years had a gap on its spindle.  He was haunted by the same unfinished tune, and she had made the hole in it. 

Her hand went to the plug out of habit.  The Bleecker booth was lit, another sleepless stranger waiting; Wren could route Tobias there, give him a kind voice, add a thirty-eighth entry to the ledger, and stay safely unknown.  That was the trick of her garden: she grew closeness for everyone else so that she would never have to harvest any. 

But the tune sat unfinished in the wire, and the disc hung within reach. 

Instead of patching him outward, she took the cord and looped the Mercer line back into her own station, so the call could go nowhere but to her.  She dipped a finger in her water glass, lifted the disc from its hook, and stroked the rim beside the mouthpiece.  The high G rose thin and silver through the tunnel and down nine years of copper. 

Tobias stopped breathing like an ending. 

"Wren?" he said at last. 

"I think I have something that belongs on your spindle," she said.  "Don't sell it." 

He laughed, or wept; on that old line the two sounded alike. 

"Wrong number," he said. 

"The best kind," Wren answered, and she opened the ledger to a fresh page and, for the first time, wrote her own name on it. 

Above the grate the streetlamps hummed and went dark, and below, he hummed the lullaby again and she gave it its last note, an ending that was only where they began.
