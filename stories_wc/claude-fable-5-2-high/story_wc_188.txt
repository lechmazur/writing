Tamsin Vey came up the goat road at dusk, chanting her recipes in time with her boots.  "Saltpeter seventy-five, charcoal fifteen, sulfur ten, and do not sneeze."  The chant was her measuring tape; each line lasted four strides, and she had walked across three countries without ever losing count. 

Ahead, the fortress of Calder Hold had given up on war and gone to flower.  Jasmine poured from the arrow slits, wisteria hung in ropes off the battlements, and bees went home through the murder holes. 

A girl sat on the drawbridge chain, kicking her heels.  "You sell fireworks?" she asked. 

"I design them," Tamsin said.  "Other people light them." 

That had been true for two winters, since the fever took her brother Ilo, who spun fire on chains and laughed at ceilings.  She drew shells on paper now, sold the drawings, and kept walking before anyone struck a match. 

"Nobody here would look anyway," the girl said.  "The ash clouds came three years ago and stayed.  No stars, so no star festival, and Gran says looking up just makes your neck sad." 

Tamsin knew that weight.  It was the heaviest thing she carried, heavier than the powder kegs on her back, and it pulled straight down. 

She walked into the courtyard anyway, out of habit, chanting to pace the ground as she paced every ground.  "Strontium for red, copper for blue, stand well back or it will shave you." 

She did not study a site with her eyes.  Ilo had taught her to let a place soak in sideways, the way a spinner knows where the flame is without watching it.  So she let the whole dusk pour through her and filtered it.  Jasmine scent drifting east meant a slow west wind.  Petals sticking to the flagstones meant damp, so the fuses would burn lazy.  Swallows skimming under the battlements meant the cloud sat low, perhaps three hundred feet, a gray lid on a stone pot. 

On the seventh line of the chant, her boot came down on something that was not flagstone.  The sound was wrong, a small glassy click her ears caught before her mind did.  She looked down. 

The old courtyard mosaic showed a faded sunburst, but someone had mended its cracked heart with chips of blue bottle glass, and the chips made letters.  The note read: TAM, IF NO STARS, BRING YOUR OWN.  Beneath it sat a small glass circle with a tail, which was how Ilo had always signed, a spinning flame. 

Tamsin sat down hard, powder kegs and all, which she would have scolded anyone else for doing. 

An old woman had come out with the girl, wiping her hands.  "The fire spinner fixed our floor the first spring of the clouds," she said.  "He said his sister would come counting out loud and step right on it.  He left this for her, and made me swear not to wash it." 

She held out a fire spinner's kerosene rag knot, the fist of wound cloth from the end of Ilo's chain, black and stiff and still smelling of him. 

Tamsin laughed, which surprised her, and then cried, which did not.  Then she stood up, because a dare from the dead is still a dare. 

She worked while the last light drained from the blossoms.  She filtered her charcoal twice through silk to cheat the damp, and the girl held the sieve with enormous gravity.  She cut every lift charge short, so the shells would burst under the cloud instead of vanishing inside it.  A lid, she explained, is only a ceiling, and a ceiling can be painted. 

Night came with no stars at all, and the fortress went so quiet that she could hear petals land.  The villagers stood along the walls with their chins down, out of practice. 

In that hush, Tamsin lit the rag knot, and it caught at once, greedy and familiar.  Her hands shook; she had not lit anything of her own in two winters. 

"Stand well back," she chanted, "or it will shave you," and the girl giggled, and Tamsin touched the knot to the fuse. 

The first shell climbed against everything that pulls downward and opened just beneath the cloud, a blue chrysanthemum wider than the keep.  The cloud caught the light and threw it back doubled, and the whole gray lid bloomed like the walls below it.  Along the battlements, one by one, the chins came up. 

Tamsin fired shell after shell until the kegs were light, and so, somehow, was she. 

At dawn she tied the scorched knot to her belt and went down the goat road, chanting a new line.  "If no stars, bring your own."
