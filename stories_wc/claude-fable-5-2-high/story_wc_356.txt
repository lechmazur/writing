The village of Hollowmere hung in the crowns of drowned-root trees, and no one living knew who had tied the first rope.  Its walkways swayed over an estuary, and everything in it moved: baskets, lanterns, sleeping children.  Ilse Marrow, the village herbalist, moved more than any of it.  She meant to be Keeper of Remedies before she turned thirty, and she had a nose to earn it.  She could smell rain an hour off, fever a day early, and a lie before it was finished.  The same nose gave her no rest.  Each gust carried fish smoke, wet rope, somebody's grief, and she flinched from all of it, hurrying from bench to still to bedside as though speed were a wall.  What she wanted most, and wrote most often, was a draught that would hold a person calm while the whole world rocked. 

She wrote it on wrapping paper.  In her family, nobody sent letters; they doodled on the inside of whatever wrapped a gift, so that the paper spoke after the present had gone quiet.  Ilse had saved every sheet, stitched them into a book, and each night she would journalize in the margins beside her mother's inked snails and her grandmother's crooked moons. 

The parcel came up by the ferryman's pulley at slack water, the pause the villagers called the tide's thought.  It was her grandmother's knot on the string, posted from the lowlands before she died in spring.  Inside lay one seed, round and heavy as a plum stone, and Ilse knew it by its faint sour breath: corpse flower.  The plant that bloomed once in years and stank of death for a single night.  For a woman with her nose, it was almost an insult. 

She turned the paper over.  Three doodles: a seed in a hanging basket, a wave drawn at the instant it leans back toward the sea, and a small figure sitting beside an enormous flower, eyes closed, nose lifted.  No words.  Her grandmother had never trusted words to keep. 

Ilse planted it because ambition told her to; a rare bloom might hold a rare medicine, and a rare medicine would make her Keeper.  She hung the basket from her porch beam, where it swung with every footstep in Hollowmere. 

Seven years it swung.  The corm fattened in its rocking bed, untroubled, sending up one green leaf like an umbrella and letting it fall, while Ilse journalized her impatience around the doodled wave until the margins were full.  She noticed, without wanting to, that the thing grew best when she stopped fussing. 

The spathe opened on an autumn evening, at the turning of the tide's thought, just as the still water below made up its mind and began to leave.  The smell struck her like a door.  Rot, old meat, a sickroom in summer; neighbors pulled their shutters, and Ilse was halfway down the walkway, sleeve over her face, before she understood that she was doing what she always did. 

She thought of the small figure with its nose lifted.  She went back, sat down on the swaying boards, and breathed. 

It was terrible for a long while.  Then, because she did not run, her nose began to do its real work, sorting the stench strand by strand the way she sorted a patient's breath.  Under the rot ran something warm, and under the warmth, thin as a thread of creek in a flood, ran a sweetness she knew from jasmine.  It was the same note, she realized; only the depth was different.  Her grandmother had drawn the wave to say so: the wisdom of a thing is its undercurrent, and you feel it only if you stay in the water. 

The porch swayed.  The basket swayed.  Ilse swayed with them, and for the first time in her hurrying life the motion did not feel like something to outrun.  There was no draught to bottle.  The calm she had chased was only this: sitting inside the rocking, nose open, letting everything pass through. 

By morning the flower had folded, and the village smelled of fish smoke and wet rope again, and she did not flinch. 

She did become Keeper, some years on, though by then she walked the rope bridges slowly and sat longest with the patients no one else could bear to be near.  When the flower finally set seed, she wrapped one in brown paper for her sister's restless daughter.  On the inside she drew a basket, a wave, and a small figure with its nose lifted.  Then she sent it down the pulley at slack water and wrote nothing at all.
