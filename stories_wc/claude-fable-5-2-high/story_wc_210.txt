The last wish Paper-Lantern Cove would ever grant went out on the evening tide, a single rice-paper lantern carrying Pell's handwriting past the breakwater.  Maren Sollis, the town's alchemist, had counted the wishes for thirty years the way she counted grains of saltpeter, and she had warned everyone the cove's supply was finite.  They had spent it anyway, wishing themselves into richer cities and gentler marriages, one lantern at a time, until the houses stood empty and the drying racks rotted.  Maren had never floated a lantern of her own.  A wish, she said, was a loan taken out against the world, and somebody else always paid it. 

Pell, her apprentice and the last to leave, had used the final wish on her.  "So you won't be lonely," he said from the mail boat, not meeting her eyes, and then the boat was gone too. 

In the morning the tide delivered his gift to her doorstep: an amber bottle with no opening, warm as a held hand, seamless from base to shoulder to rounded crown.  When she pressed it to her ear she heard the cove as it had been, the fishmonger haggling, children running the boardwalk, her own name called across the water. 

She listened for three days, because she was an alchemist and an untested substance was an insult.  On the third day she had her result: the voices said nothing she did not already remember.  The fishmonger never raised a new price, and no child ever grew.  It was a sealed thing, and a sealed thing could keep her company only by keeping her exactly where she was.  She set the bottle on the windowsill, facing out, and turned to the town that was actually there. 

There was no paper left to plan on; every sheet in the cove had gone to sea as a wish.  So she planned the only way left, in the small lit room at the front of her mind, where she had always held a formula while her hands were busy.  That room held four things at once, never five.  Mulberry bark, wood-ash lye, the old window screen, sun: she walked the shore saying them aloud until they became pulp, then sheets, then the first paper the cove had made since wishing became easier than working. 

When she tried to hold a fifth thing, tallow for the candles, the lye slid out of her head and she ruined a batch.  She stood over the gray slop, furious, and then she began to laugh, because the ruined batch was the answer.  The old cove had failed because wishing let every person get everything alone and owe nothing to a neighbor.  A mind that held only four things was not a flaw to wish away; it was a blueprint. 

That night, pacing the dead boardwalk, she let herself conceive not a repaired town but a different civilization entirely.  No one in it would be asked to carry more than a mind could hold, and so everyone in it would need the person beside them.  Its lanterns would grant nothing; they would light night boats, mark the reefs, and be sold up the coast for coin.  She built it in her head four pieces at a time, setting each handful down in finished work before picking up the next. 

Then she wrote her new paper full, four lines to a lantern, and gave the lanterns to the outgoing tide.  "Paper-Lantern Cove needs a cooper: barrels, hoops, pitch, patience."  "Needs a boatwright," said the next, and "needs a baker," and "no wishes here, only work, and someone to share it." 

For a month nothing came back but gulls.  On the worst evenings the bottle hummed on the sill with her name in it, and she let it hum and went on rendering tallow. 

Then a widowed cooper rowed in from Harrow Point with a lantern's wet skin folded in her pocket, and after her a boy who could caulk, and two sisters who baked.  Maren gave each of them four things to hold and the name of whoever held the next four.  By autumn there was smoke from nine chimneys, and the cooper was arguing with the bakers about prices nobody had ever heard before. 

On the first lantern night of the new cove, Maren carried the amber bottle out to the end of the breakwater and wedged it among the stones.  It could not be opened, so she gave it the one honest job a closed thing can do.  It glowed, steady and warm, showing strangers the way in.  Behind her, somebody she had not known a year ago called her name across the water, and she turned around to answer.
