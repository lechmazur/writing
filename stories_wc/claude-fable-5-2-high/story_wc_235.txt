The grave was a cairn above the treeline, and the slate propped against it said MARA QUILL in my own cramped handwriting.  There were no dates.  I have chronicled lucid dreams for nineteen years, eleven of them from this ranger cabin on Kestrel Ridge, and I have seen stranger things than my own burial.  I have watched glaciers recite poetry and flown through the ribs of a dead moon.  Wonder wears thin when you file it nightly.  Still, the grave undid something, because I could not remember whether I had seen it asleep or on my afternoon patrol. 

When I got back to the cabin, the birch bark scroll had come unstitched.  I write on birch because paper mildews up here and bark does not, and because the trees shed it willingly, sheet after pale sheet, which I sew end to end with sinew into one long record.  Nineteen years of dreams, in order.  Now the sheets lay across the floorboards like shed skins, and I had never dated a single one.  Dreams do not keep calendars, I used to say, rather smugly. 

Neither, it turned out, did I.  I put the kettle on and found it already boiling.  I radioed the valley station to report the morning's snow and was told, gently, that I had retired four winters ago, or would.  A grave without dates is a sentence without a verb; it does not say whether you are behind it or ahead of it. 

My whole trade is distilling chaos into narrative, taking the slurry of a night and boiling it down until it has a spine.  So I knelt among the bark and began to read myself. 

That was when the nostalgia came in.  I had always pictured nostalgia as a house cat, something warm that sat on old photographs.  Up here it had gone feral, living under the porch on scraps, and it came through the door with its hackles raised.  Every sheet I lifted bit me. 

Here was a Mara who could soar on her first breath of sleep, who wrote, "Crossed the whole range before the owl finished its question."  Here was another who needed a running start off the fire tower, and another who only hovered, knee-high, above the woodpile.  I hated them all, a little.  I had not left the ground in a dream for longer than I could now measure, and these women spent the sky like wages. 

But jealousy, I noticed, came in grades.  The hoverer I merely resented; the tower-jumper stung; the one who outflew the owl made my teeth ache.  It was the only instrument I had left, so I used it.  I sorted nineteen years by envy, the sharpest bites nearest the stove, the dull ones trailing toward the door.  It took all night, or several.  The kettle boiled and unboiled. 

Then, restitching, I began to read the seams, the last line of each sheet against the first line of the next, and the order confessed something I had not expected.  They were jealous too.  The owl-racer complained that she used to reach the stars.  The tower-jumper mourned the owl-racer, and the hoverer mourned the tower.  Every Mara on that floor believed the real flying was behind her, and wrote her entry looking over her shoulder.  Nineteen years of wonder, and not one of us had been present for it. 

I laughed, which startled the nostalgia back under the porch. 

So that was the spine of the thing: a chain of women each envying the last, and at the end of the chain one blank sheet and a slate with no dates.  The grave was not a verdict.  It was an unfinished entry in my handwriting, waiting for whoever I was to stop glancing backward long enough to fill it in. 

I banked the stove, lay down on the cot, and went under on purpose, the way I taught myself.  In the dream I stood on the porch in my socks.  I did not think of the owl-racer.  I thought only of cold boards and the smell of snow, and my heels came up, and then the rest of me. 

I did not soar well.  I soared like a woman of my years, lurching, catching my foot on the weathervane, but I cleared the treeline and came down beside the cairn.  With a chip of quartz I scratched one date on the slate, the first of the two: tonight.  The second can wait its turn. 

When I woke, I wrote it all on the blank sheet and stitched it on, and I hope some later Mara reads it and seethes.  I left a saucer of milk under the porch, too.  Feral things remember who feeds them.
