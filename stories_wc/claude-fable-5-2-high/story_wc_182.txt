The time traveler arrived between the cartography aisle and the cookbooks, smelling of dust that had not settled yet.  He was old, and he held out a botanical diary that Ysolde Marrow knew was locked in a case two floors above them.  "Tomorrow you lead a rescue mission after the Hallam twins," he said.  "You search where the books are, and you find no one.  Search where the books are not." 

Verrin's Bookstore occupied the entire mountain, a labyrinth of shelves cut into granite, galleries spiraling from the snowline shop door down to roots no clerk had mapped.  Customers wandered in for almanacs and were sometimes never rung up.  Ysolde was the store's arcane linguist, hired to price books in dead tongues, but for eleven years her real work had been rescue missions.  She had brought back forty-one browsers and failed to bring back nine, including Oren, who had taught her everything except how to lose him.  The old stories said the lost were shelved in the Library of Unlearned Lessons, a room on no floor plan, and Ysolde had wanted to find it since the day Oren's lamp came back without him. 

She did not waste the traveler's warning on questions.  She opened the diary instead.  Founder Verrin had pressed one specimen onto every page, each fern and lichen labeled with the shelf where it grew, for the mountain sprouted green wherever it breathed.  Seven pages held a label and nothing else.  Scholars called them unfinished; Ysolde, who read silences for a living, called them rests.  In every dead language she knew, meaning lived in what the grammar left out, and a composer who writes seven rests means them to be counted. 

So she researched the gaps.  She worked through the night, checking the seven labels against the inventory ledgers, and each one named a shelf with a volume missing, a dark slot the stocktakers had recorded for a century and never filled.  Seven gaps, descending. 

At dawn the Hallam twins were reported missing from Children's Atlases, exactly as promised.  Ysolde packed the way she always did, carefully, because the mountain punished waste: one lamp, oil measured by the hour, a spool of binding thread, a heel of bread, the diary.  She left the rescue party at the stairhead with the thread's end and orders to hold it. 

At the first gap, between two herbals, she held her lamp to the empty slot and felt a draft, and behind the shelf a seam of moss ran downward like a line of notation.  She followed it gap to gap, shelf to shelf, through corridors that existed only because something had been left out of them.  The deeper she went, the more the silence took on structure, a pressure with tempo, as if the granite were performing something vast just below the floor of hearing.  When the oil ran low she trimmed the wick and walked by the faint shine of the moss. 

The seventh gap opened into a cavern of reading desks.  The lost sat there, the twins among them, and Oren, gray now, each bent over a book they could not finish and could not close.  Above the arch, in Old Underspeech, ran the lease Verrin had signed with the mountain: WHAT IS LEFT UNFINISHED SHALL BE KEPT. 

Ysolde read it three times, the way she read everything, hunting the hole.  Underspeech marked every noun as person or thing, and here the marker was absent; Verrin had left a rest in the sentence, and the mountain had filled it with people.  A gap in a contract is an invitation to renegotiate. 

She had no ink, so she pinched soot from the lamp wick, opened the diary to its seventh empty page, and wrote the clause again with the thing-marker in place: the lesson shall be kept, the learner released.  She spoke it aloud in Underspeech, slowly, offering terms: the mountain would keep the abandoned books, and she would catalog them, so that no lesson need be lost twice. 

The silence considered.  Then it resolved, the way a held chord resolves, and every reader looked up from the page. 

They climbed out along her thread, the twins holding Oren's hands.  At the stairhead the traveler was waiting, already thin as a margin.  He looked at the smaller twin, who had his eyes, and smiled like a man excused from a long rehearsal.  "Forty years at that desk," he said, "and the only way out was backward."  Then he was gone, a note that no longer needed to be played. 

Ysolde shelved the diary herself, and beneath her feet the mountain went on with its symphony, unheard, every rest exactly where it belonged.
