Maren Voss walked into Hollin between one rumble of thunder and the next, and the next never came.  The village stood stopped inside that lull.  A woman in the threshing yard held her basket high, and the chaff she had tossed hung above it like a breath of gold that would not finish.  Raindrops stood in the air like glass beads on invisible strings. 

Maren did not run.  She had been exiled from the capital for eleven months, and a chess tutor who has lost her city learns to sit very still in front of strange positions.  Only her hands moved.  They always moved, knotting and unknotting the cord in her coat pocket, and more than one problem had come loose in her fingers before it came loose in her head. 

The stillness had a grain to it, and she followed the grain to a weaver's cottage at the end of the lane.  Inside, an old woman sat frozen at her loom, shuttle lifted, eyes shut.  The tapestry before her showed Hollin itself, every roof and hen, under a storm stitched in slate wool, and it was finished except for the final pass of thread.  This was the hush, then: not an absence of sound but a thing made, woven tight and held. 

Set into the beam of the loom was a disc of platinum the size of a saucer, cut with the channels of a labyrinth, and in its outermost ring sat a single garnet bead.  Maren lifted the platinum labyrinth free and tilted it.  The bead rolled, struck a wall, and somewhere outside one raindrop fell an inch and stopped.  So the bead was the hour, and the maze was the way through it. 

There were a hundred forks, and she had ruined enough with one rash move to distrust guessing.  Instead she knelt beside the loom, and her restless fingers found the tapestry's border.  It was frayed all along its length, red threads and blue, white and black, some long and some short.  Most of it was only moth and age.  But as she worried at the fringe, her thumb kept snagging on tiny knots, and the knotted threads had been cut clean.  Someone had frayed them on purpose. 

She began to winnow, as the woman in the yard had been winnowing, letting the accidental threads fall aside like chaff and keeping the heavy ones that meant something.  Four colors for four directions; long for a full ring, short for a single turn.  It read like a game score, and reading game scores was the one thing no court could take from her.  She tied each move into her cord as she decoded it, forty knots in all, and then she played the line through in her mind. 

It did not go to the center.  It wound inward almost to the heart of the maze, then turned and ran straight out through a gap in the rim, where the bead would drop and be lost.  A sacrifice, then. 

Maren sat back on her heels, and the old shame rose in her as evenly as water.  In the royal final she had watched her pupil Tobin, twelve years old, reach for the wrong knight, and she had coughed twice, the warning she used in lessons, because she could not bear to let him lose.  The arbiters heard.  He was stripped of his title, she was sent beyond the border, and every night since she had replayed that single second, holding it open, refusing to let the knight land. 

She looked at the weaver's shut eyes and knew her.  Here was another woman who had seen the blow coming for something she loved and had stopped the whole board rather than take it.  And here, in the border, was the way out she had left before she wove herself in, for whoever could bear to use it. 

"The storm was never the mistake," Maren said to her, quietly.  "Neither was the boy losing."  "The mistake was thinking we could hold a breath forever and call it mercy." 

Saying it aloud did not undo anything, but it loosened something, the way a knot gives.  She had been wrong, and she had been afraid for a child, and she could live with both. 

Her hands, for once, were steady as she tilted the labyrinth, knot by knot, turn by turn.  The bead ran inward, turned, and fell out through the rim into her palm. 

The thunder arrived. 

Chaff settled, rain struck the roof, the shuttle flew, and the old weaver opened her eyes and wept without surprise.  Maren pressed the garnet into her hand and went out into the weather to begin a letter that started, I should have let you lose.
