The last cable car left the mountaintop in April, and after that the whole town was silent.  Odile Fenn, gilded-leaf stationer, watched it sink into cloud and then went back to her bench.  She had told the neighbors she would follow once the book was finished.  She had not told them the book lacked its last page. 

The town sat inside a labyrinth of bonsai forests, terrace after terrace of knee-high pines, larches, and quince, planted by three centuries of quarrymen with nothing to do in winter.  It was blooming now, azalea and crabapple burning pink along paths no one walked.  Every grove had a brass plaque, and every plaque told a history.  Here the Larch Kings signed their truce; here the Maple Republic fell to moss. 

As a girl, earnestly studious, Odile had copied all two hundred plaques into a notepad and carried it to the archive to check the dates.  The archivist had let her search for a month before he said, gently, that the quarrymen had made up every word.  She had not cried.  She had gone back out with the same notepad, pressed a page to the oldest pine, and rubbed graphite over it until the bark came through.  The histories were invented, but the bark was not; a tree wired in 1790 still wore the texture of 1790. 

That became her life's work: to collect the textures of different times, one rubbing per decade, each transferred to rag paper and laid with gold leaf so the grain would hold the light.  The notepad, battered now, its cover soft as cloth, held forty years of first impressions. 

Thirty gilded pages were bound.  The thirty-first, the present, defeated her.  Nothing had been planted in this decade; people had been busy leaving.  She tried rubbing a doorstep, a shuttered window, the cold cable.  Each one looked like a plaque: a story about silence, not the thing itself.  Silence, she concluded, had no bark. 

She was wrong, and she learned it at dawn, three weeks after the cable car.  She had risen early to size a sheet and switched on the fluorescent lamp above the bench, which still drew power from the little turbine in the quarry stream.  With no kettles, radios, or boots in the street, she heard it for the first time: a faint hum, not steady, rising and sagging like breath.  Her gilder's water glass stood on the open notepad.  The glass happened to refract the lamp's light across the page as one bright thread, and the thread trembled in time with the hum.  Odile held very still.  Then, because she was who she was, she picked up a pencil and followed it. 

She charted the hum for nine dawns, sliding the pad a finger's width each minute, always in the gray half hour before sunlight drowned the thread.  She noted frost, wind, and the hour beside each line, as carefully as she had once checked dates.  No two lines matched.  On cold mornings the trace ran low and tight; after a warm night it swelled, because snowmelt filled the stream and the turbine sang higher.  The silence was not empty.  It was the mountain thawing, written in a hand finer than any quarryman's.  It had grain. 

She transferred the nine lines to the last sheet and laid the gold, breathing each leaf down with the care of someone closing a sleeper's door.  When she burnished it, the page looked like bark from a tree that did not exist yet. 

That thought stayed with her through the binding.  She had spent forty years quietly correcting the quarrymen, and only now, alone among their forests, did she understand them.  They had not been lying.  They had been keeping each other company through the winters, giving small trees large pasts so that a hard place would feel worth staying in. 

On her last morning she carried a crabapple seedling to the empty terrace at the labyrinth's heart and wired its first bend.  She had cut a plaque from an offcut of binder's brass.  She considered the truth, which was that an old woman had planted this alone, and found it accurate and of no use to anyone.  So she engraved one line, her first invented history, and her hand did not shake: Planted the spring the town came back. 

She left the book open in the shop window at its gold last page, with the lamp on above it.  Then she put the battered notepad in her coat, where one blank page remained, and started down the path through the blossoms.  Behind her the lamp hummed on, keeping the record.
