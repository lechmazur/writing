Odile Fenn sold maps of places she had never seen, and for thirty years the shop bell had been her only regular conversation. 

One map was not for sale: a survey of the Corley Creek caves, printed by Tobias Wray, with a small arch he had cut himself to mark a place the legend called the Doorway of Decisions. 

Tobias was a typefounder, and the copper matrix for that arch had chipped when he struck it; he pressed the chip into her palm the night he asked her to close the shop and travel with him. 

"Bring it to the doorway at midsummer, when the cavern blooms, and tell me yes or no," he said. 

"I promise," she said, and then did not go, that year or any other. 

Tobias left alone, and in April his niece wrote that he had died in Lisbon, still setting type. 

The promise had outlived him, and it lingered in the shop like the smell of old paste. 

So on midsummer morning Odile locked the door, pocketed the chip, and drove to Corley Creek with the map on the seat beside her. 

A week of rain had gotten there first. 

The creek ran brown and loud, and the lower entrance the map showed was already a throat full of water, climbing a finger's width up the twig she wedged at its edge while she watched. 

Odile was empirically intuitive, as good dealers become: she had hunches, but she had checked ten thousand of them against paper, ink, and wormholes, and she trusted only the kind that survived. 

Her hunch now concerned the ants. 

A black line of them was climbing the bank away from the creek, carrying pale brood in their jaws, and ants do not move a nursery toward anything that floods. 

She tested it the way she tested a watermark, by looking closer: the ants coming downhill to meet them were dusted yellow with pollen, and nothing was flowering on that drowned gray slope. 

Somewhere above there was dry ground and bloom, and the ants were going home to it. 

She followed them on hands and knees through fern and scree, losing the line twice and finding it again by waiting, until it poured into a fissure that breathed cool, sweet air on her face. 

The fissure was just wide enough for a woman grown thin on solitary suppers. 

Inside, the ants kept to a ledge above a dark channel where the creek was already muttering, and she kept to the ants. 

Then the passage opened, and she stood up into light. 

The cavern roof had fallen in long ago, and through the hole the solstice sun stood nearly overhead, as it did for only a few days each year, and under it the whole floor had gone to flower, yellow and white, loud with bees. 

Water was creeping among the lowest stems. 

Across the bloom, the far wall split into two passages beneath a natural arch, the exact shape of the glyph on the map. 

One passage sloped down toward the sound of the creek, the way Tobias would have walked in; the other climbed toward a second scrap of daylight, and the ants were taking it. 

On a ledge inside the arch, something caught the sun and began to glimmer. 

It was a row of metal type, eleven sorts stood on end like teeth, each cast with the arch, each arch missing the same notch at its crown where the matrix had chipped. 

Eleven midsummers he had come, left a doorway, and waited for her answer. 

Beside the last one lay the matrix itself, green with age, and scratched into the stone were four words: "Either way, go through." 

Odile sat down among the flowers and cried, briefly, because the water did not allow more. 

Then she turned the thought over as she would any hunch, and it held: the question was no longer whether to go with Tobias, but whether to go at all. 

The lower passage led back to the shop, the bell, and the maps of other people's journeys, and it was filling. 

She fitted her chip into the gap in the matrix, and the arch closed, whole in her hand for the first time since he struck it. 

"Yes," she said aloud, thirty years late, and found the promise was kept the moment it was spoken. 

She pocketed the matrix and one notched sort, left ten doorways for the flood, and climbed after the ants. 

The upper passage let her out on a ridge beside their mound, with the whole wet valley unrolled below like a sheet she had only ever sold. 

On Monday a card hung in the shop window, stamped with an arch, its crown inked closed by hand: "Gone through."
