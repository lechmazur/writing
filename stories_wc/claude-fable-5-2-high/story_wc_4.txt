Wren Abelard archived puzzles the way other people kept bees, quietly and with respect for the sting.  Her shelves in the county annex held the subtle kind, riddles that never announced themselves, each one labeled in her small hand: Unsolved, but friendly.  The only puzzle she had never shelved was the dove gray map case her father left behind. 

Tobias Abelard had been staff photographer at the Corliss Thread Mill, and he died in the fire that closed it nine summers ago.  Inside the case, rolled like charts, were forty black-and-white photographs of mill workers, taken on his final shift.  In every frame a single pale thread curved through somebody's hands, entering at one edge and leaving at another.  Wren had studied those arcs for years, and she knew a puzzle when one was looking back at her.  She also knew she could not solve it alone, because she could not name a single face. 

So in April she carried the case to Corliss, past the rusted factory ruins, to the lavender field tucked behind them.  The widows of the mill had planted it the spring after the fire, in one wide ring that nobody could explain.  Wren strung a clothesline around that ring, pinned up the forty photographs in no order at all, and sat down. 

She did not knock on doors.  Corliss had spent nine years not talking about the mill, and a knock would only teach the town to bolt the latch.  Instead she decided to linger, as plainly and patiently as the weather.  She would stay, she told the bees, until the new vines at the foot of the smokestack reached the silent whistle at its top.  The bees raised no objection. 

The vines had barely cleared the first row of bricks when a girl named Posy crept in to stare.  "That is my grandmother's thumb," she announced, pointing at a photograph, and ran home to fetch the rest of her grandmother. 

Old Mrs. Lunt came frowning, found her own young hands, and moved the picture three pins to the left.  "I stood beside Henrik at the winders," she said, "so my thread ought to run into his."  It did; the two arcs met at the edges like a sentence finding its second half.  Henrik had not come home from the fire, and Mrs. Lunt said his name aloud for the first time in nine years.  Wren noticed her shoulders drop an inch, and said nothing, and poured her some tea from the thermos. 

After that, people arrived by twos and threes, pretending they had only come for the lavender.  As the vines climbed past the broken windows, the photographs traveled along the clothesline, traded and argued over and set right.  Each joined arc came with a story, about a lunch-pail feud, or a man who sang to his spindles.  Wren stayed perceptively calm through all of it, watching closely enough to know what each silence was guarding and letting it open when it liked.  She wrote every story down, because an archivist knows a solved puzzle still needs keeping. 

At night, alone with the empty map case, she let herself admit what she wanted.  Her father had been making one great picture out of forty pairs of ordinary hands, and the fire had left it, and her, in pieces.  She wanted to transmute that pain into the masterpiece he had meant, not to cure the hurt but to give it a finished shape. 

By August the vines were a hand's width from the whistle, and the thread ran unbroken around the whole ring, hand to hand to hand.  Except in one place.  Between the first photograph and the fortieth hung a gap exactly one frame wide, and no amount of shuffling would close it. 

The town searched attics for a missing picture, but Wren stood in the gap a long while and understood.  "Nothing is lost," she said, "because this is where the photographer stood, and he could not take his own picture." 

Posy, who had opinions, put a length of garden twine in Wren's hands and an old box camera in her grandmother's.  Wren held the twine in a curve from edge to edge, the way forty neighbors had shown her, and the shutter clicked. 

When the print was pinned in place a week later, the circle closed, one thread passing through the hands of the living and the lost alike.  Nobody cheered; they simply stood inside the ring together, breathing lavender, a little lighter than they had come.  That same morning the vines reached the whistle, and Wren, who had promised the bees, found she wanted to linger anyway.  She labeled the dove gray map case in her small hand: Solved, and still friendly.
