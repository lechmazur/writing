Odile Marsh taught languages that no one had spoken in two thousand years, and she had lately grown greedy for one more.  She wanted to learn the language of old buildings, and the Calloway Arms, where she rented the top floor, had been muttering at her since the day she moved in.  Dead tongues had taught her that a text will confess anything if you interrogate it gently, so she questioned the building the same way, patiently nimble, working her fingers along its seams.  The brass plaque in the lobby thanked Augustus Calloway for giving the house to its tenants in 1931.  But the plaque sat inside the pale ghost of a larger sign, a doorway on the second landing had been bricked shut, and the framed deed beside the mailboxes carried a wax impression far steadier than the signature beneath it.  The grammar was wrong somewhere, and she could not find the verb. 

Then, last autumn, Mr. Abel from the third floor had knocked to borrow a cup of sugar.  He was eighty, thin as a bookmark, and he greeted her like someone resuming a conversation rather than beginning one.  He returned the cup full of smoked tea leaves, she felt obliged to brew them, and by winter the debt had become Thursday tea in the rooftop garden he tended among the chimney pots.  Every week he asked what she had translated, and every week he weighted the napkins against the wind with the same object from his pocket: an antique desk seal, ebony handle, brass face worn soft.  She had never asked about it.  Asking felt unnecessary, the way one does not ask an uncle where he got his hands. 

Tonight their Thursday fell on the peak of the meteor shower, and he had carried up blankets along with the pot.  They sat between the tomato cages and the lavender while thin white lines scratched the dark and vanished. 

"Falling stars," Mr. Abel said, pouring.  "Not one of them is a star, and everybody knows it, and the name does no harm." 

A meteor burned long enough to light the tray, and Odile, on an impulse older than thought, turned the seal over and read the mirrored letters on its face.  Consensus facit legem: consent makes the law.  It was the impression in the lobby wax, and suddenly the whole building parsed. 

She set down her cup and began, gently, to interrogate him. 

"Whose desk did this sit on?" 

"My father's; he was the notary in 3C." 

"Did Augustus Calloway give this house to anyone?" 

"He died owing the bank everything, in the winter of 1931, with forty families under his roof."  Mr. Abel watched the sky as he spoke.  "They met up here, in the snow, and agreed on a kinder history: a deed dated one month earlier, a signature everyone would swear to, my father's seal."  "They walled his rent office shut with the true ledgers inside, and they hung a plaque to thank him." 

"And everyone still knows?" 

"Everyone forgets, which is better, but one person must keep the seal and remember exactly which lie we agreed upon, so that nobody ever improves it."  He smiled at her with that unsettling, comfortable recognition.  "I had a full bag of sugar in my cupboard, Miss Marsh."  "I needed a neighbor who could read, and who would drink tea for most of a year before asking." 

Odile turned the seal in her fingers and thought of her students, who always asked why a dead word meant what it meant.  Her answer never changed: because enough people once agreed that it did, and a language dies only when the last of them stops agreeing.  She had come hunting the language of old buildings as if it were carved in the stone.  It was not in the stone; it was the agreement itself, and beneath her forty households slept inside its one long sentence. 

"I have only ever kept dead languages," she said. 

"Then you know precisely what it costs when nobody is left to keep one." 

Overhead, three meteors fell together, and she understood that she had already decided, perhaps on the day she first heard the stairs mutter.  She closed her hand around the seal, which was warm, as though it had been waiting in a pocket of hers all along. 

"Tell it to me exactly," she said, "word for word, the way they agreed it." 

Mr. Abel refilled her cup and began to recite, and under the falling lights that were not stars, the Calloway Arms settled on its beams with a sound she could, at last, translate.  Someday, she supposed, she would knock on a stranger's door and ask to borrow sugar.
