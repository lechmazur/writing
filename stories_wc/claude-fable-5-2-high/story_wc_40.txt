The last dictionary was printed in March, and by April the presses had been sold for scrap.  The trustees called the language complete, the way one calls a field complete once it is fenced.  Ilse Varnham, who had gathered words for them for forty years, said nothing, which was her habit.  She kept coming to the library anyway, and she kept writing slips.  Now the trustees meant to pulp the slips and lock the lexicon room, and they had granted her one hearing.  She had asked, in a note of nine words, that it be held at dawn. 

So Mr. Pell, the chairman, arrived yawning in the gray, and found her already seated beneath the dark chandelier.  The reading room was all shadow, the shelves standing like a forest that had agreed to hold still.  No tram had run yet, and no door had opened anywhere in the city.  The hush hung over them the way the chandelier did, by one old chain. 

"You may testify, Miss Varnham," Pell said, setting the closure order on the table.  "Briefly, if you can." 

She could be brief; it was speaking at all that cost her.  From her cardigan pocket she took a crystal the size of a plum stone and set it between them.  It had dropped from the chandelier eleven winters ago and landed, unbroken, in her open ledger. 

"It hung ninety years over people whispering," she said.  "It learned to listen, and now it gives sound back, though only in a quiet like this."  "My notes say sonically reflective, because I am too old to write that a stone talks." 

Pell sighed, but he did not reach for his pen. 

She bent toward the crystal and said, "Daybreak."  The word went in whole and came out in pieces, small and bright: break, then day, then breakday.  She laid down two slips, the first recording that echo six years ago. 

"The second is from the following autumn," she said.  "A ferry boy shouting that breakday had come, meaning the clean morning after a storm."  "He had never met my crystal." 

She went on that way, because it was the only way she knew how to go on.  A word, its broken echo, a slip; a word, its broken echo, a slip.  Her testimony had no argument in it, only these fractured sequences laid end to end like stepping stones.  Homesick came back as sick, then home, then sickhome, which returning soldiers now said of houses that no longer fit them.  Dead words, she showed him, came back whole and exact, like coins. 

"Only the living ones crack," she said, "and they crack where they mean to grow next."  "That is the pattern of growth I have been trying to decode, and I have only its beginning." 

It was the longest speech she had made since girlhood, and her hands shook as if she had run. 

Pell looked at the slips, at the small square handwriting, patient as labels on seed packets.  He was not a cruel man, only a tired one who liked things to balance. 

"Coincidence," he said.  "Children mangle words, Miss Varnham, and they always have."  "The dictionary is printed and the money is spent."  He leaned over the crystal, as a man humors a child, and told it plainly, "No more words." 

Ilse did not answer, because the answer was not hers to give.  The hush held, suspended, a breath drawn and not yet spent.  Then the crystal gave his own voice back to him, cracked along its seams.  Words, it said, then more, then no, and at last, very clearly, more words. 

Pell sat back as though the table had spoken his childhood name. 

Ilse felt something in her own chest come back rearranged.  She had gone quiet at nine, when a schoolmaster laughed at a word she had invented, and she had mistaken that break for an ending.  It had only been the place where she meant to grow next. 

"I do not ask you to believe a stone," she said, and her voice did not shake.  "I ask for the room, the slips, and time to listen while it is quiet." 

Outside, the first tram bell rang, and the hush came down gently, like a book set on a shelf.  Dawn reached the high windows and woke the chandelier, which scattered small rainbows across the closure order.  Pell watched them tremble on the unsigned line, then folded the paper into his coat. 

"One year," he said gruffly, "and my granddaughter says glimmerish, if that is of any use to you." 

Ilse Varnham took a clean slip, wrote the word down, and smiled like someone much younger and much older at once.
