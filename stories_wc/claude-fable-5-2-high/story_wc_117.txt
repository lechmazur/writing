Amos Bell drove up Sage Ridge with a ukulele on the passenger seat and three pairs of cardboard eclipse glasses in his shirt pocket.  It was late February, the season Inez Calloway had always called dreaming roots, when the sagebrush slept gray under frost and the whole ridge kept a hush.  Inez no longer called it anything. 

For eight months Amos had visited her each Thursday, and he had learned to work the way water works, softly adaptive, taking the shape of whatever she offered.  If she hummed three notes, he found a chord beneath them.  If she tapped the armrest, he slowed his strumming until it matched her fingers.  He never pushed a song at her, because a pushed song was only noise. 

Piper, her eleven-year-old granddaughter, met him on the porch with her arms crossed.  "She asked me who I was again," Piper said.  "Then she showed me the matchbox, like I was a stranger she felt like bragging to." 

Amos knew the matchbox.  Inside, on a square of cotton, lay a keepsake curl of baby hair, pale copper, tied with blue thread.  Inez carried it in her cardigan pocket and showed it to everyone, saying, "My Piper's, from her first haircut," while the real Piper stood a yard away with the same copper hair grown long. 

"She loves that baby," Piper said, "and she doesn't even see me." 

"She loves you in the only tense she has left," Amos said gently.  "Our job today is to link that tense to this one, and I will not promise it works."  He handed her a pair of the glasses.  "But the moon is about to cross the sun at eleven-forty, and I have never met a brain that could ignore that." 

Piper almost smiled, which for Piper lately was a parade.  Amos had seen that wonder outlasts names and dates, and he wanted to nurture the lifelong kind, in girls of eleven and women of eighty-three alike. 

They walked Inez out to the bench at the ridge's edge, where the sage ran downhill in frosted rows toward the valley.  Amos settled the glasses on her nose and she laughed at the dark.  Then she found the sun, a bright cookie with one bite missing, and went still. 

The bite grew.  The light over Sage Ridge thinned to the color of old tin, the shadows sharpened, and the wind dropped as if someone had closed a door.  A meadowlark quit mid-phrase.  Amos did not play yet.  He waited, the ukulele warm against his ribs, his own heart going faster than he liked. 

"Hush," Inez whispered.  "The roots are dreaming." 

Piper's head turned.  "You used to say that," she said.  "You said if I was quiet I could hear what they wanted to be in spring." 

Inez began to hum, a lullaby with a wandering middle, and now Amos came in under her, two soft strings, bending his tempo to her breath.  When she lost the tune, he lost it with her and let her find it again.  Piper knew the lullaby.  She sang the words her grandmother had dropped, barely louder than the frost. 

Overhead the last bead of sun went out, and the ridge lay in a silver dusk that belonged to no hour.  Amos nodded at Piper, and she slipped the glasses from her grandmother's face.  Inez gasped at the black sun and its white crown, and her hand went to her pocket the way it always did when something was too good to hold alone.  She opened the matchbox. 

"My Piper's," she said, to nobody in particular. 

Piper knelt, pulled a lock of her own hair forward, and held it beside the curl.  In that strange light the two coppers were exactly one color.  Amos kept the lullaby going, quieter, a thread and nothing more. 

Inez looked from the curl to the lock, then up the lock to the face it belonged to.  "Well, look at you," she said slowly.  "You went and got tall while the roots were dreaming." 

Piper laughed and cried at once, and Inez tucked the loose lock behind her granddaughter's ear as the first bead of sunlight broke free. 

"Glasses, ladies," Amos said, and his voice cracked with a joy he was trying to keep at walking pace.  He knew the link might not hold until supper.  He also knew there was no trick in it, only a moon, a tune, a curl of hair, and three people looking up together, which was ordinary magic and therefore the kind that could be made again. 

The meadowlark picked up its phrase where it had left off. 

"Next Thursday," Piper said, wiping her face, "teach me the chords."
