The storm took the power at nine, and the silence it left behind had weight.  Maren Oyelaran stood in the lobby of the Night Haven, her escape room in the old mill, listening to every magnetic lock in the building let go at once.  Outside, the brook that had once turned the millwheel ran quiet and swollen past the door.  She opened only after dark, for people who thought better at night, and tonight nobody had come.  Nobody except the mail. 

Two envelopes lay on the counter in the lantern light.  The first was Varga's franchise contract, due signed by morning, with clause nine underlined: every room in the building must run a proven, licensed design.  That meant the back room, the one she kept empty on purpose, the one where every puzzle she had ever been proud of had started as a mess on the floor. 

The second envelope had no return address and a smeared postmark.  Inside was a single seed the size of a hazelnut, pale and faintly ridged, and a card that said only: Give it room. 

Maren had read about mystery seed mail, unsolicited packets that turned up in strangers' mailboxes, and the standard advice was not to plant them.  But she was notably receptive, a trait her players mistook for patience.  She had built a business on noticing what a stuck team was almost saying, and this envelope was almost saying something. 

The direct question, who sent it, led straight into the smeared postmark and stopped.  So she did what she taught her players to do and worked through orthogonal approaches, turning ninety degrees from the wall instead of pushing on it. 

Not who, then, but what.  She carried the seed to her shelf of prop references, left over from a greenhouse room she had retired three years ago, and found its match in a botanical atlas: Amorphophallus titanum, the corpse flower.  A corpse flower seed needed warmth, wet air, and seven to ten years before it bloomed for a single stinking, magnificent night.  Nobody mailed a decade by accident. 

Not the message, then, but the paper.  She tilted the card against the lantern.  Green stock, rounded corners, and under the handwriting the scraped ghost of her own printing: it was a hint card from the greenhouse room.  Hint cards never left the building.  Except once. 

Only one team had ever failed that room, a retired botanist named Odalys Brandt and her grandson, and they had failed it happily, ignoring the locks to argue about how she might have built them better.  Odalys had asked to keep her unused hint.  "Your best room is the one you have not made yet," she had said at the door.  "Keep somewhere for it to happen." 

Maren had mentioned Varga's offer in last month's newsletter, lightly, as good news.  Someone on the mailing list had read it otherwise. 

She took the lantern to the back room.  It was bare boards, chalk dust, and a window over the water, and beneath the floor the old millrace, dry all summer, had begun to flow again with the storm's runoff.  She knelt and listened to it move under her knees.  The water did not argue with the foundation stones; it met them, turned at right angles, and kept going. 

That was all she had ever asked of her players, and she had not asked it of herself.  She had been treating clause nine as a door with two positions, sign or refuse.  Varga did not hate the empty room; he hated that it earned nothing and promised nothing.  So she would not defend it as empty. 

Back at the counter she uncapped a pen and wrote in the margin: Room four reserved as the proprietor's development lab; all new designs originating there offered first to the franchise.  It gave him a pipeline and gave her a wall he would pay to keep standing.  If he struck the line, she would strike her signature. 

Then she found a clay pot among the greenhouse props, filled it with soil from the bank, and set the seed just under the surface.  She put the pot in the middle of the back room floor, above the sound of the millrace, where the damp rose through the boards.  A puzzle with a ten-year clock, the first thing she had ever built that nobody could rush. 

On the scraped hint card, beneath Give it room, she wrote I did, and addressed a fresh envelope to Odalys Brandt. 

The power stayed out.  Across the hush of a possibility just born, Maren sat on the bare boards and let the brook do the talking.
