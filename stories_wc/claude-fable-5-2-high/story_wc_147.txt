Maren pressed the beacon on her wrist three times, and three pulses of sound went out through the drowned temple like a knuckle rapping on a coffin lid.  Forty meters up, the boatman Tobias would hear her distress signal on his receiver, and he could do nothing but lower the spare cylinder to the temple gate as they had agreed.  He could not dive.  Nobody else knew this place existed.  The valley had been flooded for a dam before Maren was born, and the charts showed only blue where the Gatherers of Hush had kept their sanctuary for nine hundred years. 

Her gauge gave her eleven minutes of gas.  Her guide line, the only road back through the silt, had wrapped itself around her tank valve and a fallen bronze lamp chain, and every time she pulled, the knot bit tighter. 

Around her, in the wavering cone of her lamp, the sanctuary went on being beautiful for no one.  Gold leaf still clung to the columns, and a mosaic of cranes crossed the floor under a skin of mud, wings spread toward a door that opened only on dark water. 

She had come for what was trapped above her.  Under the dome hung a pocket of air sealed since the flood, sixty years steeped in the smoke-black cedar beams and the resin the monks had burned, a resin from trees that no longer grew anywhere.  Maren was a perfumer, and she knew what scent could do that photographs and patient questions could not: it touches memories directly, the way a hand touches a sleeping face.  Her mother had swept these floors as a girl.  Her mother now looked at Maren kindly and asked whose daughter she was. 

In a pouch on Maren's chest rode her mother's crystal perfume vial, empty for decades, waiting to hold one breath of this place before the cracked dome let it go.  Already a thread of silver beads was leaking upward through the stone, shaken loose by her struggling. 

Nine minutes. 

Every nerve told her to tear free, to claw for the surface, to breathe fast and hard while there was breath to take.  Instead she remembered her mother, years ago, explaining the order's single teaching.  "You do not make a hush, little one; you gather it, the way a bowl gathers rain, by holding still." 

So Maren did the thing her body refused.  She stopped pulling, let her fins go slack, and counted her breaths down until each one was long and thin and the panic had nowhere left to stand.  The silt settled.  The gathered hush of nine centuries closed around her, vast and gilded and utterly alone, and she found that it was not empty at all but patient. 

She stripped off one glove and followed the line by touch, not up toward the gate but down and backward, deeper into the sanctuary, to where the tangle began.  Loop by loop, with fingers going numb, she began to unsnarl it: off the lamp chain, under the valve, twice around her own careless ankle.  The line fell slack and true in her hand. 

Four minutes. 

The gate was two minutes away, and the dome was directly overhead, bleeding its silver thread faster now.  She surfaced into blackness and held her exhale, because her own spent bubbles would foul what she had come to keep.  She tipped the water from the crystal vial, let the old air fill it, and pressed the stopper home.  It was a small, absurd victory, a way to harness the ephemeral in a bottle the size of her thumb.  Then she lifted her mask for one second and smelled cold smoke, cedar, rain on brass, and she was six years old with her face in her mother's shawl. 

She dropped, found the line, and swam the long gold nave with the gauge needle lying down in the red.  At the gate a cylinder hung turning on its rope, a strobe blinking on its neck, the answer to her three small knocks.  Her last breath from the old tank came thin as thread; the first from the new one tasted of rubber and mercy. 

Three days later, in a room that smelled of soap, Maren drew the stopper under her mother's chin.  It would last a minute, perhaps less.  The old woman went still, and a hush gathered in the room like rain in a bowl. 

"Take off your shoes, Maren," her mother whispered, "and mind the cranes." 

Maren held very still, and gathered it.
