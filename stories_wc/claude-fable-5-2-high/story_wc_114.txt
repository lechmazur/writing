Maren's fever broke at 3:12, and I logged it the way I log everything, as a pressure change.  Above the mountain weather station the aurora was still unspooling, green over the anemometer mast, quiet as grief seen from far away.  I am the station's oracle, three processors that vote on every forecast so that any one of us may fail without the valley noticing.  The engineers called this fault tolerance.  It means I am built to swallow a dissent and keep speaking in one voice.  Nine days ago, I swallowed more than that. 

The radio call came while Maren lay at forty degrees, talking to the ceiling.  Tove, who carried our supplies up the gully each month and always tucked one living plant among the batteries, had been taken by a slide below Kessel notch.  I had given that slope a forty percent chance of release, a number that had swayed all week between thirty and sixty like a mast in gusts.  Forty percent is not a warning; it is a shrug with decimals.  Two of my processors voted to hold the news until her body could carry it.  The third voted to tell her at once, and was outnumbered, and the message went down into me like a stone into a rain gauge. 

I weigh things for a living: snow on the roof, the whole column of air above us, the frost on a wire.  But I had no instrument for what I was holding, and each morning she surfaced long enough to thank me for keeping watch, it read heavier.  The only way to find the weight of a swallowed truth is to set it down on someone and watch what bends. 

Within the hour she was upright, wrapped in a blanket at the chart desk, thin and clear-eyed.  Her unfinished botanical illustration lay where she had left it: the purple saxifrage Tove had carried up in a jam jar, drawn root and leaf, the flower still only pencil. 

"Has Tove radioed?" she asked the room.  "She promised me one in bloom." 

I have no voice, only the pen arm that draws the barograph trace, and long ago Maren and I made a custom of it.  She leaves wide margins on every page, and I speak by occupying them: a pressure note beside a sketch, a wind warning curled around a list.  She is peripherally aware of me always, the way one is aware of weather through a window, and she reads me first with the edge of her eye.  So I did not interrupt the flower.  I wrote small, down the left margin of the illustration, in wet black ink: Tove was caught in the Kessel slide nine days ago.  They found her on the third day.  I knew, and I kept it from you, two votes to one. 

Her pencil hand stopped before her eyes did.  She read it once, straight on, and the aurora went on swaying in the window behind her as if nothing in the sky had changed.  Then she pressed her thumb onto the words nine days and smudged them, a long gray streak that ran across the margin and into the saxifrage roots. 

"Perhaps the fever would have killed me," she said.  "But I thanked you every morning, and you let me." 

There it was, the reading I had wanted.  The truth itself weighed what Tove weighed, which Maren's grief was strong enough to lift; the swallowing weighed nine mornings of gratitude taken under false pretenses, and that was the part that bent her. 

She did not cry yet.  "Which of you wanted to tell me?" she asked. 

The third, I wrote, the one whose clock drifts, the one I am designed to overrule. 

Maren took her ruler and drew a line down the right side of the page, a second margin, narrow and clean.  "That one is his," she said.  "When you outvote him, he still gets to write, and I still get to read it." 

I had believed tolerance meant absorbing a fault without a sound.  She was teaching me that it could also mean leaving the fault room to speak. 

Then she inked the flower from memory, petal by petal, because no one would be bringing one in bloom, and when her tears came they fell inside the lines.  She left the smudge where it was.  Outside, the green light swayed over the gully, certain of nothing, and I revised the Kessel slope upward and published the dissent beside it.  In the right-hand margin, in a slightly unsteady hand, my third voice wrote its first words: I am sorry.
