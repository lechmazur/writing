At the blue hour, when the sea and the sky traded the same bruised color back and forth, Odalys walked the leeward rim of the orchard with her mother's xylophone mallet in her fist.  The xylophone itself had gone overboard in a gale years ago, bar by bar, and the mallet had been hunting for something to strike ever since.  Around her the orchard rode the open swell: forty pear trees rooted in kelp mulch on a great circle of lashed driftwood, a wreath adrift with no shore to answer to.  She was the leeward tide prophet, which meant she worked the quiet side, where the wind did not shout over what the water was trying to say. 

Tonight the clouds said it first.  High mackerel rows were sliding south against the breeze, and beneath them a lower shelf of gray was curling the opposite way, like two hands wringing a cloth.  Odalys read it the way others read a face: a cross-swell was coming, long and heavy, and it would reach the ring before the last blue drained out of the west. 

Behind her, the Knockers started up again.  Three old cedar logs on the lee arc had worked loose in their lashings, and with every wave they cracked against their neighbors, shaking unripe pears into the sea.  Tobin, the head keeper, stood over them with a hatchet. 

"Cut them free before the swell does it for us," he said.  "We lose three logs and six trees, or we lose the ring." 

Odalys had spent her whole calling naming dangers so the orchard could flinch from them, and she was tired of flinching.  A raft could not steer around the sea; her mother used to say that anything you could not go around, you had better learn to play. 

"Give me until the blue goes," she said, and knelt. 

She struck the first Knocker with the mallet.  It gave back a low, round note that hummed through her knees.  The second sang a fifth above it; the third, shorter, rang higher still.  They were not broken, she realized, only free enough to speak, while every tight-bound log around them could do nothing but take the blow and pass it on whole. 

She pulled a stick of pearwood charcoal from her sash and began to sketch on the pale deck of the nearest log.  A circle for the ring; a long wavering line for each note, drawn as far as she could feel it travel through the wood before it died.  Sketched this way, the resonance showed its shape: where the logs were loose, the lines spread and faded, and where they were rigid, the lines piled up like water behind a dam. 

The ring did not need fewer Knockers.  It needed more, in the right places. 

She looked up at the nearest pear tree, black against the deepening blue, and saw her answer already growing: one trunk forking into three limbs, each limb into three branches, each branch into three twigs.  She drew the same rule onto the circle, generating the pattern the way the tree did, the whole repeated inside each of its parts. 

"Slack every third great log by a hand's width," she called to the keepers.  "Between those, every third spar by a finger, and between those, every third wedge by a hair." 

Tobin stared at the charcoal branching across the wood, then at the clouds wringing overhead, and lowered the hatchet. 

Thirty pairs of hands ran the ring in the failing light, loosening knots while Odalys followed with the mallet, testing each freed log and marking its note in charcoal.  She could not stop moving; the music was half built and the sea was already counting her in. 

The cross-swell arrived as the first star did.  It struck the windward arc, and instead of one great shudder traveling whole around the circle, the blow broke into a run of notes, then smaller runs inside those, a falling scale that scattered itself into shimmer before it ever reached the roots.  The pears trembled on their stems and held.  Wave after wave came through, and the orchard rang like something that had been waiting years to be struck. 

Odalys laid the mallet across the first Knocker like a blade on a shoulder.  "I dub you First Bar," she said, "and I dub that swell the Player, and we are done calling either of you trouble." 

Tobin laughed, a short, surprised sound, and hung his hatchet on a branch. 

The blue went out of the sky, but Odalys stayed on the lee rim, restless still, watching the dark clouds turn for whatever the sea meant to hand her next, the mallet warm and ready in her fist.
