The first page of the Ledger of Bequests said that the orchard on the asteroid Pell now belonged to Odile Maren, and that its last page was not hers to read.  Taped beneath that sentence was a pistachio shell cameo blank: half a shell sanded smooth, its pale skin lying over a violet blush, waiting for a face nobody had carved.  Odile had curated provenance at a museum on Ceres for twenty years, and she had never been handed an object without a past.  Now she had four hundred of them: pistachio trees rooted in black regolith under a blown-glass dome, blossoming white by starlight on a rock so lonely its nearest neighbor was a rumor.  Nobody she knew had died.  Nobody she knew had ever left Ceres. 

Bewildered, she did what curators do and began to inquire.  She wrote to the Probate Office asking who the testator was, and the reply dropped into the dome's mail tray the day before she sent her letter.  "The matter is simply complicated," it read.  "Tend the trees, and kindly leave the last page sealed." 

So she tended, brushing pollen from blossom to blossom because Pell had no bees, and in the evenings she read every page she was permitted.  The entries made her scalp prickle.  A curator of 2412 had received the orchard from a curator of 2530, who had received it from one of 2377, who thanked a benefactor not yet born for the gift.  Every entry mentioned the shell, always blank, always passed along uncarved, like a question nobody felt entitled to answer. 

At the museum, her best attributions had always arrived in the staff lavatory, and Pell's washroom, the only chamber with enough spin to make water fall, kept the tradition.  One night she sat on the tub's rim watching her bathwater leave.  It did not run down; in that lazy gravity it wandered, looping the basin, visiting every side, carrying one stray petal past the drain three times before letting it go.  She had assumed inheritance was a thing that ran downhill, old to young, dead to living.  Here it was handed around, not down, each keeper holding the orchard for whoever needed it next, wherever in time that person happened to stand. 

The thought delighted her, and then it did not.  A thing handed around must still have been set going by someone, and a curator who cannot name the first hand has failed at the only question her profession asks.  The answer sat under green wax, forbidden, a thumb's width from where her reading stopped. 

She lasted eleven days. 

On the twelfth, with the dome so bright with blossom that the stars looked dim, she slid a pruning knife under the seal.  The last page was written in her own hand, older and shakier, and dated thirty-one years ahead.  "Final bequest: I, Odile Maren, leave this orchard to the woman who will plant it."  "Enclosed is my likeness in pistachio shell, so she will know whom the trees are for."  Below the signature was a small oval of old tape, empty, exactly the size of the blank. 

Odile sat down among the roots.  So that was the first hand: her own, reaching backward from the end, and every curator between had only been carrying the orchard toward her so that she could give it its beginning.  She also now knew, to the day, when she would finish, which is not a thing a person should be able to look up. 

A second letter already lay in the mail tray, postmarked next week.  "Everyone reads ahead," wrote the Probate Office.  "You have learned where the book stops, which was never the valuable part." 

She carried the shell and her finest graver into the washroom, meaning to finish the business that night.  The cabinet door swung open, and its mirror caught the wall mirror and showed her what she never saw: her own profile.  It was the wrong face.  The planter was owed the woman who signed that page, the one with thirty-one harvests in her jaw and brow, and that woman did not exist yet.  The second breakthrough arrived with her toothbrush still in her mouth: the shell was blank for the same reason the last page was sealed.  Endings could be known, even inherited, but the face had to be earned between the first and last page, and that stretch could not be read ahead. 

She set the graver down, pressed the wax back over the last page with a warm thumb, and returned the blank to its tape.  Outside, four hundred trees went on blossoming toward a woman who had not planted them yet, and Odile, still uneasy, oddly lit, went out to help.
