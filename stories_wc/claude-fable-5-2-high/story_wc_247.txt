By the time the glowing tide returned to Vesper Quay, nobody in the harbor remembered Odile Marsh.  For forty years she had stood at the shoulder of three harbor masters, whispering what the tides meant and what the dockworkers would not say aloud.  She had never raised her voice, and she had never needed to; people leaned toward her the way boats lean toward a mooring.  Half her work had been done with her hands, because every net mender in the old guild was deaf, and Odile carried their sign language to the master's ear and his answers back to their eyes.  That was how she met Tomas, who mended nets by day and photographed the water by night. 

Now the harbor office ran on printed forms, the guild had scattered, and Tomas had been dead two winters.  What remained was his camera, a brass-cornered folding thing that had held no film since the chemist's shop closed.  He had gone on using it anyway.  The looking is the picture, he would sign, and then he would press the shutter against her palm so she could feel what he felt: a small, exact beat. 

Once, on this same quay, he had asked her what music was.  She had fumbled, her clever hands gone stupid, and promised that someday she would translate his silence into music instead of the other way around.  She was keeping that promise late, which was the sorrow of it, and she was keeping it, which was the joy. 

Every summer the tide carried in a living glow that slept invisible in still water and flared blue wherever it was disturbed.  So for thirty nights Odile had walked the moonlit harbor with the empty camera, mapping silence.  Through the viewfinder she hunted the places where nothing moved: the lee of the breakwater, the slack behind the ferry pilings, the black glass under the crane.  Each time she found one she clicked the shutter, keeping nothing, and inked the spot on an old tide chart with the hour it lay quietest.  The finished chart looked like a score written entirely in rests. 

Tonight the moon was full, the water lay bright as poured milk at the harbor mouth and dark in every place she had marked, and she untied Tomas's old skiff.  A lantern swung down the quay. 

"Harbor's closed after curfew, ma'am," said the night watchman, a young man with a small, sleepy daughter holding his coat.  He did not know her name, though she had whispered his grandfather out of a smuggling charge. 

Odile did not argue.  She spoke so softly that he had to step close, then closer, and by the time she finished explaining he was holding her mooring line and could not have said why. 

"Ten minutes," he said.  "Pia can't hear the curfew bell anyway." 

Odile looked at the child, and the child looked at Odile's hands.  Watch the water, Odile signed, and the girl woke all at once. 

She rowed to the first silence on her chart and stopped, letting the skiff's wake die until the black glass healed.  Then she leaned over the side and began to disrupt it.  She signed into the water itself, both arms plunged to the elbow, and every shape her hands made bloomed blue and hung there, glowing, before it faded.  Tomas, she wrote in light.  Listen. 

She rowed from rest to rest in the order the chart gave her, breaking each stillness in its turn, so that the dark harbor answered in slow blue phrases, one silence after another turned inside out.  Between phrases she pressed the shutter of the empty camera, a small, exact beat to keep time by, the way he had taught her.  It was not sound.  It was music all the same, a song made of everything the harbor had not been saying, and it was for a man who could not come to see it.  Odile wept and laughed in the same breath and did not try to separate them. 

When the last glow sank, she looked back at the quay.  The watchman stood with his lantern forgotten at his feet, and Pia was kneeling at the edge of the slip, both hands in the water, signing something back in blue.  Again, the child was saying. 

Odile rowed in and put the camera into the girl's wet hands.  There is nothing inside it, she signed, so nothing you see with it can be used up. 

Pia raised the viewfinder, found the old woman standing in the moonlight, and pressed the shutter.  No picture was taken, and Odile, for the first time in two winters, was seen.
