Three weeks after the phoenix rose from Halvard's furnace, half of the town of Sell was still asleep.  Not the people, mostly, but their things: bells, looms, recipes, the knack of whistling.  The bird's rising had scorched a drowse into whatever it passed over, and only the ember it left in the glassblower's courtyard could awaken anything again.  The ember kept accounts.  For each thing it woke, it put something else of yours to sleep.  So Sell bartered, and bartered shrewdly: people traded their stammers to wake their singing, their tempers to wake their looms, and the town grew smoother and quieter by the day. 

Osk the painter came to the courtyard last, with a concertina reed tongue in his shirt pocket, a sliver of brass shorter than his thumb.  The courtyard was walled in soot-streaked brick and crowded with cooling racks, cullet barrels, and several dozen potted plants standing on the flagstones like pieces in an unfinished game.  Halvard worked the furnace, but everyone knew his daughter Pim set the terms.  She was nine, and she had traded her voice on the first morning to wake her father's lungs, and she had spoken in plants ever since. 

She dragged a pot of tight-furled morning glory to Osk's feet, which meant: what do you want woken? 

Osk held out the reed.  "It came from my wife's concertina," he said.  "It always sounded a quarter-tone flat, and Brine built every tune she owned around that one sour note."  "She is two winters dead, and since the phoenix the reed will not speak at all."  "The instrument maker offered me a new one, perfectly in tune, and I would rather have silence." 

Pim studied him, then fetched a pot of poppies and set it down beside an empty one: and what will you put to sleep? 

Osk had rehearsed this.  "My hands," he said.  "I am a poor painter anyway; I cannot tell red from green, and my apples come out the color of the leaves, and Sell has laughed at them for thirty years." 

Pim laid the poppy pot on its side, which needed no translating. 

Then she grew busy, and Osk, who had learned long ago to read the world by shape and shadow instead of hue, watched her build a sentence.  She lined up six clipped box shrubs, round and identical, and at the end of the row she placed a geranium that had grown crooked toward the furnace light, one stem thrown out like an elbow.  She pointed at the box shrubs, then out the gate at the smooth, quiet town.  She pointed at the geranium, then at the reed, then at Osk. 

"You have watched them all trade away their crooked parts," he said slowly, "and you will not let me do the same." 

She nodded, pleased, and righted the poppies.  Then, with the look of a child palming a sweet, she snapped off the geranium's single bloom and tucked it among the poppy leaves.  To Osk it vanished at once, dun into dun, and he felt the old ache of hunting for a thing everyone else could simply see.  Pim tapped his chest, exactly where the ache sat, and then tapped the poppies. 

"The wanting," Osk said.  "Not my hands, and not my eyes, which never held red to begin with, but thirty years of wishing they did."  He laughed, startled.  "The ember counts by weight, doesn't it, and that is the heaviest thing I own." 

Pim shrugged with enormous innocence, as if she had not been herding him toward it since he walked through the gate. 

Halvard gathered molten glass on his pipe, held it over the phoenix ember until it glowed, and nodded to Osk.  Osk fitted the reed against the mouthpiece, and the glassblower breathed through brass into fire.  The bubble swelled, and the reed woke with a buzz like a wasp in a jar, a quarter-tone flat and unmistakably Brine's.  In the same breath something in Osk's chest lay down and closed its eyes. 

He looked around the courtyard.  The geraniums and their leaves were the same warm dun they had always been, and for the first time he was not looking past it for anything.  It was simply the color of the world, and it was his, and it was good enough to paint. 

He unpacked his box right there on the flagstones.  Pim watched the first wrong-colored strokes go down, then dragged the crooked geranium out of its row and set it alone in the center of the courtyard, where the light was best.  Osk understood her perfectly, and painted it leaning.
