Maro Teel, lore keeper of the Ninth Terrace, reached the waterfall an hour past midnight, when the valley pumps rested and the torrent thinned to a curtain.  It fell through shards of the old sky-mirrors lodged in the cliff, so even moonlight broke into turning wheels of violet, amber, and green.  Her grandmother had called it the kaleidoscope and sworn an orchard grew behind it.  Maro had until sunrise to prove it. 

At first light the three Houses would send surveyors with solvent cannons to strip every wall in the gorge, each hoping to uncover the stairway of ascension and, at its top, the throne that had stood unclaimed since the founders landed.  Maro wanted the stairway too, but not to rule.  A keeper's hunger was simpler and worse: she needed to know why three centuries of clever, greedy people had left a throne empty. 

She opened the dented lozenge tin that every keeper before her had carried.  Inside lay the ruby flake, a sliver of synthetic gem no bigger than a thumbnail, its back still crusted with paint in stripes: white over gray over gold over red.  Four coats meant four ages had painted over one another, and somewhere a wall was missing this piece. 

Her grandmother had raised her to be wisely adventurous, with a rule borrowed from grafting: cut deep, but only where the tree can heal.  So Maro clipped a line to a rusted pylon, counted the gaps in the water's pulse, tested each slick stone with her staff, and then, with a whoop that surprised her, sallied through the colors. 

The orchard waited on the other side like a kept promise.  Apple trees from Earth stock had gone feral in rising rows, and among them drifted pollinator drones older than any House, still humming the jingle of the company that built them.  Maro knew the tune from a cracked recording of her grandmother singing it, and for a moment the future felt like something she was remembering. 

At the orchard's foot stood a pump house, whitewashed and blank.  The surveyors would have blasted it bare in a minute and learned nothing.  Maro lit her field kettle, softened the wall with steam, and slid a palette knife under the white, lifting it in sheets while her recorder watched. 

Beneath the white, a gray mural showed the ration years: thin figures queuing at a staircase that ended in fog.  Beneath the gray, a gilded one showed the first Houses, each painting its own crest above a throne, each crest scratched out by the next.  The sky beyond the falls was already paling when the gold came away and the red appeared. 

Here the founders themselves had painted, in the plain bright style of people too busy to lie.  They were planting trees up a hillside, one sapling to a step, and at the summit sat a stone chair with no one in it.  A woman in the foreground held up an apple, and in the apple was a thumbnail-sized hollow. 

Maro's hands shook as she pressed the ruby flake home.  It clicked, drank the light of her lamp, and threw a single red thread up the hillside, touching the root of every tree in the oldest row.  She scraped at the first root with her boot and struck cut stone.  The stairway of ascension had never been hidden; it had been planted, and three hundred autumns had buried it in leaves. 

She climbed, checking each riser before trusting it, while the drones hummed their dead company's song around her.  At the top, above the mist, the throne was only a weathered bench facing the valley.  Words were cut into its back: SIT WHEN YOU ARE TIRED FROM TENDING, THEN RISE, AND LEAVE IT EMPTY FOR THE NEXT. 

Maro laughed until her eyes stung.  The founders had built a seat that could be used by anyone and owned by no one, and every age since had mistaken that for a vacancy. 

She was tired, so she sat, and watched the rim of the sun find the terraces where her neighbors were waking.  Then she rose, because that was the instruction, and because a keeper's work is not finished until the lore is given away.  She sent the recorder's whole night, every peeled layer and the red thread climbing, to the public screens of all nine terraces. 

Below, the pumps woke and the kaleidoscope began to thicken, and through it she could see the surveyors arriving with their cannons.  Maro pocketed the empty tin, shouldered her kettle, and sallied down the stairs to meet them, armed with a story the whole valley already knew.
