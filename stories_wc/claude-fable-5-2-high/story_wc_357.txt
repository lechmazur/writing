On Pomona Drift, the orchards grew on terraces beneath floating rock arches, and Ines Varro flew between them in a patched skiff called Windfall.  She was the pilot other pilots called when a graft needed delivering through a storm, because she had never once turned back.  But her own grove on the Eastern Shelf was dying, gray rot creeping tree by tree, while every other orchard on the planet shrugged off blight as if it were dew.  Something kept those orchards mending, and Ines meant to find it.  Her grandmother had only ever said that resilience came down with the water after the Turning. 

The Turning came once a season: for one hour gravity inverted, the canals let go of their beds, and every sound on the planet went away.  Sensible growers strapped themselves under their roof beams and waited.  Ines strapped herself into Windfall and waited at the edge of her dying grove with the engine warm. 

It began as it always did, with her stomach rising.  Then the canal beside her peeled upward in one long silver ribbon, and the hiss of leaves, the tick of the engine, even her own breath were plucked out of the air.  The silence was not still; it spun, a slow whirl she felt in her teeth, as if all that missing noise were draining toward some distant hole.  Her compass twirled uselessly and the horizon gauge showed nothing but sky.  So she did what no pilot had tried: she steered by the water. 

The lifted canals did not scatter.  They flowed, ribbon joining ribbon into braided rivers that slid between the arches, all bending the same way, and Ines flew Windfall close beside the current, reading its ripples like a road.  Where the water quickened, she opened the throttle; where it eddied around a tumbling boulder, she banked wide.  Forty minutes into the hour, the rivers converged on the Great Span, the oldest arch on the planet, and poured upward through its cracks into the hollow heart.  Ines tipped Windfall on its side and followed. 

Inside, sound came back all at once.  The hollow was a gathering of echoes: wind from a thousand terraces, ladders creaking, a child laughing, her own engine ticking as it had ticked at the grove, every noise the Turning had stolen circling the dark like a flock.  At the center of their circling, lodged in the stone, something small rang with all of it.  She drifted close and discovered a broken arrow tip, a sliver of green-black metal snapped from its shaft, the kind the first planters had fired to string their tether lines between the arches.  Around it the bare rock was furred with moss, and a seed spilled from her cargo net sprouted, leafed, and budded against the wall while she watched.  The tip sped growth wherever its trembling reached, and here the gathered echoes shook it like a bell.  The rivers streamed past it, took the tremor into themselves, and pooled against the ceiling, waiting for gravity to send them home. 

So that was what came down with the water. 

Her hand closed around the tip before she had quite decided to steal it.  One pull, and the Eastern Shelf would bloom forever.  Then, among the echoes, she heard her grandmother humming at the pruning, a sound eleven years gone and still circling here, still feeding strangers' trees.  The tip alone was only a splinter; it was everyone's noise that rang it, and it belonged to everyone's water. 

She let go and looked instead for her own stream.  She found it at the eastmost crack, where a slab of fallen arch had wedged itself; the ribbon from the Eastern Shelf broke against it and sprayed away into open sky, never touching the tip.  Her grove had not been cursed, only cut off. 

Her wrist clock showed nine minutes left.  She hooked Windfall's tow cable around the slab, pointed the nose at the dark, and gave the old engine everything.  The cable sang, the skiff shuddered, and the echoes took the scream of the engine and added it to their flock.  With four minutes left the slab ground free and tumbled off, and the thin eastern stream came curling through the gap, slid past the ringing tip, and joined the rest. 

Ines flew out alone into the whirling silence and raced the hour home.  She was not halfway home when weight returned, sound returned, and the eastern canal came pouring back down its bed beneath her, humming faintly.  By the time she landed, the gray branches had put out green.  Resilience, she told the other pilots afterward, was never a thing to find and keep; it was a channel to keep open.
