The fellers would come at dawn, and until then the great ash held its breath.  Three centuries ago a scholars' guild had hollowed its trunk into a library, tier above tier of shelves carved from living wood; now rot had reached the heartwood, the books were carted away, and a condemnation notice was nailed to the door.  Ilse Varn sat on the reading floor with one lantern and did not move.  Above her, climbing the curved walls, was eleven years of work. 

She was a portrait artist who could not recognize a face.  Features reached her as separate facts, a lash, a crease, the grain of a lip, and she painted each fact so exactly that her sitters wept at their likenesses while she saw only a careful inventory.  The sky was the one face she had ever known on sight.  So when the guild asked what she wanted for painting its masters, she asked for the walls, to mirror the sky inside them, where it could not walk away and become a stranger. 

Her sky was fractally precise.  Every cloud broke into smaller clouds, every edge into finer edges, each wisp carrying its own weather, and that was the trouble: the precision had no floor.  In eleven years she had finished a third of one afternoon.  Tonight she had meant to add a last cloud, and her brush had stayed dry. 

Instead she turned over the coin old Tobias, the last archivist, had pressed into her palm at the door.  It was a founding piece, centuries old, the founder's profile worn nearly smooth.  "Everyone studies her face," he had said, "and no one has ever learned anything from it." 

A face told Ilse nothing, so she did what she always did and read the rest.  Tilted against the lantern, the rim showed faint indentations, too regular for wear: seven shallow dots, a gap, three dots, and a single notch shaped like a leaf.  She had lived among these shelves long enough to know how the guild catalogued, by tier and by alcove. 

She climbed the spiral stair slowly, because the tree creaked when hurried.  On the seventh tier, in the third alcove, one book remained on the stripped shelf, a leaf stamped on its spine.  The carters had left it because it would not come free; it was pegged to the wood.  Ilse pressed the leaf, and the cover lifted like a lid. 

The pages had been cut into a secret safe, and in the hollow lay a ground glass lens in a brass ring and a folded note in a hand three hundred years dry.  We too tried to copy the heavens onto these walls, it read.  We found the limit of hands, and it is nearer than pride admits.  Behind this book is a knot we plugged in our shame; whoever reaches their own limit may open it. 

Ilse read it twice.  She thought of eleven years and a third of an afternoon, and something in her chest, clenched for a long time, set down its weight. 

Then she began to craft, as carefully as she had ever painted.  She pried the book from its pegs and worked the old plug out of the knot with her palette knife, and a thread of cold air entered the tree.  She shaped a collar from softened candle wax and a strip torn from her painting rag, seated the lens, and pared the wax until the glass sat true.  Then she went down, sat where she had started, and put out the lantern. 

Darkness, and the hush that comes before an ending; even the rot seemed to pause. 

Outside, dawn arrived.  Inside, it arrived too, upside down and whole, the real sky pouring through the knot and spreading across the far wall, over her painted clouds, pink going to gold, moving.  It was not precise.  It was soft at every edge, and it was the sky entire, the face she knew, inside walls at last. 

She understood that she had never needed to hold every part of a thing in order to greet it.  Her limit was not a wall but a window, and the guild had left it there for whoever was honest enough to arrive. 

She watched until boots sounded on the roots and a man called her name.  She climbed once more, freed the lens, and laid the coin in the empty safe for the tree to keep.  At the door the foreman's face was, as all faces were, a scatter of facts.  For once she did not try to count them.  She let him be weather, nodded, and carried the lens out under the wide original, already planning the next dark room.
