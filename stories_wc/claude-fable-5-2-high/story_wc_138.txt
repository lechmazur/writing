Vesna had been bored for roughly nine thousand years, and tonight the sun was finally doing something about it.  Far above the glacier, a solar storm leaned on the planet, and the sky answered in green curtains and a thunder too low to hear, the kind that settled in the teeth.  She felt it from inside her escondite, the hiding place she had carved in the ice and hung with a thousand hand-cut prisms. 

The prisms were not decoration.  Light bends where space leans, and Vesna, after so many centuries, had grown dimensionally aware the way a prisoner comes to know masonry.  She could feel height, width, and depth standing around her like three tired guards, and beyond them a fourth direction, thin as a draft under a door.  Everyone she had ever loved had gone out through that door.  The guards had never once let her follow. 

The reason hung at her throat: half of a lodestone amulet, dark and cold, its broken edge worn smooth by her thumb.  Her mother had bought it whole from a healer during a plague winter, a charm to hold one soul in one body through one fever.  But there were two sick daughters, so she snapped it in half.  Ilka's half failed by morning.  Vesna's half, cursed by the breaking, forgot how to stop.  It commanded the space around her to keep her, and space, dutiful, had kept her through spears, floods, and one very thorough guillotine.  She had thrown the fragment into volcanoes and oceans; the three directions simply folded, and it was at her throat again. 

Ilka had whispered goodbye that morning, and what came after it was the one chapter Vesna had never been allowed to read.  She had read everything else, most of it twice, and it bored her. 

So she had turned conspirator.  The amulet gave its orders through magnetic fields, a fact she had learned by watching compass needles shiver beside every deathbed she attended.  Whatever could carry an order could carry a rumor.  For three centuries, here beneath the auroral ring where the field lines plunge into the earth, she had intrigued against her own jailer, stroking a bar magnet along the ice in patient patterns.  To height she whispered that it was tired of standing.  To width she suggested that depth was the favorite.  To depth she promised nothing, which depth respected.  The prisms told her how the plot was going, their rainbows drifting a hair further off true each decade. 

All the mutiny lacked was a night when the captain could not shout.  Tonight the storm drowned every field on Earth; overhead, satellites went dumb, and at her throat the fragment's voice broke into static.  Vesna laid the magnet down and spoke one word along the ice. 

"Now." 

Height went first.  The ceiling did not rise so much as resign, and the prisms swung on threads suddenly miles long.  Width sulked sideways, and the walls forgot which of them was near.  Depth simply turned its back.  There was no crash, only that buried thunder, and colors pouring through the prisms that no eye had ever needed names for.  The draft under the door became a doorway. 

Vesna stepped toward it, and the cord bit into her neck.  The fragment hung behind her in the air, fixed like a nail, the last loyal thing in a deserted room.  The mutineers would no longer fetch it back to her; she understood that she could go, but it could not come. 

She closed her hand around it.  Nine thousand years she had hated this stone, and it was also the last thing in the world her mother had touched, the last thing broken for her sake.  In all her long life she had been the one left in the doorway, never the one who walked through.  She found she was bad at leaving. 

"Goodbye," she told the stone, and lifted the cord over her head. 

It did not follow. 

She stepped through. 

What happened after goodbye was not silence.  In the fourth direction nothing was far, and every farewell ever spoken to her was still there, hanging unanswered like breath in cold air, waiting for someone to arrive and finish the conversation.  The nearest was the oldest, a girl's whisper worn soft by nine thousand years of patience.  After goodbye, it turned out, the one who leaves waits too.  Vesna, who had forgotten what it felt like not to know the next line, drew a breath to answer. 

Behind her, the three directions shuffled back to their posts around an empty cave.  The storm thinned, the prisms steadied, and a cold half-stone dropped to the ice with nothing left to hold.
