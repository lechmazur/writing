Three years after every spoon in the world bent at once, and reality bent with them, Ilse climbed the observatory stairs at dusk.  The Bending had torn the isle of Vesper out of the sea and hung it among the clouds, and it had sprung open every circle anyone had ever drawn.  Rings became hooks, wheels became spirals, and the runes in her first-year primer, all built on closed loops, stopped meaning anything. 

Shadows changed too.  At noon they dropped away beneath everyone's feet, as shadows always had, but now they were slow to return.  Master Orrin's had stayed gone a whole afternoon, then a week, and then he had faded after it like breath off glass.  Yesterday Ilse's own shadow had not come back until supper.  She was the last rune scholar on the isle, barely past her alphabet, and she meant to find where shadows went at noon before hers decided to stay there. 

In Orrin's desk she had found a clockwork key as long as her forearm, its bow engraved with seven runes, and a note in his cramped hand.  "The Warden knows where they go; its awakening is sealed in the key." 

The Warden sat under the dome where it had sat since the Bending, a brass automaton slumped at a telescope that pointed, strangely, straight down into a well.  Ilse fitted the key between its shoulder plates and turned.  The key went halfway round and stuck.  She held the bow to her lamp and saw why: each of the seven runes had sprung, its ring gaping by a hair's width.  A key cannot finish its turn through an open circle.  So she would have to relearn the alphabet with her hands, by closing loops. 

Her only burnisher was the bent spoon she had carried since that last ordinary supper.  The first night she pressed too hard, and the gap in the rune for return sprang wider with a squeal.  Force was how the world had been bent in the first place.  After that she worked the way Orrin had ground lenses, quietly deliberate, one stroke and then a breath, following each line back toward the point where it began.  She managed one rune a night, because the observatory only unlocked its doors after dark, and because one was all her patience could do well. 

Each loop, as it closed, gave its meaning back to her fingers: return, descend, gather, rest, rise, bind, wake.  The primer had taught her those words as shapes to copy.  The key taught them as a journey that ended where it started.  Each noon, meanwhile, her shadow fell away, and each day it came home later and thinner. 

On the seventh night the last ring met itself, and the key turned full circle with a sound like a held breath let go.  The Warden straightened, gears ticking, and its glass eyes found her. 

"Look down," it said, and swung the eyepiece toward her. 

Through the well, far below the isle's stone belly, she saw Vesper's own vast shadow lying on the moonlit sea, and inside it a crowd of smaller darknesses, drifting. 

"At noon the sun stands straight above, so shadows fall straight through," the Warden said.  "They rest in the great shadow until the well's rim calls them home, but the rim is broken." 

Ilse knelt and read the bronze ring around the well: her seven runes, written large, and between wake and return a gap as long as her forearm.  She understood before the Warden spoke. 

"My key was cut from that rim when I was made, and the Bending widened the wound past closing," it said.  "Put it back, and I sleep again." 

"I only just woke you," Ilse said. 

"I was wound to watch shadows come home," said the Warden.  "Let them come." 

She drew the key out slowly, and the Warden froze with one hand raised toward the eyepiece.  She laid the key in the gap and burnished both seams with the spoon, unhurried, until wake ran into return without a break. 

Then she did what no one did in a nocturnal observatory: she stayed until noon.  Sun poured down the open dome, and her shadow slid off her heels into the well.  She counted three breaths.  It rose again, cool and whole, and beside it rose a taller one with stooped shoulders, which crossed the floor and settled at the Warden's feet, patient, as if its owner might follow. 

Ilse sat down between the two shadows, took up her spoon, and began, without hurry, to bend its handle into a ring.  The Warden would need a new key, and she knew the seven runes by heart now.
