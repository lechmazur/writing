In the fallow year, when the valley's fields lay resting under stubble and frost, Tamsin Reeve finally had time to be afraid on purpose.  The blight had taken two harvests, and the only clean seed left in the country slept in the crypt beneath Saint Orrin's Abbey, behind a door whose key had been lost for a century.  So had most of the abbey. 

Its founder had drawn every cell and corridor mean and narrow, to pinch the pride out of his monks, and one winter after his death the rooms simply refused the drawing.  Passages swapped ends.  Stairs climbed down.  The crypt wandered off like a cow through a broken fence, taking the key's hiding place with it. 

Tamsin had mapped caves, mine workings, and one drowned cistern, all while hating every wall that came near her.  She was claustrophobic the way some sailors cannot swim, and it had made her careful rather than timid.  She carried a line tied to her belt, chalk in each pocket, and a rule she never broke: no opening narrower than her outstretched arms.  She also carried the founder's blueprint, bought from a parish clerk, and the steel point of his drafting compass, found rusted into the vellum's center, where every arc of the abbey had been swung. 

She came at dusk to the crossing, the one place the mutiny had left alone.  The roof there had fallen long ago, so the four arms of the church met in a pool of shadow under open starlight, and she could breathe.  In that shadowed nexus stood the organ, its pipes furred with frost, lonely as a lighthouse. 

She worked the bellows with her boot, wedged a pebble on the lowest key, and let one long note go walking where she would not.  The reverberation came home in pieces.  From the north transept it returned late and thin, which meant length; from the cloister passage it came back quick and sour, squeezed.  She marked each answer on the blueprint and saw that the building was not lying at random.  Every room had fled toward width.  The cells had shouldered into the corridors and the corridors had shoved at the choir, each space elbowing for the air its maker had denied it. 

Tamsin laughed, a little unsteadily, because she knew that shove in her own ribs.  "You are not wicked," she told the dark.  "You are cramped." 

The clerk had told her how to end it: find the true center, transfix the blueprint there with the founder's point, and the stones would remember their obedience.  The crypt would come back to its drawn place, small and stale, and she would have to crawl in after the key. 

She moved the pebble up the keyboard, note by note, and walked the flagstones until the echoes from all four arms reached her in a single breath.  There, beneath a skin of moss, was a socket worn in the floor, exactly the size of the point.  She knelt with the vellum spread and the steel in her fist, and found she could not do it. 

It was a wistful sort of treason, siding with a ruin against a dead man's lines, but she had spent thirty years obeying walls and resenting every one.  Prudence kept its hand on her collar, though; a building left to riot would never give up the seed, and the valley could not eat her sympathy. 

So she took the pencil compass from her own pack, fitted the founder's point into its leg, and opened it a full hand wider than he ever had.  Then she redrew him.  Broad cells, a generous cloister, a crypt stair a person could descend standing upright: arc after arc, all swung from the old center, until the vellum was gray with her mutiny. 

When she drove the point through the blueprint and into the socket, the organ's note swelled, and the whole abbey seemed to exhale.  Stones moved like sleepers turning over.  The reverberation changed, each arm now answering full and unhurried, and beneath it rose a new sound, deep and hollow, from directly under the choir. 

The stair was where she had drawn it, wide as a wagon, with starlight falling down its treads.  In a niche at its head, where the last cellarer had hung it before the walls turned, waited an iron key, green with age.  Tamsin tested each step with her heel, paid out her line, and counted her breaths anyway. 

The lock turned, and she stood among the seed jars, underground and unafraid, listening to a lonely organ sing through rooms that finally fit.  Come spring the valley would sow old wheat, and nobody needed to know the abbey had won.
