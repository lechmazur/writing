For eleven years Ione had kept the dead oasis the way other people keep a vow.  Around it the wasteland lay scorched to black glass, and inside it stood a seep of water, nine date palms, and forty clay hives with no bees.  She had come here because the world talked everything small, and because she owed her dead sister a sentence she refused to shrink by saying late. 

Then came the morning the ghosts became visible.  Ione walked out with her water jar and found seven beekeepers standing among the hives, pale as smoke over a cold fire, veils lifted, hands empty.  The jar slipped and broke, and the astonished recluse understood that she had never spent one day here alone. 

They did not speak.  Their mouths stayed closed like sealed comb, and when she asked what they wanted, her own voice sounded crude in the heat.  So she did what solitude had trained her to do: she grew still, and she looked. 

Every one of the seven held the same gaze, fixed past her shoulder on the honey house.  On its shelf sat the thing she had dusted for eleven years and never opened, a stained glass box, its amber and cobalt panes leaded together like a tiny chapel.  She carried it into the light, lifted the lid, and found a fist of knotted cords, seven colors snarled so tightly that they made one body. 

Her first thought was a living person's thought: they want this undone.  They want their knots loosened and whatever is tied there finally said, so they can go.  She worked a fingernail under a red strand, and the seven ghosts flinched together, the way a hive flinches at smoke. 

Ione stopped.  Tranquilly perceptive, as the desert had made her, she set the box down and read the place instead of the faces.  The clues had been around her all along, patient as furniture.  No hive bore a name, but each lid was scratched with a small knot, and each knot was stained one of seven colors.  The only bench in the honey house faced the shelf, not the door, and its seat was worn by people who had sat a long time with their hands busy.  The old wax crusted on the lid's rim held thumbprints pressed one over another, a signature made by many hands and renewed for years.  And the ghosts, she noticed, never watched her mouth; they watched her fingers. 

Slowly the old custom assembled itself in her mind, the one about telling the bees.  These keepers had turned it inside out: whatever they could not say to one another, they had knotted into cord before the hives and laid in the box, each silence binding the next.  It was a tangled covenant, and the tangle was the covenant.  Pulled straight and spoken, those words would weigh no more than breath.  Kept, they held seven people to this ground even after the bees had burned away, even after death.  They were not asking for release.  They were asking her to guard it, now that the dead could be seen and the living would surely come demanding answers. 

She pressed the red strand back into place.  Beneath the knot her thumb found one cord she had missed, undyed, unknotted, coiled loosely as if left for a latecomer. 

When she looked up, the keepers had turned their gaze to the edge of the palms.  An eighth figure stood there in a hospital gown, thin as she had been at the end, and Ione knew that Maren had followed her across the glass eleven years ago and waited. 

The sentence rose in her throat, the one about the bedside she had been too frightened to sit at.  She could feel how small it would become in the air.  So she carried the box to the worn bench, took up the pale cord, and tied it slowly, one hard knot for each year, while her sister watched her hands from the doorway.  She worked the eleven knots into the snarl of seven colors until no one could have said where her silence ended and theirs began.  Then she closed the lid and pressed her thumbprint into the wax, which the heat had softened. 

Maren did not smile, and did not need to.  Her gaze rested on the box, then on Ione, with the look of someone who has finally been handed a thing whole.  Ione set the box back on its shelf, the eighth keeper of an oasis with no bees, and the sunlight through its amber and cobalt panes laid colors across the floor that nobody tried to name.
