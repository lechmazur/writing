The geese came down at dusk, the first to stop in Verrin in forty years, and settled on the dry basins as if water still stood in them.  Ines Vale, lighting the gas lamps along Cooper Street, watched them fold their wings, and the whole city went quiet around their pause.  Verrin had three hundred fountains and could not name one of them.  The council said the spring had died; the grandmothers said the fountains had swallowed the city's memories and locked them in the stone.  Ines believed neither.  She carried a flame on a pole for a living, and people said her doubt worked the same way: she walked into a rumor and it became visible.  But she imagined carefully, the way an engineer does, only as far as the evidence could bear weight. 

Her father sat each evening on the rim of the basin in Tanner's Court, unable to say her mother's name.  Once, when a gutter dripped beside him after rain, he had lifted his head and said, "She washed cherries here," and then lost it again.  So the memories were not in the stone.  They were stuck in the old people, and the sound of water was the hand that could work them loose.  And geese, she reasoned, do not land for nostalgia; somewhere under those basins they smelled damp. 

That was the night she noticed the crumbs.  Pell the baker was scattering stale bread in a thin line out of Tanner's Court, and the geese were eating along it in single file. 

"Standing order," Pell said, shrugging.  "Your grandmother Odile paid my father to keep it on the books: first night geese land here again, lay crumbs along this route." 

"Did she say why?" 

"She said somebody would eventually be rude enough to ask." 

Odile had been the last keeper of the fountains, and Ines followed her breadcrumbs with the lamp pole held low, past the shuttered dye works, down Sluice Alley, to a low iron door wearing the council's lead seal.  The line of crumbs traced, she realized, the buried main.  The door had a keyhole, and she knew at once where the key was. 

On her shelf sat Odile's stained glass box, a little casket of blue and amber panes whose lid had been fused shut with lime scale for as long as Ines could remember.  Something metal shifted inside when she tilted it.  Forcing the lid would crack every pane, so she did what the chemistry allowed: she wrapped the seam in rags soaked with warm vinegar and waited. 

The geese rested three nights.  Each night Ines sat at her window changing the rags, and each night the Swan crossed the chimney of the dye works four minutes earlier than the night before.  She had never had much use for patience, which usually meant someone wanted her to stop asking.  This was Odile's kind, a sidereal patience, exact and unhurried and indifferent to whether anyone was watching. 

On the third night the lid let go with a small sigh.  Inside lay an iron key and a note in Odile's square hand. 

"The spring is not dead, only slow.  I measured how slowly it returns and counted the wait in stars, not in councils.  If the geese have stopped again, there is water.  The valve will be stuck too; do not force it." 

Ines read it twice, looking for the superstition, and found only arithmetic. 

Before dawn she cut the council's seal with her lamp knife, quietly, the way one closes a door on an argument already won.  The key turned.  The valve wheel, big as a cart wheel and brown with rust, did not.  She gave it lamp oil and the patience she had just learned, rocking it a finger's width forward, a finger's width back, until she felt the threads unstick and the wheel begin to give.  Far below, something exhaled. 

She walked back above the buried main as the sky greyed, and the city coughed awake around her, one basin after another spitting rust, then running clear.  Nobody shouted.  Shutters opened; old people came down in their nightclothes and stood listening, and the council's watchman, who should have arrested somebody, took off his cap instead. 

In Tanner's Court the geese had stepped into the filling basin, and her father was holding his hand under the spout. 

"Marguerite washed cherries here," he said.  "You were on her hip, Ines, trying to catch the water." 

The water had held nothing, she thought; it had only knocked, and he had been the one to open.  At sunrise the geese rose and went on south, and Ines, who trusted springs no more than councils, went to find Pell and renew the standing order.
