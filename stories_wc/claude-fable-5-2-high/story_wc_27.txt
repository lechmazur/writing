Three months after the fleet burned over Kessel Ridge, the rebellion had gone quiet as a held breath.  Nobody had surrendered; people had simply drifted home to their terraces and stopped saying the word aloud.  Ilse Marrow still climbed to Repair Bay Nine every evening, boots knotted over her shoulder.  The abandoned spaceport had been poured around a stone circle older than the colony, twelve gray teeth rising through the cracked deck plates, because the first engineers found they could not shift them and then found they did not want to.  The stones hummed.  It was a sound below hearing, steady as bedrock, and Ilse had learned that it came clearest through bare soles.  So she stood barefoot among gutted hulls and dangling hoists, in the margin of daylight, and raised her grandmother's opera glasses to the hole in the roof.  They were vintage things, brass and mother-of-pearl, made for watching sopranos die beautifully on a world she would never see.  The garrison had confiscated every telescope in the valley, but no one had thought to take these.  For a few minutes each dusk, the sky went dark while the sun still struck the wreckage in orbit, and the dead fleet glittered overhead like spilled salt.  Ilse had charted that mess for ninety nights in a grease-stained logbook, because she could not accept that so much had been broken for nothing. 

"You're going to cut your feet," said Dov, the last quartermaster, who was crating tools he meant to sell. 

"Probably," Ilse said, not lowering the glasses.  "Dov, wreckage tumbles.  It flashes, it winks, it has no manners.  Eleven of those lights have not winked once in ninety nights." 

He stopped hammering. 

"Steady light means a steady hull, and a steady hull means someone is holding it steady," she went on, too loudly, tripping over the words and not minding.  "They are hiding in the wreck of themselves, waiting for us to say we are still here, and I am right about this, I checked it, um, ninety times." 

She handed him the opera glasses with the stiff ceremony of someone unused to being believed and fully expecting it anyway.  Dov looked for a long time.  When he lowered them, his face had the raw look of a man handed back something he had already grieved. 

"The launch beacon," he said.  "That is how we always told them the ground was friendly.  But the igniter coil has to sing at the reference pitch, and the garrison smashed the reference oscillator in the spring.  Without it we are guessing, and a wrong guess cracks the coil." 

Ilse looked down at her dirty feet on the deck, then at the twelve stones. 

"What do you think they calibrated the oscillator against?" she asked.  "Why else would anyone build a repair bay around rocks?" 

They worked fast to reignite it, because the garrison patrols flew at full dark and the margin was closing.  Dov hauled open the beacon housing at the circle's heart and fed the coil a trickle of power from his last cell.  Ilse stood beside it with one hand on the tuning wheel and her eyes shut.  The coil whined, thin and sharp, a mosquito against a mountain.  Through her soles came the other note, the old one, patient and enormous.  Where the two disagreed she felt a shudder in her ankles, a beat like a limping heart, and she turned the wheel until the limp slowed.  Slower.  Then there was no beat at all, only one sound, and the stones and the coil and her own bones were agreeing. 

"Now," she said, quite calmly, and Dov threw the switch. 

The beacon did not flare so much as arrive.  A column of white fire stood up through the broken roof with a roar that shook rust from the gantries, and yet inside the roar everything was still, the way the eye of a storm is still.  Ilse did not flinch.  She took back the opera glasses and looked up. 

One steady light blinked, deliberately, three times.  Then another, then all eleven, a slow applause from the dark. 

Below the bay, along the terraces, shutters were opening and lamps were being carried into the streets, one valley answering a pillar of thunder with small yellow flames. 

"The patrols will come," Dov said. 

"Yes," said Ilse.  "But now they are coming to a rebellion instead of a mess." 

She had wanted the wreckage to mean something, and it did, but only once someone on the ground was willing to answer it.  The stones hummed on under her bare feet, unhurried, holding the note.
