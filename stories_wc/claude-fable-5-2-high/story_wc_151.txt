The Golden Arcade earned its name only at dawn, when sunlight poured through the amber roof panes and turned every dead shopfront into honey.  Ione Vask unfolded her stool beside the last pillar box in the district and set her ebony clipboard on her knee.  Overhead, the Lattice hummed with a billion instant messages, each one read by machines before any human saw it.  Surface mail was different.  A paper letter rode the solar barges for nine slow days, sealed, unscanned, protected by a law nobody had bothered to repeal.  People who mailed one usually needed a name the Lattice could not trace, and Ione was the pseudonym creator they came to.  She was carefully inventive about it.  A false name, she believed, had to be truer than the real one, or the reader would smell the lie through the envelope. 

Her first client was already waiting, an old man named Teodor Brandt, clutching a letter in both hands.  "The carrier comes at six," he said.  "Then not again for a week, and I have waited eleven years already."  His daughter Marit lived on the far coast and returned anything bearing his name unopened.  He needed a name she would open. 

Ione clipped a blank form to the ebony board, its brass jaw worn pale by her thumb, and handed him a pencil.  "Answer the questions," she said, "but take your time."  She did not tell him that she never read the answers first.  The boxes on a form were where people performed; the margins were where they leaked.  Twenty years of work had taught her that meaning hid in the margins, in the doodles a hand made while the mind was busy lying. 

Teodor wrote stiffly.  Favorite memory of the recipient: none that she would want.  Reason for the quarrel: my fault.  While he frowned over each line, his pencil wandered to the edge of the page and drew small figures there: a square with a dot, a crooked ladder, a sun with three rays.  When he handed the clipboard back, the clock above the shuttered confectioner read twenty to six. 

Ione studied the margin and felt the familiar itch of something almost recognized.  Then she looked down.  Across the gilded floor tiles ran last night's chalk, pink and blue and yellow, left by the arcade children before the scrubbing drones woke at seven.  It looked like hopscotch, but Ione had watched long enough to know better.  Children whose every whisper was logged by the Lattice had kept one channel for themselves: codes in sidewalk chalk, a cipher of squares, ladders, and suns handed down from big kids to small for decades. 

She knelt and laid the ebony clipboard on the tiles to juxtapose his gray margin with their bright scrawl.  The figures matched, stroke for stroke. 

"Mister Brandt," she said, "did Marit grow up near here?"  He blinked at the floor as though it had spoken.  "Above the confectioner," he said.  "She invented a chalk alphabet when she was seven and made me learn it, because she said grown-ups should have to work for secrets." 

"You still know it," Ione said, and turned the clipboard toward him.  "Your hand does, anyway."  Checking each figure against the children's fresh squares, she read his margin aloud, one slow symbol at a time.  It spelled two words: Captain Slowpoke. 

Teodor made a sound that was half laugh and half something older.  "I was always last across the hopscotch," he said.  "She addressed every chalk note to that name, so I would know which secrets were mine." 

Ione had invented four thousand pseudonyms, and the best one she ever delivered was one she merely found.  Still, care was her trade, so she added one invention of her own.  She had him write the return name not in letters but in the chalk cipher itself, small, in the corner of the envelope, where a sorting clerk would see only a child's doodle.  Marit would have to work for that secret, and in working she would remember who had once worked for hers. 

At two minutes to six, the carrier's bicycle bell rang at the far end of the arcade.  Teodor kissed the envelope, embarrassed, and dropped it through the brass slot.  Nine days out, Ione thought, and nine days back if the coast was kind. 

When he had gone, she unclipped the form, tore off the margin, and filed it in her coat with the others she kept.  Then she picked up a stub of yellow chalk a child had abandoned and, in the cipher, wrote on the golden tiles the name she had been born with and had not used in twenty years.  The drones would scrub it at seven, but some child might read it first.
