Ilse had not slept a whole night since the armistice, so she painted instead. 
Three years after the water wars, Skerrow still kept its wartime habits: cisterns padlocked, cups measured, the causeway watched whenever the ebb bared it to the mainland. 
Twice a day the sea took the road back, and the island remembered it was alone. 

She painted what she could not sort. 
Requisition boats; her mother at the ration table, turning away a neighbor with a cracked jug; her mother dying of fever in the last dry summer, lips sealed on something unsaid. 
Canvas after canvas, Ilse beat at those years like a thresher at wet sheaves, hoping some grain of meaning would finally drop loose from the chaff. 
So far she had mostly produced chaff, and said so to anyone who asked, cheerfully, meaning every word. 

All she had of her mother was the amber brooch, a honey-colored oval with a single gnat stopped mid-step inside. 
"One moment, kept from the flood," her mother used to say, tapping it. 
Ilse wore it on her smock and resented how little it explained. 

The rains had come back that autumn, thin and uncertain, like guests unsure of their welcome. 
On the fourth sleepless night of a wet week, she carried her lantern up the hill to paint the dark, and noticed the water instead. 
Rain did not run straight down Skerrow. 
It gathered into trails, silver threads that slid sideways across the slope, ignoring the easy drop to the sea. 
Ilse, who had nothing but hours, followed one. 
It led her along a lip of turf too level to be an accident, and ended in a bramble hollow under the ruined signal hut. 

The next night she followed another trail from the north cliff, and the next a third from the sheep fold, marking each with dabs of ochre on the stones. 
"I am doing science," she informed a wet ewe, "in the sense that I am cold and nobody believes me." 
Every trail bent to the same hollow. 
By the sixth night her eyes felt like sanded glass, and the hill, seen from the top with her ochre marks catching the lantern, looked like a hand closing. 
Someone had cut those channels, long ago, patiently, so that the whole island poured itself into one palm. 

She dug with a palette knife, then a borrowed spade, then her fingers. 
Under two feet of packed earth and deliberately planted bramble lay a slate lid as wide as a cart. 
Names were scratched into it, eleven of them, the old ration council. 
The last was her mother's, and beneath it, in her mother's stubborn capitals: HIDDEN FROM THE BOATS, OPEN WHEN IT RAINS AGAIN. 

Ilse sat down in the mud. 
The council had buried the island's oldest cistern so the requisitioners would find only the small tanks, and then the fever had taken every one of them before the rain returned. 
Her mother had turned away the cracked jug because the open stores were truly empty, and because one loose word would have cost Skerrow everything. 
The hard face at the ration table and the sealed lips on the deathbed ran together at last, like the trails, into a single place. 
She levered the slate a hand's width and dropped a pebble. 
The splash came up deep and cold, and she laughed until it turned into something else. 

Water that far down needed a pump, and Skerrow had none; the boats had taken those too. 
But at dawn the ebb bared the causeway, and Tobiah the tinker came across with his handcart as he did each month, selling needles, lamp oil, and salvage. 
Lashed to the cart was a cast-iron lift pump with forty feet of pipe. 
"That is not for paintings," he said, before she could offer. 
"I know," Ilse said. 
"My paintings are terrible; I have it on good authority, mine." 

She unpinned the brooch. 
It lay in her palm, warm, the gnat still halted in its one saved moment. 
Her mother had kept a moment so that it could someday be spent; Ilse understood that now, with the awe of the very tired. 
Tobiah held the amber to the sunrise, whistled, and began unlashing the pump. 
The exchange took less time than the tide. 

By noon half the village stood in the hollow, reading the names, saying nothing. 
When the first water coughed out of the spout, the neighbor with the cracked jug held it under, and it leaked, and nobody minded. 
Ilse did not paint that night. 
She lay listening to rain find its old roads down the hill, every thread going where it had been meant to go, and somewhere in the listening she slept.
