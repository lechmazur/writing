Marisol Quince had painted nine walls that year, each louder than the last, and had not enjoyed a single one.  The city called her its most vivid mural artist, which was true, and a happy one, which was only a rumor.  On the scaffold outside the savings bank, waiting for a coat of primer to dry, she turned over the keepsake she carried in her apron: a fresco fragment no bigger than a biscuit, salvaged by her grandmother from somewhere nobody could name.  It showed half a painted mouth, laughing around a plum.  She wondered what the joke had been, and the wondering folded over her like a warm sheet. 

Inside the fold, there was fog.  It was old fog, centuries thick, the kind that had forgotten it was weather and settled in as a resident.  Through it leaned the black ribs of plum trees, row after ruined row, and the toppled walls of press-houses where a whole orchard district had once made its living and then been mislaid.  Marisol found she was holding a lantern instead of a brush, and the fragment had grown warm in her other hand. 

"Well," she told it, "you clearly know the way home." 

She swung the lantern to look around, and the swing stayed.  A gold arc hung in the fog where the light had passed, crisp as a stroke of cadmium.  She understood it at once, the way one understands things in daydreams: this fog was film, a gray emulsion that had waited centuries in the dark, and whatever light touched it would be kept.  Exposed film cannot be unexposed.  That thought made her careful, and carefulness, she noted sourly, was exactly what had been wrong with her all year.  So what was she supposed to paint here, with one shot at every stroke? 

The fragment tugged her down toward the roots of the nearest tree.  There, pale threads of fungus stitched root to root, tree to tree, running off under the whole district like gossip under a village.  When she pressed the painted plaster against them, the threads hummed, and a picture arrived in her head: a sunburnt elbow, a basket, one corner of a long table.  The tree remembered the piece of the wall it had faced, and nothing more.  No single trunk held the whole fresco, but the network did, each dead tree keeping its sliver and passing her along to the neighbor who knew the next. 

She went down the rows asking.  One tree gave her a fiddler with enormous ears.  Another gave her a dog stealing a sausage, rendered with great affection and no grasp of anatomy.  A third, stubborn and half fallen, would give up only a blue it insisted had been the sky.  After each answer she walked back to the ruined press-house and laid that sliver into the fog above it with the lantern, gingerly at first, measuring twice. 

Then the last tree in the row showed her the bottom corner, where the original painter had signed the work.  It was not a name but a thumbprint in plum red, smeared, plainly made by someone who had been eating while working. 

Marisol laughed, a short rusty bark that startled her.  The dog was wrong, the ears were wrong, the sky was one tree's opinion, and whoever made this had been having a wonderful afternoon.  Nobody had asked that painter to be permanent; the wall fell down anyway, and the joy went into the roots and kept. 

She stopped measuring.  She painted with light the way she had once painted with anything, fast and wide, the lantern looping, feasters blooming gold and plum and stubborn blue across the gray.  Where the trees disagreed, she guessed, and the guesses stayed too, which struck her as generous of the fog.  Last of all she set the laughing mouth in its place at the head of the table, and the plum finally had a face to belong to. 

Then she stepped back and let the fog display the whole feast, forty feet of harvest supper glowing over an audience of stumps.  Under her boots the threads hummed, tree telling tree, and she had the distinct impression of being applauded by mushrooms.  So that was the joke, she thought: she had spent a year guarding her film from the light, and wondering why nothing showed up on it. 

The fold opened.  The primer was dry, the bank's approved sketch was taped to the rail, and the fragment sat cool in her palm.  Marisol looked at the sketch for a respectful moment, then dipped her thumb in the red and started, grinning, with a plum.
