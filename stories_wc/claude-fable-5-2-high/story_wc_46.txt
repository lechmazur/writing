Mist came into the serpentine grotto the way breath comes into a sleeper, slow and unhurried.  Odile sat where the green stone coiled around its last tide pool, under the one hole in the roof that showed her sky.  She had been a star watcher on the headland once, before her knees refused the cliff path and made her earthbound.  Now she watched what little heaven the grotto allowed, a coin of night with three stars in it, sometimes four. 

In her lap lay the morning's gathering of sea glass, frosted green and amber and one rare blue.  She lifted the blue piece to weigh it in her palm, as her mother the lapidary had taught her to weigh every rough stone before deciding what it wanted to become.  Then she began the old method of gem polishing: coarse wet sand on a slate, then river silt, then chalk worked into a strip of soft leather. 

The work was circles, and circles loosen things.  As the frost thinned on the glass, memories resurfaced the way shells do when a wave draws back.  Her father's finger tracing the Swan.  The headland tower, and her charts pinned to its walls like pressed flowers.  The night her oil lamp tipped, and thirty years of stars went up as sparks while she stood in the heather with nothing in her hands.  She had not lit a flame since. 

Yet the village still needed light.  The boats sheltered in the grotto's winding channel, and on mist nights the fishers groped home by touch, because torches only turned the fog into a blind white wall.  So Odile had set herself a quiet task: to share light without using fire.  All summer she had wedged polished glass into the cracks of the serpentine walls, each piece tilted to catch what fell through the roof and hand it to the next. 

It had not worked.  Glass polished clear on every side passed the starlight along like a secret and showed it to no one. 

Tonight, with the blue piece half finished, a zephyr wandered down the channel and thinned the mist, and the moon slid into her coin of sky.  She stopped her circles and looked.  One face of the glass was bright as water; the other still wore its frost.  The clear face drank the moonlight and the frosted face held it, so the whole shard glowed, reflectively vibrant, like a petal lit from within. 

Her mother's voice surfaced last, and clearest.  "Every stone hides a pearl of stillness, Odile, the moment you must stop polishing and let it be."  She had never stopped; she had tried to rub everything clear, her glass and her grief alike, when the frost was what kept the light from leaving. 

A hull knocked stone somewhere in the white.  "Odile?" a small voice called.  It was Wren, the netmaker's girl, who traded sea glass for the names of stars.  "I can't find the turns, and Papa's boat is behind me." 

Odile's hand went, from old habit, toward the place where a lamp would have been.  It found the blue glass instead. 

"Stay still, little bird," she called, "and watch the walls." 

First she set the blue piece on the lip of the tide pool, directly beneath the moon.  Then she gathered her lapful of unpolished shards and edged along the ledge, and behind each clear piece she tucked a frosted one: the clear to carry, the frosted to keep. 

The grotto bloomed.  Bend by bend the light unfurled down the channel, green and amber, soft as blossom on a vine, and the mist that had blinded every torch only cupped the glow and made it gentler.  Wren's skiff came nosing around the last coil, her father's boat close behind, both faces lifted and washed in color. 

"You lit it," Wren whispered. 

"No," said Odile.  "Nothing here is burning; this is only the sky, handed along." 

Wren crouched by the pool and touched the rough side of the blue glass.  "You missed half," she said. 

"I stopped in time," Odile answered, and felt the truth of it settle.  She weighed the memory of the burning tower, as she had every night for years, and found it lighter, frosted now, something that could hold a glow. 

She would never climb back to the stars.  But a breeze moved through the grotto, sweet with kelp and night, and beside the unrippled pool the blue glass glowed, a pearl of stillness, while all along the walls the stars had come down to her.
