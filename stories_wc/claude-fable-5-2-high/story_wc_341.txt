The string broke at nine minutes past midnight, with a sound like a knuckle cracking in another room.  Odile Marsh, aviation meteorologist for the Calloway night mail field, felt the sky gondola stop tugging and begin, very gently, to rise.  Everyone called the tether cable the string, as if her wicker observation car and its gas bag were a child's kite.  Below, the lamps of the winch crew shrank to pollen, and four hundred feet of steel trailed under her, still curled in the shape of the drum it had slept on. 

She did not scream.  The bureau called her quietly explosive, a woman who never raised her voice and still left holes in rooms.  The largest hole she had left was a report, six years old, claiming that empty spaces remember.  A mail plane crossing thin, supercooled cloud, she had written, cuts a lane that stays open for hours, and a pilot who knew this could follow the absence of another pilot home.  The bureau had stamped it unverified, since no one could see the top of a cloud deck from a gondola leashed beneath it.  She had written it the winter Tomas flew the midnight mail into just such a deck and did not come out the other side. 

Now the leash was gone, and the deck came down over her like a lowered ceiling.  Her hand found the valve cord that would spill gas and set her down in the dark beet fields within minutes.  She let it go. 

Gray wool closed around the car, beaded the wicker, and then released her into a silence so wide it seemed to have furniture.  Above the deck the stars looked old and unswept, their light sifting down like dust through the slats of a barn. 

She opened the ledger Tomas had started as a joke and she had kept as a science.  In it, clouds were filed not by Latin but by emotion, because a pilot cares less what a cloud is than how it will treat him.  Fair cumulus was Contentment, high cirrus was Longing, and the smooth lens over a ridge was Held Breath.  The deck beneath her, flat and patient and full of water waiting to freeze onto anything that touched it, was Grudge.  She had come up through Grudge once already, and frost now furred the netting and the gas bag hung heavy with it.  A second, slower passage would load the bag with ice and bring her down as a stone.  So she needed, quite practically, for her dead husband's sky to have a memory. 

She unstrapped his brass telescope, its tube worn pale where his thumb had rested, and swept the deck to the east, where the ten o'clock mail had climbed out two hours before.  For a long minute there was only pewter.  Then the lens found it: a clean dark canal ruled through the cloud, its banks glittering with fallen ice, exactly where an airplane no longer was.  At the bottom of the canal, small as a dropped coin, turned the Calloway beacon. 

Something went off inside her without a sound, six years of packed powder, and all it moved was one tear and her pencil.  She logged the bearing, the width, the hour, and the wind that was carrying her toward the gap at a walking pace.  When the canal lay open under the car she pulled the valve cord in three short breaths and sank into it.  Walls of Grudge slid past on either side and never touched her.  Starlight came down the shaft with her, dusty and unhurried, all the way to the beet fields, where the trailing cable caught a hedgerow and the gondola settled like a hen. 

The winch crew found her at dawn, sitting in the tilted car, staring at the broken string.  It lay across the furrows in loop after even loop, each one the exact size of the drum.  Tomas used to say a life was not a line paid out and lost but a spool, whole at every moment, the flown part wound together with the unflown.  She had thought it a pretty thing pilots told their wives.  Here it was in steel: snapped, flung across a county, and still holding the shape of what had held it. 

That morning she filed her observations with the bureau, bearings and all, and this time no one stamped anything.  Then, to memorialize him where pilots would actually look, she opened the ledger and entered a new cloud that was no cloud at all.  Marsh's Lane, she wrote, for Tomas Marsh: the gap a flier leaves behind.  Emotion: Remembering.  Treats a pilot kindly; follow it home.
