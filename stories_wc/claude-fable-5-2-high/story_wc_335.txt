The town had forgotten the library on purpose, which is the most thorough way to forget anything.  Inside, Maren Voss kept the shard-mirrors: ten thousand slivers of glass, each holding an afternoon shadow cut from someone the council had sent away.  That was how exile worked in Dunmere.  They took your shadow at the gate, filed it here, and the gate never again opened for a body that cast nothing.  It was reversible, technically.  Stand before your shard in the exact light it was cut in, and the shadow would step home to your heels.  But the council scraped the hour off every label, so nobody ever came. 

Then the girl came.  She crawled in by the storm drain and climbed through the reading-room window, with scabbed knuckles and a chin like a thrown stone. 

"I want my shadow," she said. 

Maren found herself walking backwards, heels first, until her spine met the atlas shelf.  She had done that since childhood, whenever her nerves outran her face. 

"You walk backwards when you're scared," the girl said. 

"I walk backwards when I am being burgled."  "Name." 

"Pim Okafor."  "I hit the magistrate's son, and he deserved it." 

"I asked for your name, not your defense." 

Maren opened a drawer and lifted out a shard the length of a hand, a small dark shape leaning inside it like smoke held still.  Pim grabbed for it, and Maren raised it out of reach. 

"Hold it in the wrong light and it cracks, and then you are gone for good." 

"So tell me the hour." 

"I don't know it, but your body does."  She poured black tea, loose leaves and all, into a white cup.  "Whoever loses a shadow keeps leaning toward the hour it was taken, and what they leave in a cup leans with them."  "Drink." 

Pim drank, scowling, down to the dregs.  Maren slid a protractor, a pencil, and a sun table across the desk. 

"Find the longest line the leaves make, and measure its angle against the handle." 

"You do it."  "You're the archivist." 

"I am, and I am also old."  "They will cut you again someday, for hitting someone else who deserves it, and I may not be here."  "Measure." 

Pim measured, got sixty degrees, and looked up for praise.  Maren gave her none, and let her find in the table that sixty meant noon, when shadows barely lean at all.  It was the hardest silence Maren had kept in years, because she badly wanted to point.  A returned shadow was worth little to a girl who could not win it back twice. 

Pim swore, turned the cup, and found what she had missed: a thin chain of stems lying much lower. 

"Twenty-two degrees," she said, running a finger down the table.  "Ten to five."  Her face changed.  "That's right, they came for me after the bell, when the schoolyard was empty and one swing was still going by itself." 

"Then you have checked your arithmetic against your memory," Maren said, "which is more than the council ever does." 

They waited at the west window, which overlooked the schoolyard behind the library.  The light went amber.  The last children were called in, and the hush came down, and one empty swing went on drifting in the breeze. 

Maren realized she was walking backwards again.  Her hip struck an open drawer, and Pim, quick as a magpie, read the label on the shard inside. 

"Voss, M."  "That's you."  "You're exiled too, and you know the trick, and you're still hiding in here?" 

"I have never poured my own cup," Maren admitted.  "Somebody has to be here when a window opens." 

"That's an excuse." 

"It is a reason, and when you are older you may learn the difference."  "Now stop arguing and stand in your light." 

Pim took the shard in both hands and held it to the sun herself.  The dark shape inside it stretched, poured across the floorboards, and fastened to her heels as if it had only been out on an errand.  She stamped on it twice to be sure. 

Maren uncapped her pen to annotate the catalog card: returned at twenty-two degrees; bearer did her own arithmetic.  Pim plucked the pen away, pulled the card marked Voss, and added her own annotation in ferocious capitals. 

"Reversible," Maren read aloud, "hour unknown, archivist too stubborn to measure." 

"I'm coming back Thursday with tea," Pim said, one leg already over the sill, "and you're doing your own arithmetic." 

Maren walked backwards three full steps, casting nothing on the amber floor, and for the first time in forty years she minded.  She left the white cup out.
