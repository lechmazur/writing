Ilse carried the water clock through the fog the way her mother had carried newborns, wrapped in a towel and held against her ribs.  The town had not woken yet; the canal was only a hush somewhere to her left, and the fog had no smell at all.  Nothing had, since the fever two winters ago.  An aromatherapist without a nose, she blended oils now by label and memory, and she trusted the clock to do what scent once did.  Its slow drip gave her clients a pace to breathe by, until they noticed, astonished, that they were breathing at all.  That was the whole of her trade: unveiling ordinary miracles to people too tired to see them.  But for a month the drip had been running fast, and her clients left hurried, and she had begun to feel like a fraud with a leaking bowl. 

The repair shop's window was sweated white from inside.  Through it she could make out only a lamp and a bent shape, which told her he was already working and probably had not slept.  She knocked with her elbow. 

Teodor Brandt let her in without a greeting, among shelves of copper bowls and glass cylinders, each one ticking in water instead of metal.  The room dripped in forty different tempos, like rain that could not agree with itself.  He unwrapped her clock, tipped it to the lamp, and went straight to the regulator beneath the spout. 

"This is no clockmaker's part," he said, backing out a small brass thumbscrew. 

"It is the screw from my mother's birthing clamp," Ilse said.  "She was the midwife here, and when the old valve corroded she threaded in what she had." 

"The seat has worn smooth around it and cannot grip," he said.  "It slips, so the water runs as it likes; I will fit a proper needle valve and you may throw this away." 

He said it flatly, but he did not put the screw down. 

Once, Ilse would have known a man like him in one breath, by the camphor or the sour wool or the bread he had not eaten.  Now she had only occluded views, and had learned to read through them.  His spectacles were steamed over, so his eyes were two gray coins.  A water-filled cylinder stood between them and bent his mouth, and in that bend she saw it tremble. 

She took out the notebook she kept for clients, where she drew each person as a country: ridges where they braced, basins where grief pooled, a pass where something might still get through.  The truth of such a map was never in the contour lines themselves but in the spaces between them, how close they crowded, how steep the fall.  She read between his lines too.  His ledger lay open, every repair entered in a square hand, except for today's line, which he had ruled, dated, and left empty. 

She did not ask.  She sketched one steep ridge, one deep basin, and waited at the pass. 

"Your mother was Marta Lindqvist," he said at last, to the screw. 

"Yes." 

"Forty years ago this morning, in fog like this, she tightened this on my daughter's cord and put her in my hands."  His voice stayed level, as water tries to in a carried bowl.  "We had her nine days, and I have timed everything since so that I need not count those." 

"Then do not give me a needle valve," Ilse said.  "That screw is what humanizes the thing; without it my clock is plumbing, and nine days are only a number." 

Teodor wiped his spectacles, and for the first time she saw his eyes plainly, red and very awake.  He said nothing more, but he reached for a brass sleeve instead of a valve.  He bushed the worn seat, cut it a fresh thread, and turned the old screw home until it bit.  Then he filled the bowl, and they both watched the spout. 

One drop gathered, hung, and fell; a long patience; then another. 

Ilse felt her own breath lengthen to meet it, and saw his shoulders come down a finger's width, a contour easing on her page.  She had read him without a single scent, which meant the fever had not taken her trade, only one of its doors. 

Teodor took up his pen and, on the empty line, wrote a single name. 

"Tell me what the morning smells like," Ilse said, wrapping the clock. 

He opened the door on the thinning fog and considered it, as seriously as a repair.  "Wet rope," he said, "and coal smoke, and somebody's bread." 

Ilse breathed it in anyway, and carried the clock home against her ribs, unhurried.
