The heat wave broke at midnight, and by dawn the cobalt lighthouse at Sorrel Point stood drowned in fog.  Eleven days of furnace air had ended in one cold breath off the land, and the warm sea had answered with white.  Ines Marrow, assistant lighthouse keeper, climbed to the gallery with her hand on her throat.  A childhood fever had burned her voice down to a whisper, and Keeper Halloran had never let her forget it.  "A light that cannot shout is a candle," he liked to say. 

He was shouting now.  He stood at the rail cranking the hand horn, and between blasts he bellowed his son's name into the blank morning.  Tobin had rowed out at dusk, in the last of the heat, after the worst of their quarrels.  The cistern had been low, and Halloran had refused one more bucket for the night garden his dead wife planted at the tower's foot.  Tobin had watched his mother's jasmine and tobacco flowers crisp to straw, called his father a stone, and gone. 

Now the horn roared, the cliffs threw it back from six directions, and somewhere in the white a pair of oars kept creaking away toward the reef. 

She caught Halloran's sleeve.  "You are driving him off," she whispered.  "A horn means danger, keep away, and he was raised to obey it.  You cannot beckon with a warning." 

"Then what would you have me do, girl, breathe at him?"  His eyes were red, and not from salt. 

She had argued it with him all summer: every signal carries vibrations of intent, and the sea delivers the intent long after it drops the noise.  A shout arrives as a wall.  A whisper arrives as an open door, which is why it travels farther.  This morning she meant to prove it, because a boy's life hung on the proof and because she was tired of being a candle. 

From her apron she took the vial.  On the heat wave's fourth night she had cut the garden's last living blooms and steeped them in sweet oil, wanting to save something of Rosalind for the men who could not say they missed her.  The heat had cracked the glass and stolen the oil.  Only the vial cork remained whole, swollen dark, holding the entire garden the way a held breath holds a word.  Tobin once told her his mother swore the garden purifies intentions, that nobody could stand among those flowers after dark and keep lying about what they wanted. 

Ines pinched the cork in the lamp tongs and held it over the warm brass of the lantern vent, where the new land wind poured seaward.  The scent opened like a hand.  Jasmine, tobacco flower, wet earth, the green hush of a garden at night, all of it unspooling over the rail and out across the hidden water. 

Halloran breathed it and the horn handle slid from his fist.  She watched the anger go out of his face like silt settling, until what was left was the thing the anger had been built to guard. 

"I wanted him to stay," he said, hoarse.  "That is all I ever wanted, and I said it with buckets and rules." 

"Then say it now," Ines whispered, "and say it small, so there is room in it for him." 

The old keeper leaned over the rail of his cobalt tower, a man whose whole chest was screaming, and let out almost nothing.  "Come home, Tob.  Come home, lad, I am sorry about her flowers." 

The words went out on the perfume, soft as moths.  Ines held the cork steady though the tongs burned her knuckles, and with her free hand she beckoned at the fog as if it had eyes. 

Then the oars stopped, turned, and began again, slower, nearer, feeling their way up the thread of scent the way a hand follows a rope in the dark.  The skiff's bow slid out of the white directly below them, and Tobin's face tipped up, wet and astonished. 

"I smelled Mam's garden," he called, voice cracking, "and I heard you say sorry." 

Halloran took the stairs like a young man.  From the gallery Ines watched him wade out to his waist and haul boat and boy against his chest, neither making a sound louder than the tide.  The fog was thinning to gold around the blue tower. 

Far below, Halloran lifted his head and said something to her that he did not shout.  "We will replant it, Keeper Marrow, and you will tell me where to dig." 

She heard every word.  Ines pressed the cork back into the broken vial and answered him in her own voice, which was enough.
