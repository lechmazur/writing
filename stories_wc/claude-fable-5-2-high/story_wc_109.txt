Only one star remained above the ridge fort, and the sky around it was going the color of old slate.  Venna, who had been the oracle of the pebble shore until the sea took the shore, sat in the gatehouse with a gilder's booklet open on her knee.  The storm threw itself at the walls, drew back, and threw itself again.  She did not listen to the storm.  She listened to the drawing back, as she always had. 

On the shore, fishermen had paid her to read the pause after a wave dragged the pebbles down, the breath in which the stones stopped clicking and the sea decided what it would do next.  Her whole craft was summarizing silence: one plain line for each pause, and the lines, set in order, told a man where the safe water lay. 

Now there was no shore, and her people were somewhere on the long ridge below, climbing toward the fort in the dark.  The stars had been going out for nine turns of the fort's great sandglass, and no dawn had come.  When the last one went, the climbers would have nothing to steer by, and the ridge was narrow, with a long fall on either side. 

So Venna had decided to draw a map of the endless night, and the deciding had settled into her like a pebble in a shoe.  She was only mildly obsessed, she told the sergeant; she still ate, she still slept an hour here and there, but she could not pass a doorway without stopping to hear what the quiet did inside it. 

A night cannot be mapped by what is seen in it.  It can be mapped by where the wind stops.  For nine turns of the glass she had walked the ridge path on a rope, out from the gate and back, and wherever the gale suddenly failed, behind a boulder, in a dip, under the lee of a ruined watch post, she stood still and listened.  Each such place held what she called an ambered hush: a stillness thick and warm enough to keep a person whole inside it, the way amber keeps a fly.  No two were alike.  One hummed low, one tasted of wet iron, one let you hear your own pulse in your teeth. 

She wrote a line for each on the tissue pages of the booklet, which the chapel gilder had left behind when he fled inland.  Between the tissues lay squares of gold leaf, so old they flaked at a breath and clung to her knuckles like scales.  Twelve hushes, twelve lines, and a thirteenth page still blank. 

"It is no use to them on paper," said Sergeant Hale, watching the last star through the arrow slit.  "Nobody reads in the dark." 

"No," Venna agreed.  "So it will not stay on paper." 

She had him bring his three steadiest runners, and in the gatehouse, between gusts, she began to retell the ridge.  She did not describe the rocks.  She gave them each silence in a sentence, in order, the way she had once given fishermen the sea. 

"From the gate, forty paces with the wind on your left cheek, until it lets go of you all at once; that hush hums."  "Then down, until the quiet tastes of iron."  "Then on, until you hear your own teeth." 

The runners said it back, stumbled, and she retold it, patient and exact, moving one word, then moving it back, until the twelve lines ran through them like a rope through rings. 

Then she tore the booklet along its stitching and gave each runner four gold leaves.  "Press one on the stone at the heart of each hush," she said.  "Wet rock will hold it, and one spark from a shuttered lantern will find it, so they will know they have arrived inside the quiet and not merely wished it."  "Then go down to my people and tell them the ridge exactly as I told you." 

They went out on the rope, and the gale closed over them. 

Venna climbed to the wall and stood where the stone broke the wind, in the fort's own hush, the last leaf and its blank page flat between her palms.  Above her the last star thinned like a coal under ash.  Below, after a long while, a spark opened and a fleck of gold answered it, small and steady, and then, farther down, another. 

The night had not ended, but it had a shape now, twelve warm rooms strung along a dark road, and voices were passing the shape from mouth to mouth.  She kept the blank page for the one silence she could not yet summarize: the hush that would come just before a dawn.
