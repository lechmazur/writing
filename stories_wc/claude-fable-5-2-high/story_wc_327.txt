In the canyon of Ossa, no sound ever fully died.  Words flung carelessly between the towers of bone came back for years, tangled and wrong, so Iseth had learned to speak the way a weaver ties knots: softly, exactly, once.  She was the storyteller of the rib-towers, and the copper that the green springs had laid in her family's veins ran in bright threads under her skin.  Those threads hummed when sound passed through them, so she had always felt voices as textures: her mother's like wool, the trade tongue like wet slate. 

Now she sat with old Maro, the only other person alive who could read Hand.  Hand was never spoken.  It was carved, ridge and pit and groove, into the great bones of the towers by ancestors who believed that speech belonged to the canyon and only touch belonged to people.  Every history, every recipe for surviving the dry years, lay in those textures, and the children's fingers, busy with the trade tongue's quick profits, no longer learned them.  Maro's fingers moved on Iseth's wrist, pressing a last phrase: a language needs two.  By morning there was one. 

Iseth did not wail, because a wail would have haunted the canyon for a decade.  She thought instead, the way she spoke, in small precise pieces.  If sound could become texture in her veins, then texture could become sound, and sound was the one thing Ossa kept forever. 

She needed a point hard enough to ride carved bone without wearing down, and the canyon held exactly one: the Widow's Eye, a diamond the size of a thumb joint, locked in the ossuary because every cutter who had tried to cleave it swore it screamed.  A feather-shaped flaw ran through its heart, and people said the flaw was a trapped curse.  The ossuary keeper handed it over with tongs. 

"It is not cursed," Iseth told him quietly, feeling the stone tremble against her copper-threaded palm.  "It is hollow where it is broken, and hollow things sing." 

She bound the diamond into a splint of bird bone and drew it along a carved rib.  Ridges rang high, pits knocked low, grooves sighed, and each note raised in her veins the very texture that had made it.  The translation was true.  But the open canyon ruined it within a breath, folding the notes into the old tangle of auction cries and dead men's laughter. 

There was one clean place: the Throat, a tunnel worn through the base of the tallest tower, where the marrow had once run.  Sound that entered the Throat rightly circled its spiral walls without fading, a tunnel of echoes that had held one ancestor's single clear whistle for three hundred years.  Sound that entered wrongly, against the wind, shattered and soured everything already inside.  No one living knew how the whistler had judged the wind. 

Iseth sat below the Throat for forty dusks and watched the swifts.  The flock nested in the tower's hollows, and it flew the air's hidden shape: scattering like thrown gravel when the currents quarreled, braiding when they agreed.  On rare evenings the whole flock drew itself into one dark rope and poured through the Throat from end to end without a single bird touching the wall.  That rope was the wind made visible, and by its pattern she could harness the tunnel.  She learned the signs that came before it: three false braids, a wheel, a pause. 

On the forty-first dusk the swifts wheeled, paused, and Iseth was already kneeling at the Throat's mouth, where the first carved line of the oldest history began.  The flock roped past her shoulders, wings brushing her hair, and she set the diamond to the bone and followed them in. 

The Widow's Eye screamed, as the cutters had promised, but it screamed in Hand.  Ridge, pit, groove; flood year, seed year, the names of the first builders; each note lifted off the wall and rode the wind the birds had drawn, circling, settling, staying.  She walked the whole spiral with her arm aching and her veins blazing with every texture her grandmothers had ever cut.  When she came out the far end, the swifts were gone into the dark, and behind her the tunnel was telling the history by itself. 

Hand had died with Maro; Iseth never pretended otherwise.  But children came to the Throat because it sang, and stayed because the song had a shape.  One afternoon a girl stood humming along, her small hand resting idly on the carved wall, and then she stopped, frowned, and moved her fingers back over a ridge. 

"This one is the high note," she said. 

Iseth took the girl's wrist and, softly, exactly, pressed a word into it: two.
