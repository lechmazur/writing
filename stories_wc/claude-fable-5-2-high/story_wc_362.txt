Mist filled the quartz arbor the way breath fills a cold jar, and Wren Halloway stood inside it with her boots planted wide.  Her grandmother had raised the arbor on the quarry ridge, six arches of rough white stone, and her father had parked his station wagon beneath it every night of his life.  Now the wagon was hers, and so was the question. 

Wren made her living as a mystic, which mostly meant paying attention.  She noticed which pocket a widower kept touching, which teacup a daughter would not wash, and she told them what they needed: the watch remembers him, the cup still holds her.  Truthful lies, she called them privately.  She believed every word and could not prove one, and a firmly grounded woman does not enjoy selling what she cannot weigh. 

Her father had said as much.  "You sell fog, Wren," he told her, the year she hung her sign.  He was a lens grinder, a man of tolerances, and for forty years he carried a glass prism in his shirt pocket, over his heart, the way other men carry a pen.  When she was six he had told her it caught rainbows so they would not get lost.  When she was thirty he told her it was only glass.  One of those was a lie, and she intended to find out which. 

If objects absorb emotions, then forty years against a man's chest would have soaked that prism through, and whatever he truly felt about his fog-selling daughter was in there.  She expected disappointment. 

The method was her own, tested on clients' heirlooms through three winters: she communed through the patterns in frost on a car window.  Frost is water deciding where to settle, and it decides differently near a thing that has been loved or hated.  Last night she had set his prism on the dashboard against the driver's side of the windshield, and on the passenger side a new prism from a catalog, never held by anyone but a packer in gloves.  A control, because fog should at least be measured.  The quartz would do the rest; her grandmother swore the arches hummed under pressure, and Wren had felt it in her molars often enough to stop arguing. 

Now, at dawn, she laid her bare palm on the cold hood and waited.  To commune was not to ask.  It was to be quiet in the right direction until the quiet answered. 

The mist had its ordinary hush, drip and distance, a held breath.  Then the hush shifted.  Nothing grew louder; the silence simply changed its weight, the way a room changes when someone you know walks in behind you.  Wren wrote the time in her notebook, because attention without a record is only a feeling, and then she looked at the glass. 

The passenger side had frosted the way any car window frosts: loose ferns, feathers, nothing.  She turned to the driver's side ready for needles, the hard straight crystals that had grown around a client's divorce decree. 

There were no needles. 

From the prism outward the frost had grown in arcs, hundreds of them, nested one inside the next like the bands of a spectrum, each bent over a smaller one.  In three winters of ledgers she had seen arcs exactly twice, around a baby's rattle and around a mother's thimble.  Arcs meant shelter.  Arcs meant something large curved over something small, for a long time, on purpose. 

Her assumption did not crack politely; it shattered, and she had to put her other hand on the hood to stay standing.  She had been so sure of his scorn that she had built a control to measure it. 

She remembered, now, where he had stood when he said she sold fog: here, under these arches, in weather like this, with his hand flat over his shirt pocket.  A man of tolerances, standing in fog he loved, telling his daughter the only way he knew that she had gone into the family business.  "Only glass" had been the lie.  The rainbow story, told to a six-year-old, had been the truth dressed as one. 

She photographed both sides of the windshield, noted the temperature, and sketched the arcs, because the sun was coming and proof made of frost keeps short hours.  Then light came through the mist, struck the quartz, struck the prism, and laid a small spectrum across the back of her hand.  The arcs ran to water.  The hush shifted back, lighter by one. 

Two photographs would convince no newspaper, but the control had convinced her.  Wren put her father's prism in her own shirt pocket and went to open the shop, to tell the truth the only way anyone would hear it.
