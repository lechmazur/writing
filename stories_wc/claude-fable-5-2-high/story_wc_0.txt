Three days after the flood, Ines unlocked her late mother's greenhouse with a clipboard under her arm.  The storm had peeled a third of the roof panes away, and river mud lay ankle deep across the brick paths.  Her brother Tomas had called it a massacre on the phone, the night everything their mother built was murdered.  Ines had come to strip that needless drama out of the insurance statement.  It was what she did.  For nineteen years she had timed neutron stars, dead suns the size of a city that tick like clocks, and every tick reached her only after software had scrubbed it.  Interference removed, glitches flagged, outliers dropped.  Somewhere in those years the habit had left the observatory and moved in behind her eyes.  She preprocessed reality before she let herself feel it, and what survived the filter was usually small, cool, and manageable. 

So she began with measurements.  The waterline ran waist high around every wall, a brown ruled stroke, and below it the glass had been rinsed bare.  Above it the panes were not bare at all.  They were filmed with something that shimmered bronze and blue when the afternoon light came through sideways.  She touched one pane and her fingertip came away glittering.  Dust from butterfly wings.  Her mother's swallowtails and blue morphos had beaten against this glass for thirty summers, each one shedding a few scales toward the light.  Ines nearly wrote old soiling, irrelevant, and moved on. 

Then she saw that the dust lay in bands.  Near the ridge it was thick as felt.  One step down it thinned to a haze, then to a faint bloom, and each band ended in a scalloped edge, the arcs of a wiping cloth.  She knew this kind of record.  A pulsar never announces that it is slowing; you learn it by stacking decades of arrival times until the lag, a few millionths of a second, becomes undeniable.  Decay does not arrive; it accumulates. 

She set down the clipboard and read the glass the way she read residuals.  The thickest band marked the year her mother stopped climbing the tall ladder.  The haze beneath it marked the year the stepladder went into the shed for good.  Lower still, the scallops shrank and drifted to the left, toward the arm that still worked, until the last clean arcs barely cleared the flood line.  The frame agreed with the dust.  Putty had crumbled from the glazing bars years ago, and the ridge bolts the storm snapped were rusted through to the core.  There had been no massacre.  The storm had only finished, in one loud night, a sentence that had been writing itself for a decade. 

For a moment Ines felt the dry satisfaction of being right.  Then the data turned and looked at her.  Her mother had phoned every Sunday.  The top panes are getting away from me, she had said once, and my shoulder is being silly, and the morphos keep to the south corner now.  No alarm, no spike, nothing a filter would pass.  Ines had heard chatter, classified it as noise, and answered with news about her telescope time.  She had been so careful to remove drama from the world that she had removed the quiet signal with it.  Tomas shouted about one night because one night was all he could bear to see; she had seen nothing at all, and called it rigor. 

She stood in the mud with glitter on her finger and let that be true without dressing it up.  It did not need drama either.  It only needed to be entered in the record.  She picked up the clipboard and wrote the statement plainly: storm damage to a structure in long decline, owner unable to maintain it, family did not notice.  It would shrink the payout, and it was accurate. 

Something moved in the south corner.  A single morpho, ragged, clung above the waterline where her mother had said they would be, opening and closing like a slow blue pulse.  Ines fetched the stepladder from the shed, a cloth, and a bucket of rainwater.  She wiped one pane as high as she could reach, in arcs, and left the thick band at the ridge exactly where it was.  One pane a day would take until autumn.  That seemed about the right speed for learning to look.  Then she called Tomas, and for once she asked him to go slowly and tell her everything, noise included.
