Ione Varga glowed faintly in the dark, and she had never believed the reason anyone gave her for it.  The clinics called it a phosphor fault in her childhood gene patch; her grandmother had called it weather.  Ione, who audited climate ledgers for a living, trusted neither answer.  But her grandmother had walked through eighty years inside a private drizzle, a soft gray rain that followed her into kitchens and tram cars, and no ledger had ever explained that.  So Ione climbed the lavender bluff above Sorrel Valley in the week the forests held their elections, because she wanted to learn why some people carry weather with them.  Her grandmother had gone there every election decade and come home quieter, smelling of wet pine. 

Everyone knew how the elections worked now, since the root networks had been mapped: every tenth autumn the woods voted through fungus and pollen on where the canopy should migrate next.  Below her, the pines released their ballots in pale drifts, and the aurora unrolled above, green as old radar screens.  It was a terrestrially cosmic sight, solar wind poured over dirt and flowers, and it made her miss the weather satellites that had been retired when she was nine. 

She took off the tin compass ring her grandmother had left her.  Its needle, no longer than an eyelash, had never once pointed north; Ione had tested it against three calibrated instruments and filed it under sentiment.  Still, she did what the old woman had taught her and laid the ring on her tongue.  "Tin tells you who is coming," her grandmother used to say, "if you let the taste change." 

At first there was only cold metal and lavender dust.  Then the tin went salty, the way air does before fog, and a man came up the eastern path with mist wound around his shoulders like a scarf.  Coincidence, Ione wrote in her notebook, next to the time.  The taste turned to warm peach, and a girl arrived from the south in a pocket of July, crickets and all.  It turned to struck flint, and two brothers climbed out of the west sharing one small thunderhead between them.  By the ninth flavor she had stopped writing coincidence. 

They were converging on the bluff from every direction, strangers nodding to one another like commuters on a platform that no longer existed.  The fog man glanced at the ring in her hand and smiled. 

"Marta's granddaughter," he said.  "She told us you would come to prove us wrong." 

"I came to find out what you are," Ione said. 

"Ballots," said the girl in July, as if it were obvious. 

The man explained it gently: roots can only carry a vote as far as roots reach, so each distant grove tucks its chosen weather into someone who loved it as a child, and every tenth autumn those people carry it here to be counted. 

"Counted by what?" Ione asked, because a skeptic is owed a mechanism. 

He pointed at her ring. 

She looked down, and the eyelash needle was trembling.  As the last carriers stepped onto the bluff, their weathers began to converge: fog leaned into thunder, July folded into frost, and the separate breezes braided into one steady wind.  No single cloud knew the way, yet together they made a direction, an emergent compass that existed only while everyone stood there.  The lavender bowed northeast, the pollen ballots streamed northeast, and the tin needle, for the first time in Ione's life, held still and agreed.  It had never been broken; it had been one voter's instrument, waiting for a quorum. 

Then the wind faltered, as though one vote were missing.  Ione put the ring back on her tongue to see who was late, and tasted snow and ozone, the flavor of the sky itself, though the paths below stood empty.  Overhead, the aurora dipped toward the bluff like a hand, and the glow under her skin rose to meet it.  She remembered, suddenly, a birch wood far north, a sleeping bag, her grandmother's drizzle ticking on the tent, and green light she had loved before she knew what doubt was. 

"Not a phosphor fault," she said. 

"Not a fault at all," said the fog man. 

Ione stepped into the circle, and the wind steadied and swung two degrees north, her birches' small correction.  In the valley, the pines loosed their seed along the line the needle held. 

She opened her notebook, crossed out coincidence, and wrote the answer plainly: some people carry weather because a place once trusted them to bring it home.  Then, being who she was, she added a question mark, and slid the tin ring back onto her finger to wait ten years for more data.
