For eleven years, Maren Voss had been chained by the ankle to the iron counter of the prize redemption center at Halloway's Carnival.  The Authority thought it a fine joke: a seed smuggler sentenced to guard dead prizes in the shadow of the Wall.  The joke was better than they knew, because she had been caught trying to reach this very building.  Somewhere beneath its floor, her grandmother's cooperative had hidden a clandestine vault of outlawed seed: sorghum, tepary beans, desert ash, every stubborn line the Authority had banned to sell its own thirsty patents. 

Her chain allowed twelve paces, just enough to reach the broken piano that had once serenaded winners at the counter.  Inside its bench, inspectors had found only junk: seven brass keys pinned to felt that someone had ruled with five faint lines.  Maren, who had sung in the cooperative choir as a girl, saw a staff.  Each key sat on a line or in a space like a note, and read left to right they spelled the opening of the old harvest hymn: G, E, D, C, D, E, G.  The prize bins behind the counter were lettered A through G, cheapest whistle to grandest bear.  Unlock them in the hymn's order, each key in its turn, and the floor would open. 

She understood all of this in one afternoon, and then she did nothing for nine years.  A vault opened early is a vault burned, and she meant to plant resilience in dry soil, not ash.  So she smiled at the inspectors, dusted the bears, and prepared.  She worked the bolts of the tall prize shelves loose a quarter turn each month.  She learned exactly how much slack the chain had at the double doors, and how many times it would wrap their handles.  She listened to the Wall. 

This spring, the Wall began to answer.  Hammers rang on its far side every night, then on the near side too, and the patrols grew thin and frightened.  On the last night, an arrow dropped through the broken skylight and buried itself in a giant giraffe.  Its fletching was not feather but three winged seeds of desert ash, papery as moth wings, bound with red thread.  Maren sat down on the floor and wept.  Desert ash had been extinct outside the vault for a decade, or so the Authority claimed; her sister Ilse had kept one tree alive somewhere, and Ilse was telling her the fields beyond the Wall were furrowed and waiting. 

That night she finally played the hymn: G bin, E bin, D, C, D, E, G, seven keys turning like notes, and the floor beneath the counter sighed open on rows of sealed jars. 

The Authority's answer came at dawn: two men with fuel cans, ordered to burn every building along the Wall before the retreat.  Maren could not stop them, but she had never planned to; she needed only to impede them until the Wall finished falling.  She wound her chain four times through the door handles and threw her whole weight against it.  The doors shuddered, the men cursed, and the chain that had held her for eleven years held them instead.  When they ran for the side window, she kicked the nearest shelf, and nine years of loosened bolts let the whole row topple, burying the window under timber and bears. 

Then the sound came, a long groan like the earth changing its mind, and the Wall went down.  Dust rolled over the midway, and behind it came voices, hundreds, singing. 

One of the men ran.  The other, a boy really, stood with his fuel can as Maren unwound the chain and opened the doors.  She had spent eleven years composing what she would say to the Authority.  What she did was put a jar of sorghum in his hands. 

"Your mother's field is dry too," she said. 

He set down the can and carried jars until noon. 

Ilse came through the rubble with her bow on her back and a hammer that tore the chain from the counter but could not crack the shackle, and Maren would not wait.  She walked out dragging twelve paces of iron, staggering on legs that had forgotten distance, over the rubble and into the cracked field beyond.  There she fell to her knees, which was where she had meant to be.  She pulled the arrow from her belt, pressed its head into the dust to open a hole, and planted the three winged seeds of its fletching.  Around her, strangers from both sides of the fallen Wall knelt with jars, her chain lying slack in the furrow like a root.  Nothing would show for weeks, and Maren, of all people, knew how to wait.
