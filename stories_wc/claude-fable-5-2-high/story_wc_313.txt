The wind chime on the gauge hut fell silent, and Odile Marsh looked up from her ledger.  In nineteen years as levee warden she had learned that silence was a reading too.  No wind meant no waves against the earthwork, and no waves meant the reservoir would turn to glass.  She stepped out onto the Midnight Ridgeway, the crest road named for the hour the valley was flooded, and twilight lay on it like a held breath. 

Below, the drought had done its slow work.  The town of Callow was resurfacing: a steeple first, weeks ago, then chimneys, then the gray ribs of roofs, each one doubled in the still water so that the town seemed to be rising to meet itself. 

In her coat pocket was the Authority's order.  Before the autumn refill, she was to expunge Callow from the levee ledger: every house number, every family name, anything a descendant might use to file a claim.  She had not yet lifted the pen. 

Odile was known downstream as precisely humane.  She released water by the inch, never the foot, and before each release she telephoned the farmers in the order their fields would wet.  Kindness, to her, was a matter of measurement.  Erasure could not be measured, and so it troubled her. 

At the first gauge post, something pale was pinned beneath a stone.  A photograph: Callow's main street, bunting strung between lampposts, strangers squinting into an old sun.  She checked the slope for trespassers, as vigilance required, and saw only the second post, and a second stone. 

The photographs led her along the ridgeway as the light thinned.  A schoolroom.  A wedding on the chapel steps.  A brick building with DISTILLERY painted across its brow, and at the next post, its doorway, closer.  Someone had walked her rounds ahead of her, knowing she never skipped a post. 

The last photograph lay at the sluice house.  A man knelt on the distillery doorstep beside a small girl, and the girl held a bottle in both hands, its sapphire-hued label bright even in the faded print. 

Odile knew the girl's crooked collar.  She knew it as she knew her own face in still water, a moment late.  Her mother had carried her out of the valley when she was five and never spoken of Callow again, and Odile had mistaken that silence for nothing having happened. 

Now the memory rose as the town had, roof by roof.  Her father's hands, blue with ink from the label press.  The last bottle of Callow Blue, the plum liqueur the whole town drank at weddings and wakes.  His voice as he let her smooth the label flat: we will leave this under the step, Odie, for whoever tells the story next. 

She took her lantern and went down the cracked reservoir bed, past the mirrored steeple, to a doorstep she found without counting streets.  Old Mrs. Venn was sitting on it, a camera case in her lap. 

"Your father paid my husband in bottles to photograph everything," she said quietly.  "I heard what the Authority wants, and I thought the warden should know whose town she was erasing." 

Together they lifted the loosened stone.  The bottle lay in a waxed tin, and when Odile lifted it out the liqueur label showed sapphire in the lantern light, her own small thumbprint dried into one corner. 

She understood then what her measurements had been missing.  A town was not its deeds or house numbers; it was the stories its people could still tell one another, and a ledger could hold those as surely as it held water levels. 

On the ridgeway that night she did expunge one line: the Authority's entry reading no inhabitants of record.  Then she did what she always did before the water moved.  She telephoned every Callow family Mrs. Venn could name, in the order their doorsteps would drown, and promised the refill would rise one inch a night so that each of them could walk home once. 

They came at twilight for eleven evenings.  Odile poured Callow Blue in thimble measures, and each person who drank owed the ledger one story, which she wrote down exactly as it was told and read back until the others nodded.  The stories did not agree, but laid side by side they made something the whole valley could stand on. 

When the bottle was empty she soaked off the label and pasted it inside the ledger's cover, where an erasure would have been.  The water climbed over the last doorstep on the eleventh night, and Callow and its reflection closed into each other.  Up on the Midnight Ridgeway a breeze returned, and the chime on the gauge hut began, softly, to speak again.
