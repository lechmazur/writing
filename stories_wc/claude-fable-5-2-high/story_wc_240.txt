Ines Varga found breaches for a living, and she had not found this one in eleven nights.  The Hollow Quarry farm grew bioluminescent mushrooms in old limestone tunnels, and something kept getting into Gallery Six.  No alarm tripped, no lock failed, yet each morning the humidity logs showed a small wound of fresh air.  The owners had hired her as their intrusion detection specialist because she could feel a building the way other people feel weather.  She was spatially gifted to an inconvenient degree; after one walk she carried all nine galleries in her head, every volume, every draft, turning like a model in her hands.  It was the same gift that still told her, exactly, the shape of the empty half of her bed. 

Mara had been dead two years.  Ines kept her wife's laser pointer in her breast pocket, the one Mara had used to name stars for schoolchildren, its button worn to a soft dish by a thumb that no longer existed.  She took night contracts because nights were when she failed to sleep, and because she wanted, in some unbillable way, to understand the physics of letting go.  Everything she knew professionally was the physics of holding on. 

On the twelfth night she entered Gallery Six at four, well before the echo of morning birdsong, which the quarry walls threw down the vent shafts at dawn like a second, slower flock.  The mushrooms burned green on their oak logs without heat, tier above tier, a fire that had decided to be patient.  Sensors had told her nothing, so she tried light.  She pressed the laser pointer button, and Mara's red thread crossed the gallery through the spore haze.  In sealed air the motes inside the beam should have hung still.  They were drifting, steadily, toward the mesh curtain that divided the gallery from the old ventilation adit.  She held the button down and walked the beam along the current, and the model in her head corrected itself: the adit, bricked up on every drawing, was open. 

Through the mesh the red dot found a shoulder in a gray wool coat.  She knew the coat.  For six years she had ridden the first bus behind it, behind the old man inside it, who sat two rows ahead and gave her the same small nod every morning.  They had never exchanged a word; they were familiar strangers, fluent in each other's umbrellas and winters and nothing else.  He had seen her grow thin two years ago and had nodded anyway, which she had found almost unbearable in its kindness. 

"You are my breach," she said through the mesh. 

"I planted this farm," he said, not moving, his hand still on a rusted damper.  "The new owners sealed it like a vault, and a sealed mushroom dims."  "They glow to be found, and they need somewhere for their spores to go, so I open this for an hour before the birds." 

It was true; Gallery Six was the brightest room on the site, and she had logged that as an anomaly.  She should have called it in.  Instead she asked the question she had carried into every locked room for two years. 

"How does it let go of them?" 

He seemed to understand that she was not asking about security.  "It doesn't throw them," he said.  "A drop of water gathers at the foot of each spore, and when the drop finally touches it, the weight shifts, and that small shift is the entire launch."  "Nothing pushes."  "It only stops being dry." 

Ines stood in the green light with her thumb in the dish of the button, and something in her chest caught the way dry grass catches, without a sound.  She had not cried at the funeral.  She cried now, quietly, and it moved through her like a fire through a field at night, low and thorough, leaving room.  The beam trembled, and inside it the spores went on passing through the mesh, each one a released thing that was also a beginning. 

By the time she wiped her face she knew what her report would say.  She would not recommend more seals; she would revolutionize how the farm guarded itself, replacing its doors with membranes that breathed and sensors that read whatever crossed them.  Detection through permeable barriers, she would call it: know everything that passes, and let it pass. 

"They will need a consultant who understands airflow," she said.  "I am Ines." 

"Tobias," said the coat, the nod, the six years. 

She lifted her thumb from the button, and the red thread went out, and the room was no darker for it.  Down the open shaft came the first birds, and then, a breath later, their echo.
