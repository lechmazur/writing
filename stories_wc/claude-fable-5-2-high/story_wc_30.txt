After tomorrow disappeared, Oru still wound the titan gears of the library each dawn.  That had been ninety identical todays ago, by her tally of chalk marks on the brass.  The great calendar wheel had sheared the one tooth that carried the town forward, and now each dawn brought the same pale wintersun slanting through the same glass roof.  Oru was a titan-gear librarian, the last of them, small as a sparrow among cogs the size of barns, and she liked things shelved where they belonged. 

The townsfolk no longer belonged anywhere.  With no tomorrow to grow into, their selves had grown restless instead, slipping loose in sleep and migrating downhill like fish seeking a river.  Every night the sea climbed the library steps and filled the tidal atrium knee-deep, and every night the wandering identities swam in with it, slim and silver as smelt. 

So Oru had taken her grandfather's bamboo creel down from its hook.  He had been a fisherman, and she had always insisted she was nothing of the kind.  Yet each ebb she waded the atrium, scooped the bright selves into the creel, and carried them home before breakfast: the baker's patience to the baker, the ferryman's river-sense to the ferryman. 

The catch fluctuated with the moon phases.  At the dark of the moon only a few selves strayed, and the creel hung light as a hat; at the full, the whole town poured through the doors, and the wet bamboo creaked against her hip. 

It was a routine, and Oru feared routines the way she feared rust.  A day repeated without wonder would seize like an unoiled gear, and then the town would not even have today.  So she made herself notice one new marvel each morning, and wrote it in the catalog: how the baker's patience glowed amber, how a child's self leapt twice before letting itself be caught. 

Between tides she worked at the furnace under the calendar wheel, trying to cast a new tooth for tomorrow.  Eleven times she had poured pure brass, sorted and skimmed as carefully as any shelf, and eleven times the tooth had cracked on its first turn. 

Then came the ninetieth night, a full moon, and a catch she could not sort.  At the bottom of the creel lay a single fish that was plainly the baker and plainly the ferryman, amber and river-green in one skin.  Oru carried it to both doors, embarrassed, as if she had misfiled a book. 

"Leave it be," said the baker, who had spent the week dreaming of currents.  "I knead better for knowing how water moves, and he steers better for knowing how to wait."  The ferryman, flour somehow on his sleeves, only nodded, and the two of them shared the fish's light like bread. 

Oru walked back through the low gold light with an empty creel and a full head.  In the creel's weave, as always, clung the silver scales the selves shed in passing, flakes of moon she had been rinsing away for ninety mornings.  She looked at them, then at the furnace, then at her own two hands, which smelled of both ink and brine. 

Pure brass cracked because a day was never pure.  Tomorrow had to be the same as today and different from it, hard enough to bear the wheel and soft enough to give. 

So she stopped sorting.  She shook the scales into the crucible, added the brass plates from her own catalog drawers, each stamped with one tidy name, and began to smelt them together.  She did not skim the melt.  She let fixed names and wandering silver, librarian and fisherman's granddaughter, swirl into one stubborn, shimmering alloy, and she said aloud that all of it was true. 

The twelfth tooth came from the mold the color of a winter noon.  She seated it at slack tide, with the atrium floor shining wet and bare, and leaned her small weight on the lever.  The titan gears groaned, caught, and turned one notch. 

Tomorrow arrived quietly, looking a great deal like today, except that the sun stood a finger higher in the glass.  The selves still wandered a little after that, most of all near the full moon, and nobody asked her to stop them.  Oru hung the creel on its hook beside the catalog, where it belonged and did not belong.  Each morning she waded out, wound the gears, and wrote down one new marvel, and she never once ran short.
