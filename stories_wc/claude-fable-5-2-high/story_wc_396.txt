"Will you open the gate, peacekeeper, or will you only understand us?" 

Elder Brannoch's question hung in the frozen air, and Ilse Varn drew a breath to answer it.  The breath had weight. 

Behind her, the lighthouse orchard clung to its cliff, frost-pear trees ringing the old tower like pale congregants.  Below, the ice floes shifted and groaned, breaking a month earlier than any winter on record, and on them waited forty sealer families with their sleds packed.  Between ice and orchard stood one iron gate, and behind its bars the orchard keepers gripped their pruning hooks. 

Ilse understood both sides; that was her trade.  Every quarrel she had ever settled had left a thread of shadow stitched beneath her skin, until her hands and throat were laced dark with other people's grievances.  The sealers feared drowning.  The keepers feared boots on shallow roots and cookfires thawing trees that needed cold to fruit.  She felt each fear as precisely as a splinter, and her order's law told her that feeling was where her duty ended: a peacekeeper speaks, and never touches. 

In her coat pocket, her thumb found the broken crystal ball fragment.  The ball had belonged to the last lighthouse keeper, who read the floes in it until sealers and orchardists, each wanting its forecasts, tore it from one another and let it shatter on the tower stairs.  Ilse had been given the one shard nobody stole, and alone it showed a single thing: ice cracking, endlessly.  Half a vision, like half an understanding, was only dread. 

But inside the breath, she was standing again in Tamsin's vintage store, three nights ago.  The keeper's widow sold wreck-salvage from the lighthouse's ground floor: moth-soft naval coats, cracked teacups, and a wall of old mirrors, each silvered in a different decade, each tarnished its own way.  Ilse had gone in only to get warm.  Then some quiet instinct, the same one that told her when a shouting man was about to weep, made her take out the shard and hold it to the lamplight.  She did not reason it through; she simply turned, calmly, and let the mirrors look. 

Each glass caught a different facet and gave back a different glimpse.  A gilt oval showed the iron gate standing open.  A foxed shaving mirror showed sealers packing snow around pear roots with their broad, careful mittens.  A cracked ship's glass showed tents pitched on the bare rock past the last tree, and keepers' children learning to read the ice from sealers' children.  Glimpse by glimpse, moving between the frames, she had begun to reconstitute the keeper's final vision, the whole sphere of it assembling in her mind the way a face assembles from remembered features. 

The last mirror, a tall pier glass gone smoky with age, had shown her the piece she did not want.  A figure laced with shadow stood at the gate with her bare hand on the latch. 

"That's not possible," Ilse had said.  "No peacekeeper has ever touched a latch." 

Tamsin, counting buttons into a jar, had not looked up.  "My husband called that sort of thing an anomaly," she said.  "One floe drifting against the current.  He said those were the ones worth watching, because they were either lost or courageous." 

Now, on the cliff, the breath was nearly spent.  Ilse looked at Brannoch's cracked lips, at the white knuckles behind the bars, at the dark lace on her own wrist.  All those threads of shadow were empathy she had collected and never spent.  She had carried a map of everyone's pain for twenty years without once walking the ground it described.  Understanding, the vision insisted, was a direction, and a direction meant nothing until somebody moved. 

If she spoke kindly and stepped back, the law would be kept, the gate would stay shut, and the ice would decide.  If she moved, her order might unstitch her name from its rolls.  Her pulse was steady.  She already knew, the way she had known to lift the shard. 

Ilse let the breath go and answered. 

"Both," she said, and walked to the gate.  "Tents on the bare rock past the last tree, no fires among the roots, and every sealer packs snow for the pears," she told them all.  "I have seen it work, and I will stay to see it kept." 

Then, before anyone could decide to stop her, the peacekeeper put her shadowed hand on the cold iron and lifted the latch.  The gate swung inward, and below, against the current, the first sled began to climb.
