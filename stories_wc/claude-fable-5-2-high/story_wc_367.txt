The last print newspaper on the coast came out on a Tuesday, and Odalys Brenn laughed when she read her own column in it.  She always laughed when weather was coming; it was why the Harbor Ledger had called her the storm herald for thirty years.  The laugh rose in her before any barometer dropped, a bright helpless thing, and the fleet had learned to check page two before untying a single rope.  Now there was no page two.  Her final forecast had ended with a joke, because she could not bear to end it with a goodbye: "Tomorrow the news will be written on the clouds, and the dawn will come up green."  Her jokes had an awkward habit of coming true.  She had once quipped that the mayor would be outvoted by a goat, and nobody on the council liked to discuss that spring. 

So on the first night without the Ledger she climbed the emerald lighthouse under a sky crowded with stars, and began the routine as her mother had taught it.  The tower was built of green glass blocks, older than the town, and it burned no oil.  Its lantern held a wick of spun glass, a braid of clear threads as long as the stair, with one end resting in a basin on the roof where starlight pooled like cold milk.  Every night she skimmed moths from the basin, thanked the stars by name, and coaxed the gathered light down the wick.  The light could not escape the glass; it struck the inner walls too shallowly to pass, and so it bounced along every curve, whole and trapped, until it reached the cut end and poured out as a beam.  Total internal reflection, her mother had called it, though she had also called it keeping a promise.  For thirty years Odalys had only pointed that beam at the rocks.  The telling had been the paper's work. 

At three in the morning the laugh took her, so hard she had to sit down on the iron stair.  A bad one, then: a gale by breakfast, and forty boats due to leave at four with nothing to read.  She wiped her eyes, still hiccuping, and looked at the old screens.  They ringed the lantern room, green panes each bearing an iron silhouette forged by heralds dead before the Ledger set its first line of type: a gull, an anvil, a spiral, a closed fist.  Her grandmother had sung their meanings as a nursery rhyme, and the old fishermen still hummed it over their nets without knowing why.  Fist for gale, spiral for turn back.  Ancient shadows, waiting for a page large enough to hold them.  Odalys looked out at the cloud bank massing low over the harbor mouth, pale and wide as a fresh sheet of newsprint, and laughed again, differently. 

She unclamped the wick from its bracket, which no keeper had done in living memory, and took the bright end in her gloved hands to steer it.  At once the beam guttered.  She had bent the braid too sharply, and at the kink the light met the glass too steeply and bled out through the wall, spilling uselessly across the floor.  A promise broken by haste.  She breathed, eased the wick into a long gentle arc over her shoulder like a sleeping cat, and the beam came back whole.  Then she steered it, slow as a tiller in fog, through the pane with the fist. 

On the clouds above the harbor, a hand of darkness a mile tall closed inside a field of green.  She held it until her arms shook, then swung the light to the spiral, then back to the fist.  Down on the quay, lanterns stopped moving.  One by one, the small lights turned away from the boats and climbed the hill toward home, and somewhere below an old voice was singing the rhyme aloud. 

The gale arrived at breakfast, as heralded, and broke itself on an empty sea.  Behind it the sun came up, and she caught its first light in the basin and steered it down through the emerald walls, so the whole town woke inside a green dawn.  She thought of the Ledger's presses standing cold, and the ache of it sat beside her joy without spoiling it, the way a shadow sits inside a beam and gives it something to say.  Tomorrow night she would skim the moths, thank the stars, and write the news again.  She had only been joking, of course.  She laughed anyway, and this time no weather was coming at all.
