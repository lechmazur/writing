At the Anchor, sound arrived the way light arrives through snow.  The chapel sat bolted over the shaft where a great brass weight hung on its line, pinning this world to its own vertical so that nothing here could drift elsewhere.  That was why they brought the possessed to Father Enoch, and why the felted walls made every voice soft and every meaning plain. 

Dessa carried her sister in at dusk and set her on the bench like a folded coat.  Wren, a painter, did not blink, and on her lap lay a palette scraped gray except for one thumbprint of vermilion. 

"It ate the blue on Tuesday," Dessa said.  "The greens went yesterday, and when the red goes, she goes." 

Enoch nodded and unrolled the cable of his loop pedal across the floor.  He had stopped shouting commands at such guests years ago, because a command only tells a thing what you want, and a possession does not stand on wants.  It stands on assumptions nobody has looked at, and his whole ministry was to bring a lamp to them.  So he worked the way the Anchor worked: he lowered a question like a plumb bob and watched where it hung. 

He pressed the switch and spoke into the microphone.  "What does she lose by resting?" 

The question circled the chapel, wool-soft, and on its third pass Wren's mouth opened without her. 

"Nothing," said the Stillness, in a voice like dust settling.  "She is tired, I am peace, and peace is still." 

Enoch caught the last three words and looped them.  Peace is still, peace is still, peace is still, until the phrase wore thin enough to see through.  Repetition was his oldest device, and antithesis his second, so he laid one over the other. 

"A held breath is still," he said.  "Is a held breath peace?" 

He did not need an answer; a rhetorical question is a lamp, not a door.  On the palette, the vermilion dulled at its edge like a coal going out. 

Then the Stillness borrowed his method.  "You leave rests in every loop, priest," it said.  "You hang silence in this chapel the way others hang icons, so what is your silence but me, kept as a pet?" 

It was true that he decorated with silence.  He placed pauses the way a florist places lilies, and he had never asked why they comforted him.  Now he had to plumb himself, quickly, while a rim of red remained. 

He stopped speaking and listened to his own rest come around in the loop.  Each time it came, the room leaned toward the next word.  That was the difference, muffled but exact: his silence assumed a next note, and the Stillness assumed there would never be one.  And a guest that sure of itself had been invited by someone who believed the same. 

"Dessa," he said, "what was she painting when the colors began to go?" 

"Our mother," Dessa whispered.  "From memory, after the funeral, and everything was done except her red scarf." 

There it hung, the line gone taut and true.  Wren had believed that an unfinished portrait kept its subject unfinished, and that if nothing moved, nothing could end.  She had not been invaded; she had held her breath, and something had moved into the pause. 

Enoch knelt beside her, cleared the loop, and recorded one last question with four beats of silence after it. 

"If nothing may end, Wren, what was the red for?" 

The question went around, and the rest followed it, open as a held door.  The Stillness tried to fill the gap with itself, but the loop kept returning, and every return made the gap a waiting instead of an ending.  On the fifth pass it began to come apart.  It did not flee; it unraveled, thread by thread, into the things it had been knotted from: a swallowed sob, a shaking hand, a long breath finally let go. 

Wren blinked.  Her thumb found the vermilion, which was bright again and wet, and she drew one red stroke down the gray wood of the palette, the shape of a scarf in wind. 

"She is gone," Wren said, crying, and it was the first thing she had finished in a month. 

Dessa held her.  Beneath the floor, the great weight hummed on its line, steady.  Enoch switched off the pedal and left the quiet where it fell, an ordinary ornament now, one that expected morning. 

"I will need blue," Wren said, soft and perfectly clear.
