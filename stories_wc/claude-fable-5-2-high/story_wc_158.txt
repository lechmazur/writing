Oru had not slept properly since her brother died at the Seventh Whorl barricade.  Her people lived inside the Murmur, a gargantuan shell some drowned leviathan snail had left tilted on the reef, its mouth wide as a harbor, its spire lost in cloud.  The Lip clan held the bright lower chambers; the Spire clan held the high whorls, where rain collected sweet in the nacre.  For three hundred years they had killed each other over light and water, and nobody remembered that they had once been one family. 

So Oru sat awake in her alcove and wrote poems on scraped kelp-paper while the tide below threw reef shadows rippling up the pearl walls.  She envied the moonvine that climbed the shell's central column through both territories.  It took harsh noon glare and made leaves of it.  That was all she wanted from her sleepless nights: to photosynthesize pain into beauty, to stand in the burning fact of her brother's death and grow something green.  The poems stayed gray. 

Then, one night, the wind changed.  Wind in the Murmur always blew off-key, whistling flat through ten thousand wormholes bored in the ancient wall, but tonight it carried a voice down from the Spire.  Someone up there could not sleep either, and was half singing a lullaby. 

Oru's pen stopped.  She knew the tune, or rather she knew the hole in it.  Every Lip mother sang the same cradle song, and every one broke off after the fourth line, because the rest had been lost before anyone's grandmother was born.  The stranger was singing the lost half, beginning exactly where her own people fell silent. 

She was a shy woman who read her poems to no one.  Still, her mouth opened, and the first four lines went up the spiral on the crooked wind, barely louder than breath.  A long pause followed.  Then the far voice answered with the ending, and for the first time in three centuries the song was whole. 

It went on for weeks, half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind, two enemies finishing each other's sentences across centuries of longing neither had known was theirs.  Oru blushed every night and sang every night anyway.  Her aunt, who kept the clan's relics, called her shyly unstoppable, like the vine, which never argued with a wall and never once turned back. 

It was the aunt who showed her the galaxy postcard.  The founders had carried it from wherever people lived before the shell: a stiff card printed with a spiral of stars, coiled exactly like the Murmur seen from the reef.  On the back, in brown ink, a Lip founder had written to her sister in the Spire.  The quarrel is stupid, and the baby will not sleep without your half of the song; come down when the moonvine blooms.  It had never been delivered, because the first blood was spilled that same week. 

Oru read it and understood what her gray poems had been missing: an address. 

The moonvine was in bud.  On the first night of bloom she climbed to the Seventh Whorl, knees shaking, singing her four lines so the Spire sentries would hear a cradle song coming instead of a raid.  No arrow came.  At the barricade, where her brother had fallen, white blossoms were opening through the stacked shields like slow applause.  She found a hollow in the central column at the exact line between territories, and there she chose to ensconce the postcard, propped among the flowers with its message facing upward, toward the Spire.  Beneath it she tucked one poem, the first that had ever come out green.  Then she sat down, in plain bowshot, and waited. 

Near dawn an old Spire sentry came down with a lantern, read the card twice, and wiped his eyes with the back of his glove. 

"You sing flat," he said. 

"So does the wind," said Oru. 

He laid his spear among the blossoms, and because nobody on either side could bring themselves to fight over a niche full of flowers and a grandmother's handwriting, nobody did.  The elders named it the Blossom Armistice: no blade drawn while the moonvine flowered.  It was meant to last nine days.  On its first night the whole shell sang the lullaby, both halves, badly, and Oru, for the first time in a year, slept. 

But gardeners from both clans began, quietly, to tend the vine, feeding it rainwater from above and light from below, until it forgot how to stop blooming. 

Oru still mourned her brother; the joy that came was not the cloudless kind.  It was reef-shadow joy, dappled and moving, brighter for the dark that swam through it.  She wrote that down, and it came out green.
