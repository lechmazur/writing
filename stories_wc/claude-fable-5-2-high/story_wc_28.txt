After the great fire, the sky over Qadim Plaza stayed broken.  The heat had cracked it like glaze in a kiln, and now it hung above the old stones in pale shards with darkness in the seams.  Sefa knelt beneath it, steel hands flat on a stepping stone.  The foundry had made her the arms after she spent her own pulling neighbors out of the burning arcades.  They were cold in the morning and warm by noon, and she had decided that was the right temperature for a mosaicist with opinions. 

Every citizen of Qadim had a name stone set in the plaza at birth, a square of mosaic you walked across all your life.  When the council condemned someone, the stone was smashed, and a person without a stone was given to the desert.  Sefa had argued against that custom for twenty years, softly, at funerals and weddings, never once raising her voice.  People called her the gentle radical and went on smashing. 

The stone under her hands belonged to Idris, the lamp boy of the minaret, who was twelve.  At dusk the council would come with hammers, because the fire had started in his lamp room. 

Falling masonry had chipped the stone, and the chips were the trouble.  Since the fire, Sefa could not touch a broken mosaic without falling backward into what had broken it.  She could not picture tomorrow anymore, not a meal, not a roof; her mind ran only one direction, and the chipped patterns were its gutters. 

She traced the first chip, a crescent near the border, and the plaza went dark around her.  It was the night of the fire again, with wind howling off the sea and the fishing fleet lost beyond the breakwater.  She traced the second chip, a ragged star, and saw Idris at the top of the minaret, pouring the whole winter's oil into the great lamp.  He was not careless.  He was making a beacon bright enough to bring forty boats home, and it did bring them home, and then the wind took the flame down the stairs and into the city.  A mercy, then, but an aberrant one, the kind that saves a fleet and burns nine streets. 

Sefa came back to daylight with her cheek against the tiles and the boy himself crouched beside her, holding out a waterskin. 

"You go away when you touch it," Idris said. 

"I go back," she said.  "I have watched you pour that oil thirty times, and I cannot find the wickedness in it." 

"Nine streets," he said, and opened his fist.  In his palm lay a single tessera from the minaret's mosaic, a blue glass cube he had dug out of the ash.  "I kept it so I would not forget what I did." 

Sefa looked from the cube to the stone, and for the first time she read the chips as a mason instead of a witness.  The fire had knocked strokes out of his name, and what remained was one blue cube away from another word entirely: Saqi, the water bearer. 

The law knew two things to do with a guilty stone, smash it or spare it.  Sefa wanted a third thing, and she wanted it with a hardness that surprised her.  Pity alone would melt by dusk in front of the hammers; she needed to cradle empathy in arms of steel, to give it a shape the council could not bend. 

"I can rename you," she said.  "Idris will be gone, and no one will ever call you by it again, not even your mother.  Saqi will carry water to nine streets of builders until the last roof is raised, and by the oldest law a stone that names a debt cannot be smashed while the debt is owed." 

"That is not mercy," the boy whispered. 

"It is the only kind I have that will hold," Sefa said. 

He looked up at the cracked sky for a long moment, then set the blue cube in her steel palm.  She bedded it in lime where the ragged star gaped, and the old name closed into the new. 

When she pressed her hand to the stone, nothing took her backward.  The pattern was no longer a record of breaking; it was an instruction. 

The boy was shaking, so she gathered him against her chest, and her arms, cold all morning, had taken the noon sun and were warm.  "Saqi," she said, to teach them both. 

Then, for the first time since the fire, she thought about dusk, and the council, and what she would say to the hammers.  Overhead, the shards of sky held.
