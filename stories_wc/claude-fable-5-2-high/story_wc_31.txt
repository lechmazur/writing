Ines Varga lit the pier lamps on the late shift, when the day lighters had gone home and the fog came in to be counted.  She had been deaf since a fever at seven, so she knew each of the forty lamps by its heat on her knuckles and the tremble of its glass.  Tonight was the lantern release, and the town was gathering on the shingle with paper globes and brushes, inking the promises they would send up at the turn of the tide.  Luminous vows, the old people called them.  A girl selling lanterns held one out and shaped the question with her hands: would Ines send a vow this year?  The lamps come first, Ines signed back.  It was true, and that was the trouble with her.  For thirty years Ines had hidden behind true things: the lamps needed lighting, she could not hear, her brother had never said which night.  The girl tucked the lantern under Ines's arm anyway and ran off. 

In her coat pocket, worn smooth as a river stone, lay a brass tag from the Hotel Corvina, the steamer that had served as the town's floating hotel until a storm sank her at her mooring past the last lamp.  It was the tag for the skeleton key, the one that opened every door aboard, though the key itself had gone down with Teo.  He had been the night porter, and on his last evening he pressed the tag into her palm and signed, under lamp eleven, I will show you the color of the bell.  She had asked him once, as a child, what color sound was, and he had never laughed at the question.  On the back of the tag he had scratched 7-C, which was no cabin number the Corvina ever had. 

She lit lamp forty and did not turn back.  The ebb that night was the lowest of the year, and the wreck's upper deck stood out of the black water like a shelf of slate.  Ines crossed the slumped gangway and went in the way she walked her route, by wandering purposefully: no map, only the pull of where light was owed.  She let the tilted corridors turn her, past the ballroom everyone remembered, toward a door no one did.  It had rotted off its hinges, so the missing key had never mattered. 

Beyond it lay the forgotten library, knee-deep in still seawater, its swollen books fused on their shelves like sleeping barnacles, one pane of its skylight long fallen in.  Brass plates marked the bays, and she waded along them, reading with her fingertips, until she touched the seventh bay, shelf C.  There were no books on it.  There was a small closing bell on a bracket, green with thirty years, and beneath it a cork-stopped bottle holding a card in Teo's square hand.  For Ines, it said: a basin of water, a light above, strike the bell, watch.  The basin was gone, but the sea had made a basin of the whole room. 

Ines stood very still, because the oldest true thing was waiting to shelter her: she could not hear, so what could a bell be to her?  Behind it crouched the thing she had never signed to anyone, that as long as the vow stayed unkept, Teo was still on his way to keep it. 

Above the skylight, the first lanterns went up.  Then hundreds followed, a slow gold drift, and their light came down through the glass and lay trembling on the flooded floor.  She had no striker, so she took the brass tag from her pocket and rapped the bell with it.  The note climbed her wrist and settled in her teeth.  The water answered: rings ran out from the shelving, fine as thread, and each one caught the lantern light and threw it up across the ceiling and the ruined spines.  Amber, she saw, deepening to rose at its edges, thinning and going out exactly as the feeling left her bones.  So that was the color of a bell. 

She rang it again, and again, until she was sure she would know it anywhere, and then she let the room go quiet in the way the sky does after a meteor, when nobody wants to be first to breathe.  The vow was kept, and Teo was not coming.  Both were true, and for once she did not stand behind either.  She unfolded the girl's lantern, and with a burnt match wrote on it: I will come in from the end of the pier.  She hung the tag on the bell's bracket to signify that someone had answered, lit the lantern from her pole, and let it rise through the broken pane to join the rest.
