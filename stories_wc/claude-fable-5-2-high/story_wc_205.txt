Ilse Varrow had once written slogans that toppled a ministry, and now she wrote catalogue numbers on bags of dust.  The Tessaly Uprising had lasted nine weeks before it curdled into committees, and she had called herself a defunct revolutionary ever since, the way one names a dead star still showing in the sky.  The asteroid Cantabile suited her.  Its vanished builders had carved every corridor into gilded scrolls and garlands and hung stone cherubs over the reactor vents, as if excess were a form of prayer.  Since the Unveiling, when time became visible, the place glowed.  Time fell everywhere now as a fine brass light, thin over new things and thick as honey over old ones, and on Cantabile it lay in drifts.  Sometimes it blew in storms.  The builders had left instructions for those, cut in verse above every doorway: cover the mirrors when the hours rise, for glass returns what it receives.  The expedition obeyed, because an early surveyor had not, and was found before an unveiled mirror as a suit of clothes with nobody in it.  A mirror turned a storm around, and whatever stood in the reflection was carried backward down its own years. 

The dispute began with a crystal vase shard.  Ilse had sieved it from the floor of the Great Rotunda, where several hundred such fragments lay under the brass drifts.  Director Pell had his reading ready before she finished bagging it.  "A mob," he said.  "They rose against their own splendor, smashed the state vessels, and died of the victory, as such people do."  He did not look at her when he said it, which was how she knew he meant her. 

Ilse turned the shard under the lamp.  Its broken edge was scored, straight, deliberate, and one face carried half a line of engraved verse: each shall carry.  "Mobs do not measure," she said.  "Nor do they leave evidence that flatters poets," Pell answered, and filed his report that night: collapse by insurrection. 

She lay awake composing rebuttals in couplets, an old habit, and discarding them, a newer one.  If Pell was right, a whole people would enter the record as her own failure written large, and she could not bear to give the dead her ending.  She wanted to reimagine them entire, from one sliver of crystal outward, the way she had once tried to reimagine Tessaly from one line on a wall.  So she began to devise a proof out of the very thing the builders feared.  When the next storm came, she would uncover one mirror in the Rotunda, set the shard before it, and let the returning hours carry the crystal back to the moment it broke.  She would stand outside the reflection, holding the cord of the linen shroud, and let it fall after a count of ten. 

The storm arrived four days later, a brass dusk rolling through the galleries, thickening on every gilded curl.  While the others shrouded the mirrors in the dormitories, Ilse walked to the Rotunda and raised the linen.  Pell found her at the count of three, shouting, and then he stopped shouting. 

In the reflected light the shard lifted from its stand.  Several hundred others rose from the floor to join it, sketching the ghost of a vase as tall as a child, most of it missing.  Around it, faint as breath on glass, moved the builders.  The hours ran in reverse, and Ilse read them as she would a poem from its last line.  There were no clubs and no fire.  An old woman scored the crystal with a jeweler's wheel, and a long line of people each received a piece and carried it toward the docks.  The few hundred who were too old, or too fond of the gilding, laid their pieces on the floor and sat down beside them to wait. 

It was neither Pell's riot nor the clean farewell she had wanted.  It was a people dividing itself gently, and leaving some behind. 

At the count of nine the reflection widened to her sleeve, and for one breath she was twenty-six, hoarse and certain on a barricade.  She let the cord go.  The linen fell, the vase came apart into the dark, and she was old again, which she found she preferred. 

"No journal will accept a vision," he said at last, and it was nearly an apology.  "Then I will write it as a poem," Ilse said, "and let them dispute that."  She picked up the shard, which no one had carried for a thousand years, and closed her hand around its half line of verse.  Outside, the storm thinned, and time settled on the cherubs like dust on a finished page.
