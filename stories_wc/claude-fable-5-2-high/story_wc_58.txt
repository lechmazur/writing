The last ship of the year slid past the breakwater, and Tamsin Vale stood on the quay and did not wave.  She had let it go on purpose, which meant the island would hold her until spring.  In her satchel rode a ledger of obituaries, every one written for someone still breathing.  She wrote them for people about to leave, so that those who stayed behind would have an account to hold instead of a silence.  In the back pages she kept a map, inked town by town, of the places where she had watched people mend.  She called it the geography of healing, and she did not yet understand it.  A ferryman had told her the island's palace kept a tree that explained every absence, and no map of hers had such a thing on it. 

The palace stood above the harbor with its shutters closed, gilt flaking from the eaves like a second fall of leaves.  Behind it lay a walled garden already deep in shadow, though the sky over the wall was still the color of brass.  Yew hedges had grown into black corridors, and wet quince leaves lay on the paths like dropped coins.  At the center stood a bare medlar tree, and from its branches hung hundreds of threads in crimson, indigo, and saffron, each knotted to a paper tag.  An old man on a ladder was tying up one more. 

"The cook's boy," he said without turning.  "Sailed an hour ago to learn glassmaking." 

His name was Oswin, and he was keeper of the great carpet in the audience hall, which no one had walked on since the court dissolved.  Each time someone left the island, he drew a single weft thread from the palace carpet, wrote the reason on a tag, and hung it here.  When someone came home, the thread went back into the weave. 

"A gap you can name is only a gap," he said.  "It is the unnamed ones that unravel the whole." 

Tamsin read the tags by the last light: married south, gone for wages, gone to nurse a sister.  Then she found one thread of pale gold, thicker than the rest, with a tag that said nothing at all. 

Oswin came down the ladder slowly.  "From the carpet's center," he said.  "My wife, Hedda, walked out of this garden nine autumns ago and left no word, and I have never known what to write." 

Tamsin's hand went to her satchel as if to a bruise.  Her father had gone the same way when she was nine, between supper and morning, and every obituary since had been an explanation she wished someone had written for her.  She said none of this, but Oswin looked at the ledger and then at the empty harbor. 

"Nobody misses the last ship by accident," he said, and with that he intimated all he needed to: that he knew a blank tag when he saw one walking. 

"I cannot explain her," Tamsin said.  "But I can write her, if you will let me work the way I work." 

Her method was to project a presence into the place where a person had been.  She sat where he said Hedda used to sit, held the gold thread across her palm, and asked him to speak as though his wife had only gone in for a shawl. 

Haltingly, then in a flood, he did.  He told the dusk that she hummed while she pruned, that she hated the carpet's border, that she had been sad for a year and he had talked about wool.  Tamsin wrote by lantern, and what she wrote was not a reason but a woman: green-thumbed, sharp-tongued, humming, loved.  When she read it aloud, the garden seemed briefly peopled, the hedges leaning in, the threads stirring on the medlar like a held breath let go. 

Oswin took the blank tag and wrote, in a shaking hand, "Left unexplained; missed exactly." 

Tamsin understood then what her maps had been circling.  Healing had no town or coast; it lived in the short distance between an empty seat and whoever was willing to sit in it and listen. 

Oswin went inside and returned with a sea-gray weft, freshly drawn, and a clean tag.  "For whoever left you blank," he said, sitting down across from her.  "Speak as though they had only stepped out." 

The harbor below was dark, the palace above them shuttered and magnificent, and winter was coming with no ship in it.  Tamsin wound the gray thread once around her finger, looked at the old man waiting in her father's place, and began, for the first time in her life, to tell it.
