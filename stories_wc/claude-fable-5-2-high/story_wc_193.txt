The year concepts became physical, the pressure Maren Okafor carried turned to gravel.  It filled her coat pockets, then sacks, a black pebble for each sleepless night since the Lumina Circle collapsed, the pyramid scheme that swallowed her savings and, worse, the savings of eleven friends she had recruited. 

Everyone was adjusting that year.  Grudges grew thorns, rumors scuttled under doors, and promises condensed into hardware wherever they were spoken.  Maren had spoken hers aboard the Halcyon, a retired research submarine moored in the flooded quarry, where old Teodor ran a bioluminescent mushroom farm and had hired her when nobody else would.  "I will pay back every one of them," she had said, and something clinked against the deck. 

It was a submarine porthole bolt, brass, and it could not decide whether it existed.  Teodor, who read physics for pleasure, called it a quantum promise: kept and broken at once, threaded and stripped, until somebody settled the matter.  By morning it had screwed itself into the one empty socket of the observation porthole, and after that nobody dared dive.  That was a problem, because the abyssal lanterncaps, the farm's best crop, fruited only at the bottom of the quarry, where the hull groaned and the dark pressed close. 

Maren's phone had its own opinions.  Since the bolt appeared, it buzzed with messages from her own number, stamped with impossible dates.  "i w aited to see if it wou1d hold," one read.  "it d1dnt, tell Odile s orry."  Another said only, "pebbles go cle ar at the bott0m, bring them ALL."  Teodor thought the bolt had split her promise across parallel dimensions, and every other Maren was texting back her results.  Maren thought about gravel, and about what deep pressure does to carbon. 

So when Teodor asked the crew who would take the Halcyon down for the harvest, and the crew studied their boots, she raised her hand to volunteer.  "It is my bolt," she said.  "I should be the one it drowns." 

Teodor forbade extra ballast, so she wheeled six sacks of pebbles aboard under a tarp labeled COMPOST, whistling, the same way she slipped free glowcaps into the crates of customers who counted their coins twice.  The Circle had taught her to bend rules for profit; she was teaching herself to bend them for kindness. 

The Halcyon sank through green water into black.  Inside, the mushroom racks glowed in seams, blue stitched along shadow, as if the dark were a coat coming apart to show its lining.  At forty fathoms the hull began to sing, and the bolt began to flicker: brass, absence, brass.  A bead of water swelled at the porthole rim. 

Her phone convulsed.  "d0nt watch it," wrote one Maren.  "watch ing is how mine br0ke."  "a prom ise isnt s een," wrote another, the letters crawling, "its d one, be the wr ench." 

Maren understood then what the Circle had trained out of her.  For three years she had waited to learn whether she was a good person, as if goodness were a payout that would arrive from above if she only stood still in the right tier.  She set down the phone, braced her boots against the racks, and closed both hands around the flickering bolt.  For one breath her palms held nothing but cold.  Then she named them aloud, Odile first, all eleven, and turned.  The brass bit into her skin, real as a debt, and the thread caught, and the bead of water stopped growing.  She held it all the way down. 

On the quarry floor the lanterncaps opened like slow fireworks, and under the tarp the sacks cracked and chimed.  Every pebble of pressure had gone clear, squeezed by the deep into small rough diamonds that caught the mushroom light and threw it back in seams.  Maren laughed until the hull stopped singing. 

Topside, Teodor looked at the tarp, then at her, and said the compost seemed unusually valuable this season. 

She mailed eleven mushroom kits that week, each with a pouch of diamonds buried in the soil and a card in the Circle's own gaudy font.  "Congratulations, you have been selected," it read.  "No purchase necessary, and please recruit no one." 

The bolt stayed brass.  That night she typed one message to her own number and sent it into the dark between worlds: "it holds if you hold it."  It arrived, she hoped, glitched but legible.
