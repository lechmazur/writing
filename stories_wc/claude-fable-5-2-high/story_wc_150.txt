Nadira came back to Qasr Tuffah with her grandmother's house key and no belief that it would fit anything.  The house was gone, but the catacomb orchard remained: roofless burial galleries where villagers had planted apple trees centuries ago, and where roots now snaked between the bones in their niches.  She had taken a job translating for the team excavating a sealed tomb at the orchard's heart, mostly as an excuse to stand where her grandmother had stood. 

Teta had told one story until she died in a Detroit kitchen: every child of the village walked the Hour Path through the orchard to the sundial, and whoever finished the walk belonged to the place forever.  Teta had been halfway along it, age nine, when the trucks came.  Nadira's mother called the story a comfort for exiles, and Nadira, after three countries and no home in any, had stopped believing that belonging was a thing a person could finish.  Still, she wanted to be wrong. 

The week the diggers lifted the tomb's seal stones, she found the sundial.  Or rather its base: a limestone drum under the oldest tree, carved with a ring of empty sockets and a square hole at the center where the gnomon should have stood. 

"The hour stones went missing long before the trucks," said Yusuf, the caretaker, who pruned around skulls as gently as around buds.  "Roots carry things.  Nobody has finished the Path in my lifetime." 

"Carry them where?" Nadira asked, because she asked everything; she had already learned the diggers' children's names and which niche held Yusuf's great-aunt. 

He shrugged.  "Walk and see.  But walk like you mean it, and do not pull at anything the trees are holding." 

So while the team cleared the tomb's entrance shaft, she wandered.  Not aimlessly: each dawn she chose one root from the sundial tree and followed it wherever it went, down stairways, past shelves of femurs furred with moss, noting what it gripped.  The orchard seemed to notice.  Windfalls rolled a little ahead of her, or she imagined they did, and she kept her hands in her pockets and her voice low, the way one behaves in a stranger's house that might yet be one's own. 

The first hour stone lay in the lap of a seated skeleton, a wedge of limestone marked with three strokes, cradled by a root as thick as her wrist.  She did not pull.  She sat down opposite and asked the dead woman, feeling foolish, who she had been and whether she minded; when Nadira finally reached out, the root had slackened, or had always been slack, and the stone came free like a ripe apple. 

By the time the diggers reached the burial chamber, she had found nine stones and fitted them into their sockets with lime mortar Yusuf mixed for her.  By the time they had brushed the dust from the occupant, she had all twelve.  But a rebuilt dial without a gnomon told nothing, and no root led to one.  She felt the old flatness return: another journey that stopped short, like Teta's, like her own. 

Then the lead archaeologist called her down to translate an inscription. 

The tomb held no king.  It held a stonecutter, buried in walking sandals, and the words above him read: He died on the road home from the quarry, carrying the shadow-maker; let whoever completes his errand complete his rest.  Across his chest, under folded finger bones, lay a bronze blade with a square tang. 

Nadira read the line aloud twice, the second time in the village dialect, and the diggers went quiet.  So the dial had never once told the hour; the village had built its whole rite of belonging around a man who did not arrive, and walked toward him anyway. 

The archaeologist hesitated over permits and museum cases; Yusuf, at the rim of the shaft, said only that the dead man had left instructions.  They agreed on one noon, recorded and supervised, with a cast to stand there afterward. 

On the day the tomb was resealed, Nadira walked the Hour Path from the orchard gate, slowly, the bronze wrapped in her scarf, past niches she now knew by name.  She walked for the stonecutter, and for a nine-year-old girl halfway along, and, warily, for herself. 

The tang slid into the sundial base as if the stone had been holding its breath.  A thin shadow fell across the noon stone, and somewhere above her an apple let go of its branch. 

Nadira did not decide she belonged.  She only noticed that the question had stopped feeling like a door locked from the other side, and that the key in her pocket was warm. 

"Tomorrow," Yusuf said, "the children will want to walk it."
