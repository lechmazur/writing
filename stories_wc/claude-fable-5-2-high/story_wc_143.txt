Ilse Varga had finished inspecting the crater by sundown, and the verdict was the one the Launch Authority wanted.  The dormant bowl atop Mount Kessa was stable enough to hold a tracking dish, and tomorrow the helicopters would bring concrete.  She should have radioed for her ride down.  Instead she stood on the moonlit summit with a camera around her neck, watching a rocket climb from the coastal plain like a slow match, and thought about a flower she had never seen. 

Orrin, the summit's old nightwatch, had described it to her on every inspection for nine years: the frostwort, pale as breath, the last one left on the mountain.  He had never said where it grew, and he had died in March. 

Curiosity, for Ilse, was not idle; it was a tool she pointed at things, and tonight she pointed it at his empty hut.  On the table, beside the brass frame of his broken hourglass, sat a tobacco tin with her name scratched into the lid.  Inside were three blank paper strips sealed under wax, a stoppered vial holding a single grain of sand, and a note in his square hand.  The note read, "Break the wax and let the air do my talking; I mixed each ink to darken at its own hour, and you cannot hurry chemistry." 

She laughed once, because he had known her exactly, and broke the wax.  Then she waited, which was not her habit, while the wind worked at the shutters and the moon rode high above the crater. 

Near midnight the first strip browned like toast, and words surfaced: "Still here, good; now outlast the wind." 

She fed the stove and interrogated the little vial.  Why would a man who had turned that hourglass every hour of every watch for forty years save one grain of it? 

The second strip answered toward three, its letters rising violet: "Look at the grain the way you look at craters." 

She tipped it onto her palm and bent over it with her field loupe.  It was not the black scoria that covered the summit but green olivine, glassy and bright, and her own survey map showed olivine in exactly one place: a pocket beside the east vent, where the old volcano still breathed a little warmth.  Orrin had filled his hourglass from the flower's own bed. 

She returned the grain to its vial, pocketed it with the last strip, and climbed down into the crater by moonlight, testing each foothold as she had all week, for a different reason now.  Beside the east vent, in a hand's width of green sand, she found a knot of gray leaves around a bud no bigger than a thimble, shut tight. 

The wind came over the rim and went through her coat as if it were paper.  She set the camera on its tripod, crouched in the lee of a boulder, and did the one thing left to do, which was outlast it.  The hours went by without any glass to measure them. 

When the east had gone the color of weak tea, the third strip finally darkened in her glove: "It opens only in the hush, for one turn of the glass; I have no glass left to give you, so I kept back a grain." 

Then the wind stopped.  It did not ease; it ended, as though the mountain had drawn a breath and held it.  No engine sounded from the plain, the moon thinned to a chalk mark above the western rim, and in that dawning hush the bud loosened and opened, white going faintly gold where the first light touched it. 

Ilse photographed it eleven times, hardly breathing, and then lowered the camera and simply looked, because Orrin had looked, alone, for forty years.  She understood the timed strips now.  He could not stand watch beside her, so he had rationed her curiosity through the night, one answer at a time, until she was standing where he used to stand at the only hour that mattered. 

A breeze returned, and the petals folded as quietly as they had opened. 

She could not unwrite her report; the crater was sound, the dish would be built, and people would someday be glad of what it heard.  But she unstoppered the vial and let the green grain fall back into the sand it came from, the nightwatch's last minute returned to its bed.  Then she shouldered the tripod and climbed toward the rim in the thin gold light, carrying eleven frames that would have to outlast the flower, and behind her the summit kept its quiet a little longer.
