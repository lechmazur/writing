Ilse's father built bunkers for the hour when the world let go.  Once each spring, at dawn, gravity inverted for exactly sixty minutes, and anything unroofed fell into the sky.  So he poured concrete rooms with padded ceilings and sold them to frightened families, and he tested every one on his daughter. 

She could not bear them.  The walls leaned in, her ribs leaned in, her breath folded small as a letter.  Her father, who was gentle and methodical, treated her fear the way he treated any hazard: by limiting exposure. 

"Ten breaths inside, then out," he said.  "A dose you can measure is a dose you can survive." 

During those ten breaths, with nothing to look at, Ilse learned to taste.  Silence, she discovered, was never empty; it was intangibly tangible, a thing no hand could close around that still sat on the tongue with weight and temperature.  The hush of curing concrete tasted of chalk.  The hush after a hatch sealed tasted of tin.  The hush at the table where her mother no longer sat tasted of cold milk. 

She kept them in a tiny porcelain doll, hollow, no longer than her thumb, with a cork where its hair should have been.  Her father's rule governed that too: uncork for three breaths, no more, because a silence listened to overlong stopped being a flavor and became a room. 

By fourteen she owned every silence a small life could offer, and all of them were gray.  Peddlers spoke of another kind, a gilded hush that existed only above the clouds, only during the inverted hour, when even the wind forgot which way was down. 

So on the spring dawn, while her father counted customers into his newest bunker, Ilse stayed in the open yard with the doll in her fist.  He shouted her name once through the closing hatch. 

Then the ground let go. 

She fell up through cold fog, through the gray belly of the clouds, stretching, her ribs unfolding one by one, until something caught her as softly as a held breath.  It was a trellis, a lattice of pale living wood as wide as a county, grown across the top of the sky.  Vines had been trained into hammocks, and from their knots hung lanterns, blankets, and kettles, as if the whole structure had been expecting company. 

A woman with bark-brown hands pressed a warm cup on her.  "Guests fall, we catch," she said.  "When the hour ends we lower everyone home on the vines; nobody has ever been lost to the sky, whatever they tell you in the cellars." 

Ilse barely heard.  Sunrise was pouring sideways through the lattice, and the cloud tops burned like poured brass, and over everything lay a quiet so wide it had no walls at all. 

She uncorked the doll. 

One breath: honey.  Two: warm bread, bell metal just after the ringing stops.  Three, and she wanted a hundred, wanted to lie in that gold until she forgot the shape of doors. 

But a silence listened to overlong becomes a room, and she understood suddenly that a sky could be a bunker too, if you hid in it.  She thought of her father counting breaths in the dark for her sake, tasting only tin. 

She pushed the cork home. 

The woman, watching, snapped a green cutting from the trellis and tucked it into Ilse's collar.  "Plant it somewhere that needs a way up," she said. 

The hour ended like an exhale.  Ilse rode a vine down through the clouds, contracting again into weight and weather, and landed in the yard as the hatch wheel turned. 

Her father climbed out gray-faced, saw her, and could not speak.  That silence she knew without tasting: an hour of believing her gone. 

When the last customer had gone, she took his hand and led him back down the ladder into the room she hated.  "Three breaths," she said, "your rule," and she pulled the cork. 

The gilded hush came out of the doll like light from under a door.  It could not be seen, yet the concrete seemed to step back from it; the padded ceiling lifted; her ribs, astonished, stayed open.  Her father breathed once, twice, three times, and on the third he wept, a man tasting honey after a lifetime of tin. 

She corked the doll, because some doses should be saved. 

They planted the cutting beside the hatch, and he left the hatch open.  Within a week it began to sprout rungs, pale and living, climbing a little farther every morning toward the place where guests are caught.  Ilse sat beneath it each dawn, doll uncorked, three breaths, collecting the newest flavor: the silence of a door that does not need to close.
