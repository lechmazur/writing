The week after Maren Oksa forgave herself for the fire, she climbed to the sanctum in the glacier hauling a sled with a brazier, wax, clay, bronze, and a small organ she had never played.  Forgiveness had not arrived like a bell.  It had come like thaw, one drop, then another, until she could say plainly that a spark from her casting pit had burned her mother's street organ and that she had been nineteen and tired, not wicked. 

The organ had mattered because of its book.  Every midwinter her mother had cranked the folded cards through it on the harbor wall, and each run of punched holes had sung one name of someone the ice had taken, names so old that no living mouth knew how to shape them.  The town knew the names only as melodies.  After the fire it knew them as nothing. 

Maren had saved one scrap of punched card, a scorched hand's width holding the nine notes of the last name in the book, and for eleven years she had kept it in a tin like evidence.  Now she carried it against her chest like a key. 

The sanctum was a chapel of blue ice, hollowed by the first sealers, with one wall shaved so thin it worked as a window.  Beyond it the aurora was already moving, green over the pack ice, slow as breath.  From the roof hung the rope.  It was four centuries old, thick as her wrist, and every keeper since the founding had braided each new name into it while the organ played, nine strands for nine pipes, a strand lifted clear of the weave wherever its note sounded.  The rope was memory transcribed twice, voice into music, music into hemp.  But the keepers were gone, the braid had been pulled round and tight for processions, and nobody remembered which strand meant which pipe.  Yet the last braid on the rope and the scrap in her tin held the same name. 

She lit the brazier, and the sanctum began to drip. 

People in town called her technically gentle, meaning her hands were kind and the rest of her was not, and she had never argued.  A lost wax caster learns to touch a thing without leaving herself on it.  She warmed the frozen hemp with her palms until it gave, then began to reconfigure the last braid, easing the round plait open into a flat ribbon, never cutting, never changing the order in which one strand passed another.  Flattened, the weave spread, and a small gap opened under every lifted strand. 

She drew the ribbon across the ice window and held a sheet of pale beeswax behind it.  The aurora brightened, and the sky came through the rope and began to illuminate the wax with nine rows of green points, trembling, exact.  She laid the scorched scrap beside them.  Hole for point, row for row, the card and the light agreed. 

Maren made a sound she had not made since she was nineteen, half laugh, half something melting.  The scrap was no longer what she had failed to save; it was the one page that could teach her to read the rest. 

She worked up the rope all night, braid by braid, pricking each point of light into wax with a warm needle, then coaxing every braid back to its old roundness.  When the aurora thinned she had eleven names in wax and four centuries still hanging above her, and the thought did not frighten her.  Winters here were long, and she had stopped spending them on penance. 

At the brazier she packed the first wax sheet in clay, baked it until the wax ran out and left its absence behind, and poured bronze into the shape of what was missing.  That was the whole craft, she thought: you lose the soft thing on purpose so the lasting thing can take its place.  Bronze would not burn. 

She broke the clay and lifted out a thin plate pierced like the card, like the braid, like the sky.  From the sled she took the small organ she had built over eleven guilty years and never once dared to crank.  She fed in the bronze plate and turned the handle. 

Nine notes rose into the dripping blue, and they were a name, the last her mother ever played, spoken in the only voice it had left.  The ice window answered with a faint ringing, and meltwater ran down it in threads that caught the last green of the sky. 

Maren played the name again, slower, the way you say something you intend to keep.  Then she tied a red thread above the eleventh braid, so tomorrow's sky would know where to begin.
