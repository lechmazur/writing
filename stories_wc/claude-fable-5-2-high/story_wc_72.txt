After the secret left it, the statue waited for the woman to breathe again.  Her regulator hissed, then stuttered, and bubbles climbed toward the drowned dome like startled thoughts.  Ilse Vell did not murder the Regent: seven words, threaded from marble into a living skull.  The statue had kept them for three hundred years in the physic garden of Orrin's Reach, where kelp now climbed the trellises and the benches sat patiently for no one.  No chart remembered the city; only the garden remembered itself. 

Maren Vell had come down certain.  The statue had felt that certainty approach like a hull, sealed and riveted: my ancestor was a poisoner, I carry her blood, and I will bring up the proof and be done.  A stone mind cannot enter a sealed one.  Thought, like the sea, gets in only through structural weakness, and the statue's own voice leaked from the crack the flood had opened across its chest. 

Then Maren had lifted the ampoule from the silt beside the Regent's bench.  Its glass was stained foxglove purple from the inside, and along its length ran tiny etched lines.  Dosage marks, she had thought, and then: why would a murderer measure?  That hairline doubt was all the statue needed. 

Now she floated before it, gripping the ampoule like a verdict.  "You are a garden ornament," she thought at it, furious. 

"I am," the statue agreed.  "I was carved to hold a watering can, and I held it while I watched."  It threaded the next truth through that doubt carefully, the way Ilse used to thread seedlings through wire.  "Foxglove steadies a failing heart; Ilse gave the Regent two marks every night for nine years, and he lived on them." 

Maren's certainty groaned, and the crack ran wider.  One truth loosened the next, as one pane had once loosened its neighbors overhead. 

"Then she was innocent," Maren thought, and the statue felt her reaching for a new hull to seal herself inside. 

"No," said the statue, as gently as a scalpel.  It had learned from Ilse to be incisively kind, to prune exactly where the rot began and nowhere else.  "On the last night he asked for the whole ampoule, and she knew what the whole ampoule would do." 

"Why would he ask?" 

"Because that morning the glaziers told him the keystone pane was flawed, the cheap glass he had bought himself and sworn was sound."  "He could not bear to tell the city, so he told Ilse alone, and asked her for sleep."  "She wept, and poured, and ran to warn the wardens, and they exiled her for his death instead of listening."  "Eleven days after her exile began, the pane let go." 

Maren drifted, and around her the dead garden seemed to lean in: urns with their open mouths, the sundial still pointing at a sun it had not seen in centuries.  "So what was she?" 

"A woman holding a glass," the statue said.  "Not a poisoner, not a saint; she gave a mercy that was also a death, and her hands shook the whole time."  "I did not reach you to trade one certainty for another, only to make room inside it for her." 

Maren looked at the crack across its chest for a long moment.  The channel ran both ways, and through it she felt what the marble had not offered: the Regent's mind had split that last night, and the statue had heard everything. 

"You knew about the pane before she did," Maren thought.  "And you told no one." 

"I was certain stone should only watch," the statue said, and three hundred years of silt seemed to settle on the words.  "I have been certain ever since that I drowned them." 

Maren almost laughed into her regulator.  "Every mind in that city was sealed except one dying man's; whom could you have told?" 

"Ilse, perhaps, while she wept." 

"Perhaps," Maren agreed, cutting exactly where it had taught her to.  "Then you are a statue holding a watering can: not a drowner, and not blameless either." 

Something in the marble eased that had not moved since the flood. 

Maren threaded a cord through the ampoule's neck and hung it at her throat, no longer proof, only something worth being asked about.  "I will tell it whole," she promised, "marks and all." 

She rose toward the broken dome, and below her the kelp went on tending the trellises.  A small silver fish nosed along the crack in the statue's chest, and the statue, uncertain at last, let itself wonder what might grow there.
