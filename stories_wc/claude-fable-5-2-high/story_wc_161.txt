Ines Varga kept her pigeons in the attic above Tobias Hale's vintage bookshop, where mildew bloomed like pale roses on the withering spines.  She trained courier birds for one purpose: to preserve the echo of lost songs.  Her valley lay under a reservoir now, its last singers scattered to hill farms with no telephones and no patience for strangers.  So the verses came home on onionskin, one curled slip at a time, a paper trail of music tied to pink legs. 

Tobias had asked where she was from on the day she rented the attic.  "A lighthouse," she said, because the truth had been stolen from her once already.  A folklorist named Corwin had recorded her grandmother, signed his own name to every song, and sold them to a label that spelled the valley wrong.  Since then Ines had reinvented her history for everyone who asked.  To the postman she was a circus orphan.  Tobias only nodded and wrote "lighthouse" in his ledger, for he was a man who believed in provenance. 

In October he climbed the stairs with a small envelope that had fallen out of an astronomy atlas.  Inside lay a Mars meteorite chip, rust-dark and no bigger than a lentil, with a certificate tracing it from desert to dealer to collector.  "It fell forty million miles and kept its papers," he said.  "You said your brass weights were lost." 

The chip weighed exactly what an old bird could carry without strain.  From then on she balanced each outgoing slip against it on her scale, trimming the paper until song and Mars hung level.  She took the stone but not the kindness; kindness was how Corwin had begun. 

Winter withered the list of singers.  Each time a farm stopped answering, a song on her wall stayed unfinished, like a bud frozen half open.  Yet the slips themselves multiplied, pinned in rows beneath the skylight, hundreds of thin petals stirring.  Tobias brought tea and left it on the top step without coming in.  He did that for four months, and she began, against her will, to listen for his tread. 

In March he finally spoke through the door.  "The postman tells me you are a circus orphan," he said mildly.  "I don't mind which story is true, but loose paper dies in a damp attic, and I can bind."  "One book, never for sale, with every singer's name beside her verse."  Ines did not answer, and he did not ask twice. 

That week a slip arrived from Marit Oszlar, ninety-one, the last woman who knew the river lullaby whole.  It held three verses in a shaking hand and the words, "Send again soon, I am tired."  The fourth verse needed a bird in the air that day, and only old Saffron knew the route to Marit's farm.  Saffron was fading too, and a spring gale was shouldering the roof. 

Ines had always chosen her launches by mapping the swirl of dust motes under the single skylight, chalking their drift on the floorboards until the room's breath showed her the sky's.  A steady upward spiral meant a calm seam overhead.  But she could not chalk the dust, hold the skylight against the gale, and launch a frail bird with only two hands. 

She opened the attic door. 

Tobias came up slowly, and she put Saffron into his spotted hands, the worst thing she could imagine doing.  He held the bird as he held first editions, firmly and without ownership.  She weighed her request against the chip of Mars, cut away two words, and tied it on.  Then she knelt in the light and chalked the motes as they tumbled, scattered, gathered.  "Wait," she said, three times.  The dust drew itself into a rising thread.  "Now."  She threw the skylight wide, Tobias opened his hands, and Saffron climbed the calm seam like a note held past breath. 

While they waited, Ines found herself talking, and for once the story did not change as she told it: the valley, the water, her grandmother, Corwin.  Tobias crossed out "lighthouse" in his ledger and wrote nothing in its place.  "You fill that in," he said, "when you want it on paper." 

Saffron dropped through the skylight at dusk on the second day, exhausted, carrying the fourth verse. 

Marit died in April, and Saffron in May, head under her wing.  By June Tobias had sewn the slips between green boards, each verse beside its singer's name.  Ines wrote her true name and her drowned village in Tobias's ledger, and then on the flyleaf, so the songs would have provenance as honest as a stone from Mars.  Under the skylight a young bird was learning the route, and the dust rose, and fell, and rose.
