The town of Marrow Quay went silent on the fourth day, when the last evacuation bus left and the church generator ran dry.  Ines Okafor stayed, because an accident investigator is paid to arrive after everything has stopped.  She had come to hate that about the work.  Eleven years of reports, each one exact, each one late. 

The cargo plane had come down inside the container terminal, a towering labyrinth of steel boxes stacked six high along the harbor.  Its leaking solvent drums had emptied the town, but the automated cranes ran on their own power and kept working in the fog, restacking boxes for ships that would not come.  She wore a respirator and counted her steps. 

The cockpit recorder gave her one useful sentence, spoken by the flight engineer nine seconds before impact: "It is pushing us from three sides."  Wind does not usually do that. 

So she began to survey the yard the way she would survey a city, documenting wind patterns between buildings, except these buildings were forty feet long and moved every night.  She tied strips of orange tape at every corner, held an anemometer overhead at each junction, and wrote speed and bearing in a grid notebook gone soft with damp.  Lane C ran eleven knots from the sea.  Lane F ran nine, bent south by a wall of refrigerated units.  The fog made each number feel approximate even when it was not. 

By the second morning her map was wrong.  The cranes had rebuilt a third of the yard overnight, and the lanes she had measured no longer existed.  The labyrinth was rapidly adapting to a schedule nobody was left to cancel, and she would have to do the same.  She stopped drawing the yard as it was.  Instead she pulled the stacking plan from the terminal office, read it like a timetable, and drew each lane as it would be twelve hours on. 

It was on the third afternoon, in a dead-end court she had not planned to visit, that she found the flight engineer's patch.  It lay four hundred meters from the wreck, a winged gear in gray thread, among gull feathers, foam cups, and a drift of orange tape she recognized as her own.  Everything light in the yard ended up here.  She stood still and felt it: air from Lane C on her left cheek, Lane F on her right, a third stream at her back, all meeting where she stood and going up.  A singular convergence, the textbooks called it, one point where separate flows agree. 

She ran the stacking history backward to the night of the crash.  That night the convergence had stood directly beneath the approach path, a column of rising air with a sudden hole beside it.  It had existed for perhaps forty minutes, between one crane cycle and the next.  That was her finding, and it was precise, and it was late. 

She turned the patch over in her glove.  A flight engineer's whole trade was reading gauges for what they would say next, and this one had read correctly with nine seconds to spare.  Ines wanted more than nine seconds.  She wanted the habit of seeing forward and then moving, foresight as something done with the hands. 

So she ran the plan forward instead.  At dawn a heavy-lift helicopter was due to hover over the wreck and raise the fuselage.  By her drawing, tonight's restack would close Lane H at 0410 and walk the convergence east until it sat exactly there.  Her radio gave only static; the relay tower had gone quiet with the town.  A warning could not travel, so she would have to change the wind itself. 

She climbed to the crane control room, found the manual override under a film of salt, and deleted one move from the queue: the four boxes that would have sealed Lane H.  Then, because a guess is not a measurement, she walked to the wreck and tied the patch to a length of tape on a bent stanchion, where it could tell her what the air was doing.  She waited through the night with her notebook, logging it every ten minutes. 

At 0410 the cranes passed Lane H without stopping.  The patch streamed inland, steady, eleven knots from one direction only.  At dawn the helicopter came out of the haze, held its hover as if the air were a floor, and lifted the fuselage clear.  Nobody aboard would ever know what had not happened.  Ines untied the patch, bagged it for the engineer's family, and wrote the time in her notebook, early for once.
