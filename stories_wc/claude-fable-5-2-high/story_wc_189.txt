"Come inland with us before winter, Aunt Ilse," Tomas had said, and then he had run out of words. 

The question hung in the grotto like the drip that never quite fell from the longest stalactite. 

Ilse did not answer. 

She had learned in forty years of chronicling tidal pools that the truest things were never found by staring straight at them. 

So she looked at the pool instead. 

Above them the grotto's roof had fallen in before anyone kept records, and through the gap the stars leaned down, doubling themselves in nine basins of black water. 

It was slack tide, the hush between ebb and flood, when the sea had finished leaving and had not yet decided to return. 

Her ledgers were full of such hours. 

Other chroniclers before her had counted limpets and measured salt; Ilse had spent her life on a stranger question, which was what time felt like when you ran a thumb along it. 

She had found that the long middles were smooth as wet slate and told her nothing. 

The grain showed only at the seams: dusk, slack water, the week a hermit crab went naked between shells. 

And now, she supposed, this pause. 

She glanced sideways at her nephew, the way she watched shy blennies, so the looking would not startle him. 

Tomas was thirty, broad from hauling nets, and he was turning his cap in his hands exactly as he had at nine, when he broke her lantern and confessed before she noticed. 

He had carried her basket down the ninety steps tonight without being asked. 

He had counted, she realized, how often she stopped to rest. 

She loved him for the counting, and it stung anyway. 

On the ledge beside the ledger sat the jade cicada. 

The first chronicler had left it there, a thumb-sized carving gone cloudy with age, and each keeper since had used it to weigh down the open page. 

Ilse had always thought it an odd totem for a sea cave, a creature of summer trees. 

Tonight, in the gap Tomas had opened, she finally understood it. 

A cicada spends seventeen years in the dark and one afternoon splitting its own back, and the afternoon is the part worth carving. 

The old keepers had not been weighing down a page. 

They had been marking the place where one keeper stopped and the next began. 

In the lowest pool the reflected stars shivered, though no wind had found the grotto. 

A periwinkle unsealed itself and began to climb. 

Ilse never watched the sea to foresee the flood; she watched the small lives at its edges, who always knew first. 

By the same sidelong reading she could foresee the rest of this night. 

If she said no, Tomas would nod, carry the basket up, and come back every month with the question worn a little thinner, until one of them broke. 

If she said yes, the ledger would close, and forty years of seams would have no one to feel them. 

Either straight answer led somewhere drowned. 

But his cap kept turning, and his eyes, she noticed, were not on her at all. 

They were on the periwinkle. 

He was watching it climb with the stillness of a man who had forgotten he was afraid. 

Warmth moved through her like the first push of the flood. 

She lifted the jade cicada from the page, and the paper stirred, free. 

The stone was cool, then quickly not, taking her heat the way the pools took the day's. 

Here, then, was the texture she had hunted through four decades of margins: not smooth, not rough, but a ridge you could only feel while crossing it. 

She was crossing it now, and she found she did not want to hurry. 

A star slid off the rim of the gap; water whispered in the channel. 

Ilse took her nephew's wrist, stilled the cap, and set the cicada in his palm. 

"Tell me what that snail does next," she said, "and write it in your own hand, so I have something to read by your fire this winter." 

Tomas closed his fingers around the cicada, and the tide came in.
