After the magic trick failed, the fair went quiet enough to hear the cursed lake creak.  Corin had promised an orchard from a single seed, but when he whisked away the cloth there was only bare dirt, and then laughter.  Hesper the druid watched from the back with her arms folded, as she watched everything.  She knew the seed.  It had left an empty socket in the locked chest where she kept the dangerous things, and it was a gorgemaw, a flesh-eating plant seed that sleeps in soil and wakes only against warm blood.  Corin, her pupil of six years, to whom she had taught weeding and nothing else, pocketed his failure and walked out onto the ice.  Hesper took her sickle from her belt, tested its edge, and followed without hurry, the way a general walks to a parley she intends to win. 

The curse of Lake Verrow was simple: any truth half-spoken above it froze in the air and fell into the ice unheard, so the surface stayed a perfect mirror in every season, returning to each visitor only the face they brought.  Hesper had come here often, alone, hoping to discover what mirrors don't show.  The lake had only ever shown her a composed woman with a blade. 

At the lake's heart the drowned orchard turned.  A hundred apple trees, rooted in nothing, wheeled through the mist in a slow vortex, every trunk bowed toward the center, where lost things were said to sink.  Corin's footprints entered the swirl and were wiped away by the wind. 

This did not trouble her.  Hesper found lost things; it was why villagers who feared her still knocked at her gate for rings, goats, and once a child.  Her method never changed.  She stood on the last certain print, breathed until her pulse was level, and began to walk through expanding circles, each one wider than the last.  The vortex wanted her to spiral inward with everything else.  She refused it step by step, calm as a held shield line, widening against the pull. 

On the fifth circle she found the words.  They hung between two wheeling trees, a ribbon of rime letters in Corin's slanted hand, frozen in the act of being said.  It read, "I stole it because asking you for anything is like knocking on a tree, and I only wanted you to see me make something grow, and I am," and stopped.  The frozen confession was unfinished, which was why the lake had kept it. 

Hesper read it twice.  Here, at last, was what no mirror had shown her: herself, seen from the other side of the gate.  She did not flinch, but she lowered the sickle. 

He was where the sentence ended, crouched under a bowed tree, blue-lipped, his right hand trapped in his coat pocket.  The gorgemaw had woken against his thigh.  A pale stem had hooked out through the wool and bent itself twice around his wrist, and its first toothed leaf was already red. 

"Do not cut it," Corin whispered. 

"I know," she said, because a cut gorgemaw roots deeper, and she had fought enough of them to respect the enemy.  It could not be forced; it could only be persuaded to unbend, and it bent toward one thing only, which was warmth. 

Hesper laid the sickle on the ice, knelt, unfolded her arms, and offered the plant her bare palm.  It wanted more heat than her skin could give, and on this lake only a whole truth left the mouth warm. 

"I locked the chest," she said into her cupped hand, "because I was afraid you would learn everything and leave, and I have been alone so long I mistook it for strength." 

The words did not freeze.  They steamed. 

The gorgemaw began to unbend, coil by coil, leaving his wrist for her palm, and the instant its root pulled free of his pocket she closed her fist and pressed seed and stem against the ice until the cold put it back to sleep.  It bit her once, and she accepted the wound as the price of the lesson. 

"Sorry," Corin said, finishing aloud what the lake had stopped. 

Where her blood and their breath touched the ice, the silver went out of it, and the clearing spread through expanding circles, ring after ring, exactly as she walked.  Beneath the vanished mirror lay green depth, old roots, and a thousand fallen sentences beginning to rise.  The orchard slowed, and one by one its trees stood straight. 

Hesper put the sleeping seed in Corin's good hand and closed his fingers over it. 

"Tomorrow," she said, "I teach you how to hold it."
