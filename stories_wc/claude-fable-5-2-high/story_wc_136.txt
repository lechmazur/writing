Ines Marrow mapped bus routes for the city, and she had never drawn a line she had not walked twice.  Her colleagues called her maps analytically artistic, each transfer measured to the meter and then inked like a river in a prayer book.  Her father, a night conductor, had called them something else: beautiful ways of not saying anything.  He died in April, and they had left nearly everything unsaid. 

What he left her was his ticket punch, a nickel tool whose die cut a tiny tower instead of a circle.  He used to call it his phantom train ticket punch, because the Night Loop it served had been erased from every map before Ines was born.  "It ran to the fortress of unspoken truths," he would say, grinning, and she would roll her eyes and go back to her drafting table.  Tucked in the punch's leather case was a card in his slanted hand: I dare you to map the line I never showed you. 

For eight months she did what she always did with hard things, which was to retrace safe routes and swallow the words.  Then, on the last night of December, walking home down Tanner Street, she noticed the pavement.  A crack forked beneath a streetlamp into a clean chevron, and ten paces on another did the same, pointing the same way.  Ines did not trust wonder, so she measured it.  She took bearings on nine arrows with her pocket compass, and they agreed within three degrees.  Her hands shook as she sketched them, because the answer arrived whole: old rail ties were rotting under the asphalt, and the street was sagging along a buried track bed.  The Night Loop had not vanished; it had only been paved over, and it was drawing itself. 

She looked at the dare in her coat pocket, then at the road toward her warm apartment and its tidy silence.  "All right," she said to no one, voice unsteady, and followed the arrows.  At each chevron she marked her sketch map with the punch, and the little towers fell like confetti into the gutter.  The cracks led her past shuttered tanneries, through a gap in a hoarding, and down to the river flats where mist stood waist deep. 

There the last arrow ended at a terminal she had never seen on any survey.  It had a squat stone clock tower, the exact shape of the holes in her map, and a train shed whose glass had long since fallen from its iron ribs.  Inside, the vault was roofed with stars, and mist drifted along the platforms like steam from an engine that was almost there.  Her watch read four minutes to midnight, the hour that divides bad habits from new starts. 

In the conductors' room behind the ticket window stood a rack of pigeonholes, each labeled with a name.  Under MARROW she found a bundle of old tickets, one for every New Year of her life, each cut with a tower and bearing a single penciled sentence.  I should have been home more.  Your maps are better than any line I ever rode.  I came here every year to write what I could not say, and I never once dared to hand you one. 

So this was the fortress, Ines understood: not a joke, but the place where a quiet man had walled up his heart one ticket at a time.  And she had inherited the masonry. 

She carried the bundle onto the platform, under the open stars, with the mist curling at her knees.  Her whole body trembled, yet she had never been surer of a route.  Across the river the first bell of midnight began to toll. 

"I was at the depot for your last run," she said aloud, to the rails, to the dark.  "I stood behind the timetable board because I did not know how to tell you I was proud."  "I am sorry, and I miss you, and I am done drawing around it."  The words hung in the cold, visible as breath, and the mist took them down the line. 

On the twelfth bell she wrote her own sentence on a corner torn from her sketch map and punched it, and the click rang through the vault like a door unlatching.  She slid it into his pigeonhole, a ticket for a train that would never come and had, somehow, just arrived. 

Then she walked home through the first hour of the year, following the arrows backward and practicing, out loud, the things she would say to the living.  By spring the city's new transit map carried a thin silver loop that no bus served, labeled in her finest hand: Night Loop, terminating at the truth.
