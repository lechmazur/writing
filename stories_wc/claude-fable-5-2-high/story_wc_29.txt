The signal rose every night at 2:14, when the empty patch of sky between Lyra and Hercules cleared the roof of the chemistry building.  Ines Okafor knew this because she had penciled it onto the astronomical charts that papered the basement walls of KZRN, the university radio station nobody had cared about until the stars started talking.  Her title on the grant paperwork was celestial signal interpreter, which mostly meant she drank burnt coffee and decided what the static was saying.  Lately it said too much. 

To the people camped on the east lawn with candles, the voices promised that the dead were waiting just past the ionosphere.  To the people on the west lawn with bolt cutters, the same voices whispered that the dish was a door and something hungry had its foot in it.  The town had split down the middle of the quad in nine days, and every night the chanting from both sides came through the basement vents like weather. 

Ines had stopped believing either message the night she laid the recordings against the charts.  Each voice claimed a different star for a home, Vega for the comforters, Rasalgethi for the doomsayers, but the dish had never moved.  Everything came from one dark coordinate where old Professor Halloran, forty years dead, had inked a small question mark and the words nothing here, twice checked.  One source, two stories, each tuned to the fear of whoever was listening.  That was not contact.  That was a con, and Ines had grown up with an uncle who ran three-card monte outside the bus depot.  She knew tricksters when she heard them, even ones without hands. 

Upstairs, the humans were running their own power plays.  Provost Dell wanted the transmitter seized and the feed sold to whichever agency bid highest.  Tobias, the student manager who had hauled concert speakers onto the quad so both camps could hear, now wanted to cut off the west lawn and be crowned prophet of the east.  Both had visited her booth that afternoon, and both had left believing she was on their side, because she had let them.  Her uncle would have approved. 

What she had not told either of them was what lived under the voices.  Slowed and stretched on the spectrum display, the signal carried a second layer at eleven hertz, far below hearing, a pulse those big speakers turned into pressure instead of sound.  It was the reason her sternum ached at 2:14, the reason the east lawn wept and the west lawn clenched its fists.  The words were only the patter; the subsonic hum was the hand moving the cards. 

So she would interfere with the hand.  Cutting the feed would only prove each camp's worst theory, and a filter would not stop the pulse that came humming down the steel of the dish into the ground itself.  It had to be met, wave for wave.  Using Halloran's charts, she plotted the dark coordinate's climb minute by minute, because the pulse shifted as its source rose, and a counter-signal that lagged would only feed it.  She soldered a tone generator from a dead turntable motor and a drawer of capacitors, burned two fingers, and patched it into the board beneath a strip of tape labeled DO NOT. 

At 2:13 the vents carried both chants down to her, hymn and curse, braided.  At 2:14 the voices arrived, silver and intimate, and under them the pressure leaned on her ribs like a thumb on a scale.  Ines pushed the fader. 

Her own eleven hertz went out through the speakers and the floor, inverted, trough against crest, and the coffee in her mug shivered into rings and then went flat.  The ache left her chest. 

The tricksters noticed.  The pulse jumped to nine hertz, then fourteen, feinting, and the voices sweetened and began to say her name, her uncle's name.  She followed the charts instead of the voices, turning the dial to where the pencil said the source would be, not where it pretended to go, and met it there each time.  Without the hum beneath them, the words were just words, thin and pleading, a salesman talking to an emptying room. 

Through the vents, the chanting faltered on both lawns at once.  Somebody laughed, uncertain, and somebody on the other side answered it. 

At 2:51 the coordinate set behind the library and the sky went back to being sky.  Ines knew it was not won; Dell and Tobias would come pounding at dawn, and whatever sat in that dark patch would bring a new trick tomorrow.  But she sharpened her pencil, marked the next rising beside Halloran's question mark, and let herself hope, cautiously, the way you carry a full cup down stairs.
