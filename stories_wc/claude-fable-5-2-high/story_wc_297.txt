The future arrived eleven years early, wearing muddy boots and carrying a hedge clipping.  Maren felt it through her roots before she saw it: small footsteps crossing the last turn of the maze, into the cobalt glade where she had sat, bound to the earth, for thirty years.  Dusk had turned the bluebells and the blue-needled hedges to one deep color, and the girl stepped out of it like a thought Maren was not ready to think.  Her heart leapt and lurched in the same beat. 

She had dreamed this meeting many times: a scarred palm offering a clipping, and maze walls standing twice the height of a man.  By her reckoning, hedges that tall were eleven years off, and the palm belonged to a woman grown. 

"You're the one in the roots," the girl said, not frightened, only checking a fact.  "I'm Tess, and I have questions, and I brought this so you'd know I mean them."  She held up the clipping, a sprig cut from the topiary hare that guarded the maze gate, its severed end still weeping sap. 

Maren's breath caught, because that was the old sign.  Once, the village had kept a covenant with her: they trimmed the maze and brought a clipping each solstice, and she grafted it into her own bark-rough skin and dreamed alongside them.  That was her whole purpose, the reason she had let the roots take her: to find the intersection of dreams, the one place where what she foresaw crossed what her people longed for, and to tell them how to walk there.  Then the mill came with its bright lamps and steady wages, and the covenant was eclipsed.  Not broken, Maren had told herself for twenty unvisited years, only shadowed, the way the sun stays whole behind the moon.  Without clippings she dreamed alone, and her visions drifted like seeds with nowhere to land. 

"You are too soon," she said, and heard her own voice shake between joy and dread.  "I saw you coming when the hedges had doubled." 

Tess looked back at the towering walls, then at Maren, with the patience of someone fitting keys to a lock.  Her curiosity was never idle; every question was aimed at a door.  "They have doubled," she said.  "Nobody has trimmed them since before I was born, so they got there early."  "You counted years by a hedge that somebody used to cut." 

Maren stared at her.  Ten years of being heeded, twenty of being forgotten, and she had never once practiced the other side of it.  The vision had been true; her arithmetic had been proud. 

"Why did you come, Tess?" she asked, and made herself wait for the answer. 

"Because my grandmother has a scar across her palm, and the old shears in our shed have a notch that fits it, and when I asked her why, she cried."  Tess opened her own hand, where a fresh pink line crossed the lifeline.  "I tried the shears to see, and now I have one too, and nobody in the village sleeps well anymore, and I think it is all the same question." 

The scarred palm.  Maren laughed, a cracked, astonished sound, and the fear did not leave but made room. 

"Then heed me now, and I will heed you," she said, turning her wrist to show the ladder of old graft scars, one for every solstice the covenant had held.  "Wounds are where living things join."  "Lay the cut end against my last scar, and your scar over both." 

Tess did not hesitate, which frightened Maren more than hesitation would have.  Sap, bark, and small warm palm met, and the glade tilted. 

Maren fell into the village's sleep: looms clattering through every dream, a grandmother's hands closing on shears that were not there, children running down corridors that never turned.  And Tess, gasping, fell into hers: a hundred drifting futures, bright and unclaimed.  Where the two dreamings crossed, they both saw the same thing, as plainly as a path seen from above: this glade, the walls cut low enough to see over, lanterns moving through the maze toward a woman who was waiting. 

Tess pulled her hand away, eyes huge.  "That is where they meet," she whispered.  "It is not far at all." 

"It never was far," Maren said, "only dark."  The clipping stayed where they had set it, already drinking from her. 

"I will bring the shears tomorrow," Tess said, backing toward the maze, "and my grandmother, even if I have to ask her why a hundred times." 

Then she was gone into the blue, and Maren sat trembling in the last light, unsure whether anyone would follow the child back, and glad, for once, to be unsure.
