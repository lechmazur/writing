Oru mapped the city of Hesp by kneeling on it.  Hesp rode the backs of seven gargantuan turtles, and every plate of their shells grew outward in a slow spiral, adding a ridge whenever the animal changed course.  Streets drifted apart by a finger's width each year, so the builders needed a spiral-shell cartographer to tell them where their own doorsteps had gone.  Oru did the work without complaint and without conversation, which people mistook for contentment. 

The noon light lay white on the rooftops and on the sea, and at the edge of the sea, as always, stood the watchers.  They were tall pale shapes spaced along the whole horizon, too regular to be rocks, too still and silent to be anything else.  Nobody in Hesp looked at them for long. 

Eleven years ago, forty people had sailed out through the Brine Gate to question them, Oru's brother Tam among them.  They carried reed whistles, the old turtle drivers' tools, and promised to signal home in the drivers' code of long notes and short.  No signal came.  The council stopped oiling the gate, salt did the rest, and once the hinges had rusted into a single brown scab, people called it a mercy.  Oru had tried the gate with a pry bar exactly once, at night, and had gone home with bleeding palms and a decision to be patient. 

She still wore her own whistle on a cord under her shirt.  Tam had cut the pair from one flawed reed, so both stuttered on the short note, a doubled chirp he had never managed to sand away. 

This season she was charting the youngest growth on the lead turtle, out past the tanneries where the shell was still soft as a fingernail.  The ridges there bothered her.  Old ridges were scattered like weather, one for each storm dodged or current caught.  The ridges of the last eleven years came in clusters, thick and thin, with gaps between, and the clusters repeated. 

She took rubbings home and pinned them around her room until the walls looked like sheet music.  A cartographer reads in patterns, and Oru knew two pattern languages by heart: the grammar of shell growth, and the drivers' code Tam had drilled into her on the seawall when they were children.  It took her nineteen nights to crack it, laying one language over the other.  A thick ridge was a hard turn, a long note.  A thin ridge was a slight turn, a short note, and every thin ridge was doubled, a hairline beside a hairline, like a stutter. 

Someone out on the water was whistling, too far off for human ears, and the turtles were obeying.  The message had been written into the ground beneath Hesp for eleven years, and it was one word long. 

Come. 

Oru sat very still among her rubbings.  Then she fetched the founding charts from the archive and counted.  The first cartographers had drawn the watchers as ninety hairs on the rim of the world.  At noon she climbed the dead gate, shaded her eyes, and counted one hundred and thirty, no longer hairs but fingers.  Forty of them stood a little shorter than the rest. 

The sun was warm on her neck, the gulls were quarreling over fish heads, and the whole bright city was sailing gently toward the thing it had locked itself away from.  The gate had never mattered.  Hesp was the expedition now. 

She could have run to the council, but the council had let a gate rust rather than ask a question, and Oru had waited eleven years to ask hers.  She walked out to the soft rim of the lead turtle, where the newest ridge was still forming, and drew the reed whistle from her shirt.  In the drivers' code there was no word for why, so she blew the nearest thing: long, short, long, the call a driver used to ask a distant herd, who steers?  Her short note stuttered exactly as his did. 

Beneath her the shell shuddered, and for the first time in eleven years the great animal hesitated between two voices.  Somewhere behind her a street peg cracked like a knuckle.  Oru knelt, as she had knelt all her life, and laid her palm on the growing edge to read whatever answer came up through it. 

Far out in the white glare, one of the shorter watchers lifted something thin to its mouth.
