Nell Okafor had been sent to condemn a machine nobody could find.  The demolition permit for the Brandl Circus winter quarters listed "one passenger lifting device, ticket office," and the county wanted an elevator technician's signature saying it was dead.  The ticket office was a plywood octagon ten feet across, with no shaft, no rails, no pit.  Outside, its painted facade still promised WONDERS ALOFT, though cracks ran through the gilt letters like lightning through a grin.  Inside, the contractor's generator fed a single fluorescent tube that buzzed over her head like a trapped wasp. 

Nell liked the buzz.  In twenty years of shafts and machine rooms she had learned that the dead kept quiet under humming lights, and she had built a career, and a face, on never looking at them.  They rode her elevators anyway, patient passengers who weighed nothing and somehow made every car feel overloaded. 

So she did what she was good at and read the room.  Dust tells the truth about a building; she had diagnosed leaking hoistways and slipping sheaves from nothing but where the gray settled and where it refused to.  Here it lay thick as felt on the counter, the cash drawer, the rolls of unsold tickets.  But around the seller's stool it dipped into a hairline square, a seam where decades of gray had sifted down through the floor.  At each corner of the square sat a tiny cone of white plaster powder, lying on top of the gray, which meant it was newer than everything else in the room.  Four points, directly overhead, where something was still straining. 

She looked up, the way she had looked up since she was six and her father lifted a ceiling tile to show her the secret pulleys above their apartment elevator.  Every ceiling since had been a lid, and she had never once been able to leave a lid alone.  This one was plaster painted with clouds, split by a crack that followed the edges of a square too neatly to be an accident. 

Then the generator coughed, and the fluorescent buzz died off. 

In the sudden hush, in the gray light leaking through the cracked facade, a man was sitting on the counter.  He wore a watchmaker's loupe pushed up on his forehead and held his arms raised, palms up, like someone bracing a shelf that was about to fall. 

Nell's habit was to check her phone, hum, wait for the lights.  But his arms were shaking, and she knew that shake from her own shoulders at the end of every shift. 

"What are you holding?" she asked, the first words she had ever spoken to one of them. 

His eyes went to the painted clouds. 

She climbed onto the stool and pushed; the cracked square swung up on hinges, raining plaster.  Her flashlight found what the ceiling had hidden: four cables on four drums, and at the center a brass cylinder the size of a hatbox, engraved H. VOSS, HOROLOGIST.  A mainspring barrel, the heart of a clock, scaled up to lift a ticket girl through the clouds for the finale. 

It was wound tight.  Sixty years of coiled steel leaned on one cracked ratchet tooth, and the cables it hauled against ran down to a platform with nobody on it.  All that force, held ready for a weight that had stopped existing. 

"You wound it for the last show," Nell said, "and the show never came." 

Below her, Voss nodded, arms still up, a man holding nothing and unable to set it down. 

If the wreckers' claw bit that barrel, the spring would come out like a saw blade through whoever stood near.  Beside the ratchet she found the part he could no longer touch: a governor with a little leather brake, disengaged, its lever folded back.  Nell oiled the pivot, set her weight against the lever, and engaged it.  Then she lifted the pawl off the cracked tooth with a screwdriver and let go. 

The barrel turned, slow and whirring, the governor's fans beating the stale air like wings.  Under her boots the platform rose one last foot, lifting her gently among the drums, and then the spring sighed out its sixty years and was only metal. 

When she looked down, Voss had lowered his arms.  He flexed his empty hands, astonished at them, and thinned into the light coming through the cracks. 

Nell signed the form: one lifting device, decommissioned.  Outside, the generator caught and the tube began to buzz again, but she left the hatch open and did not hurry back under the noise.  Her shoulders, for once, were carrying only her.
