After the shutdown, Perihelion Station kept only its echoes.  Ione Vask walked the tether corridor toward the time dilation observation deck, and each footstep came back to her twice, as if the silence were practicing her.  She carried the family sketchbook against her chest.  Its cover bore a gilded compass rose applique, stitched there by her great-grandmother, every point bright except north, which had been left as bare gray thread.  Inside the cover, in faded ink: North gets its gold when someone finds it. 

Tonight was Arrival Night, the anniversary of the Vasks coming aboard, and custom said a Vask must go down to the deck and sketch the night sky.  Ione had kept the custom sixty-one times, beside four generations of hands.  As a child she had fallen asleep on the deck during a surge of the dilation engine, and the slow field had never entirely let her go.  She was temporally stretched: each of her breaths spanned three of anyone else's, and she had stayed young while her parents, then her cousins, then their children grew old.  The crew called her blessed and pressed their infants' foreheads to her sleeve for long life.  They also ate at other tables, because watches drifted near her and conversations lagged. 

When the shutdown order came, the evacuation pilots said her drag would spoil their jump timing.  They left her one shuttle, a blessing chalked on its hatch, and no seat among them.  On their way out they switched off the beacons.  Without beacons the shuttle's guidance showed only a blinking question, and for nine days Ione had sat in the cockpit, lost before she had even left. 

The deck door sighed open.  The deck was a glass blister at the tether's end, built so scholars could sit inside slowed time and watch the universe hurry.  In the old days the engine ran steady, and the stars wheeled past slowly enough for a pencil to follow.  Now the dying engine dragged time unevenly, and the sky beyond the glass was one shivering white smear, like chalk wiped by a sleeve.  There was nothing to sketch and nothing to steer by. 

She sat on the cold bench anyway, because custom was the only company she had, and opened the book across the reading lamp.  The pages were tracing-thin.  Lit from beneath, sixty-one skies showed through one another, and Ione stopped breathing, which for her took a while.  Every star her family had ever drawn, always from this bench, slid from page to page in a slow arc as the station precessed.  Every star but one.  Near the upper strut, a faint dot sat in the same place under her great-grandmother's firm pencil, her mother's quick one, and her own patient hand.  The arcs all curved around it. 

Her mother used to say that true north was not where a needle pointed but whatever held still while you turned.  Ione had taken it for comfort, the kind offered to a child who held still while everyone else turned away.  It had been a bearing all along. 

But a dot on paper was a guess until the sky agreed, and the sky was a smear.  She took out her pencil and looked up.  Fast eyes would find only shiver in that glare, but hers had always gathered light slowly, the way a long exposure does, the way that made faces trail and talk outrun her.  She let her gaze go slack and stopped fighting the lag.  The shiver averaged out.  The smear combed itself into rings, bright circles nested inside circles, and there, beside the upper strut, exactly where sixty-one pages promised, she caught a glimpse of one small steady point that was not a streak at all.  Then the engine lurched and the glare closed over it.  A glimpse was enough; the sky had agreed. 

Her laugh went out along the tether and came back twice, and for once the echo sounded like company.  She drew the sixty-second sky as it truly was, rings around a dot, and signed it.  Then she picked the stitches of the applique loose with her thumbnail, lifted a flake of gilt from the south point, and pressed it onto the bare gray north. 

In the shuttle she fixed the compass rose to the canopy glass, turned the ship by hand until its gold north covered the steady star, and locked the gyros there.  The evacuees had gone the other way, toward crowded ports where she would be blessed and avoided again.  Ione did not follow them.  She had a bearing no one else could see, years enough to spend on it, and a book with blank pages for skies no Vask had drawn.  Behind her the silence echoed once, and she flew.
