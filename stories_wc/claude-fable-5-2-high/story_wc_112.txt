Odile Varga fixed whatever Kessin Station asked her to fix.  The xenobiology research station hung from tethers above an ice-sheathed world, and its scientists asked often: a stuck vent, a frosted lens, a kettle.  She said yes to all of it, in the same level voice.  Dr. Hale once called her mechanically warm, like the heating ducts: dependable, metered, never asking anything back.  Odile had taken it as a compliment, because it was easier.  A sky mechanic who obliged everyone was a mechanic nobody watched.  That mattered, because Odile had an errand of her own on the mast. 

At the top of the central mast sat the magnetic nexus, a knot where the planet's field lines pinched together.  Every twilight the sills came to it, ten thousand glass-winged drifters the station existed to study, and folded themselves around the coils like frost around a nail.  The scientists logged their songs, their diets, their migrations.  Odile, scraping rime from the collar each evening, had noticed their wings.  At rest, the overlapping membranes carried fine rippled fringes, and the fringes were never the same twice.  She knew that pattern from repairing the station's imaging plates, which worked on holographic principles.  It was how a hologram looks before you light it: nonsense on the surface, a whole scene folded inside. 

Her brother Tam had taught her that, years before he fell from a tether on another station.  Break a hologram, he used to say, and every shard still holds the entire picture.  He believed time worked the same way.  Moments did not line up behind you and vanish; they lay beside one another, each one carrying all the rest, and time moved sideways through them like a hand along a shelf.  People had smiled at him kindly.  Odile wanted to prove him right, not to get him back, only so that he would stop being behind her. 

The proof had to be made within the hush of beating wings at rest.  Only then did the flock hold still enough for a reference beam, and only at twilight did the nexus pull every wing into a single surface.  So that evening she finished Hale's kettle, said it was no trouble, and climbed. 

The wind at the masthead was thin and blue and cold enough to ring.  Around her the sills slept, and their silence had a texture, like snow settling on snow.  The collar that housed the alignment laser was iced shut.  A torch would have woken them, and a hammer would have scattered them into the dark.  She set her pry bar under the lip and began to lever it, a finger's width at a time, breathing with the flock.  The ice gave with a small dry click.  One sill near her glove opened a pale eye, considered her, and closed it again. 

She seated a salvaged imaging plate beneath the collar and swung the laser, at its lowest power, across the folded wings.  Red light went into ten thousand sleeping bodies and came out as a picture.  Above the plate hung a small bright sky, and in it the flock was already flying: a rising spiral, a split into three streams, a long bend east.  It was not yesterday's departure; she had filmed that one, and it had gone west in a single ribbon.  She covered half the plate with her hand, and the whole spiral remained. 

Then Odile did something she had not done in six years on Kessin.  She keyed the radio and asked for a favor.  "Dr. Hale, I need you at the masthead before dawn," she said.  "Bring a camera, and please be quiet." 

Hale came up stiff and puzzled, and stood beside her without a word while the cold went through their boots.  Dawn on that world was not a blaze but a slow graying, and the sills woke the same way.  A wing unfolded here, another there, with a sound like pages turned in a distant room.  Then they lifted: a rising spiral, a split into three streams, a long bend east. 

Hale looked from the sky to the plate's recording, stamped nine hours earlier, and back again.  "They were carrying this morning last night," he said slowly. 

"Beside them," Odile said.  "Not ahead, not behind." 

Something in her chest that had been asleep for years opened one pale eye and, this time, did not close it.  It was not joy, exactly; it was quieter, a wing unfolding.  Hale asked, almost shyly, whether she wanted tea, and whether she would let him make it.  Odile, who had mended that kettle four times, said yes.  Beside her on the frozen rail, where there was room for one more, the morning went on arriving.
