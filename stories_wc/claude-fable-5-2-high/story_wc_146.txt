Odalys reached Kessock at dusk, when the hush of the empty town ran wildest.  Thistles owned the doorsteps, a fox watched her from a bakery window, and wind combed the roofless houses without making a sound she could name.  At the middle of town, where the upper street met the lower, stood the ancient archway, and in its crown hung the bell she had been hearing wrong for forty miles. 

She took the ebony tuning fork from her coat, knocked it on a cobblestone, and pressed its stem to the cold pier of the arch.  Ebony has no voice of its own, which was why her guild used it; the black wood carried only what the stone had to say.  The stone said it was tired. 

"It leans," said a man behind her.  He was old, with a cooper's thick wrists, and held his lantern as though he might throw it. 

"It listens," Odalys said.  "It has heard nothing for a while." 

His name was Brannoch, and he was the last of Kessock.  Nine years ago, he told her, the well had gone brackish, upper town blamed the tanneries below, lower town blamed the mine above, and each side swore never again to walk through the arch to the other.  Then, having nowhere left to walk, they all went away. 

"The masons who raised this used no mortar," Odalys said.  "They set it in balance and paved the street so every footfall would shake the stones snug, the way you tap a measure of grain to settle it."  "Feet from above, feet from below; it was built to be held up by people who needed each other." 

"And now?" 

"Now frost lifts it a hair each winter and nothing sets it down."  "The spring thaw will finish the argument, and anything worth doing here must be done before the collapse." 

Brannoch laughed sourly and told her to prop it with timber. 

"A crutch is not a balance," she said, which he found unhelpful. 

In the morning she began to walk.  From the upper well through the arch to the lower gate and back, heel and toe on the cobblestones, at the pace of a slow heart.  Where the paving had been pried up for somebody's vanished wall, she barrowed the stones home and began to rebuild the street, one cobble laid between each crossing.  Each evening she touched the fork to the pier and listened. 

Brannoch watched for eleven days, waiting for her to quit.  She was infinitely patient, and it unnerved him the way weather does.  On the twelfth day he asked what the fork said. 

"That one walker is only a rumor," she answered. 

On the thirteenth he fell in from the lower gate, grumbling, because he was lower town and would not pretend otherwise.  She only nodded and adjusted her pace until their steps crossed beneath the bell like the two hands of a clock.  That evening she let him hold the fork, and when a faint, even trembling came up the wood into his teeth, his face changed. 

"My mother's town had an arch," Odalys said, the only plain thing she ever told him.  "I was six, and I was the one supposed to count the steps." 

He wrote letters after that.  Through the winter they came back in ones and twos: a tanner's daughter with a baby, two brothers from the mine, a widow who had worn her house key on a string for nine years.  They came mostly to be proved right about their neighbors.  Odalys made no speeches; she handed each a cobble, pointed at the gap, and showed them the line to walk and the fork to hear.  People who would not speak to one another found they could at least keep time.  Keeping time, they mended a roof, then another.  Smoke rose from four chimneys, and the fox quit the bakery in disgust. 

The thaw came in a single night.  Odalys woke to meltwater ticking everywhere and found the arch sweating, its stones heaved a finger's width out of true, the bell muttering to itself.  She did not shout.  She walked, and her steps on the cobblestones were summons enough; doors opened along both streets.  Upper town came down and lower town came up, thirty pairs of feet passing under the arch, slow as a heartbeat, nobody speaking.  The hush was enormous and alive. 

She pressed the fork to the pier and felt the trembling steady and gather, until the stones eased into their seats with a sound like a held breath let go.  Overhead the bell, unstruck, hummed one true note. 

"Fixed?" Brannoch whispered. 

"Balanced," Odalys said.  "That is never finished; walk again tomorrow." 

She pocketed the fork and, for once, fell into step behind them.
