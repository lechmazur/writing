Three weeks after the memory surfaced, Ines still tasted woodsmoke when she woke.  It had come while she soldered a brass shutter, one spark leaping from the iron: another life, a girl walking flooded country with a clay firepot, relighting dead hearths.  A pilgrim of sparks, the villagers had called her.  She had died on a bridge with one ember still glowing, one house still cold. 

Ines did not know if she believed it.  She believed very little and investigated everything; she had sat with Quakers, chanted with monks, and once interviewed a dowser for an afternoon.  But the memory left her a question that would not settle: the warmth meant for that last house, never delivered, had to have gone somewhere.  Where was unused love stored? 

So she did what she always did with questions, and built a dark room around it. 

The disused railway viaduct east of town was in bloom that May, red valerian and buddleia pouring off its parapets, wisteria roping the piers.  She boarded up one empty arch beneath it, limewashed the back wall, and drilled a single hole in the boards.  Into the hole she fitted the lens she had bought from a temple-goods seller, a drop of rock crystal the woman called Kwan Yin's Tear, shed, she swore, by a statue of She Who Hears the Cries of the World.  Ines had paid for the optics, not the legend. 

At twilight the fog came up from the river, and the Tear gathered what light remained and laid the world upside down on the lime: blossoms hanging like lamps from a ceiling of grass, the sky a pale floor.  Everything was blurred and yet exact, the way a dream is exact.  She sat in the dark and waited for an answer, and got a visitor instead. 

"Is this the picture house?" an old man asked at the curtain.  "I walk the viaduct every evening, and I saw your sign." 

His name was Corwin; he had been a signalman on this line, and he sat down on her bench with the care of someone carrying something full.  In the dark she could not see his face, so she listened to him instead.  His breathing was slow and even while the inverted flowers swayed.  Then a breeze parted the wisteria, an iron bench showed through beside the seventh pier, and his breath stopped as if a hand had closed on it. 

Ines knew that catch; her own breath had been doing it for three weeks, every time she remembered the bridge. 

"Who sat there?" she asked. 

"Margit," he said, on a thin thread of air.  "She brought my supper up there for forty years, and I never once told her what it meant; I was always saving it for a better moment." 

He was still not breathing properly, only sipping, guarding the rest.  And there, in the fogged dark, Ines understood with a strange clearness what she was hearing.  This was the storehouse: the held breath, the chest locked around everything that had never been said or given.  Unused love was not kept in graves or in heavens; people carried it in the pause before the exhale, like an ember in a clay pot with the lid tied down. 

"Then this is the better moment," she said.  "Tell it to the bench, out loud, and let all the air go with it." 

He was quiet so long that she thought he would refuse.  Then he spoke her name, and forty years of thanks came out behind it on one long, ragged breath. 

The arch was cold, and his breath made mist, and the mist drifted through the thin beam from Kwan Yin's Tear.  For a moment the twilight hung in midair between them, each droplet lit, a handful of sparks.  Ines let her own breath go into it, the one she had been holding since a bridge in another life, and the sparks doubled.  Something in her chest that had been clenched for three weeks, or three centuries, began to heal. 

Corwin's breathing, when it steadied, was deep and unguarded, the breathing of a man setting down a full vessel at last. 

"She heard that," he said, not asking. 

Ines looked at the crystal, named for the one who hears, and for once did not investigate. 

Outside, the fog had taken the viaduct, and the blossoms glowed in it like banked coals.  Tomorrow she would open the arch again for whoever came; after that there were other towns, other cold houses, and she knew now what to carry to them.  She was a pilgrim of sparks still; only the fire had changed.
