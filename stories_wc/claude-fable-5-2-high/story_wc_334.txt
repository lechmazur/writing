Maren Voss left her tape recorder on the passenger seat, and that was the first habit to break.  For thirty years she had carried it into kitchens and net sheds, collecting other people's memories because one summer of her own was missing.  Now, decades later, she walked down through the dunes to find it. 

The orchard greenhouse sat where her grandfather had sunk it, six feet into a sandy hollow, out of the wind.  Around the rim lay his other windbreak: seven shipwrecks he had salvaged off the reef and dragged ashore, bows inward, like cattle around a trough. 

She remembered the hulls.  She remembered the summers before she turned nine, and the funeral the winter after.  Between them sat a blank as clean as a wiped slate.  Her mother had blamed a fever.  The village had blamed the fairy ring under the apple trees, and Maren, chronically curious even then, had grown into a folklorist mostly to find out which. 

She was the hands-on kind.  She had never written down a charm she had not tried: she had tied the wind knots, baked the bannock, and slept on the yarrow.  Only one rule had she kept without testing.  She walked around rings, never through them. 

The door stuck, then gave.  Inside, the air was cold and sweet, like a cider press in October.  The apple trees had gone feral and pushed their elbows through the walls, but the turf beneath them was still green, and in it stood the fairy ring mushrooms, small tan caps with a faint smell of almond.  They made three quarters of a circle.  On the east side the ring simply stopped, a gap as wide as a doorway. 

She crouched and pressed her palm to the bare turf.  Rings spread outward every year by habit; they did not leave gaps without a reason.  On one of her oldest tapes, a neighbor quoted her grandfather: the ring keeps what it takes until habits break.  She had played it a hundred times and never understood whose habits he meant. 

She looked up. 

He had painted the night sky on the roof panes in whitewash, to shade the trees and to teach a child her stars in daylight.  Forty winters had broken the constellations.  The Swan had lost a wing, the Plough its handle, and Orion was down to his belt.  Maren dragged a crate under them and studied the damage the way she studied any story, looking for the part that did not fit. 

The gales came from the west, and the west panes had fallen inward, their shards still lying in the moss where they had dropped.  But over the gap in the ring, on the sheltered east side, a single pane was missing from the Northern Crown.  She climbed the crate and ran her thumb along the empty frame.  The lead was bent outward.  No shard lay beneath it. 

That was how she detected it: one star had been broken from inside, by something thrown upward, by someone standing exactly where the mushrooms refused to grow.  Someone small. 

She climbed down and stood at the gap.  Every habit she owned told her to go around.  She stepped through. 

Nothing happened, so she did what her hands wanted, which was to pick a windfall from the grass and weigh it in her palm.  The weight did it.  The almond smell rose, the apple was cold, and the summer came back in one piece, sharp as a first bite. 

He had lifted her onto his shoulders here to paint the Crown, seven dots, her fingers white to the knuckle.  He had told her here, in August, that his heart was failing and the orchard would be sold.  She had thrown an apple straight through her own stars.  He had not scolded her.  He had knelt in the ring, held her wet face, and said, "Forget it, pet; forget the whole sorry summer." 

That was the whole theft: she had obeyed him, as she always did, and the ring had kept the summer until she was ready to disobey. 

Maren stood among the caps and let it hurt, the paint, the shoulders, his voice, all of it hers again.  Then, because she was who she was, she tested one more thing.  She pinched a mushroom from the end of the arc and pressed it, gills down, into the bare turf of the gap, to see whether a circle could learn to close. 

Back at the car, with the seven hulls small behind her, she picked up the recorder, pressed the red button, and for the first time told it a story of her own.
