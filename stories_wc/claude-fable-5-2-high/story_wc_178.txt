The king had fled Babylon at noon, and the conqueror was not expected before morning.  In the dusk between them, the Palace of Nine Courts belonged to no one except Ninsha, the astronomer, who was counting lamp oil.  The old king had rebuilt the palace as an instrument rather than a home.  Its walls were slotted for sightlines, its canals dripped the hours, and its glazed bulls carried gnomons instead of crowns.  Now the priests had carted off the moon god's statues, the foreign gods had not yet arrived, and the instrument pointed at nothing official. 

Ninsha had three jars of oil, one stylus worn to a thumb's length, and a contract tablet she had smoothed and reused eleven times.  She wasted nothing, because her teacher Belu had wasted nothing.  On the tablet she had drafted a covenant: I will keep the watch under any king, and in return let the sky keep being worth watching.  Every contract needs a second party, and there the clay was still blank.  She could not name a god who might be melted down by spring.  She wanted to seal a covenant with the unknown itself, and the unknown had no name. 

Belu, dead two winters, had left her his oil ledger and a cipher.  The cipher was a blue tile set in the threshold of his cell, a magic square of nine smaller squares, each stamped with a number.  Every row, column, and diagonal summed to fifteen, the days from a dark moon to a full one.  Only the center was left unglazed, bare clay where the five should be.  For years she had taken it for a craftsman's flaw, the kind of economy Belu would have excused.  Tonight, with no master to bill for her time, she decided to test it. 

She worked the way he had taught her, through diagrams, because a thing drawn small can be checked.  On the back of her tablet she scratched the tile's grid.  Beside it she scratched the palace, and felt her neck prickle, because the Nine Courts made the same grid, three by three.  She walked the plan with a measuring cord before the light went.  In the court that matched the tile's one, a sighting slit faced the slit in the court of nine; two faced eight, three faced seven, four faced six.  On her schematic the four lines crossed in the central court, the one Belu had kept shut under a roof of felt and entered in the accounts as a cistern. 

Then she opened the oil ledger, which she had always read as a sermon on thrift.  Night after night, Belu had written the same entry: no lamp lit, oil unspent.  She tallied the entries against the calendar and drew them as a third diagram, a strip of marks under the phases of the moon.  The unlit nights ran fifteen at a time, dark moon to full, across forty years, and beside each run he had drawn one small empty square. 

He had not been saving oil.  He had been sitting in the dark at the crossing of his four lines, keeping vigil with something he never named, under three kings who each required a name.  The irony was one she could verify to the finger-width: the thrift she had copied from him as a discipline had been his worship, and her own careful arithmetic had just proved that the most important figure was the one left out. 

She carried the tablet to the central court and hauled on the ropes to uncloak it.  The felt slid away in gray folds, heavy with two winters of dust.  Beneath lay a square pool, black and perfectly still, and above it the last violet of the evening.  Stars came out along the pool's edges, the Bull, the Twins, the Wagon, each where her charts promised.  In the middle of the water, held like a number in a frame, floated a darkness with nothing in it.  Belu had spent his life adding the sky up around that gap and refusing to fill it in. 

From the Ishtar Gate came a faint drumming, the new regime tuning itself beyond the walls, no more urgent than the canals dripping the hour.  She did not light a lamp.  She rolled her cylinder seal across the foot of the covenant and left the second party's line as smooth as the tile's heart.  The blank was not an omission now; it was an address.  She set the tablet at the water's edge, where the dark patch and its reflection could both read it, and settled in to watch.  In the morning she would open a ledger of her own: no lamp lit, oil unspent, one night.
