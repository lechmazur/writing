Oriel Vane worked only when the moon went red.  Certain locks show their keyholes in no other light, and for thirty years she had chased eclipses, opening what others could not, then leaving before the hinges finished their sigh.  She had never once stayed to mend anything. 

Tonight she climbed the last ice-glazed stair to the library above the clouds, carrying her grandfather's driftwood box against her ribs.  He had built it from a plank the tide gave him, and filled its little compartments with his clockmaker's gear assortment: brass wheels, pinions, an escapement no bigger than a fish scale, all sorted by a logic he never explained.  "Opening is a trick," he used to say, mending the harbor clock with salt on his knuckles.  "Repair has a rhythm, and you have to stand still to hear it."  She had not stood still, and he had died while she was away opening a vault in another country. 

The library had been frozen for a century.  Frost furred the spines, and every aisle ended in white silence.  At its center stood the hall of mirrors, a ring of tall silvered panels that had once turned on hidden wheels, passing daylight down through the stacks like water through a mill until the whole building was warm enough to read in.  Then the wheels had stopped, and the cold had come in to stay. 

Under the full moon the hall was hauntingly bright, each mirror throwing the glare to the next until the room became a furnace of cold light in which no one could find the mechanism's door, let alone its lock.  Oriel waited at the threshold with her eyes shut.  When the earth's shadow finally slid across the moon, the brightness drained to the color of old copper, and a seam appeared in the floor at the center of the ring.  The lock took her four minutes. 

Beneath the hatch lay the gear train, rimed and still, with five empty arbors where wheels had cracked and fallen away.  This was where she would ordinarily pack her picks and go.  Instead she looked up, and thirty copper-lit Oriels looked back, each one already half turned toward the stairs.  She did not like how practiced they seemed. 

She opened the box, but the compartments told her nothing; she knew a hundred ways into a mechanism and not one way to make it whole.  Scratched inside the hatch lid was a single line from the last keeper: For the one who opens, look where the cold began.  The cold would have begun in the corner farthest from the mirrors, so she went there, and behind an atlas welded to its shelf by ice she found a tobacco tin of paper scraps. 

They were prophecy, torn small, in a hand shaky with cold.  You will come in a red hour.  You will have opened everything and mended nothing.  Seat first the wheel that bears the most, and last the one that bears least.  Between each, wait for the train to settle, and count the waiting, not the teeth.  Oil it with something you cannot spare. 

She carried the scraps back and laid them along the hatch's edge like a tide line.  Then she looked into the box again and understood her grandfather's sorting at last: not by size or metal, but by burden, in the very order a stopped thing asks for its parts.  He had been leaving her instructions all along. 

In the last compartment lay his vial of clock oil, sealed since the week he died.  She had never dared uncap it, because the smell inside was his workshop, low tide and warm brass, and a smell can only be spent once.  Her thumb rested on the cap a long moment.  Then she uncapped it, and he was briefly everywhere in the copper dark. 

She oiled the arbors and seated the first wheel.  She waited, counting the waiting, while the train creaked and settled like a hull finding its mooring.  Second wheel, longer wait; third, longer still.  Somewhere in the fourth pause she noticed her hands had stopped hurrying, and that the pauses were the rhythm, the stillness he had meant. 

The fish-scale escapement went in last.  A tick answered, then another, and around her the mirrors shuddered loose of their frost and began, very slowly, to turn. 

The eclipse was ending.  Under the soft hush of a distant morning, hours off yet at the rim of the clouds, the mirrors were already angling to meet it.  In every pane, Oriel saw a woman kneeling by an open box, not leaving.  She recapped the empty vial, set it back in its compartment, and stayed to listen.
