The terrace at Anvar Heights had outlasted nine empires, and its flagstones showed every one of them in cracks.  Maren Oduya climbed to it on midsummer evening with an ebony clipboard under her arm and a war older than her grandmother at her back.  Above the broken balustrade, the two wandering stars the Vesh called the Sisters and the Karr called the Duelists were drifting toward each other, as they did once every sixty years.  Both peoples agreed on one thing only: while those lights danced, no blade could be drawn on this stone.  Maren had built her whole plan on that one shared superstition. 

She had negotiated eleven truces, and all eleven had died of eloquence.  Fine words about brotherhood floated off like smoke, because nothing held them to the ground.  This time she wanted peace nailed to something heavy: names, wells, dates, a debt that could be counted. 

The delegations arrived from opposite stairs, General Tascar of the Karr with his scarred jaw, Speaker Ilun of the Vesh with her dead son's ring on a cord.  Maren greeted each in a low, even voice and poured their water herself.  Her gentleness was not a temperament; it was a tool, chosen the way a surgeon chooses a fine blade, because rough hands tear what they mean to mend. 

"Before we speak of borders," she said, "we will hold a muster." 

Tascar's eyes narrowed, for every soldier on both sides knew the dawn muster, the roll called before a march, the answering shout of the living.  Maren laid the clipboard on the old altar stone between them.  It was black ebony, split down one corner and mended with a brass staple, and she had carried it as a Vesh artillery captain long before she carried it as a negotiator. 

"Tonight we call the roll of those who cannot answer," she said.  "Each of you will read ten names of the other side's dead, and say where they fell, and the stars will witness that you said them." 

Ilun's hand closed around the ring.  "You ask me to honor my son's killers." 

"I ask you to count them," Maren said softly, "because what we will not count, we will spend again." 

Then she did what she had dreaded for thirty years, and read first.  The top sheet was her own muster, written in her own hand: forty-one Karr villagers of Hollin Ford, burned when her battery fired on a granary she had been told was an armory.  She had not been told to check, and she had not checked.  Her voice cracked on the ninth name, a child's, and she let it crack, and kept reading until the sheet was done. 

Tascar stared at her across the altar.  "My mother's sister lived at Hollin Ford," he said. 

"I know," Maren said.  "She is the twenty-third name." 

"And what do you pay for this, Captain, besides a pretty confession under pretty stars?" 

She had expected the question; she had written the answer into the draft treaty as its first article.  "When the truce is signed, I surrender my safe conduct and go to Hollin Ford, to rebuild the granary with my hands and stand before whatever court its people choose."  "Redemption is not a feeling, General."  "It has a price, and I would rather pay mine than preach yours." 

Overhead the two stars had closed to a finger's width, one faintly red, one white, neither quite matching the other.  The warm wind carried thyme up from the ruined gardens. 

Tascar took the clipboard, turned to the second sheet, and in a voice like gravel began to read Vesh names.  He mispronounced three of them, and Ilun, weeping with fury, corrected him each time.  The tenth name was her son's, and he said it correctly.  Then Ilun read the Karr dead, slowly, as if each syllable were a stone she had to lift. 

It was not forgiveness.  By the time the stars touched, what they had was smaller and uglier than peace: one year's truce, three shared wells, a joint ledger of the dead to be kept on this terrace.  They signed it on the ebony board, and Tascar's pen skipped where the brass staple crossed the crack. 

Maren left the clipboard on the altar, for it belonged to the ledger now, and unpinned the white negotiator's cord from her collar.  Without it she was only an old captain walking down the Karr stair toward a burned village and a judgment she could not predict.  Behind her the Sisters, or the Duelists, began slowly to part, and for the first night in her life, nobody was firing.
