The whistle of the evening train struck the gorge walls, came back once, thinner, and was gone.  Ilse Varga released the brake, and her gondola slid off the valley dock into the quiet that followed.  Ahead, the prism terrace drifted on its long tethers, a raft of faceted glass riding the thermals between the low rail line and the high one.  Nobody lived there.  Everyone who stepped onto it was in transit, between trains, between towns, between whoever they had been at the last station and whoever would climb down at the next. 

The crossing took eleven minutes, and for eleven minutes the gorge rose around the cabin like a cathedral with the roof taken off.  Ilse's father had filled that vault by humming.  Since his funeral she had run the car without a voice worth lending it, and she had watched what the hush did to people. 

She had also measured it.  Beside the brake lever sat a brass sound gauge, and after every crossing she logged its needle in her father's ledger.  The pattern never varied: forty seconds after the echo died, the needle jumped.  Coughs, shuffled boots, talk about nothing.  The quieter the gorge, the louder the cabin. 

Ilse reasoned the way water finds a valley, following whatever slope the evidence offered.  If people poured noise into silence, then they felt silence as a hole.  A hole could not be argued with, but it could be reshaped; a hole with a purpose was a cup.  So the problem was not to end the silence but to teach it how to comfort, as her father's humming once had. 

Her means hung from the ceiling hook: his storm glass vial, a sealed finger of clouded liquid he had used to guess the weather.  Her ledger had caught something he never noticed.  On the rare crossings when the needle stayed low, the cabin stopped trembling with fidgets, and the vial grew crystals, white ferns climbing the glass.  Stillness made something visible.  But the passengers needed to see the ferns in order to be still, and needed to be still in order to see them.  A closed loop wanted a catalyst, one small thing that starts a reaction and is not used up by it. 

Tonight there were four passengers, and the smallest was a girl of about nine with a cardboard tag on her coat, traveling alone from one parent to the other.  At thirty seconds the girl pressed her mittens over her ears, as if the quiet were the loud thing.  At forty the needle twitched upward; a man cleared his throat, and a woman began to describe her luggage to no one. 

Ilse could not hush four strangers, but she might catalyze one.  She unhooked the vial and knelt. 

"I need a second pair of hands," she said, low.  "This only grows when somebody is listening, and I have to steer." 

The girl lowered one mitten.  "Listening to what?" 

"To this," Ilse said, and let the gorge answer for her. 

The girl took the vial in both hands and held it the way one holds a cup filled to the brim.  Her feet stopped swinging.  Ilse went back to the lever and watched the gauge instead of the child, because the gauge would not flatter her. 

The needle hesitated, then sank.  The woman, noticing the girl's fierce attention, let her sentence about luggage go unfinished.  The man leaned in to look.  In the clouded liquid a first white thread appeared, then branched, then branched again. 

A gust shouldered the car, and the terrace swung wide on its tethers so that Ilse had to pay out cable to follow it.  The girl gasped, the needle leapt, and Ilse braced for the old flood of noise.  Instead the girl whispered, "Shh, it is still growing," and three adults obeyed her. 

The needle settled lower than Ilse had ever logged it with passengers aboard.  The hush did not press down now; it arched over them, high and held, and four strangers stood inside it together as if under one roof. 

They docked at the terrace, where the last sun broke through the prisms and laid slow colors across the ferned glass.  The girl handed the vial back with care. 

"Does it come with me?" 

"The vial stays with the car," Ilse said.  "The listening goes wherever you do." 

The girl nodded, considering this, and walked toward the high line platform with her mittens in her pockets. 

Ilse made the return alone, the vial on its hook again, used but not used up, ready for the next crossing.  The needle lay at zero, and the quiet in the cabin sounded, at last, like her father resting between songs.
