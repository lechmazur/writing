Where five alleys crossed beneath a ragged hole in the rooftops, the locksmith called Wick kept her stall.  Nobody knew her other name, or where she slept, or why her only fee was the empty envelope a secret had arrived in.  She pinned them to her awning like shed skins, each one proof that an idea had slipped out from behind somebody's fear.  People named the crossing the Knot, and they came at dusk, because Wick worked only across the hush of cosmic possibility, between sundown and full dark, when a single star owns the sky and nothing has been decided. 

Tobias Renn arrived with a brass cylinder under one arm and a gray envelope held like a wound. 

"I built the lock myself," he said.  "Three rings, no numbers."  "I spun them with my eyes shut so I could never undo it in a weak moment." 

"And inside?" 

"Drawings for a bridge across Sorrow Gorge, one span, no piers."  "The guild laughed, only my wife Lena believed, and when she fell sick I grew afraid her believing was the only thing holding it up."  "She watched me lock it away."  "She left me this the week before she died." 

Wick opened the envelope without asking, the way other people sweep a floor, as if boldness were simply a chore that needed doing.  Out slid a lock of dark hair tied with blue ribbon, and a card in a woman's hand: Ask the first star. 

"I have asked every star there is," Tobias said. 

"That is your trouble," said Wick.  "Too many opinions." 

She was a small woman with a voice like a drawer closing, so it startled him when she took his shoulders and turned him.  Three times around, four, five, until the alley mouths smeared into one dark ring and he could not have said which one led home. 

"What are you doing?" 

"Fear is a careful navigator," Wick said.  "It always knows the way back to where it is safe."  "So I disorient it." 

He stood swaying in the shadowed crossing with nothing fixed anywhere except the torn patch of sky.  And there, low above one alley mouth, the first star came out, small and patient as a lit window. 

"Which alley is that?" Wick asked. 

Stripped of every other bearing, he knew it the way a tongue knows a missing tooth.  "The Ropewalk."  "It runs to the gorge."  "We walked it every twilight, and at the edge she would pull the ribbon from her hair, hold it up against that star, and tell me the far side was only three knots away."  "I thought she was teasing." 

Wick lifted the lock of hair into the starlight.  Along the ribbon's tail, almost too small to see, sat three knots, unevenly spaced. 

"You shut your eyes when you spun the rings," she said.  "She did not." 

She wound the ribbon around the cylinder, bow against the seam, and rolled each ring backward until its notch met a knot.  The first ring clicked, then the second. 

Tobias caught her wrist before the third.  "What if it cannot stand?"  "What if she was only being kind?" 

"Then it will fail in the open, where somebody can mend it," Wick said.  "Nothing gets mended inside brass."  "And a woman does not count clicks at her husband's shoulder, then tie them into her own hair, out of kindness."  "That is a wager." 

He let go. 

The third ring clicked, and the cylinder sighed open like something that had been holding its breath for years.  Wick turned her face away, as she always did; every secret came to her enveloped in others, and the innermost was never hers to read.  She heard paper unroll, and then a sound from Tobias that was half laugh and half sob. 

"Her handwriting is in every margin," he said.  "She checked every sum before I locked them away, and I never dared look."  "She was never just believing." 

He rolled the drawings and bound them with the ribbon, hair and all.  Then he looked around the Knot, still lost, and found he knew only one direction.  He walked down the Ropewalk toward the gorge, under the star, carrying a bridge. 

Wick pinned his empty envelope among the others.  One envelope up there was still sealed, addressed to a name nobody in the city knew, and she touched its corner the way a person touches a door they are not ready to knock on.  More stars would arrive soon, crowding the hush with their opinions.  While the first still had the sky to itself, Wick shut her eyes and turned, once, twice, three times, to learn how it felt to lose the way back.
