Maren turned hourglasses for a living, three hundred of them, in the amber warehouse beneath the cliffs.  Its walls were poured resin, older than the town, and daylight came through them the color of steeped tea.  Most of her glasses measured small things: a loaf rising, a debt, a mourning.  Only the Last Glass, tall as a door and bolted to the floor, was never to be turned.  Beside it, on a shelf of its own, sat the canyon echo in a jar.  Everyone in Harrow Drop knew what the jar said, because the first turner had carved it over the door: WHEN THE SAND ENDS WE WILL FALL SILENT.  The sand had nine days left.  Already the town was practicing; half the houses stood shuttered, the bell rope was cut, and people spoke as if returning borrowed words. 

Maren had spent her life proving that whatever ran down could be set running again, and the prophecy offended her like a jammed neck.  She wanted the future loose again, raw and pourable, sand instead of stone.  She did not know how until the baker's boy, Tobb, came asking after a button. 

It had torn off his coat on the rim path, and the warehouse kept an old rule: whatever fell from the cliffs was shelved in the bay beneath where it landed.  Maren found his button in the fifth bay's drawer, and under it, stuck in a century of dust, another.  Its tag read, in the first turner's hand, Gathering Night.  That was the night the prophecy was spoken from the rim. 

Quietly inspired, she said nothing to Tobb, but after he left she went bay to bay with a lamp.  Eight bays ran along the cliff foot, and each drawer held one button with the same tag.  Eight buttons; eight words over the door.  Something in her went still and bright, the way a glass does in the instant after turning. 

That night she unlidded the jar alone, without the carved sentence to tell her ears what to hear.  It was not one prophet.  It was eight voices tumbling over one another, each word arriving in whatever order the canyon walls had thrown it back.  The stone had shuffled them, and one frightened man had dealt them out again as doom. 

To unscramble the echo, she needed to know who had stood where, so she began cataloging the lost buttons.  Bay, material, wear, thread: she wrote each down as carefully as a turning time.  They must have leaned out shoulder to shoulder, she thought, so far that the rock took a button from every coat.  Bay one held a horn toggle worn glossy by a slow thumb, and in the jar an old woman's voice said will.  Bay four held a painted wooden bead off a child's smock, and a child's voice said silent.  Bay eight held brass with flour still caked in its holes, and a hoarse miller said ends. 

Button by button, voice by voice, the sentence over the door came unraveled.  Read from the west bay, the words were gravel.  Read from the east, the way the sun and her own rounds traveled, they said: Will we fall silent when the sand ends? 

Maren set down her pen.  It had never been a verdict.  It was a question, and eight neighbors had each carried one word of it to the rim so that no single mouth could own the whole.  That was their wisdom, shared out like bread: the future belongs to whoever is left to answer. 

On the last morning she carried the jar, the ledger, and the buttons up to the square.  She made no speech.  She laid the buttons in a row on the well curb, east to west, and people drifted close, recognizing a great-grandmother's toggle, a family's brass.  Then she opened the jar and touched each button as its voice came. 

Afterward there was a silence like something dug up, old and soil-dark, still holding the shape of what had been buried in it.  Tobb broke it.  "No," he said, to the question, and the baker said it after him, and then the whole square. 

They followed her down beneath the cliffs and stood in the amber light while the last grains fell.  Turning was her trade, but she only drew the bolts.  Forty hands took the frame and heaved the Last Glass over, and the sand, astonished, began again. 

Later they climbed to the rim, and Maren held a fresh jar open while each of them shouted a word of their own into the canyon.  She caught what came back, sealed it, and did not write the order down.
