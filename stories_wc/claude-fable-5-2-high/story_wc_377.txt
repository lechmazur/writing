Every year, when the first frost climbed the whispering bluff, Oriel's voice left her.  It went the way the swallows went, without quarrel, and came back with the thaw.  Nobody in the village above the clouds minded anymore.  By winter, words had become unnecessary there anyway; the wind hissed its gossip through the grass, and Oriel's bells said the rest. 

She was the bell tuner, as her mother had been, and she filed bronze lips until forty bells agreed with one another.  The bluff had a saying: a tuned bell cannot lie.  So each solstice the village gathered for the symphony of truth, when the bells rang out the year exactly as it had been, lean harvests and all. 

Three days into her silence, a porter climbed out of the cloud sea with a bundle of sailcloth steaming cold on his back.  Inside lay a fragment of iceberg, chipped from a floe in the true sea that no one on the bluff had ever seen.  It was his payment for a winter's lodging, and it was blue.  Not sky blue, which the bluff had in tiresome plenty, but a deep, old, swallowed blue that seemed to glisten from the inside.  The children went quiet in front of it, and Oriel saw the same color arrive in their faces.  She knew it at once: it was the color of wonder, and it was already dripping. 

The elders voted with their hands to bury the fragment in the root cellar under straw, where it would last until summer.  Oriel shook her head so hard her braid struck her shoulder.  In the dark, the ice would keep its body and lose its blue, because a color nobody sees is not preserved, only postponed.  She could not say this, so she rang the smallest bell in her apron, one clear note that meant give me time.  They gave her until solstice. 

She worked the way she tuned, steadily, with small exact motions and no wasted heat.  First she listened.  Tapped with a fingernail, the fragment answered in five thin tones, and when it cracked in the night it gave a sixth, low as a held breath.  She filed six bells until each one matched the ice so closely she could not tell which had spoken. 

Then, on the windward lip of the bluff, she raised a wheel of ash spars and scrap bronze taller than the granary.  Sailcloth vanes caught the whispering wind and turned the wheel; the wheel swung six hammers past six bells.  At the hub, in a cradle of wire, she hung the fragment where the low sun could reach it.  Around it she set polished tin leaves that tilted as the wheel went round, throwing scraps of that swallowed blue across the snow, the walls, the upturned faces. 

The elder Hask frowned up at it and opened his palms: and when it melts?  Oriel pointed beneath the cradle, where the six bells hung mouth upward like cups, waiting for the drip.  Every drop the ice surrendered would lower a bell's voice by a hair.  The wheel would not pretend the ice was staying; it would play the leaving, truthfully, note by deepening note.  Hask listened to one slow revolution, then lowered his hands. 

At solstice the forty old bells rang the year, and then the village turned toward the wheel and let it finish the symphony.  Of all the bluff's symphonies of truth, people agreed with nods and wet eyes, this one told the most: that there was a sea, that it was vast, and that marvels do not keep still. 

All winter the wheel turned.  The fragment shrank to a fist, a plum, a bead, and the six voices sank toward something like surf.  Children learned the changing tune and hummed it at their chores. 

On the morning the swallows returned, the cradle hung empty, and Oriel woke with her voice lying in her throat like a tool left out overnight.  A small girl named Wren found her at the wheel, a newborn brother on her hip, and asked her to tell him about the blue, now that she could.  Oriel opened her mouth and found the words were there, and that they were smaller than the thing.  So she closed it, smiled, and turned the baby to face the wheel. 

The wind obliged.  Six bells, brimming with what had once been an iceberg, sang low and wide, and the tin leaves made the meltwater glisten across every wall.  The baby went quiet.  Oriel watched the color arrive in his face, deep and old and unmistakable, and she said nothing, because nothing needed saying.
