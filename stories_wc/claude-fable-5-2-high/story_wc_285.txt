The ferry at Sallow Reach would not run until the rain gutters stopped ticking.  That was the ferryman's whole rule, chalked on the landing-house door: while the eaves still dripped, the fog was still thickening, and no one could see the far lantern.  Tobiah Fenn, coachman of the Gloom Hill line, had waited out that rule for forty years and did not mind it.  He unhitched nothing, blanketed the horses, and sat on the landing bench where spindrift lifted off the unseen river and settled on his sleeves like cool breath. 

His only passenger sat beside him with a gray shawl in her lap.  Mrs. Quarrow had boarded at the top of Gloom Hill with one bag, a fat envelope, and a fare to Saltmeadow on the far bank, where her daughter kept the lock.  The knitting was finished; she was only weaving in the loose ends with a blunt steel yarn needle, and she had been weaving in the same end since the last milestone. 

Tobiah did not ask why.  Asking, he had learned, made people hand you the tangle.  He was closely attentive in another way, the way he drove in fog: not staring at any one thing, but letting everything arrive.  The gutter ticked into its barrel.  The horses shifted.  Her needle went in, came out, and stopped whenever the ferry bell clanked idly on its post.  Her thumb kept finding the envelope, measuring its thickness, and leaving it.  He let these things percolate through him as rain goes down through gravel, slowly, losing its silt, until what reached the bottom was clear. 

She was not afraid of the river.  She was afraid of the far bank, and the envelope was too heavy to be anything but an explanation. 

"Eleven pages," she said at last, as if he had spoken.  "I told Wren I would come for her wedding, then for the baby, then by the spring floods, and each time I wrote to say why not.  These pages are all the reasons together, and I cannot hand them to her, and I cannot arrive without them." 

Tobiah took the waybill from inside his coat.  The Gloom Hill line issued a coachman one sheet of vellum for life; after every run he scraped it with his knife and wrote the next passenger's name and destination over the ghost of the last.  He tilted it toward the landing lamp so she could see the layers, forty years of pale lines lying under one another like ripples under ice. 

"The bottom one is mine," he said.  "Home before the thaw, I wrote, for my wife, the first winter I drove.  I was nine days late, and I have never managed to scrape it out.  She told me to leave it, because every promise I wrote on top would have to answer to it." 

Mrs. Quarrow looked at the sheet for a long while.  "So nothing written over is gone." 

"No, but only the top line has to be true tonight," Tobiah said.  "Your daughter can already read every layer under it; she has been reading them for years.  What is the one line that is left when the eleven pages boil off?" 

This was the only craft he had besides horses: a road, however it wound, came down to two places and a word given between them. 

She turned the envelope over to its blank side, but the fog had got into his ink and the pen left only a gray tear.  She considered the yarn needle in her fingers.  Its blunt tip would not cut paper, but pressed hard it left a furrow, and she scored her line slowly into the envelope's back, over the old creases, over the faint shadow of her own eleven pages showing through. 

I am here, it said. 

Tobiah rubbed a little lamp soot into the grooves until the three words stood up dark.  Then she threaded the last loose end of the shawl, wove it in with six quick passes, and snipped it. 

Neither of them noticed the exact moment, only that the quiet had changed shape.  The gutter hung one bright drop from its lip and did not let it fall.  Out over the water the fog thinned to gauze, and a small yellow lantern appeared on the far bank, steady as a kept word. 

The ferryman rang the bell once.  Tobiah tucked away his waybill, her name on top and his own old promise beneath it, and led the horses down the ramp.  Mrs. Quarrow followed with the shawl over her arm and the envelope held with its three words upward while the river went by underneath, soft and unhurried, carrying its spindrift to the sea.
