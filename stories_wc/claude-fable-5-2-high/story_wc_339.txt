Three weeks after they raised the first clear images of Amelia Earhart's Electra, the world had its answer and Ines Varga had her old emptiness back.  The same sonar sweep had caught a second shadow nine miles east: the Calliope, a lending-library steamer that once carried books between Pacific outposts and sank in 1938 without anyone troubling to remember her.  Nobody else on the expedition wanted her, so Ines dove alone. 

The reading room lay canted at twenty degrees, its shelves still gripping rows of swollen books, and in the bolted armchair by the desk sat Death, legs crossed, patient as silt. 

"Evander," she said through the comm she kept switched to no one. 

"Ines," he said, the way a colleague answers. 

They had been on a first-name basis since her fourth body recovery, when she had grown tired of pretending not to see him at the edge of her lamp.  Eleven years of salvage had made them something close to friends; then two winters ago he had come for her husband Tomas at ninety meters, and she had not forgiven the courtesy with which he did it.  Her wrist computer still stored his last transmission, four seconds long: "Line's clear, love, coming up."  She had played it before every dive since, 611 times, and she noted the number as she noted everything, quietly, as though grief were a tide table. 

"You found the aviator," Evander said.  "People seem pleased." 

"People are pleased because they can stop listening," Ines said.  "'We are running north and south.'"  "Nearly ninety years that sentence was a door, and now it is only what a tired woman said." 

"And you came down here to ask me again." 

"I want the physics of letting go."  "You attend every instance; you must have observed the mechanism." 

"I am only where the letter arrives," he said.  "I never see it sent." 

So she turned to the desk, because work at least answered questions.  The bindings of a hundred books had rotted, and their sewing threads had drifted free and knotted into a pale skein that cocooned the desktop.  Cutting would have clouded the room with pulp, so she proceeded by untangling threads, one at a time, tracing each back to its dead spine before easing it loose.  She noted on her slate: linen, waxed, knots formed by current, not intention.  It took forty minutes, and she found she did not mind; a tangle, followed patiently, turned out to be only single lines that had leaned on each other too long. 

Under the last threads sat a snow globe, and beneath it, a brass pencil tin sealed with candle wax.  The globe was intact.  A tiny painted lighthouse stood inside, and when she lifted it, snow rose and fell again, slow, water inside water. 

She stared at it longer than she had stared at the Electra.  The sea had crushed the ship's lamps, its barometer, every sealed and hollow thing aboard; yet this thin glass had survived nearly nine decades of the same weight.  She noted the reason almost before she felt it: the globe held no air.  It was already full of what pressed on it, so the pressure found nothing to break. 

Inside the tin, dry as the day it was closed, lay a note in a librarian's upright hand, dated 13 July 1937. 

"I keep the wireless as well as the books."  "Eleven mornings ago I heard the flier say they were running north and south, and then nothing."  "I have listened every night since."  "Tonight I switched the set off, and the silence afterward was the same silence."  "She was never in the static."  "I had only been holding a space open and calling it her." 

Ines read it twice, photographed it, resealed the tin, and set it back under the globe where its keeper had left it. 

"There is your mechanism," Evander said gently. 

"No," she said.  "There is mine."  "You do not let go by opening your hand."  "You stop keeping the sea out, you fill with it, and you hold your shape anyway." 

She brought up Tomas's four seconds on her wrist and did not press play.  She did not delete them either; a library had just taught her that keeping and listening were different acts.  She noted the count on her slate, 611, and drew a line beneath it. 

"Will I see you?" Evander asked. 

"Eventually."  "Not socially, for a while." 

He inclined his head, and then the chair was only a chair. 

Ines shook the globe once, left its small weather settling over the lighthouse, and began her ascent, venting air as she rose, letting each breath go at exactly the rate the water asked.
