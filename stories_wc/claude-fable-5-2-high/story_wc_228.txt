The mirror cracked at dawn, when a gust off the harbor tipped it from the easel onto the stones of the quay.  Anselm Pike had used that mirror for forty years to check his canvases in reverse, the old trick for catching a lie in a composition.  Now he lifted it and found the lie at last.  On one side of the crack sat his painting, the quay of his boyhood, crowded with tan-sailed luggers that had been firewood for decades.  On the other side stood the real quay: the ferry ramp, the humming ice machine, young Ines Calloway mending nets in a yellow slicker.  He had painted here every morning since he was nineteen and had never once painted any of it. 

He knew every shoulder on that quay, who favored a bad knee, who whistled before rain.  Yet he kept his back to the chandlery wall so no one could watch him work, and he answered every greeting with a nod.  Intimately aloof, his late sister had called it: close enough to count their freckles, too far to learn their children's names. 

His hand went to his coat pocket, to the scallop shell he had carried there since he was twelve.  On its outer curve he had painted the harbor in boat enamel on the last morning his father's lugger sailed out.  The inside he had left bare, and he had made himself a promise he never spoke aloud.  When he found a morning worth as much as that one, he would paint it there and carry the shell to the Throats, the caves under the headland where his father said the sea kept whatever it was given.  Instead he had spent forty years repainting the first morning, as if repetition could make it stay. 

The crack ran straight between what he loved and what was there.  The tide was going out.  He folded the easel, pocketed his travelling paintbox, and walked the shingle to the headland before he could talk himself back into habit. 

The Throats opened where the ebb uncovered them, a dozen black mouths breathing cold kelp air.  His father had taken him in once, by lantern, to a ledge above the high-water line: third mouth, then right at the pillar shaped like a kneeling man.  Anselm found the third mouth, but fifty winters of surf had worn the kneeling man to a stump, and he passed it without knowing and bore left where the passage forked.  The tunnel narrowed, climbed, and bent twice, and his torch showed nothing he remembered.  He was about to turn back when he saw green light ahead. 

The passage let him out into a chamber whose roof had fallen in long ago, leaving a ragged well of sky.  A freshwater spring ran down the wall into a clear pool, and around it grew ferns, sea lavender, and one stunted fig.  Sunlight stood in the place like a held breath.  Then he noticed the wrack line in the tunnel behind him, weed strung shoulder-high along the rock, and understood.  Twice a day the sea shut the door on this garden, and twice a day it opened it again for an hour or two.  It was a temporary eternity, whole while it lasted and not his to keep.  So had that first morning been, he saw, and he had insulted it by pretending otherwise. 

He sat on a warm stone, opened the paintbox, and turned the shell over to its bare inside.  He did not paint the garden.  He painted, from memory that turned out to be exact, a yellow slicker, a lap full of net, the ferry ramp, the ice machine, the quay as it had looked an hour ago.  It took the whole of the slack water. 

When he heard the returning sea begin to boom in the tunnels, he set the shell on a ledge beside the spring, old harbor down, new harbor up.  It was not the ledge his father had shown him, and the promise was kept anyway.  He waded out through knee-deep water with his heart going like a boy's. 

On the quay he set up the easel away from the wall, out in the middle where anyone could stand behind him.  Ines Calloway brought him tea, as she had for six years without thanks.  "You've moved," she said. 

"The mirror broke," he said, "so I need someone to tell me when I get it wrong."  He stepped aside and revealed the canvas: the quay as it stood that very hour, and in the middle of it a small figure in yellow, already begun.  "It will be out of date by supper," he said.  "That is rather the point, Ines." 

She laughed, and stayed to watch.
