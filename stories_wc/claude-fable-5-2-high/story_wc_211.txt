The foghorn had sounded over the harbor every misted night of Ilse Marrow's eighty-one years, and tonight, decommissioned, it did not.  The silence settled on the orchard like a hand laid on velvet. 

The orchard was older than the tower beneath it.  Three centuries ago a merchant had sworn its pear trees would never be felled, so when the city wanted the ground, the builders lifted soil, roots, and oath together, sixty storeys into the fog.  Someone had to keep such promises, and for fifty years that someone had been Ilse, oath scribe of the roof. 

People climbed to her among the gnarled trunks to swear things: marriages, debts, vengeances, truces.  She took their oaths in dead languages, Old Tidal mostly, the harbor tongue that had drowned with the last ferrymen, because a dead language cannot shift its meanings behind your back.  She fed them the syllables, they repeated them into the brass horn, and the needle wrote their voices into wax. 

The words were never what she wanted.  Words lied; the tremor underneath them did not, and wax caught tremor the way moss catches dew.  Her true archive, shelved in the potting shed, was a catalogue of feeling: the hitch before a bride's vow, the flat calm of a man swearing revenge, the wobble of a debtor who already knew.  One pattern was missing, and she had hunted it for decades: the voice of someone promising without knowing it.  You could not ask for that.  Asking spoiled it. 

Now, in the new quiet, she heard the orchard as she had not since childhood: leaves ticking, a pear letting go, the faint hiss of her own breathing.  And the quiet opened a door she had thought painted shut. 

Third grade, a field trip, twenty children herded out of the elevator into these same trees.  The scribe then was a stooped man named Tobiah who let each child swear something silly into the horn.  When Ilse's turn came, the foghorn was bellowing, and under its cover, too shy for real words, she had mumbled a string of nonsense that simply felt nice in her mouth.  Nobody heard it but the wax. 

Afterward Tobiah shaved the cylinder clean for reuse, and she cried to see her voice pared away in one long brown ribbon.  So he coiled the shaving into a tobacco tin and pressed it into her hands. 

"Wax forgets nothing," he said.  "It only gets thinner." 

Then, the curious part, the part that had snagged in her for seventy-three years: he had shown the class how a brittle shaving could be immersed in sun-warmed cistern water until it relaxed enough to lie flat under a needle.  Why teach that to eight-year-olds, unless he expected one of them to need it? 

The tin was still in her desk, beneath the seals.  She carried it to the stone cistern, where the day's heat lingered, and immersed the dark curl until it stopped resisting.  It loosened slowly, like a fist deciding to trust.  She laid it along a slate and drew a needle fixed to a paper cone down its groove by hand. 

First came the foghorn, tiny and enormous, the dead thing bellowing again.  Then, beneath it, a child's whisper. 

Ilse's hand stopped. 

It was not nonsense.  It was Old Tidal, clean as a ferryman's, a tongue she would not begin to learn for another twenty years. 

When the horn sleeps, the keeper of promises will give her own. 

She played it again, and again, listening now as an archivist.  There it was, the pattern she had hunted: no hitch, no wobble, a promise as unguarded as breath, made by someone with no idea she was making it. 

The disquiet came softly afterward.  In fifty years she had witnessed eleven thousand oaths and sworn none.  Had the child foreseen that, or had Tobiah heard more than he admitted and handed a weeping girl her whole life in a tin? 

Either way the horn slept, and the wax was waiting. 

She mounted a fresh cylinder, wound the spring, and leaned to the brass.  In Old Tidal she swore that the next child who mumbled into this horn would leave with a tin, and that the orchard would have a keeper after her.  Her voice shook on the last word.  She noted the tremor in her catalogue, as she would have for anyone: fear, held inside devotion. 

Then she shelved the ribbon beside the cylinder, the unknowing promise next to the knowing one, and sat among the pear trees in the quiet that had been promised to her.  Below, the fog came in anyway, unannounced.
