Vesna had ninety seconds left of her hour, and she meant to spend them all on him. 

Three hundred years ago, Aldous the alchemist had pawned the sun over his orchard greenhouse to buy his dying bride sixty more minutes.  She died before he ran home with the receipt, a carved stone bead cut from their sundial, so the hour came to her dead instead.  The night stayed.  Her wedding veil burned to ash in the last sunset, and she wore the ashes still. 

Aldous died in the dark and was born again, and again, each time drifting back to keep the sleeping orchard without knowing why.  This lifetime he was called Tobiah, and he talked to the trees.  There were seven of them under the black glass: a quicksilver pear, a sulfur plum, a salt-white almond, and others, each holding a seventh of the stolen dawn asleep in its roots. 

Vesna owed him a sun. 

She had squandered the rest on earlier lifetimes, weeping in mirrors at men who fled, so now she rationed herself through ephemeral cameo appearances, a few seconds in a pane, a few words, gone. 

On the first frost of the year, she surfaced in the glass beside his lantern.  "Under the quince, you beautiful idiot," she said, her voice lovingly venomous, the way the orchard's poisons were also its medicines.  "Dig." 

Four seconds. 

He dug, because some buried part of him had always obeyed that voice, and found the bead, grooved like a sundial's face. 

Days later she flickered across the alembic's copper belly: "Strike it on the pear's trunk, my darling disaster." 

The bead rang.  The note was small and pure, and the pear answered at its own pitch, sap brightening, buds splitting silver in the dark.  Something answered in Tobiah too: the memory of a hand in his, a wedding morning, light.  He stood shaking among the blossoms while the sound carried out over the hush of winter-cracked fields, where frost had split the furrows and nothing else spoke. 

So the winter went.  Each tree needed its own striking place, and only Vesna knew them, so she would ghost along the panes for a breath, name a root or a graft scar, call him something tender and terrible, and vanish.  Each ringing woke a tree, and each tree woke a lifetime in him: the shepherd he had been, the glazier, the widow who had patched these very panes.  Beyond the glass, the horizon paled by sevenths. 

He began waiting for her.  He left lanterns by every pane and begged her to stay longer, and she, who wanted nothing else, called him a sentimental leech and left mid-word, because every second she gave his loneliness was stolen from his sunrise. 

By the sixth tree he remembered her name.  By the seventh, the quince, he remembered the bargain, and understood what the bead's grooves counted.  The night and her hour were one purchase.  Return the sun, and whatever remained of Vesna went back with the change. 

He set the bead down on the potting bench and would not lift it. 

"I'll keep the dark," he told the empty glass.  "I'll keep you in it." 

She had forty seconds left.  She spent thirty of them at once, rising in every pane together, a hundred ashen veils around him like a congregation. 

"You sold the sun for an hour of me," she said, "and I have hated you for it, tenderly, for three hundred years."  "Every field that cracked, every child raised by lamplight, went on my account."  "Do not make me owe you twice, you selfish, precious man."  "Strike the graft, and let me pay." 

It stung as she intended, and it healed as she intended. 

Tobiah, who was Aldous, who was everyone who had ever kept faith with this glass, picked up the bead.  He was weeping, and he was smiling, and he struck the quince at the old graft, where two woods had been married into one. 

The last note rang, and all seven trees sang back, a chord so full the black glass shivered with it.  Dawn came up over the cracked fields like a held breath finally let go. 

Vesna had ten seconds, and she used them to stand in the doorway, in true light, with her veil lifted.  She said nothing barbed. 

"Paid," she said, laughing, and the sun went through her like water through lace. 

The ashes of the veil settled on the quince roots, where ash does a tree good.  Tobiah hung the bead from the lowest branch, and whenever wind crossed the thawing fields it rang, faintly, and everything that heard it woke.
