The aurora came down over Port Halloran like green smoke, and every radio in the container yard began to hiss.  Then the floodlights died, and the Stack, forty acres of steel boxes piled six high, became a labyrinth lit only by the sky.  Marit had maintained that labyrinth for six years: greasing twist-locks, replacing lamps, repainting the twelve zodiac signs that named its lanes.  Numbers blurred in fog; nobody mistook a ram for a fish.  She knew the place by ear, and she knew its people by their walks. 

So she named the foreman before he rounded the corner: Dobbs, heavy on the right heel, impatient, bullhorn knocking his belt buckle. 

"Tobin's still in there," he said.  "First week, no radio, no lights, and when the grid comes back the driverless carriers wake up blind." 

He raised the bullhorn and roared the boy's name.  The shout struck corrugated steel and shattered, returning from nine directions, each one a different liar. 

In the silence afterward, Marit listened.  Far down the lanes, gravel answered: a quick step with a dragged left toe, Tobin's, turning, turning back, chasing every echo. 

"You're spinning him," she said. 

"This yard runs on lungs," Dobbs said, as he had said every time she proposed anything quieter. 

Her grandfather, a tug engineer, had taught her otherwise: orders went down a ship's voice pipe in a murmur, because brass turns a shout to mud.  Whispers travel farther than shouts, he said, if you give them a road.  She had waited six years for a night that would let her prove it. 

"A challenge, then," she said, lightly, as if the idea had just blown in off the water.  "Two minutes of silence.  If my whisper doesn't walk him out, I'll carry your bullhorn for a year." 

"And if it does?" 

"You learn to fold a map." 

Dobbs snorted, checked his dead radio once more, and lowered the horn. 

From her jacket she took the yard plan, soft as cloth from refolding, because the Stack changed daily and a flat map lied about distance.  Folded her way, along one old crease, the gate bollard touched the farthest lane, Pisces, half a mile of turnings reduced to a thumb's width.  The crease followed something real: the speaking tube of the old steamship pier, buried when the yard was paved, surfacing at a small grille in every lane, each one quietly kept clear by her. 

Around her neck hung a square-shanked key that had come with the job and fit no door in the port.  Dobbs had once called it her lucky charm.  She knelt at the bollard, set the key over what twenty coats of paint had disguised as a rivet, and turned.  A brass cap swung aside, and the dark beneath it breathed. 

She did not shout. 

"Tobin," she whispered into the tube, each word placed like a bolt.  "Stand still.  Take out the coin I gave you." 

On his first morning she had flipped him a zodiac coin, offhand, for luck, the way she did with every new hire; she had ordered two hundred of them.  Its twelve signs ran around the rim in the order of the lanes, the whole labyrinth folded down to pocket size. 

"Find the sign painted above you.  Walk the wheel backward, sign by sign, until you reach the Ram.  The Ram is the gate." 

Then she put her ear to the brass and listened, because a road for whispers carries footsteps home as well.  For a long moment there was only the tube's seashell hush.  Dobbs's hand drifted toward the bullhorn; she lifted one finger, and he held. 

Then the walk came, faint and unmistakable: the dragged toe, no longer circling, steady as a man counting.  It passed one grille, then the next, growing nearer by constellations. 

"Twins," she murmured, mostly to herself.  "He's two lanes out." 

Dobbs stared at her, then at the open cap. 

Tobin came out under the painted Ram with the coin clenched in his fist, pale green in the aurora and trying to look as though he had never been lost. 

"Somebody was talking out of the ground," he said.  "Really quietly.  I heard every word." 

Ninety seconds later the floodlights slammed back on, and deep in the Stack the carriers woke, whining, and began to roam.  Dobbs watched them for a while with the bullhorn hanging from his hand.  Then he spoke, and for once she had to lean in to hear him. 

"Show me the fold." 

Marit locked the cap, opened the map across the bollard, and guided his thumb along the crease as if she had only just thought of it.
