The carnival on Jubilee never stopped, because stopping meant admitting the city was only rafts.  Four hundred of them rode the swell in loose clusters joined by gangplanks, each one painted to the waterline and loud with calliopes.  Nessa Vale walked them every morning in a gray Consortium blazer, feeling the pattern underfoot the way a tuner feels a string. 

Her title was culture shock absorber.  The Meridian Consortium paid her to stand between its buyers and her own neighbors and take the impact of both.  She explained to investors why the stilt walkers would not sign releases, and explained to the stilt walkers why the investors flinched at fried eel.  She was good at it because she honestly owed something to each side.  The Consortium's money had fixed the desalinators and paid for her mother's lungs.  The rafts had raised her. 

In nine days the Harbor Trust would privatize Jubilee.  Surveyors would map the city exactly as it floated that morning and sell it raft by raft to whoever bid highest.  Nessa had seen the bids.  The fortune tellers' row would become a spa, and the families who had kept the music going for sixty years would be priced onto the mainland ferries. 

She had also read the survey code, all of it, because order soothed her.  Clause forty said any deck joined by an unbroken chain of fixed lashings to a founding vessel counted as common hull, sold only whole, with residents holding first refusal.  Jubilee had a founding vessel, the Old Barge, drifting forgotten at the eastern edge under a broken carousel.  It carried the foundation stone, a block of mainland granite the first families had hauled aboard before the coast drowned.  Nessa wore a chip of that stone on a cord, knocked loose by her grandmother's mallet on launch day. 

She could not simply announce the clause.  The Consortium read her messages, and if they dismissed her, no one would stand in the room when the lots were drawn.  But Jubilee had always talked to itself in paint.  Every hull wore graffiti, and every child learned the old carnival signs: a knot meant tie here, a gull meant move tonight, a number of fish meant how many berths west.  Buyers photographed it as local color. 

So each night Nessa took off the blazer and painted.  She did not need a chart; she closed her eyes and the whole city arranged itself behind them, every raft sliding to the place where it belonged, like beads finding a thread.  Her murals told the clusters how to reorganize: the Old Barge towed to the center, eight spokes of rafts lashed outward from it, no gangplank left where a rope could go.  Beside each mural she pressed the stone chip into wet paint, leaving its jagged print, the one signature every founding family would trust. 

For three nights nothing moved, and she lay awake hearing the calliopes and thinking she had spent her nerve on pictures.  On the fourth night the juggler's raft cast off behind the fireworks and slid six berths west. 

By day she led buyers through the midway, smiling, translating, steering them away from fresh rope.  "Does it always shift around like this?" asked Mr. Aldous, her supervisor, frowning at a map that no longer matched.  "It is a carnival," Nessa said, which was true, and hated how easily the truth could serve as cover.  She did not want him gone.  She wanted him to arrive as a neighbor instead of an owner, and only the clause could make him one. 

On survey morning the city lay in a great painted wheel, and the surveyors stopped at its hub.  "Any barge can claim to be a founding vessel," Aldous said, pale, already understanding.  Nessa knelt at the granite block, scraped sixty years of carnival paint from one corner, and found the scar.  Her hands shook as she lifted the cord over her head.  The chip slid into the stone with a small click, grain meeting grain, and the surveyor wrote something down. 

"You work for us," Aldous said quietly. 

"I do," she said.  "I work for them too, and now you will need me more than ever, because you can still buy in, but only as one voice among four hundred." 

The Trust might still rule against the wheel, and the residents might never raise the price of their own home.  Nessa knew that as surely as she knew every knot beneath her feet.  But the calliopes were playing, the ropes were holding, and for one more morning Jubilee floated in a single piece.
