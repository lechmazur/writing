Vega went out at nine minutes past midnight, and Odile Marsh started the clock.  Her workshop stood on the ridge, roofed and walled in panes of sawn topaz, so the remaining stars came through the color of weak tea.  Lately the stars had been vanishing one by one, about forty minutes apart, and she did her real work in the gaps. 

The town called her cryptic, which was fair.  She printed notices that could be read two ways, and she answered questions in whatever voice had last walked through her door.  Borrowing personalities was a habit as old as her apprenticeship: the baker's bluster on Monday, the midwife's hush by Thursday.  She wore other people because, measured honestly, she could find no reading of her own. 

And measuring was the point.  Odile believed emotion had a mathematics, as type has picas and leading, and she meant to learn it before the sky ran out. 

Her instrument lay locked in the chase of the old platen press: a halo fragment, a curved sliver of pale fire that had dropped onto the ridge the night the first star vanished.  Set among the lead like an oversized parenthesis, it left no mark on paper.  It did something stranger.  Whoever read a sheet it had pressed felt, for a few minutes, what the author of the words felt, while the author received the reader's feeling in exchange.  Not sympathy, which adds, but empathy transposed, like two numerals trading columns. 

The exchange only took, she had found, when the text disoriented its reader.  A plain sentence let a person stand firm inside himself; a deliberately ambiguous one, where "I" and "you" could not be pinned down, tipped him loose long enough for the fragment to work.  So ambiguity was her method, and her ledger filled with results: a widow's grief traded for a debtor's shame, entered in columns that never balanced. 

At twenty past midnight the door opened on Tobias Renn, the observatory's last astronomer, carrying his star log like a hymnal. 

"Vega needs a notice," he said.  "I have her coordinates and her time of death, and no words at all." 

"Customers supply the words," Odile said, and heard his clipped cadence already in her mouth.  "I supply the arrangement." 

Tobias looked at her for a long moment.  "That is my voice you are using," he said.  "Last week you were the baker.  I am asking what the printer thinks about a star going out." 

Something in her chest moved like a tray of loose sorts sliding.  She had never authored a sheet herself, because a trade requires goods on both sides, and she had assumed her side of the scale was empty.  But an empty pan was itself a measurement, the zero her ledger lacked. 

"Twenty minutes until the next one goes," Tobias said. 

She took up her composing stick.  The line came slowly, letter by reversed letter, and she built it to tilt: THE LIGHT YOU LOST IS STILL ARRIVING WHERE I AM YOU.  With no comma, the ending could not decide who was standing where.  She locked the type beside the halo fragment, inked the disk, and pulled a single proof. 

Tobias read it once, frowned, read it again, and she watched him lose his footing in the sentence.  Then the fragment flared, and the exchange went through her like weather. 

His feeling arrived first: a grief so orderly it had decimal places, forty years of names checked nightly against the dark, each absence logged in ink.  She could think with perfect clarity while it tossed her, and she noted, calmly, that her hands were shaking. 

Across the press, Tobias had gone gray.  "This is yours?" he whispered.  "It is enormous.  It is like standing in a house where every room is rented out and no one ever asks for the landlady." 

Not zero, then. 

Odile gripped the press and did the sum she had waited years to do.  Her ledger had never balanced because she had assumed a feeling kept its size when it changed hands.  It did not: his grief, crushing to him, sat in her as a weight she could lift, while her loneliness, invisible to her for thirty years, had bent him in seconds.  A feeling could be weighed only by the one who received it, and that was the first true line of the mathematics. 

The fragment dimmed, and each of them came home to themselves, breathing hard. 

"I think a star going out should be told to somebody," Odile said, in a rough, unhurried voice that belonged to no customer.  "We will print two hundred." 

Through the topaz roof, Altair went out, and this time they both wrote down the time.
