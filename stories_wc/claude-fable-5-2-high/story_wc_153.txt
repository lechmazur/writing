Neve had asked her question, and now the apology stood in Isaura's throat like a heron in shallow water, unmoving.  Everything that follows happened inside that waiting. 

Outside the stilt house, the mangrove labyrinth breathed its green dark, and the spirits sang in the roots.  They were the ones who had died with words unsent, and they sang them now to the tide, over and over, because the tide never answered and never refused.  Neve had poled a skiff through that singing for two days to stand here, nineteen and salt-cracked, with a bundle of letters bound in fishing line.  She had dropped them on the table like a net full of something still alive. 

"Twelve of these, one for every birthday you missed, and not one sent," she had said.  "So tell me plainly: was there a moment, before you decided to leave me, when you might have stayed?" 

Isaura, who told strangers their dead in riddles, found the riddle rising out of habit.  "The tide remembers what the shore denies," she said. 

"Don't," Neve said, low and shaking.  "I am not one of your customers." 

She was not, and that was the whole wound.  For twelve years Isaura had hunted the same moment herself, the breath before deciding, and had never once had the courage to call it up. 

She took the shard from its hook above the stove.  It was a curve of gray stone the length of her hand, broken from the rainspout of the chapel gargoyle the night she left, when the storm tore it down at her feet.  She had carried it as scripture: a gargoyle exists to throw the storm away from the wall it loves.  Water still obeyed it.  Each evoking cracked the stone, and moonlight always knit it whole again, which she had long taken for forgiveness and now suspected was only patience. 

"Watch the glass," she said, which was as plain as she could manage. 

She pressed the shard to the window frame and began to evoke, not a ghost this time, but an hour.  The singing outside bent toward the house.  The swamp's wet breath poured through the stone as rain once had, and the pane went white with condensation.  Then letters opened in the fog, stroke by stroke, in a younger hand that Isaura knew as her own. 

She is seven and already hums their songs in her sleep.  If I stay, they will follow me to her bed and sing her hollow.  If I go, she will hate me.  Let her hate me and live. 

The shard split along its length with a sound like a knuckle.  Neve read the window twice while the words began to run. 

"So you loved me," she said, and it was not a surrender.  "You decided what I could survive, and you never once asked me." 

Isaura looked at the dripping glass and saw what twelve years of hunting had hidden.  The moment she wanted had never lived in that night, which held only a frightened woman deciding alone.  It lived here, in this held breath, with her daughter an arm's length away and nothing yet chosen. 

She set the broken shard on the sill and pushed the door wide to the singing.  "Listen to what they sing nearest this house," she said. 

Out among the roots, a dozen thin voices carried a single line: Neve, the egrets came back today, and I saved you a feather. 

"Every night I wrote to you on that window with my finger," Isaura said.  "Every dawn the sun took it back, but the dead were listening, and they do not forget an unsent thing." 

Neve's jaw worked.  "That is still a riddle, Mother." 

"Yes," Isaura said, and at last the heron lifted.  "I am sorry.  I was wrong to choose for you, wrong to stay gone, and wrong to hide in fog what I owed you in ink.  I will not ask you to forgive it.  I am asking what you want, which I should have asked a child of seven." 

Neve did not soften, exactly.  She cut the fishing line with her teeth, shoved the first letter across the table, and said, "Read it out loud, and do not skip the cruel parts." 

Isaura read, and wept, and did not skip them. 

The moon cleared the mangroves somewhere in the third letter.  On the sill, in its white light, the cracked shard drew slowly closed, the way a channel does when it is finally allowed to carry something home instead of away.  Outside, the spirits sang on, softer now, as if listening.
