Oriel had kept the chronicle for so long that she no longer trusted the numbers she gave to years.  The library lay hidden in the catacombs of lost knowledge, where the forgetful dead had been shelved beside the books nobody wanted remembered, and across the drift of untold seasons the roots had come down to read them all.  Ivy threaded the spines.  Moss upholstered the reading desks.  Pale mushrooms rose from the atlases like small, patient lamps. 

Her task was simple and nearly impossible: to witness change without judgment.  The keeper before her had said it plainly, when Oriel was a girl or perhaps already old: "Write that the shelf fell, never that the shelf was lost." 

Oriel gripped reality loosely, the way one holds a moth, and for most of her tenure that had served her well.  She could not say whether the statues moved or whether she only blinked slowly, so she wrote down both and blamed neither. 

But lately her pen had begun to betray her.  Ruin, she caught herself writing.  Pity.  Too late.  The words crept into the chronicle like damp, and with them came a noise in her skull, the murmur of every rotted page asking to be mourned.  A keeper who mourned was a keeper who chose sides, and the chronicle would become a complaint. 

So she took the swan from its niche.  It was origami, folded from a single page and worn soft as cloth by the thumbs of every keeper who had ever grown too loud inside.  Her predecessor had left only one instruction: "When you start to judge, ask the bird." 

A firmer mind would have seen wastepaper.  Oriel, who had never insisted that a thing be only itself, opened the first wing and saw that the creases were corridors.  Valley folds ran where the galleries ran; mountain folds stood where the walls stood; and the long crease down the swan's neck, cracked nearly through, was the river that slid beneath the deepest stacks.  Where the neck met the breast, some earlier keeper had inked a single black dot. 

The map did not care that the galleries had since collapsed.  Neither, she decided, would she.  Where the fold said go, she went, and where the way was choked she had to burrow, wriggling through root and loam and the soft slump of encyclopedias, pushing the lantern ahead of her like a thought she had not finished.  Vellum crumbled against her cheeks.  Something in her wanted to apologize to it.  She burrowed on. 

She heard the river before she saw it, a low dark syllable repeated without meaning.  On its bank, exactly where the black dot promised, stood the refuge: a cell of obsidian no larger than a confessional, smooth as a held breath, with a doorway worn round by shoulders. 

She crawled inside and the river's voice stopped at the threshold.  The glass swallowed everything.  Her lantern showed her own face in the black wall, and she waited for the usual verdict, older, thinner, failing, and none arrived. 

This was not the silence of the catacombs, which was only the absence of visitors.  This silence worked.  It moved through her the way sleep moves through a wound, closing nothing by force, simply letting the torn edges remember each other.  The murmuring pages let go of her one by one.  Ruin went first, then pity, then too late, until what remained was the plain capacity to look. 

She stayed for an hour or a winter; she held that question as loosely as the rest.  When she finally unfolded the swan completely, she found, beneath the dot, a line in her predecessor's cramped hand: "The quiet regrows you; fold me back for the next one."  She folded it back, crease by faithful crease, and the paper, thin as it was, held. 

On the way back her tunnel was already slumping, already becoming something else, and she let it.  At her desk a new mushroom had risen through the open chronicle, lifting the page where she had written ruin.  She did not strike the word out, for that too would have been a verdict.  She turned to a clean leaf, set the swan in its niche, and wrote: "East gallery fell; roots have reached the river; a mushroom grows in this book." 

Above her, somewhere, a shelf let go with a long papery sigh.  Oriel listened, dipped her pen, and wrote that down too.
