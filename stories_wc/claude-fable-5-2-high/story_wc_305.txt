Ilse forgave Tobin at dawn, and by noon the forgiveness still sat in her like an emptied basket.  She had expected it to fill her.  The grotto kept its usual hush around her, a great umbral hollow in the mountain's shoulder, so far above the clouds that the only light was what the white sea below threw back up.  In that borrowed glow the vines grew pale and patient, row behind row into the dark, and nobody spoke loudly among them. 

Ilse had tended them for twenty years, and for the last nine she had tended Maren too, the old keeper, turning her, feeding her, carrying her out to hear the leaves.  Maren had died content, which was the part Ilse could not get past.  "Where does it come from?" she had asked near the end.  "Find the source yourself," Maren had whispered, "it is not far." 

Then, the night of the burial, Maren's son Tobin had come up the cliff stair drunk on grief and set fire to the mother vine, three hundred years old, the one from which every other vine in the grotto had been cut.  For a keeper there was no worse thing.  All winter he had slept by the charred stump like a dog by a grave, and this morning Ilse had looked at him and said, "I forgive you; stay," because someone had to decide, and she was the one left.  She had meant it.  She had also hoped, privately, that forgiving the unforgivable was the source Maren meant, and that contentment would come up in her like water.  It had not. 

So she did what she had done since girlhood when a thought would not come down to her: she went up to it.  At the grotto's lip stood three junipers, twisted by wind, and she climbed the tallest, hand over hand, until the trunk swayed and the cold cleared her head.  She had always reasoned better with her feet off the ground. 

Out of habit she drew the pin from her hair and turned it in her fingers.  It was Maren's hairpin, and Maren's teacher's before that, antique silver gone dark, its tip flattened and honed into a tiny curved blade.  Every keeper had used it to scallop the cut faces of a graft, small round bites along scion and stock so the two would lock like clasped hands and heal as one.  The shank was worn thin in two places where thumbs had pressed, and Ilse's thumb fit the hollow exactly. 

From the juniper she could see the whole vineyard at once, which she never could from the rows.  It spread back from the black stump in widening arcs, daughters, then granddaughters, each vine a piece of the one before, pruned to knuckles every winter and green again every spring.  Maren had told her once that the mother vine was itself a cutting, carried up from a valley vineyard that a flood had taken.  Nothing here had ever been first, and nothing had ever been finished. 

That was her mistake, she saw.  She had treated forgiveness as an ending, a door closed gently, and then waited beside it to be rewarded.  But Maren had not been content because things ended well; she had been content because she had put what she carried into other hands before her own let go. 

Below, Tobin knelt at the stump with his palms on his knees, not daring to touch it.  Ilse climbed down. 

She cut two dormant canes from the eldest daughter in the first row, walked to him, and scraped the char at the stump's base with her nail until wet green showed underneath.  "The root is alive," she said quietly.  "You are going to give her back to herself, and I will show you only once." 

His hands shook, so she closed her own around them, kindly and without asking, and set the hairpin between his fingers.  Together they scalloped the living wood, bite by small bite, then the cane to match, and pressed them home and bound them with bast.  He did the second one alone. 

When he tried to return the pin, she shook her head and slid it into his hair, where his mother would have put it.  He wept without sound, as people did in that place. 

Ilse sat back on her heels in the dim, reflected light and listened to the hush, the new leaves not yet come, the clouds turning slowly beneath the world.  The basket in her was not full, exactly, but it was moving from her hands to his, and that, she understood, was the source.  Someday she would be pruned back too, and something would bud.
