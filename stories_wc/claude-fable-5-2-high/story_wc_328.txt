Under the shuttered ice house in Kettle Notch, Odile Wren's garden grew by lamplight, thyme and wintergreen breathing in the dark while frost furred the stairwell above.  I had been her apprentice for six weeks, which was a lie.  My real trade was cryptids, and my real name was on a dissertation the university had laughed out of the building.  Its subject was the Ridge Lifter, the thing my father went looking for twelve winters ago and never walked back from.  He used to say the universe was one long joke and the only sin was refusing to get it.  I had been the punchline for twelve years, and I wanted proof enough to turn that bitterness into a cosmic joke I could tell myself. 

Then, in November, the seven people missing from the ridge came home changed, and I came to Kettle Notch behind them.  They left shallow footprints.  They hummed, each a different note, a chord that never resolved and never soured.  They would not speak to doctors or reporters, only to Odile, and only through the old cordless phone she kept on the potting bench. 

"Clean lines hurt them," she told me, tamping soil around a valerian root.  "Static is the only thing loose enough to carry what they have to say." 

So each night, while she mixed tinctures for their sleeplessness, I crouched behind the lovage with the spare handset pressed to my ear, taking notes.  The confessions came through hiss and crackle like voices under river ice.  A widower admitted he had climbed the ridge hoping not to come down.  A girl said something had lifted her off the snow like a cat testing a cup at a table's edge, curious, almost laughing. 

"It took the weight I was sick of," she whispered, "and gave the rest back." 

Not one of them described a monster.  They described a prankster with gravity for a toy. 

Around my neck hung the only instrument I trusted, a flint arrowhead my father had pried from the ridge when I was nine.  "Somebody tried to pin it down before us," he had said, grinning, and then he tossed me so high the sky seemed to keep me a second too long.  Near the Lifter's old haunts the arrowhead always tugged at its cord.  In Odile's garden, whenever the phone rang, it rose off my collarbone and hung there, pointing up the airshaft. 

On the longest night the phone rang and no one hummed.  Odile listened, then walked straight to the lovage and held the handset out to me. 

"It's asking for Maren Voss," she said, which was not the name I had given her.  "I have known since your necklace first floated, dear." 

The static was colder than the stairwell.  Inside it a voice assembled itself the way frost assembles on glass. 

"Mouse," it said, and nobody living had called me that.  "I have a confession.  I was never taken.  I stayed up here too long, and anyone who stays must metamorphose, so I did.  I send the grieving home lighter because it is the only trick I ever knew.  I could not send myself." 

My notebook slid from my knee.  Twelve years of fieldwork, one ruined career, and the creature I had sworn to catalogue was my own father, still tossing people at the sky.  I waited for the bitterness to rise, and instead I laughed.  It was an ugly, cracked laugh, and it hurt like blood returning to frozen fingers. 

"You got the joke," the static said, delighted.  Every watering can in the garden lifted an inch and set itself down again, and the thyme leaned upward like an audience. 

"Will you come down?" I asked. 

"No, Mouse.  I am not a thing that comes down anymore, and I will not fit in your book." 

I knew it was true.  The returned could never sing in unison, and their chord was beautiful precisely because nobody conducted it, a harmony untamed.  Proof would only be another arrowhead, aimed at something that had never once done harm. 

I carried the handset to the foot of the airshaft, untied the cord, and set the flint on my open palm. 

"Somebody tried to pin you down before me," I said.  "Take it back." 

The arrowhead fell upward, slowly, turning, past the frost and into the black, and far above something caught it.  Snow drifted down the shaft and paused at my face, each flake hanging a second too long. 

Odile handed me a trowel.  "The valerian needs dividing," she said, "and they will call again tomorrow." 

I knelt in the cold soil, lighter by one stone and one grudge, and began to hum my own wrong note.
