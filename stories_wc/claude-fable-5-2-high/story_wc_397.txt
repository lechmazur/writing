Odile Varga woke in the harbor hostel at Hallvik with the ringing gone from her ears.  It had whined there since the diagnosis, fourteen months of a kettle that never boiled, and now there was only wind, and under the wind, something creaking like a stair.  She went to the window and saw that the sea had come ashore as rubble.  Overnight the gale had driven drift ice into the marina, and the floes had shouldered among the fishing boats, cracking hulls, snapping masts, folding the pontoons like wet cardboard.  For a moment she took the white field for her grandmother's lace laid out to dry, and said good morning to it.  The tumor did that; it had loosened her grip on what was real until the world sat in her hand like a bird she had no wish to squeeze. 

She had come north to die.  The clinic stood across the fjord, the papers were signed, and the ferry that should have carried her there on Thursday now lay pinned against the breakwater.  "Nine days, maybe ten," the clinic's coordinator told her by telephone, apologizing as if for a late train. 

Odile found she was not angry.  Her patience, which she had pressed flat and thin for this journey, had simply been folded, and inside the fold was a pocket of days belonging to no plan. 

In her coat she carried the tip of her grandmother's parasol, a ferrule of bone capped in silver, all that the moths had left of four generations of lace.  Her grandmother used to tap it on pavements, cellar doors, the lids of things, and say that anything about to end would tell you so, if you knocked and listened.  Odile had never once heard it, and she did not want to meet her own ending as a stranger. 

So she walked down to the harbor office, where a relief station was forming out of thermoses and shouting, and wrote her name on the volunteer sheet.  They gave her soup to ladle, then, seeing she did not tire of cold, a place in a skiff with a boy named Jonas, whose father's boat lay somewhere under the floes.  Their work was to trawl the black leads between the ice with a weighted net, hauling up fuel cans, crab pots, planking, a child's lifejacket that was mercifully empty.  While Jonas hauled, Odile leaned out and knocked the parasol tip against each floe they passed. 

"You talk to the ice," Jonas said on the third day, not unkindly. 

"I am asking them a question," she said.  "Listen: the sound ones answer low, like a door in a good frame." 

He listened, because loss had made him patient with strange adults, and he heard it. 

By the sixth day she knew the other answer as well: a thin, high tick, almost cheerful, the note a floe gave when it had already decided to part.  Because she held the world so lightly, she did not argue with what she heard, as the harbor men did with their gauges and their pride. 

On the eighth day the net snagged on something heavy, and Jonas saw blue paint through the water, his father's wheelhouse.  He was over the gunwale and onto the nearest floe before she could speak, kneeling to free the line. 

Odile reached out and knocked.  The ice answered thin and high and almost cheerful. 

"Jonas," she said, in the voice her grandmother had used for cellar doors, "come back now." 

He looked at the paint, then at her, and he came.  The floe opened behind his boot with a sound like a page turning, and the two halves swung apart, and the wind went through the gap and across their faces. 

Jonas shook for a while.  Odile sat very still, holding the ferrule, astonished at how gentle it had been.  She had expected an ending to arrive like a slammed door, and instead it had cleared its throat and waited to be heard. 

That evening the coordinator rang to say the ferry would sail on Monday. 

"Keep my papers," Odile said, "but give me a date in spring, after the ice goes out." 

It was not a cure and she did not pretend it was; it was one more fold in her patience, with room inside for a harbor that still needed hands.  She wanted to hear the whole marina let go in April, every floe ticking its small farewell, so that when her own note came she would know it and answer low. 

In the morning she gave Jonas the parasol tip to knock with, and stood on the quay while the wind carried the sound back to her, steady as a door in a good frame.
