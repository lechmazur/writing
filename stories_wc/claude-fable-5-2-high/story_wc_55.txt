Rain seldom climbed as high as the Sanctuary of Unrung Hours, so when Odile woke to the smell of it, she lay very still.  Wet stone, cold iron, something green underneath: petrichor, the scent of her father's foundry yard after a storm.  It reached her through the shutters like a letter from a town she had fled. 

The sanctuary sat beneath the lip of a glacier, and the ice drank sound the way wool drinks water.  No bell had ever hung in that soundless place; no voice rose above a breath.  The keepers called it the glacial hush, and they did not mean mere quiet.  They meant a silence that moved as the ice moved, slowly, wearing grief smooth the way it wore down stone. 

Odile was the clock scribe.  Since nothing could chime, she sat alone in the gear loft watching the great mute movement turn, and at each hour she wrote the time on a slip of paper and slid it down a brass chute to the refectory.  It suited her.  She had always preferred to be read rather than heard. 

That preference had cost her the only thing she ever wanted.  At twenty she had been commissioned to build a singing clock for the square of her hometown, and she had cast its first bell herself, small as a pear, tuned to the note her father hummed while he worked.  Then she had pictured the whole town turning its face toward her sound every hour, forever, and she could not bear it.  She left before fitting the clapper.  The bell had never once been struck. 

For nineteen years it had lain wrapped in felt beneath her cot, and at every rare rain she had stayed in bed until the smell faded.  This morning her hands were older than her father's had been, and she got up. 

She bore the bell down the loft stairs in both arms, though it weighed less than a loaf.  Some weights have nothing to do with bronze. 

Outside lay the forecourt, the one place in the sanctuary that was permitted a voice.  The founders had paved it with hollow-veined crystal quarried from under the glacier, and after rain, as water threaded down through a thousand hairline channels, the whole court whispered.  It was whispering now.  The sun was rising somewhere behind the ice wall, hidden, so that light arrived without a source, gray going to pearl, and in that hush the wet crystal breathed out a sound like pages being turned in another room. 

Odile stood at the threshold with her heart going like an unregulated escapement.  Nobody else was awake, which was the only reason she could do this at all.  She walked to the center, knelt on the cold shining stone, and unwrapped the felt.  The bronze had gone the brown of old tea. 

She had meant only to leave it here, to give the dream back to weather and be done.  But when she set the bell mouth-down on the crystal, the whisper went up inside it.  The hollow she had shaped at twenty gathered those thousand small breaths and rounded them into a single tone, her father's note, so faint she felt it in her knees before she heard it. 

It was excruciatingly gentle.  It did not ring out over any square or turn a single face; it asked for no one, and it hurt precisely because it was so kind.  Odile wept without a sound, as the sanctuary had taught her, rain-smell in her throat and the foundry yard all around her, her father wiping his hands and waiting to hear what she had made. 

She had believed the dream was to be heard by everyone.  Kneeling there, she understood it had only ever been to give time a voice, and that a voice need not be loud to be true.  She had fled the wrong thing, and the bell had waited anyway. 

As the channels drained, the tone thinned and the glacial hush came back over the court, unhurried, and this time she could bear it, because it was no longer the silence of something unfinished.  She left the bell where it sat.  It would sing for no town, only for rain, a few mornings a year, to whoever happened to rise early and cross quietly. 

Back in the gear loft, the mute movement was nearing the hour.  Odile took a slip, wrote the time, and then, for the first time in nineteen years, added a line beneath it.  Sunrise, hidden; the forecourt sang after the rain, and I will show you where.  She slid it down the chute and sat back, hands open in her lap, at peace with the sound of it falling.
