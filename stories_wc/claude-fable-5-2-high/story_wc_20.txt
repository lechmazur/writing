The search party disbanded at dusk, hanging its lanterns back on the foundry hooks one by one.  Nine days of rain had washed their chalk marks from the slag flats, and nobody said the boy's name anymore.  Ilse Varn, assayer of the Harrow Foundry, stayed behind when the last boots left.  Rain fell through the roof vents, hissed on the banked furnace, and ran in bright threads across the casting floor. 

They called her the balanced alchemist, and not kindly.  She weighed everything: ore, wages, faults, mercies, each to the grain, and she had never once let a pan hang crooked. 

On her bench lay two halves of a bronze zodiac coin.  Every worker at Harrow carried one, birth sign on the face, name on the back, and this one showed the Water-Bearer on one side and PER and RIN on the other, parted by her own chisel. 

Perrin Holt, her apprentice, had miscounted a pour and cracked the mold of the cathedral bell.  She had set his fault against his three good years, found the fault heavier by a hair, and done what the guild rule allowed.  One blow on the anvil, his name in two pieces, his place on the roll struck out.  He had looked at the halves, then at her, and walked out into the rain without his coat. 

Now she set both halves on her finest balance.  It was a fact at Harrow, entered in the ledgers as plainly as rainfall, that a coin grew four grains heavier the hour a name was cut into it, though cutting only takes metal away.  Nobody explained it; they simply corrected for it.  Perrin's halves came up four grains short. 

So that was what she had taken from him, and she did not know what it was.  She had weighed a boy without ever learning the weight of his name. 

Ilse knew one honest way to learn anything, which was to push it until it stopped.  She began at the limits.  She said his name flatly over the pan, as she would read an inventory, and the needle did not stir.  She shouted it until the rafters rang, and loudness weighed nothing either.  She warmed the halves to the edge of melting, close enough that the Water-Bearer's jar began to soften, and the scale read exactly the same. 

Near midnight she remembered the traveling potter who had mended her cracked mortar with a seam of gold.  Kintsugi, he had called it, and when she complained that the crack still showed, he had laughed.  "The gold is only the intention made visible," he said.  "It says: here it broke, and here someone stayed." 

She drew a thread of gold from the assay stock, entered it against her own wages, and ran it into the break.  The first time, she worked wanting only her error gone, the ledger of that day made clean.  The seam held, and the coin weighed bronze plus gold and not one grain more.  That was the limit of metal, then, and the limit of wanting to be forgiven. 

She broke the seam along its length and began again.  This time, while the gold ran, she said aloud what she had done.  She said that a hair's difference on a scale is not a verdict, that three good years were not ore, that she had watched him go without his coat.  Then she said his name the way one calls a child in from the rain. 

"Perrin." 

The pan sank four grains, paused, and sank a fifth. 

Ilse sat very still, feeling the same small weight settle behind her own ribs.  So that was the fact of it: a name weighs exactly what the one who speaks it agrees to carry.  She had carried nothing of his, and had called that balance. 

There was a brass dish at the foundry gate where a struck worker, pardoned, could redeem his place by laying down a whole coin.  The rule said the bearer must lay it there himself.  Ilse had kept every rule to its limit; tonight she walked past one. 

She carried the coin through the rain-washed yard, the gold seam catching the furnace glow like a small bright road across his name, and set it in the dish.  She wrote Perrin Holt back onto the roll in her own hand and hung his coat by the gate, under a lit lantern. 

She did not know whether the river had him, or some dry barn beyond the flats.  Mending could not promise that.  It could only say: here it broke, and here someone stayed. 

Ilse left the gate unbarred, stood crooked in the doorway with his name heavy in her, and waited for morning.
