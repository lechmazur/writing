The last ticket stub of the season fluttered into the bin, and Tamsin Averill locked the gate of Averill's Hedge Maze for what she believed was the final time.  From the heart of the maze, Calloway Finch felt the click of the padlock as a small gray square behind his eyes.  He had always been blessed that way: sounds arrived wearing colors, and in life his tenor had been a blue ribbon, applause a spill of amber.  Death had not taken the gift, only the audience. 

He stood on the mossy boards of the orchard stage, hidden where the hedges opened into a garden of apple trees that Tamsin's grandmother had planted around her summer theater.  Visitors rarely reached it now; the paths had grown shy. 

The trouble had begun on closing night of 1931.  Rosalind Averill hand-painted a tarot coin for every show, the Star on one face and the Tower on the other, and Calloway flipped it at the final bow to decide whether the crowd had earned an encore.  That night he tossed it high, lightning split the oldest apple tree, and the coin never came down.  Time split with the trunk.  Ever since, the evening had hung in the orchard in pieces, one unfinished night per tree: here a version where the Star landed and he sang on, there a version where the Tower fell and everyone went home in the rain.  Calloway belonged to none of them.  He was the performer left holding his breath between the bow and the song, and for decades he had wanted only to be asked back. 

Tonight, though, he heard something worse than silence.  In the ticket booth Tamsin was reading a developer's contract aloud to no one, and her voice was the color of dishwater.  She would sign at dawn; the hedges would be bulldozed, the orchard paved, and every hanging night would go out like a lamp. 

Calloway understood that his encore and her future were the same unlanded coin.  Somewhere among the broken evenings a possibility still lingered for this place, and possibility, like an audience at the exit, had to be coaxed back. 

He could not touch her or speak to her, but the maze had always loved his voice.  He sang the opening phrase of the old encore, a lullaby Rosalind had written, straight into the green corridors.  The hedges gave it back, but not once.  Each echo returned from a different piece of the broken night, and each wore its own color: a copper echo from the evening the Star landed, a violet one from the evening of rain, a pale green one from a night that had not happened yet.  Only he could see them, and so only he could do what came next. 

He caught the echoes as they passed and began to intertwine them, copper over violet over green, the way stagehands once braided rope.  He sang again, and again, feeding new strands into the braid until a single thick cord of sound ran from the stage, through every turning, to the ticket booth. 

Tamsin lifted her head.  She knew that tune; her grandmother had hummed it over cider presses and scraped knees, and it smelled, impossibly, of windfall apples and greasepaint.  She set down the pen and followed. 

The paths, no longer shy, unfolded for her as they had when she was seven and running ahead of the lantern.  With every corner the song grew fuller and the years grew thinner, until she stepped into the orchard and found the trees in blossom and in fruit at the same time. 

Calloway drew the braid tight.  The separate evenings slid along it toward one another, tree by tree, and with a sound like a struck bell the split trunk closed.  Something small fell out of ninety years of air. 

Tamsin caught it without thinking.  In her palm lay a wooden coin, its paint still bright, the Star face up, eight yellow points in her grandmother's unmistakable brushwork.  She looked at the empty stage for a long moment.  Then, laughing and crying at once, she did what an Averill does when the Star lands: she clapped. 

One pair of hands, and the whole orchard flooded amber.  Calloway bowed, and at last sang the song all the way through.  She could not see him, but she heard it, and she stayed until the final blue note faded into the leaves. 

At dawn the contract went unsigned into the bin with the ticket stubs.  A new sign went up by the gate that week: SUMMER THEATER RETURNING, ENCORES DECIDED BY COIN.  In the orchard, Calloway Finch waited in the wings, patient now, listening for amber.
