The final warning arrived on the queen's breakfast tray, folded inside a napkin of white lace.  "Majesty, the ridge keeps what climbs it," her chancellor had written, in a hand as pretty as embroidery.  "We would grieve so beautifully." 

Queen Ysolde read it twice, buttered her bread, and sent for her walking boots. 

She had stopped fearing Chancellor Vane the same winter she stopped believing in her crown.  The people adored her, and that was the trouble.  They adored her when she was unjust, when she was absent, when she yawned through their petitions, and love that could not be lost had begun to taste like nothing at all. 

Then there were the dreams.  Every night for a year she had stood in a cave of deep cobalt stone while a star, pinned somewhere overhead, bled its light down the walls in slow silver threads.  Every night she held her breath, and every morning she woke with her lungs aching and her question unanswered. 

Shepherds called the place the ember grotto on the ridge, because its mouth glowed red at dusk above the cloud line.  Vane called it forbidden, three times, each warning more exquisitely polite than the last. 

So, after the final one, she began to climb. 

Two guards moved to block the postern gate.  "You will step aside," Ysolde said, hardly louder than the wind, "and you will tell the chancellor I admired his napkin."  They stepped aside; she had never needed to raise her voice, and lately she wondered whether that was her gift or someone else's stitching. 

The path wound through wet fog, then through the top of the fog, until the clouds lay below her like a tablecloth spread for a quiet poisoning.  At sunset the grotto's mouth smoldered ahead, and she saw that the embers were not fire but beads of clotted light, crusted around the entrance like old sealing wax. 

Inside, the red gave way to blue.  The chamber was cobalt from floor to vault, exactly as she had dreamed it, and across the far wall hung a woman-shaped brightness sewn to the rock with a thousand fine stitches.  From every stitch, starlight seeped.  It ran in threads down the stone, pooled in a carved channel, and trickled away toward the valley, toward the wells her people drank from. 

"You are late," the star said, in a voice like frost on a windowpane.  "Your great-grandmother came with a needle, and your chancellors have kept me mended ever since." 

Ysolde looked at the channel, and her year of tasteless love arranged itself into sense.  "They drink you," she said. 

"They drink me, and they adore whoever wears the crown, and Vane rules them through your pretty silence." 

"Then why send me dreams?" 

"Because I felt you stop believing, and a disenchanted queen is the only kind who might cut a thread." 

Ysolde drew her belt knife and sawed at the nearest stitch; the blade skated off as if off diamond. 

"Steel will not do it," the star said.  "She sewed me on a held breath, hers and mine together, both of us afraid, and what was fastened in fear holds until it is let go in wonder." 

"I cannot wonder on command," Ysolde said.  "I have not managed it in years." 

"Nor I," said the star, almost kindly, which was worse than the chancellor's lace. 

Far below, a horn sounded; Vane's men were on the path, coming with fresh thread and, no doubt, beautiful regrets.  Ysolde sat down on the cold floor beside the bleeding wall, because there was nothing else to do, and watched the cave mouth darken. 

It was the first warm night of the year.  Out of the cloud sea, impossibly high, a single green spark rose, then a dozen, then a drifting constellation of fireflies, small lights that belonged to no one and fed no well. 

Ysolde had not seen the first fireflies of summer since she was a girl with no crown.  The star had not seen free light in ninety years.  Neither of them meant to, but they breathed out together, one long astonished exhale, and the sound of it was a thousand stitches parting. 

The brightness peeled from the wall, whole and no longer bleeding, and went out among the fireflies and up.  The channel ran dry. 

Ysolde stood, lungs easy for the first time in a year, and knew she would sleep without dreams.  By autumn her people would love her or not, as she deserved.  She gathered one cold ember from the entrance, a keepsake for the chancellor's breakfast tray, and started down to meet his men. 

"You will step aside," she practiced, softly, and smiled like a needle.
