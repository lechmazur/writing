The last jar of honey had gone hard as amber by the time Oriel Sabine admitted the beekeeper was never coming back.  He had said so himself on his final visit, standing in the museum's dripping vestibule with frost in his beard.  "The upland hives are dead, and nobody learned the keeping from me," he told her, as if reporting weather.  Then he paid for his lamp oil with the jar and with a thing he had pulled from the mud of drowned Marrowmere: a single emerald-tinted opera glass lens, its brass rim scratched with the letters I.V. 

Oriel was a guide at the Museum of Carried Things in Cascara, the grotto city that sat behind the monumental waterfall called the Veil like a breath held behind teeth.  She could date a teaspoon by its hallmark and recite the provenance of every loom in the east gallery, and visitors called her cultured, which she privately translated as lonely.  In winter the waterfall froze into a white curtain, the city went quiet, and the rosewater the bakers used drifted through the tunnels, sweet as a funeral. 

This winter the quiet had a deadline.  The council had decreed that when the Veil thawed, barges would carry the Marrowmere families, displaced three years ago by the dam, downriver to scattered labor camps.  Oriel knew, the way she knew dates, what scattering did.  Her own great-grandmother had come through the falls as a displaced child, and of all that vanished people's arts exactly one still lived: the family custom of baking bread shaped like initials, a loaf for each person at table, so that nobody sat down nameless.  The rest lay in her cases, labeled, silent, dead.  She would not label Marrowmere too. 

But the families in the lower galleries would not speak to her.  They had seen her cases, and they did not intend to end up inside one. 

So she studied the lens instead.  Held over the museum's one Marrowmere relic, an opera score whose pages seemed blank but for a faint pink stain, the green glass turned the stain black, and staves of notes rose up like drowned streets at low water.  Marrowmere's prompters had inked their scores in madder, to be read through green glass in the dark of the pit, and I.V. had been one of them.  Oriel could see the music now, but she could not hear it, and that was the whole grief of her profession. 

That night she set the beekeeper's jar beside the oven to thaw, and when the honey ran again she worked it into dough with rosewater, the way her great-grandmother had.  She shaped two loaves, an I and a V, tucked the score under her arm, and carried them warm down the wet stairs, past doors that closed as she came.  "In my family, this means a place is kept for you," she told the dark corridor, and she set the bread on the stones and waited, feeling foolish and afraid. 

A door opened.  The woman who came out was old and upright, with hair the color of ice, and she looked at the letters for a long time before she looked at Oriel.  "Ilse Varga," she said.  "Nobody has set a place for me since the water came." 

Oriel did not ask for an interview or a donation; she held out the lens, and then, because it was harder, she let go of it.  Ilse raised the green glass to her eye as if she had never left the pit, and Oriel opened the score.  The voice that came out of the old woman was cracked at first, river ice in March, and then it thawed and ran.  Doors opened all along the corridor, and children crept out, following the smell of bread, mouthing the tune. 

All that month Ilse taught in the east gallery among the silent looms, and Oriel baked: a T for Tomas, an M for Mirela, a letter for every child, Marrowmere or Cascaran, who could carry an aria out of the dark.  She wrote no labels. 

On the morning the Veil broke, the ice came down with a sound like a cathedral falling, and green light poured through the freed water into every street.  The barges were already nosing into the lower dock.  Oriel could not stop them, and the dread of it sat on her tongue with the rosewater, sweet and impossible to swallow.  But forty children were singing on the stairs, and Ilse Varga, lens to her eye, was beating time.  At the gangway a Marrowmere girl pressed something warm into Oriel's hands: a clumsy loaf, shaped like an O.  Wherever the river scattered them, a place was being kept.
