Three days after the mooring tower fell, its dust still sifted down on the glass dome like slow, patient rain.  Inside, Ilse Vane walked the rows of desert roses and taught them, as she did every noon.  "Hold your water," she told the swollen trunks, pouring each a single measured cup.  "Bloom toward your neighbor, not over her."  She pruned only in calm, never in temper, and grafted the generous bloomers onto the stubborn roots, so that patience and giving grew in one body.  Her mother called it nonsense.  Yet the rows Ilse had schooled in restraint had kept their flowers through the fall, while the wild ones by the wall dropped every petal at the shock.  Since the fall she had been weaving a shade mat for the seedlings out of whatever the wind brought from the wreck: rope ends, signal ribbon, a strip of someone's sleeve.  Broken things, she believed, could still be made to shelter new ones.  The sun stood white above the dome, and the pink blossoms burned in it without moving, fire that had learned to sit still. 

Ilse noticed things.  She noticed them senselessly, without wanting to and without any use for them: that the third pane from the door hummed in an east wind, that the tower had taken eleven heartbeats to fall, that her mother's left boot was resoled.  So she noticed, now, that the leaves in the ninth row were trembling, though the air was still. 

A boy lay curled behind the oldest trunk, soot-black, in a rigger's harness, one fist locked around a scrap of canvas.  "They say I cut the ballast," he whispered.  "They say I sent the Halcyon into the tower." 

"Did you?" 

"I grabbed the bag when it went, and this is all that stayed in my hand." 

She took the scrap of sandbag and turned it in the light.  Guild double-lock stitching, a quartermaster's lot stamp, and one edge cut so clean it shone: a long curved stroke, made from above, by a blade far broader than any rigger's spike.  She knew that curve.  Only Guild warrant blades carried it, and she had watched one hang at her mother's hip for nineteen years. 

Dessa Vane came in out of the glare with a warrant folded in her belt and that blade already bare.  She stopped, as she always stopped, at the entry pane.  Years ago Ilse had engraved one word there with a diamond stylus, QUIET, and beneath it the dome's only law: no edge drawn under this glass except to tend.  The letters threw their thin shadow across Dessa's scarred knuckles. 

"He is here," Dessa said.  "The Guild pays four hundred, and four hundred rebuilds a great deal." 

"It rebuilds nothing," Ilse said, and did not raise her voice, because she never did.  She stepped between the blade and the ninth row, hands open, holding only the scrap.  "You always said I see everything and it is good for nothing."  "Look at the cut, Mother." 

Dessa looked.  She was a hunter; she read edges the way Ilse read leaves.  Slowly she laid her own blade against the canvas, and the curve matched like a key in its lock. 

"A warden cut that bag," Ilse said.  "The Guild brought the tower down, and it is paying you to bury the one witness." 

The heat pressed down, enormous and calm.  Somewhere beyond the glass the city was still shouting, sorting its dead, choosing whom to hate. 

"If I let him go, I am finished with them," Dessa said.  "I do not know what I am after that." 

"Neither did the roses," Ilse said.  "I taught them anyway, one noon at a time, and when the tower fell they held."  "Somebody has to be the part that holds." 

Dessa's eyes went to the engraved word, then to the blossoms burning steady in their rows, then to her daughter's open hands.  It took her three breaths to sheathe the blade.  The click of steel going home was the loudest sound the dome had heard in years, and then the quiet closed over it, deeper than before, as if engraved into the air itself. 

Dessa left at dusk with the boy beside her and the cut edge of the scrap in her belt where the warrant had been, bound for the pilots' court instead of the Guild hall.  Ilse kept the rest.  She wove the frayed canvas into the shade mat, between the signal ribbon and the sleeve, and stretched it over the seedling bed.  Then she knelt in the last red light, poured a single measured cup, and began the lesson again. 

"Hold," she told the smallest rose.
