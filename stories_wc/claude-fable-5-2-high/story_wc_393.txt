Rain had driven everyone off Calloway Street except Chaplain Tobias Wren, who stood dripping inside Pell's Antique Music Box Repair with a parcel held against his chest.  The shop was quiet the way a choir is quiet before the downbeat.  Hundreds of cylinders and steel combs waited on the shelves, every one of them holding a song behind its teeth.  Lena Pell did not look up from her bench until she had seated the screw she was turning.  Then she looked at his collar, at the word HARROW stitched on his state-issue coat, and at his face, in that order, giving each its time. 

"You wrote me a letter," she said. 

"I did, and you sent it back unopened, which was fair."  He unwrapped the parcel on her counter.  Inside the flannel lay a clockwork heart, a brass movement no bigger than a fist, shaped with chambers and a mainspring where the pulse should be.  "A man at Harrow built this in the workshop over eleven years, out of clock scrap and patience." 

"My father," Lena said, and did not touch it. 

"He left it to me, God knows why, and it has never run."  "I wanted to bring it to you running, but a city clockmaker gave it four minutes and called it seized scrap, so I am here for a second opinion." 

The words soured in his mouth, because he had been a second opinion once himself.  Two winters ago the prison psychologist had recommended Amos Pell for parole, and the board, uneasy, had asked the chaplain what he thought.  Wren had thought about it for the length of a hallway.  Amos never came to chapel, never said the word sorry aloud, only bent over his brass, and so Wren had told them, "He does not yet deserve it."  Amos died in the infirmary that March. 

"Your letter was an apology," Lena said. 

"It was words, and I had punished your father for lacking words while his hands said everything."  "So I came to turn the apology into action: carry this where it belongs, pay for its repair, do whatever the work asks." 

Thunder rolled over the roof, and a few combs on the shelves hummed in sympathy, a chord too faint to name.  Lena pulled on cotton gloves, one finger at a time.  She was aptly deliberate, slow in exactly the way a hairspring demands, and Wren suspected she had learned it from the man he had hurried past.  She turned the heart under her lamp, tried the winding arbor with two fingers, and felt it refuse. 

"Well?" he asked. 

"Four minutes buys you a four-minute opinion," she said.  "Lamplight flattens brass, and I read a movement by the way light breaks through storm clouds, low and slanted, when every scratch throws a shadow."  "Sit down, Chaplain, and we will wait for the weather." 

So they waited.  She set the heart on a square of felt in the front window, and he sat on a stool among the silent boxes.  The rain thinned to a whisper.  Nothing in the room moved, yet everything in it seemed wound, as if one loosened pin would set four hundred songs going at once.  Wren had sat through many silences at Harrow, but those were the silences of things shut.  This one felt like something about to open. 

"Did he deserve it?" Lena asked, not turning from the glass. 

"I do not know," Wren said.  "I only know I answered before I had looked." 

She touched the small brass key she wore on a cord at her throat.  "He mailed me this nine years ago with no note, and it fits nothing I own," she said. 

Then the clouds over Calloway Street tore along one seam.  Light came down through the gap in a long gold blade, crossed the wet cobbles, entered the window, and lay sideways across the clockwork heart.  Every chamber stood up in relief.  Lena bent close, and Wren heard her breath stop. 

"It is not seized," she said.  "There is a stop pin set across the mainspring on purpose, and a keyhole under the lower chamber, and writing."  She read the hairline scratches aloud, slowly: "Not broken, only waiting; the chaplain carries it, my girl has the key." 

Wren understood then that Amos had given a second opinion too, and had judged the man who failed him worth an errand. 

Lena lifted the cord over her head and fitted the key beneath the lower chamber.  "I am not saying you deserve this," she said, "but he asked for both of us, so hold it steady while I turn." 

He cupped the brass in both hands, and in the gold light, in the last of the hush, she began to wind.
