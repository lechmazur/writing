The story famine had ended three winters ago, though nobody in Harrow Vale had yet been seen to eat.  The Quiet Laws were repealed, the libraries were ash, and people who had gone thirty years without a tale now flinched from one, the way starved bellies refuse a feast.  Ilse Varn understood small portions.  She was copyist to the Vale Philharmonic, and for nineteen years she had ruled staves and inked every flute part, every rest, without one blot.  Wordless symphonies were the only music the Laws had allowed, so she knew better than anyone how much could be carried in the gaps.  Her teacher, old Maren, had died saying the famine left folk as dust: dry, settled, unable to hold a shape.  Ilse, who disliked waste, had decided to teach dust to tell stories. 

So each Thursday, after rehearsal, she climbed through thistles to the old lime kiln above the vale.  Charcoal burners still banked embers in its draw alcove, and the mist that pooled on the hill every dusk swallowed the faint red breath of the place.  Behind a loose brick she kept a shortwave set rebuilt from a confiscated one, its aerial threaded up the flue.  The Bureau of Frequencies had outlived the Laws it served, as bureaus do, and still sent a warden to seize unlicensed transmitters. 

Ilse lit no lamp.  The amber glow of the dial was light enough, and it was the only light her manuscript answered to.  By day the pages were a dull viola part; under the dial, the words she had written between the staves in kiln-phosphor ink rose up, secretly luminous, pale green as glowworms. 

She did not broadcast stories.  She broadcast songs, plain little tunes, and she cloaked the story inside them, one sliver per verse.  A fox owes the miller a winter.  The debt is kept in a thimble.  The lyrics were elusive on purpose; they slid away from the ear unless a listener caught hold and wondered, and wondering was the first mouthful. 

Tonight she sang the ninth verse into the microphone in her small, exact voice: the fox at the mill door, the thimble empty, the miller's lamp going out.  She had never written a tenth. 

Gravel shifted behind her.  A man stood at the mouth of the alcove with mist on his shoulders and a Bureau badge on his coat.  Ilse finished the bar, because one finishes the bar, and switched the microphone off. 

"Warden Brandt," she said.  "You have thistle burrs on your trousers." 

"I have had them for six Thursdays," he said.  "The detector van found your signal in the first week."  He was perhaps thirty, born in the famine's hungriest year, with a voice like a drawer of dry paper. 

"Then you are slow to make arrests." 

"I am slow at something."  He looked at the set, the glowing dial, the green words breathing on the page.  "My mother sits by our wireless with her face in that same amber light, and last week she said the fox might have a daughter."  "She has not said might in my lifetime." 

Ilse kept her hands folded, which hid their shaking tolerably well.  "And you, Warden?" 

"I need to know what the miller does."  He said it stiffly, like a man confessing to theft.  "The form requires me to take the transmitter tonight, and I find I cannot file it without the ending." 

Here was the choice she had been copying toward for nineteen years.  She could hand him an ending, neat as a final chord, and he would carry it and the set down the hill together. 

"There is no tenth verse," she said.  "I left the stave empty for whoever was hungry enough."  She switched it on again and held the microphone out to him. 

Brandt stared at it as dust must stare at rain.  The embers ticked.  Across the vale, in a hundred kitchens, the dials glowed and the carrier wave hissed and waited. 

"The miller," he began, and stopped, and began again in something near her tune.  "The miller opens the door, because the fox's daughter has brought the winter back, folded small." 

It did not scan, and Ilse, who corrected everything, corrected nothing. 

Afterward he wrote on his form that the signal's source could not be located in such mist, and picked the burrs from his trousers one by one.  "Thursday," he said, "my mother will want a verse." 

"Then she must mind the thistles," said Ilse, and in the phosphor ink she ruled an eleventh stave, empty, beneath the tenth.  Under the dial's glow its five blank lines shone, patient and green, like dust beginning to hold a shape.
