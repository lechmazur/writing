Eight months after receiving the donor heart, Vesna Orlo climbed the rope ladder to the tea house on the floating rock.  Below her, the valley had vanished into mist, so the great stone seemed to hang from nothing, tethered by a single chain that sang when the wind leaned on it.  She climbed slowly, counting beats that were not originally hers. 

For forty years Vesna had been a dye master, and the trade had made her watchful.  She could see the instant a vat of indigo turned, the breath between green and blue, and she had always watched people the same way, from the edge of the room, saying little.  Nobody had ever told her anything. 

Then came the surgery, and afterward strangers began handing her their lives.  A nurse confessed a broken engagement; a ferryman described his drowned brother; a child in the market explained, gravely, the whole history of her doll.  Vesna did not trust a color she could not account for, and she wanted to learn why some people magnetize stories while others, like her old self, repel them.  The donor family's letter had given her one thread: Odile kept the tea house above Sennet Valley, and everyone who climbed up told her everything. 

Odile's brother, Pell, kept it now.  He poured smoked tea without meeting her eyes, a broad man gone silent, and when she explained who she was he only nodded at her chest as if greeting someone behind her. 

"She left no secret," he said.  "Only that." 

Above a low stool in the darkest corner, pinned to the wall, hung a single blue morpho wing.  In that shade it was the brown of a dead leaf. 

Vesna knew the wing the way a sailor knows a reef.  She had spent a decade trying to dye that blue and failed, because the morpho holds no pigment at all; its scales are tiny empty ridges that only return whatever light arrives at the right angle. 

"Why hang it where no light reaches?" she asked. 

Pell shrugged.  "She called it iridescent caution, and never explained." 

So Vesna did what she knew how to do: she watched.  The tea house walls were slatted, and as the rock turned slowly on its chain, blades of sun crept across the floorboards.  The boards were scored with faint curving scratches, hundreds of them, worn pale as old thread.  She crouched and followed one with her fingertip, then another, and by tracking sun trails through that whole long afternoon she understood what the scratches recorded. 

They were the drag marks of the guest cushion.  Hour by hour, season by season, Odile had hauled it after the light, so that whoever sat down to tea sat in the sun, while she herself kept to the stool in the dark. 

Vesna dragged the cushion along the freshest groove to where the last gold bar lay, and asked Pell to sit.  He sat, grumbling, his cup in his hands.  She took the stool. 

Nothing happened for a moment except mist breathing through the slats.  Then the sun struck the tea in his cup, glanced upward, and the dead leaf on the wall went off like a struck match, a quietly explosive blue that made no sound and changed the whole room. 

Pell stared past her shoulder.  "It did that whenever anyone lifted a cup," he said, and his voice cracked open.  "I thought it was her doing." 

"It was you," Vesna said.  "It was always the guest." 

She saw it whole then, the way she saw a vat turn.  Odile had not drawn stories with any color of her own; she had been careful enough to hold still, plain as a closed wing, and to arrange each visitor in the light until their own brightness came back to them from her corner.  That was the caution, and that was the iridescence.  People had not confided in Odile because she shone, but because she let their own light illuminate what they carried.  And Vesna, guarding a stranger's heart, had lately gone still in a new way, no longer watching people to judge their color but simply leaving room for it. 

Pell was already talking.  He told her about Odile at nine, catching fog in a jar; about the chain she oiled every solstice; about the morning the rock felt too light. 

Vesna did not interrupt.  When the sun bar slid, she nudged his cushion a hand's width along the groove, then settled back into the shade, where the borrowed heart kept its steady, listening time.  Above her the wing burned blue, and below them the valley mist glowed as if it, too, had finally been asked a question.
