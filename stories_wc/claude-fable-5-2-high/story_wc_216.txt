After numbers changed meaning, Odile kept time for Harrow Flats without them.  The digits still sat on clock faces and price tags, but they had slipped loose of amounts, and no two people agreed what a seven counted anymore.  So she worked from the silo, a grain tower some hopeful co-op had sheathed in copper, which the rain had since turned emerald.  A slot in the roof dropped a blade of sunlight down the inner wall, and when it touched each of her painted marks, she rang the bell.  The town planted, ate, and slept by that bell. 

Odile herself was living on borrowed time.  A doctor had once given her heart six months, back when six meant something, and she had no way to know how far past the loan she had wandered.  If she could not learn how much time she had, she wanted at least to learn what it was made of, its grain, its texture as it passed. 

Maren, who kept the bell before her, had claimed to know.  "I wrote it inside my urn," Maren had said, dying cheerfully, "and the urn goes in the town capsule, so you will have to wait like everyone else." 

The capsule was a steel drum set under the silo floor, its plaque promising an opening in twenty-five years, a phrase that now promised nothing.  Then the spring thaw heaved the floor, and Odile found the drum split along its seam like a dropped egg.  It had been broken a long while; floodwater had carried most of the contents out through the old grain drain and down into town.  What remained was pulp, rust, and one fragment of a green-glazed cremation urn.  On its inner curve, in Maren's slanting hand, ran three words: "is not how."  The broken edge bit her thumb when she picked it up. 

She carried it in her apron pocket after that, worrying it like a coin, and thought about how to rediscover the rest.  A notice would not do; people in Harrow Flats distrusted anything that asked outright.  But everyone read the cards at the garage sales.  Since prices had gone meaningless, things were traded by description, and a good card was studied the way sermons once were.  So Odile began selling her dead clocks, one each sale day, and hid her question inside what she wrote about them. 

"Mantel clock, walnut, stopped; kept company with a green pot that broke; if a piece of that pot sits on your windowsill, look for writing inside," read the first card.  The second, tied to a cuckoo clock, added that the pieces wanted to meet again in the green silo. 

The town answered the way she had hoped, in kind.  A card on a dented colander mentioned that its owner had a green shard reading "what it wears," and wondered whose voice that was.  A card on a rocking horse confessed to two pieces and a guilty conscience.  People started arriving early at the sales just to read, guessing at the sentence, arguing over it, and Odile felt the waiting itself thicken into something she could almost hold. 

Then they came up the hill, on a day she did not try to name, with shards wrapped in handkerchiefs and egg cartons.  Inside the emerald silo they fitted the urn together on her workbench, a boy named Tavish holding the base steady with both hands.  Odile's fragment went in last.  She pressed it home and noticed, with a small shock, that the edge no longer bit.  All that carrying, all that worrying with her thumb, had worn the break smooth as a river stone. 

Tavish peered down its throat and read aloud, "Time is not how much, it is what it wears smooth, and who stood near while it," and there the words fell into a gap, one piece still missing.  Nobody groaned; a few people laughed, because of course Maren would. 

Odile looked up and saw the blade of sunlight had slid well past its mark, the bell unrung, and not one neighbor had turned to leave.  The afternoon had gone suddenly timeless, and so, she realized, had she: the loan she had fretted over was not a sum to be repaid but an edge to be worn smooth in company.  She rang the bell late, and it sounded the same. 

At the next sale, a card appeared on a lidless teapot: "Its owner has something green, with writing, and is not ready to part with it yet."  Odile read it twice, smiled, and wrote on her last clock's card that the timekeeper could wait.
