The river came apart in the ravine the way a chord comes apart into notes.  Ines Varga, polymer chemist, stood ankle-deep where one channel became five, each strand of water sliding around gravel bars and willow islands before braiding back together downstream.  On every strand rode her foam. 

She had built the molecule eleven years ago, a surfactant with a backbone so stable that nothing in nature could find a place to bite it.  She had heard it then as she heard everything, symphonically: one long unbroken melody of carbon, a line with no rests.  The company called it Evermore, and it washed out of a million laundry rooms and never stopped singing. 

Her brother Tomas had told her so, the night their paths split.  He was a cellist who had quit the conservatory to count mayflies in this ravine, and he had stood in her kitchen holding a jar of stiff white froth from his survey site.  "You wrote a piece that cannot end," he said.  She chose the patent, he chose the river, and for eleven years they sent each other nothing but birthday cards. 

Then his heart stopped in March, and a box arrived: his field notebooks, his waders, and a film canister holding an empty chrysalis.  On the lid he had written, in pencil, For Ines, when she is ready. 

She had not understood it, so she had come here instead, every weekend since, wearing his waders, to finish his survey.  It was the only form of atonement she could think of: to stand in what she had made and count what was left. 

Today his protocol asked her to map the foam.  She climbed a gravel bar, opened his notebook, and began tracing the white lines onto his hand-drawn chart of the braid.  At first the patterns seemed like accusation written in cursive.  But her pencil kept doing something her mind had not yet caught up with.  Wherever a bar divided the current, the foam line tore, thinned, and vanished; wherever two strands met again, it gathered into a bright seam, drawn along the exact line where the waters joined. 

The river was not broken by its dividing.  Each divergent path carried cold water to a different bed of roots and larvae, and every parting was already leaning toward a confluence.  The foam was simply showing her the score: here the voices separate, here they return. 

Her hand went to the canister in her vest pocket.  The chrysalis lay in her palm, weightless, a pale green husk split along one clean seam.  It was a polymer too, she thought, chitin and protein, strong enough to hold a dissolving caterpillar through a storm.  But it had been written with its own ending inside it, a line of weakness placed exactly where the wings would need to leave.  And afterward the husk did not linger; rain and fungus would take it apart and hand it to the milkweed. 

The pieces fell into place the way a final movement gathers its themes, and as they fell she spoke the chemistry aloud to the water.  Ester links, set into the backbone every twelve carbons, like rests in a measure.  Strong in the bottle and the wash, but cut by the enzymes of river bacteria into short fragments those same bacteria could eat.  Every break in the chain would become a meal, then a mayfly, then a trout.  Separation, translated into connection. 

She saw, too, what Tomas had been translating all along.  He had not mailed her a rebuke; he had mailed her a design brief, and a forgiveness that had only been waiting for her to split along the right seam. 

She cried then, but not the way she had in March, when grief had been a single clenched thing with no place to bite it.  This came in pieces, loosened, and each piece went somewhere useful. 

She could not unmake Evermore, and she would not pretend that a better molecule erased the old one.  To atone, she decided, was not to be forgiven but to keep wading: to publish the new chemistry unpatented, under both their names, and to count this ravine every season until the foam lines faded from the chart. 

She set the chrysalis on a milkweed leaf at the head of the bar, where the next rain would find it.  Then she waded into the widest strand, humming the cello line he used to practice through her bedroom wall, and let it rest where it needed to rest.  Downstream, out of sight, the five waters were already finding each other.
