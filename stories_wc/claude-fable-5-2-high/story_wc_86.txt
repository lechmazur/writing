Mist climbed the storm-worn parapet of Hallow Keep and the wind combed it sideways, so that Ilse Marrow seemed to stand on the rim of nothing.  Neither the Vael highlanders nor the Corrin river towns claimed this ridge; the ruin was neutral territory because both had failed to hold it.  That was why a prisoner exchange negotiator could work here, and why everyone spoke low. 

In her coat pocket she turned an antique drawer pull, a brass ring on a worn backplate, smooth from three years of her thumb.  It had come off the desk where she once kept two calendars in one drawer, the Vael counting from thaw and the Corrin from harvest, and never laid them side by side.  They had disagreed by a single day.  On that day a Vael escort waited alone at a ford, decided it was an ambush, and in the panic a captive Corrin boy named Tobin Dane was killed.  Ilse had pried the pull loose before she burned the desk, because a mistake thrown away teaches nothing, and she meant to recycle hers into wisdom. 

So now she set no dates.  Both sides looked up at the same moon, and no clerk could miscopy it: terms were agreed at the new moon, prisoners were counted at first quarter, and the exchange came on the night of the full. 

At the last new moon, across a table in this same ruin, she had finally said to Hesper Dane, who led the Corrin escort, that she was sorry for her son.  Hesper had signed the terms and answered nothing.  Half a month later the silence still stood between them like a third delegation. 

Tonight nine captives waited under guard at each stair, north and south, each line roped wrist to wrist, each lead rope run out along the parapet to the chalk mark where Ilse stood.  She drew out the brass pull and knotted both ropes to its ring, so that neither side could move first without her feeling it. 

Hesper came out of the mist, her face furrowed by weather and by the other thing.  "There is no moon," she said quietly.  "Your rule, negotiator: no full moon, no exchange." 

Behind her the Corrin guards shifted, and across the stones the Vael captain, Orrin Thace, put a hand on his hilt.  Ilse felt the old ford open under her feet, one side waiting, one side doubting, and the mist ready to be called treachery. 

But she was uncommonly thoughtful, made more so by one bad day, and she had long since asked herself what a ridge does to a sky.  "At first quarter the sky was clear and we all saw the half moon together," she said.  "I gave each of you a cord that night and asked for one knot at every dusk."  "Lay them here, side by side." 

Orrin came forward, and after a long breath so did Hesper, and two cords went down on the wet coping stone.  Ilse counted aloud, touching each knot, seven on the Vael cord, seven on the Corrin. 

"Seven dusks past the half," she said.  "The moon is full above this mist whether or not it shows itself to us." 

Nobody spoke; the wind worried the ropes; then Orrin's hand left his sword and Hesper gave one slow nod.  Ilse loosened the two knots on the brass ring together and let both ropes fall in the same instant. 

"Untether them," she said, only loud enough to carry, and at both stairs fingers went to the wrist ropes.  Eighteen people walked past one another along the parapet without a word, shoulders brushing, and vanished down opposite stairs. 

Hesper did not follow.  She looked at the brass in Ilse's hand and asked what it was. 

"The handle of the drawer where I kept the day your son died," Ilse said.  "It opens nothing now, so I make it hold what I cannot afford to drop." 

Hesper took it, weighed it, and ran her own thumb around the ring where Ilse's had worn it bright.  "The cords were wise," she said at last.  "Tobin would have liked a thing he could count with his hands." 

It was not forgiveness, and both women knew the shape of the word well enough to hear its absence.  But Hesper pressed the pull back into Ilse's palm and closed her fingers over it. 

"Bring it at the new moon," she said.  "There are still forty to trade." 

Then she went down the south stair, and the mist above the ridge paled, lit from behind by a moon Ilse could not see.  Something roped tight in her for three years slackened by one knot.
