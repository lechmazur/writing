Rain had just left Skerrow, and the wind that followed it combed the islet's one bent pine like a patient hand.  Teodor Hale stood among his dripping bonsai and chose his words the way he chose branches, slowly, and usually too late.  "You have cut the leader, Pim," he told the boy who rowed over for lessons, each word arriving so slowly that it landed like a verdict.  Pim set down the shears, flushed, and went to sulk on the jetty.  Teodor meant to add that the little juniper would recover, but the kind half of his thought, as always, was still being pruned when the listener left. 

That was how he had lost Maren.  Islanders called that night the severance, because the storm broke the causeway to the mainland, but Teodor used the word for the quarrel he had let stand an hour before it.  His wife, a dyer and lacemaker, had waited at the door for two words, received silence, and taken the ferry Linnet into weather that never gave her back.  "Everything unsaid has a color," she used to tell him, "and I am tired of living in your gray."  He had wondered ever since what color an apology turned when nobody spoke it. 

Afterward, the fishermen swore the Linnet still crossed the channel at dusk, lampless and silent, and in the spring her compass came ashore in the wrack below his garden.  A ghost ship's compass has no use for north.  Its needle, green with brine, pointed only at what was owed, and for a month it pointed stubbornly at a drawer in his own house.  Inside lay Maren's last lace collar, worked in the antique Skerrow pattern her grandmother had taught her, its thread an odd chalky white. 

The old lacemakers of the islet had hidden messages for their sailors in such work, and Teodor, who taught children to count buds before cutting, knew how to count.  Picots were letters, twisted bars were pauses, and over many evenings the instructions came loose from the pattern like a knot from wire.  After rain, at the ebb, go where the tide remembers.  Give the water this lace, say what you kept, and you will see the color. 

He did not know where a tide remembered, but the evening he read the last row, the needle swung free of the drawer and settled toward the north shelf.  He waited nine dry days for rain, and now, with the clouds gone and the ebb running, he left his sulking pupil on the jetty and followed it. 

The needle led him over wet thrift and slick basalt to a pool he recognized, the one where Maren used to rinse her skeins.  Rainwater lay sweet on top of the salt, and beyond the shelf the sea breathed in and out, rhythmically placid, as if nothing had ever been taken.  He knelt and laid the collar on the water. 

"Maren," he said, and for once he did not trim the sentence first, "I was wrong, and I am sorry, and I should have said it at the door." 

The chalky thread darkened.  Then color began to diffuse from the lace, slow as his own speech, unfurling through the pool in threads and then in clouds: cerulean, the blue of a sky seen from underwater. 

He understood then what he had been too slow to ask.  Maren had mixed this dye and locked it in the thread long before the storm, which meant the pattern had never been written for his apology alone.  She had been carrying her own unspoken ones, stitch by stitch, waiting for a rain and a husband quick enough to go to the pool with her.  The blue was the color of two silences, not one, and that hurt more and weighed less. 

The tide turned while he knelt.  The first wave over the lip of the shelf thinned the cerulean and drew it seaward, and he let it go, knowing now how remembrance worked on an island.  It went out, it came back, and what it carried each time was a little more diffuse and a little easier to stand in. 

In his palm the compass shivered.  The needle left the pool, swung past the open sea where the Linnet kept her rounds, and steadied on the jetty, where a small figure was untying a rowboat. 

Teodor Hale, who had not hurried in years, hurried.  "Pim," he called across the wet garden, the words out before he could prune them, "I am sorry I was sharp."  "The juniper will grow a new leader; come back and I will show you how." 

The boy looked up, the rope slack in his hands, and behind Teodor the pool held one last thread of blue.
