The carnival left Port Halden on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the wind had already begun sorting its confetti into the gutters.  Ines Okoro, eleven weeks into her internship with the county engineer, walked through the litter to the abandoned spaceport passenger terminal with a clipboard and a demolition checklist.  The carnival had rented the apron for two weeks, and for two weeks the town had remembered how to gather.  Now the rides were gone, and her supervisor wanted one number: what it would cost to knock the terminal down.  Ines was the kind of person who could not write a number without asking what it left out. 

Inside, the concourse was a maze of intentions.  Four generations of planners had each rebuilt it for a different future: a military gate, a tourist arcade, a quarantine ward, a cargo shortcut.  Their corridors crossed and contradicted one another, and arrows on the walls pointed toward departures that no longer existed.  Rainwater stood ankle deep in the baggage hall, black and patient.  She noted it on the checklist under structural liabilities, then stopped, because the water was moving.  A thread of confetti drifted across the surface, slow and deliberate, toward a wall where no drain was marked.  She followed it. 

Behind a collapsed ticket counter she found a founders' display case, cracked open by some carnival trespasser, and in the dust beneath it a curved bar of brass.  It was a piece of an explorer's sextant: the graduated arc and the index arm, its mirror still bright, its telescope long gone.  The placard said it had belonged to Captain Adaeze Varga, who surveyed this plateau on foot before anything flew from it.  Ines turned it over in her hands.  Her laser level had died that morning, and the office would not issue another to an intern.  So she tied her bootlace to the index arm, hung her house key from it as a plumb weight, and made herself an inclinometer.  It was not elegant, but it read slopes to half a degree, and that was enough. 

For three evenings she measured the floors of the maze.  What she found made her sit down on a dead baggage carousel and breathe carefully.  Every planner had left a gradient behind.  The military gate drained north, the arcade south, the quarantine ward inward to sealed sumps, and the cargo shortcut down a long ramp to the old coolant channels beneath the launch apron.  The intentions fought each other, so the water went nowhere and the building drowned by inches.  But the channels still fell eleven meters to a gravity turbine that had once run the terminal's emergency lights. 

She wrote the head, the flow, and the losses in her notebook, and under them, half joking, a line she did not show anyone: hope equals what remains times where you send it.  She had come to this town because nobody else applied, and she had stayed curious because the alternative was to become another arrow pointing at nothing.  The demolition number was easy; she wanted the harder equation, the one that priced what a place could still do. 

On Saturday she came back with sandbags the carnival had abandoned, a pry bar, and two neighbors who asked no questions.  They did not fight the maze.  They dammed the military threshold, opened a quarantine sump with the pry bar, and laid ride tarps down the arcade so that every old intention emptied into the cargo ramp.  Ines checked each joint with the sextant piece, shimming a sandbag here, lowering a lip of tarp there.  The water hesitated, then agreed.  It ran down the ramp in a single dark sheet, into the channels, and somewhere below them a bearing groaned like something asked a question after a long silence. 

Nothing happened for four minutes.  Ines stood in the draining hall and did not pray or plead; she simply watched the level fall and counted.  Then, along the concourse ceiling, the emergency strips came on one by one, amber and unhurried, until the whole maze was lit and every corridor could be seen for what it was.  Not a mistake, she thought, but a sum of people who had each wanted something to leave from here.  One neighbor laughed once, quietly, the way people had laughed at the carnival.  She had not resurrected a spaceport, only a few hundred watts, but the terminal was dry and bright and standing. 

On Monday she returned the brass to its case and handed her supervisor two numbers instead of one.  The first was the cost to knock it down.  The second was smaller, and beneath it she had drawn the flows, every arrow finally pointing somewhere.
