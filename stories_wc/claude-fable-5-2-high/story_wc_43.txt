The blood work came back at noon, and by one o'clock Odalys Brennan knew she had four days, maybe five, to get it right.  Saint Aldric's Hospice sat on a ridge so high that the weather happened below it.  Most mornings the clouds filled the valley like poured milk, and the building floated on top, all glass and gold light, a place the dying called the Lantern.  Odalys had been its music therapist for eleven years.  People described her as serene, and she let them, because serenity was a discipline she practiced the way others practiced scales.  Underneath it lived one stubborn ambition: to execute, for every patient, a flawless goodbye.  Her own father had died mid-argument, a television blaring, nobody holding his hand, and she had spent a career correcting that single afternoon. 

Walter Quist, room nine, was her current project.  He was a retired elevator repairman with a failing liver and a black, jagged piece of a broken vinyl record that he kept on his windowsill like a slice of night. 

"Numbers are bad," he said when she came in with her guitar.  It was not a question. 

"They are," Odalys said, and sat.  She never softened a true thing into a false one; kindness, as she understood it, was a decision, not a mood.  "Days, Walter." 

He nodded slowly.  Outside, the cloud floor glowed, and the room held the kind of quiet that has a heartbeat in it. 

She did what she always did then.  She did not play Bach or the old hymns; she hymned the mundane.  Over three soft chords she sang the linen cart squeaking down the hall, the kettle in the nurses' station, the good gray socks on Walter's feet, each ordinary thing lifted up as though it were holy, because by now it was. 

Walter's breathing eased.  "Sing the kitchen," he said. 

"Tell me the kitchen." 

"Sunday morning, Marguerite peeling oranges at the sink, that record going."  He looked at the shard.  "Our girl Tess dancing on my shoes.  That smell, orange peel and warm dust off the turntable, I used to think that was what hope smelled like." 

Odalys sang it, and then asked where the rest of the record was. 

The story came out in pieces of its own.  After Marguerite's funeral, he and Tess had fought over who failed her, and the record had snapped between their hands.  Tess walked out with the larger part.  That was nineteen years ago. 

"Do not call her," Walter said.  "I want to go quietly, without a scene." 

Odalys considered this for exactly as long as it deserved.  A goodbye with the main person missing was not flawless; it was merely tidy. 

"I will not go behind your back," she said.  "But I am asking you now, plainly, to let me call, because nobody guards a broken thing for nineteen years unless he hopes to mend it." 

Walter stared at the clouds for a long, pulsing minute.  "Call," he whispered. 

Tess Quist came up through the overcast two days later and stepped into the bright lobby white-knuckled, clutching a padded envelope.  She had kept her piece too. 

Odalys had planned everything: the lamp angled low, the chairs placed close, an orange from the kitchen in her pocket, a script of gentle openings.  None of it survived the doorway.  Tess said, "You look terrible," and Walter said, "You took the good half," and for one awful second the old fight stood up in the room. 

Odalys did not smooth it over.  She dug her thumbnail into the orange instead.  The scent opened like a window, bright and bitter and sweet, and she began, very quietly, to sing the kitchen: the sink, the Sunday, a small girl dancing on her father's shoes. 

Tess made a sound like something unlatching.  She crossed the room, shook her piece from the envelope, and Walter lifted his from the sill with trembling fingers.  On the blanket over his knees, father and daughter reunified the record, edge to jagged edge, a black circle with one thin scar.  It would never play again.  It did not need to, because Tess was already humming it. 

Walter died three mornings later with his daughter's hand in his, orange peel drying on the windowsill, the clouds below them lit like a floor of pearl.  Odalys played three soft chords and noted, serenely, that nothing had gone according to plan.  Flawless, she decided, had never meant unbroken.  It meant every piece in the room.
