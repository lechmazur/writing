Every autumn the clouds migrated south across the dunes, low and unhurried as grazing whales, and Maren Okoye followed their shadows down to the lagoon.  It lay beneath the dunes where an old lava tube had collapsed, a bowl of obsidian holding seawater so still that kelp drifted in it like slow handwriting.  Around its rim stood the sea-fig grove her father had burned twenty years ago. 

The village still called her the arsonist's daughter, even after eleven years of her carrying their children out of smoke.  She did not argue with the name.  She simply wore her firefighter's jacket to the lagoon, so that both halves of her arrived together. 

The council meant to fell the grove once the clouds had passed.  Dead wood, they said, charcoal with roots, a scar nobody needed to keep looking at.  Maren had a scar of her own, a pale river down her left forearm, and she had come to prove that scars can sing. 

Her method hung at her throat: a foreign coin, drilled through and strung on red thread.  Her father had carried it home from some far harbor and once swung it from his fingers to read the wind before he lit anything.  He had mailed it to her from prison with one line: I cannot make this clean, but you might. 

Now she let the coin swing against the first black trunk and listened.  Clack.  Dead through, dry as a spent match.  She moved on, gracefully alert, the way she moved through burning houses, weight low, every sense open, stepping where the obsidian shelved and never where it sheared.  Clack.  Clack.  At the ninth tree the coin struck and the trunk answered with a low, wet hum, the note living sapwood makes beneath char.  Maren rested her palm there until her breathing matched the lagoon's. 

Footsteps came soft down the dune path behind her, and she knew them without turning.  Her father had been released in the spring, and he stood at the grove's edge like a man waiting outside a church he had robbed. 

"They told me you were down here," he said.  "I can go." 

People had asked her for years when she would finally stop visiting him, stop writing, stop saving him a chair.  She had never found the place in herself where the stopping was supposed to begin.  Her compassion was not soft; it was the hardest thing she owned, harder than the glass under her boots, and it did not yield to the village or to her own anger. 

"Stay," she said.  "I need a second pair of hands." 

From her pack she drew green scions cut that morning from the one sea-fig the fire had missed, and a pouch of black sand from the volcanic beach where the blaze had finally starved.  As a girl she had thrown that sand on his flames by the fistful; it was the first tool she ever trusted. 

She pared the char until pale cambium showed, cut a cleft, and began to graft, seating the living wedge into the burned wood so green touched green.  "Hold it steady," she told him.  His hands shook, then didn't.  She kneaded the black sand into warm pitch and packed the wound, because the sand held the day's heat and would keep the join from chilling when the mist came. 

The mist was why it had to be now.  Only during the migration did the clouds sink low enough to breathe through the broken roof of the dunes, a week of gentle wet in which a graft could take. 

They worked tree to tree, the coin finding the humming ones, her knife opening them, his hands holding, until six trunks wore green.  At the last, she lifted the red thread over her head and placed the coin in his palm. 

"You read the wind with it once," she said.  "Tie it above the graft, so anyone who comes with an axe has to listen first." 

He knotted it with the care of a man setting a bone. 

The first cloud slid over the gap in the dunes and let down its fine gray breath, and the kelp turned once in the dark water, as if rolling over in sleep.  A small wind followed.  The coin swung on its red thread and touched the scarred trunk, and the trunk hummed, low and wet and unmistakably alive. 

Her father wept without noise.  Maren rolled her sleeve to the mist, let it cool the pale river on her arm, and stood beside him listening while the clouds went on migrating and the grove, note by note, began to sing.
