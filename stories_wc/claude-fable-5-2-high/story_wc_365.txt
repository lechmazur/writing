Odile Varr made maps for people who could not trust their eyes: charts of smell, hum, draft, and grit that guided blind pilots through stations and miners through smoke.  She had never mapped Bay Nine of the Calloway spaceport, because her mother had forbidden it.  Maren Varr had been chief mechanic there the night the freighter Thessaly launched under her green signal lamp and broke apart with Odile's father aboard.  The port died within a year.  Maren lived twenty more, and on her last evening she said, "There is something about the Thessaly I never told you," and then sleep took the rest. 

She left a toolbox, and in it a green glass shard, curved like a piece of lamp lens, its edges worn smooth by twenty years of her thumb.  So Odile carried the shard to the abandoned repair bay to do the only thing she knew: map a place until it confessed.  She wanted to discover what a withheld truth weighed, pebble or anchor, and whether her mother had been right to carry it alone. 

The bay smelled of cold flux and rust, and wind hummed in the empty launch cradle.  She knotted each sensation into her thread chart, then lay down on a workbench with the shard in her fist. 

It happened in the murmur of half-remembered dreams, in that shallow water where she could still hear her own breathing.  The flux smell turned warm, the wind became the whine of welders, and through the green glass she saw the bay lit and crowded around a ship she did not know.  Then it folded shut like a book. 

It took her nine nights to understand the seam.  It was cyclically spontaneous: it arrived every night as surely as tide, yet it opened wherever it pleased, onto whichever neighboring world it pleased.  She saw a Bay Nine flooded to the catwalks, one consecrated as a chapel, one where the Thessaly sat rusting, never flown. 

A mapmaker does not accept drift.  She knotted every world's sensations into her chart and found that the worlds lay in order, like notes on a string: coffee and fresh sealant toward the living ports, ash toward the dead.  And the shard steered.  Tilted toward a scent, it bent the seam after it, so she could redirect each crossing, weaving between worlds the way she wove thread between knots.  Night after night she traveled through parallel universes, following one faint trace: her mother's hand soap, lye and orange peel, still in use somewhere. 

On the twenty-third night the seam opened onto a bay as dead as her own, except for a lantern and an old woman mending a kettle at the bench.  A green shard hung on a cord at her throat. 

"You have my daughter's walk," Maren said, "but she would never come here." 

"My mother died last month," Odile said.  "She began to tell me something about the Thessaly." 

Maren's hands went still.  "Twenty years, and she never said it." 

"Say it now." 

"The coupling was cracked, and I showed your father."  "He said if the Thessaly missed her window the contract went, and the port with it, four hundred families."  "He ordered the green, and I gave it, and afterward I broke the lamp." 

Odile had braced for rage.  What came was quieter and far heavier: her father, choosing. 

"I told my Odile when she was nine," Maren said.  "I thought the truth was owed, and she has hated him ever since, and me for obeying."  "She maps mine shafts by touch on the outer rocks and does not write." 

"So that is what it weighs," Odile said. 

"Someone carries it either way," said Maren.  "Yours kept the whole of it so you could grieve him cleanly; I handed mine to a child." 

The seam was already tugging at Odile's edges, thinning the lantern light.  She had her answer, and it was not enough to simply take it home.  She unrolled her chart, twenty-three nights of knots, and at its center the bay itself: flux, coffee, cradle hum, orange-peel soap. 

"Send her this," she said, laying it on the bench.  "Her fingers will read it; it is a map of the father she loved before she knew, and of you."  "It cannot change what he chose, but it might redirect her." 

Odile woke on the cold workbench in her own silent bay, the chart gone, the shard warm in her fist.  The truth sat in her chest like an anchor, and she understood that her mother had hauled it for twenty years without once setting it down on her.  She took fresh thread and began a new map, and the first knot she tied smelled of orange peel.
