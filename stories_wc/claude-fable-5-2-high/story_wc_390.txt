The sun came down over Hollow Quay like a coin dropped into honey, and Marit Oyen knew she had perhaps twenty minutes of it.  Below the lighthouse, the drift-ice marina groaned, its moored skiffs shouldered by floes that the thaw had set walking.  One floe, broad as a chapel, was grinding toward the breakwater's root, where the crypt held the Absolving Bell.  When the tide peaked at dusk, the ice would crush that vault like an eggshell. 

She buttoned her azure coat, the Light Service blue that let an engineer be seen against white, and felt the crypt keeper's key swing warm against her breastbone.  Old Tobiah had pressed it into her palm the week he died.  "A keeper prays, but an engineer listens," he had said, "and the bell needs listening now." 

Marit sorted her feelings the way she sorted bolts, by size and by what each one was meant to hold.  Fear was a fact, and it said hurry.  Grief was a fact too, and it said Ansel, her brother, lost in fog two winters back while her foghorn lay in pieces on the workbench.  The rite of absolution needed her horn to answer the bell, and she had refused it since, so the bell hung bound in mourning felt and every unforgiven drowning sat in the harbor like ballast.  A feeling that large was not noise to be filtered out; it was a load, and loads needed a path to ground. 

She ran down the breakwater stairs with the gold light slick on every step.  The key was bronze, two-tined, cut from the bell's own casting, and it turned in the crypt door with a shiver she felt in her wrist.  Inside, the bell hung in its felt like a sleeping animal, and the cold smelled of salt and old candle fat.  It weighed two tons and was pinned into the rock; nobody was carrying it anywhere.  But ice has a voice of its own, and anything driven hard at its own voice will shake itself apart. 

Marit cut the felt away, then put her bare palm on the seaward wall, where the floe already leaned, and felt its groan climb her arm into her teeth.  She struck the key on the bell's lip and held its stem to her jaw.  The two notes, ice and bronze, throbbed against each other in a slow beat, nearly twins.  Of course: the old founders had cast the bell to answer the harbor ice.  If her horn could find that pitch, the bell would wake in sympathy and pour the note through the rock straight into the floe.  A bell is built to ring, she reasoned, and ice is not. 

She climbed to the horn gallery with her lungs burning and the sun already half swallowed.  The horn had one note, a nasal bray tuned to carry through fog, and it was wrong.  So she would adapt it, and this time she would finish before the dark.  She filed the brass reed thinner, sleeved the trumpet with a length of stovepipe, and bled air through the valve in short tests.  After each one she struck the key and clenched it in her teeth, and the beat between horn and bronze pulsed in her skull, fast, then slower, then slower still.  Her hands shook, and she let them, because shaking hands were information: this mattered, so measure twice.  One more stroke of the file, and the beat stretched out, thinned, and vanished into a single clean line. 

She opened the valve full. 

The note went out over the marina, and beneath her boots the bell answered, a sound less heard than entered, moving through stone and marrow alike.  It filled her chest like a second breath, and for a moment she could not tell her ribs from the lighthouse or her pulse from the sea.  Out on the water the great floe whitened with a thousand hairline cracks, sighed, and fell into brash that slid harmlessly around the breakwater.  Along the quay, doors opened, and people stood bareheaded in the last gold, hearing the rite they had gone two winters without. 

Marit understood then what Tobiah had meant.  The note did not argue with her guilt; it matched it exactly, tremor for tremor, and what is matched exactly can at last be set down.  She wept, noted that she was weeping, and found the observation sound.  Then the sun went under, the horn fell quiet, and the key cooled against her skin while the broken ice chimed softly among the hulls.
