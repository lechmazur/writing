By noon the first rush was over, and Oona Verrel stood alone at her stall among the glass trees.  The orchard library drifted on its swirling sea of clouds, and every branch hung heavy with books grown like fruit, their spines clear as ice.  Her sign, chalked that morning, read CLEAN SLATES, FAIRLY SOLD.  It was her opening day, and this was its lull. 

On the counter sat her bottled moonlight, corked by the Wardens on the night they released her.  They called it a glimmering parole: while the light held, she was free.  It would hold only if she took nothing that was not given and accounted for every absence she made. 

In the low cities she had made a great many.  She used to scrape whole pasts off people for a coin, hers first, and call the wreckage a fresh start.  One man went home unable to name his daughter, and the Wardens came for Oona before the week was out.  She had asked to serve her parole here because she had sold beginnings for years without knowing where one was kept.  Surely a library would know the place where stories begin. 

Her wares were not stacked.  She had hung the blank slates one to a tree, far apart down the rows, because speed and reach were how a daughter got erased.  Her memory she kept the same way, in pieces, each one set down on purpose where she would have to walk to it.  Mindfully scattered, her parole clerk had written, and meant it as praise. 

Now, in the quiet, she opened her ledger to do what the parole required.  She did not count what she had; she counted what was gone. 

Slates hung at dawn: twelve.  Slates remaining: one.  Absences made: eleven. 

Eleven was only a number until she walked the rows to check it.  At the first tree the hook was empty, and so, just above it, was a stem.  A book had been picked there within the hour; the glass still rang faintly when she touched it.  At the second tree, the same: bare hook, bare stem, a small bright ringing. 

She remembered the buyers in fragments, the way she remembered everything.  A widow's gray gloves.  A boy with ink on his chin.  A ferryman who paid in cloud salt and would not say what he wanted to begin.  Each had taken a slate, and each, it seemed, had then reached up. 

By the eleventh tree her tally matched the orchard's exactly: eleven slates gone, eleven stems bare.  She stood very still while the pieces slid together.  She had come up here hunting a room, a root, some first shelf where every story started.  But the count said otherwise.  Stories began at the gaps, wherever someone had been left room and had the nerve to reach into it.  The place was not on any map of the orchard because it was a measurement, not a spot. 

That left the twelfth tree.  It was a sapling at the orchard's edge, where the floor ended and the clouds turned slowly below like milk in tea.  Her last slate hung there, unsold, beneath a branch that had never borne a book. 

Oona took it down and understood that one absence was still missing from her ledger.  Her own first chapter, the one she had scraped away before she ever touched a customer, had never been entered.  She could not recover it, but she could measure it.  She knew its edges by what bordered them: a harbor smell, a song without its first verse, a name people had stopped calling.  The hole was about the size of a childhood. 

In the ledger she wrote: Absences made, twelve. 

Then she took the steel stylus she had only ever used for scraping, and for the first time she used the point.  She did not chalk the slate, since chalk could be wiped; she chose to incise it.  The line came slowly, gray dust curling off the stone: I do not know where I started, so I start here. 

She hung the slate back on the sapling, not for sale.  Above it, on the branch that had never borne anything, a bud of clear glass had begun to swell. 

When she returned to the stall, the bottled moonlight was glimmering harder than it had since her release, throwing silver across the open ledger.  Footsteps chimed down the rows; the lull was ending.  A girl stopped at the counter and asked what a clean slate was for. 

"Room," Oona said.  "Exactly as much as you are missing."  Then she lifted a fresh slate from the crate and walked the girl out to choose its tree.
