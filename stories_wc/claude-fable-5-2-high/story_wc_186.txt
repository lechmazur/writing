Forty winters after the cities went dark, Ilsabet kept a beacon tower that no longer burned.  Custodian was the word carved over the door, so custodian she called herself, though she had inherited nothing but thistles, wind, and a lantern room made entirely of glass.  The old builders had tuned every pane, and the pavilion hummed in any weather, a low chord like a finger circling a cup.  At its center, on a brass cradle, sat the crystal prism, broken, one long facet missing like a tooth. 

Ilsabet had a gift for which she had no good name: when she touched a thing, she saw what had happened to it.  Her brother Corrin, before the fever took him, had called it a blessing wasted on a world with nothing ahead of it.  He had also said that everything was accident, the collapse, their survival, even her wandering half-starved to the one ruin that needed its past read.  She had never won that argument, and she meant to win it now.  If a woman who saw backward had landed by chance at a tower whose light could be mended only by looking backward, then chance was a kind of handwriting, and she would prove it by rekindling the old beacon. 

So each dusk she laid her palm on the prism.  Each dusk the same night opened: the last keeper, stooped and gray, watching fires crawl across the plain while families hid in the cellars below.  The beacon was drawing raiders the way a lamp draws moths.  He did not smash the prism; he pried one facet free with a chisel, weeping, and the great light died. 

Then the vision always failed.  The instant the facet left the crystal, sound drained from her sight, the hum, the wind, his breath, and the picture dimmed to nothing, as if he carried silence itself in his fist.  She called the missing piece the shard of hush, because her gift could not follow it. 

But the glass remembered what the prism could not.  On the eastern panes, under decades of grime, she found wax-pencil lines: arcs, dates, and small crosses where a star had stood on a given night.  Touching them, she saw the keeper on later, quieter nights, sighting through the wounded prism along a blue star's path and marking where it crossed, leaving the last arc unfinished and one empty circle drawn beyond it.  He had been writing to someone who would know how to read. 

All summer Ilsabet tracked the star.  She scavenged a stub of candle wax, and each clear night she set her eye where his had been and extended his arc by a thumb's width, cross after cross, creeping toward the circle.  Cloud stole whole weeks.  On those nights she talked to Corrin and to the thistles, which had taken the hillside and were going to seed, loosing down that rose past the glass like slow sparks. 

The star entered the circle on the first night of frost.  Its light struck the broken prism and came out as a single pale thread that fell through the western panes onto the slope, and in the middle of that thread lay a notch of dark, the shape of the missing facet. 

Ilsabet ran down ninety steps with a trowel.  Where the notch rested, the thistles grew in a ring around a flat stone, and when she knelt there the pavilion's hum, which carried for a mile, simply stopped.  Under the stone was a tobacco tin, and in the tin, wrapped in oilcloth, the shard.  Her gift found only quiet in it, deep as a held breath, but the oilcloth remembered: the keeper kneeling here, saying to no one, not forever, only until someone comes who can wait for a star. 

She climbed back with her heart knocking and slid the facet into its wound.  The hum changed, the low chord gaining the one note it had lacked, and the prism drank the blue star and every star behind it and poured them out as a white beam across the dead plain.  Rekindled without a drop of oil, the beacon swept the dark, and thistledown blazed in it like a second sky. 

She waited, palm on the warm crystal, afraid of how much she wanted.  Far to the north, on a ridge she had always taken for bare rock, a small light woke, wavered, and steadied.  Someone else had been keeping a dark tower, watching the same star, waiting for a reason. 

Call it accident now, Corrin, she whispered.  The hush had never been an ending, only a breath held for forty years.  Ilsabet opened a pane and let the thistledown in, each seed a coincidence looking for ground.
