The envelope had waited inside a timetable for a bus that no longer ran. 
Vesna Halka found it while emptying her dead mother's coat: Route 14, Observatory Line, cancelled the winter the Ministry shuttered the dome, and wedged between its soft pages, a sealed envelope. 
Across the flap ran her mother's slanted script: Open above the weather, when masks slip. 

So she climbed. 
She followed the dead route on foot, stop by rusted stop, as a poet who has been censored learns to follow anything: by the shape of what is missing. 
Six years ago the Ministry had published her only book with black bars laid over every line that named a place. 
She had written it to understand the cartography of scars, how a burned valley might be charted by its wounds, and the bars had become the newest wounds on the chart. 
Since then she had been furiously tranquil, giving readings of the permitted half in a voice like still water over a clenched fist. 

The path broke through the cloud deck at dusk. 
Above it the sky was bare and enormous, and below it a storm was walking the valley, lighting the clouds from underneath so that the whole white floor pulsed like a lantern with a moth inside. 
The observatory stood on its spur of rock, slit dome flashing violet with each stroke. 
Vesna laughed aloud, surprised that she still could. 

Inside, beneath the great blind telescope, hung the vigil lamp that her mother and the other dismissed astronomers had climbed in secret to light for colleagues taken away, and beside it lay the logbook, open to her mother's last entry: Vigil paused, not ended. 
Four years of pause, dust on the wick, a held breath the size of a mountain. 

She opened the envelope. 
A glass negative slid out, wrapped in a note. 
Print it by storm light, the note said, ninety seconds in the tray, and rarefy the developer by half, because up here everything true works better thin. 
Then decide about the lamp. 

The darkroom was a closet off the stairwell, its shelves stocked with sealed tins, its red bulb dead, and on the bench sat her mother's darkroom timer, a fat steel clock with a single sweeping hand. 
She mixed the powder with meltwater from the cistern and rarefied it by half, as the censors had once rarefied her, except that this time the hand on the bottle was her own. 
She laid the negative on paper under the dome slit and let three strokes of lightning do the work of a lamp. 
Then, in the dark closet, she slid the sheet into the tray and wound the timer to ninety. 

It ticked. 
For ninety seconds she had nothing to do but stand inside the pause, and she understood that her mother had built it for her on purpose: a vigil within the vigil, a small measured waiting where no one was watching her be calm. 
Her tranquility, that careful mask, slipped; she wept with her teeth bared, six years of fury coming up as plainly as an image. 

The timer rang. 
She carried the print, dripping, to the slit and read it by lightning. 
It was the valley, photographed from this dome on a night when the storm had torn the clouds open and the land's mask had slipped: the quarry pit, the burned mill district, the straightened river, the camp road, every scar pale and exact. 
And along each scar, white as chalk where her mother had inked the glass, ran a line of poetry. 
Vesna knew them before she could read them. 
They were hers, the barred ones, every line the Ministry had struck, copied from the manuscript she had once mailed home and set down on the wound each one named. 

The censors had not erased her map; they had surveyed it for her, each black bar a pin driven into a place they could not bear to hear named. 
A scar, she saw at last, was not the wound but the record that the wound had happened here, and a country's scars, read together, were directions home. 
Her fury did not leave her. 
It settled, clear and cold as the air at this height, into something she could finally use. 

She cleaned the wick and lit the lamp. 
In the logbook, under her mother's line, she wrote: Vigil resumed. 
Then she wound the timer to ninety again and began a new poem against its ticking, rarefying every line until it was thin and bright enough to pass through cloud. 
Below, the storm tore the white floor open, and for one long stroke the valley looked up and saw a light.
