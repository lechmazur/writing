Three years after the Slackening, Tamsin Roe still threw punches.  Every fist on earth now went soft before it landed, hers included, so each swing at Bram Ostler's smug shoulder arrived as a pat. 

"Gentle today," the tally clerk said, not looking up from his ledger. 

"Choke on a barnacle," she said, and walked the crane boom back to the quay, sixty pounds of chain on one shoulder, feet finding the narrow steel as if it were a ballroom floor. 

The other dockhands called it grace.  Tamsin called it footwork, because Roes were pirates, and a pirate's balance was for boarding and cutting, never for anything so useless as beauty.  Now that nobody could cut anybody, she did not know what her body was for, and that made her meaner. 

Around her neck, knotted in oilcloth, hung the proof of her blood: a palm-wide slab of whalebone, scrimshawed by the pirate Hesper Roe herself.  It showed two figures squared off on a hump of sand, which the family had always read as a duel.  Family lore also said the bone unlocked guarded hearts, that Hesper pressed it into captives' hands and they wept out where the gold was.  Tamsin kept it wrapped because, held bare, it made her say what she meant, and she had not meant anything aloud in years. 

That evening the tide drew out farther than anyone living had seen, past the pilings, past the mussel beds, down to the sublittoral fringe where the kelp forest began and the sea usually kept its skirts down.  Then a whale called from the deep, one long note that came up through the dock planks into her shins.  The whalebone hummed against her breastbone like a struck fork.  Out in the fringe, among the laid-flat kelp, a back of pale sand shouldered up into the last light. 

"Hesper's Lie," Bram said beside her.  "My grandfather swore it surfaces once a lifetime and is gone within the hour." 

"Then your grandfather was a gossip." 

"And you are a coward, Roe, standing here clutching your neck." 

She swung at him; her knuckles opened on his jaw like a blessing; she swore, and at the whale's second call she bent and detached the tally skiff from its cleat. 

"Get in," she said.  "You can count my mistakes." 

They rowed out bickering over whose stroke was crooked, and grounded on sand that smelled of iodine and cold.  There was no chest.  There was only a ring of stones set by human hands around a slab of bedrock as level and bare as a floor. 

Tamsin unwrapped the whalebone to compare, and in the low red sun the carving finally told her the truth.  The figures' hands were not on hilts.  They were on each other's waists.  Hesper Roe, terror of three coasts, had rowed out here once in her life to dance where no crew could see her. 

The bare bone was warm in her fist, and the words came up like water through a sprung seam. 

"I never wanted to hit you," she said.  "I wanted a reason to put my hands on you, and hitting was the only one a Roe is allowed." 

Bram closed his ink-stained fingers over hers and the bone, and his ears went red. 

"Three years I have stood exactly where your fists would land," he said.  "You think that was an accident, you stubborn, beautiful ox?" 

"Ox," she said, outraged, and stepped onto the stone. 

She did not board it or brace on it.  She turned, arms loose, spine long, every balance she had ever hoarded for a fight that could no longer come spending itself at last on nothing but the turning.  It felt like setting down sixty pounds of chain.  She hauled Bram in by his collar, told him he danced like a dropped crate, and did not let go. 

The whale called a third time, lower, and the sea came back across the fringe, lifting the kelp upright, sliding cold over their ankles.  Tamsin understood it as she would a foreman's whistle: shift over, let go the line.  She detached the whalebone from its cord and wedged it among the ring stones for whichever guarded Roe the tide uncovered next. 

"That is your inheritance," Bram said. 

"My inheritance is the footwork," she said.  "The bone was only the key, and I am done being locked." 

By the time they shoved off, Hesper's Lie was a pale shape under green water, then only water.  Halfway home Bram's oar clipped hers, and she swung at him out of pure habit.  Her hand landed open against his cheek, the way every blow in the world landed now.  This time she left it there.
