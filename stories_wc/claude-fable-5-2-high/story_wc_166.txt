After the nostalgia ban, the wardens ground every keepsake in Halcyr to powder and called the city clean.  Sevrine watched them crush her mother's moonstone cabochon under a brass heel, and she did not weep.  She bent as if to fasten her sandal and took up one speck of the dust beneath her thumbnail.  Then she left.  No one banished her; exile was the last thing in Halcyr a person could still choose, and she chose it the way she chose her footing, without looking back. 

She had been the city's most skilled tide dancer until the wardens named her craft a relic.  A tide dancer does not watch waves.  She watches the gaps between them, the black intervals where sand holds for one breath, and she steps there, and there, and the sea goes hungry. 

Three miles of flats lay between Halcyr and the drowned terraces where she stopped.  It had been a garden once, and at twilight it still was: sea lavender, black hollyhock, a broken sundial, and the tide sliding in among the roots while the first stars came out overhead.  She told herself it was a resting place between crossings, nothing more. 

What she wanted was larger than a place: to pen a cosmic verse on mortal skin.  The wardens burned paper and sanded stone, but skin walked, and skin died, and a thing that dies cannot be accused of clinging to the past.  She would write the sky the way she danced the sea, by observing gaps: not the stars, which everyone remembered, but the dark between them, which no law had thought to forbid.  Her own back was beyond her reach, and a verse worn in exile is read by no one.  So she waited, charting the dark each dusk with a reed in wet sand, letting the tide erase it. 

The girl came in the second autumn, half drowned, a harbor orphan named Oona who had followed rumor and nearly paid the sea for it.  "Teach me to cross," she said, spitting brine.  "They are forgetting everything over there, and I want to carry something back that they cannot grind." 

Sevrine looked at the girl's narrow shoulders and saw a page.  She was honest about it, and Oona, shivering, laughed and agreed. 

Through the winter they worked at twilight under the stars.  Sevrine taught her to stand in the flooding garden with her eyes unfocused until the water showed its pauses.  "Do not chase the wave," she said.  "Step into what it leaves."  Afterward, by a shuttered lamp, she pricked the verse into Oona's back with a thorn and squid ink, line by line, each stroke a channel of darkness, the bare skin between left to stand for light.  Oona bore it without a sound and grew sure-footed, and Sevrine began inking more slowly. 

By the equinox the verse lacked only its ending.  A poem of gaps needs one fixed point, or the eye cannot tell absence from mere emptiness.  Sevrine had known for months what that point must be, and her hand kept going to the bead of wax at her throat.  Inside it lay the moonstone speck, the one thing she had carried out of Halcyr, the one piece of the past she had never let the tide take.  Kept, it was a grave she wore; set in living skin, it would be a star that walked. 

On the last twilight she cracked the wax.  The speck lay on the thorn's tip, no larger than a breath, holding a blue that was not quite light.  She pressed it under the skin below Oona's left shoulder blade, where the last line of darkness ended. 

"What does it say?" Oona asked. 

"That the dark is not empty," Sevrine said.  "It is where the footing is." 

The tide was turning.  Oona stood at the terrace edge, and Sevrine, who had led a thousand crossings, kept her own feet still, because no one can fledge behind another's wings.  The girl unfocused her eyes, found the first black interval, and stepped into it.  Then the next.  She went out across the flats toward the lamps of Halcyr, the sky's silence written on her back, one blue mote answering the stars, and she did not look behind her. 

Sevrine waited for the old pull, the drifter's itch to follow the ebb.  It did not come.  Water closed around her ankles among the black hollyhocks, and she was spontaneously anchored, without vow or decision, like a hull that finds bottom in the dark.  She had chosen her exile; the staying chose her.  Her throat was bare, and overhead the gaps between the stars went on speaking.
