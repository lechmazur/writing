The knife hung above the canvas, and Maren Oyelaran stood inside that hanging the way she stood between worlds.  All around her the Verdigris Market swayed on its cables, a hundred alchemists' stalls strung from chimney to bell tower high above the city's rooftops, their retorts glowing like caught stars.  Orrin Vale, who rendered old paintings into pigment, had paused only because she had said one word: wait. 

It was her trade to say words that crossed.  Maren translated between the waking city and the Tidewise, that other world laid under this one like a reflection under water, where the forgotten spoke in pulses of light.  Clients paid her to carry apologies and recipes across.  Nobody paid her for what she actually listened for, which was the names the city had never bothered to write down. 

Her hand went to the cord at her throat.  The lens fragment hung there, a curved tooth of glass from the harbor lighthouse, and as always it was elusively tangible: cold and edged one heartbeat, then nothing but a weight of brightness, like trying to hold the place where a beam had been. 

She was seven when her grandmother, the last keeper, pressed it into her palm.  The lighthouse was coming down that week so the market's great anchor cable could be bolted to its stump, and the riggers who would string the sky were already climbing. 

"A translator who crosses without anchors does not come back," her grandmother said.  "Not facts, child; facts dissolve over there.  Anchors of intuition: the things you know before you know why.  Look through this at whatever the world calls finished, and trust the tug." 

Between that palm and this throat lay every year she had.  At nine she held the glass to a harbor scene in a pawnshop and found a face no bigger than a seed painted into a window's reflection.  At fifteen she found letters braided into painted rigging, a name hidden in the rope.  The unsigned harbor painter had been burying people in plain sight, and each one Maren uncovered settled in her chest like a small iron hook, holding her steady whenever she crossed. 

Twenty-three riggers had fallen while stringing this market.  The guild ledgers recorded the cost of the cable.  She had found nineteen of them, canvas by canvas, and for a year the tug had been pulling her toward Orrin's stall. 

Now she raised the fragment, and the instant opened like a door. 

Through the glass, the painting on his scraping frame came apart into its secrets.  It showed the half-built market at dusk, and in the gull flecks, the chimney smoke, the fraying of a painted cable, four faces looked out, each with a name curled beside it fine as hair.  And on the gallery of a lighthouse that should already have been rubble stood a keeper with a brush, and beside her a child lifting a shard of light to one eye. 

Maren was seven and thirty-four at once.  Her grandmother had painted them all, and had painted her into the finding.  Every hook in her chest pulled the same direction, and she understood what the anchors were for: not to keep her safe, but to keep her here long enough to speak. 

She read the four names aloud, first in the city's tongue, then in the Tidewise way, tilting the fragment so each syllable left it as a pulse of light.  Then the nineteen she carried. 

The other world answered.  Down the whole length of the market, lantern after lantern flared and dimmed in a slow rhythm, twenty-three beats, the swing of a lighthouse beam relearned by a hundred small flames.  Hagglers fell silent.  Orrin lowered his knife and looked at his own hands. 

"What did they say?" he asked. 

"That they held the cable," Maren said.  "That your stall hangs from their hands." 

He stepped back from the frame.  She began to unfasten the canvas, clamp by brass clamp, and he, after a breath, loosened the last one for her. 

They hung the painting from the great anchor cable, where the market's first bolt bit the lighthouse stump.  Then Maren did what the tug had been asking since she was seven.  She unfastened the cord from her throat and tied the fragment to the frame, at the height of a child's eye.  Her neck felt bare, but the hooks in her chest held; they had never been made of glass. 

A spice-grinder's daughter wandered up, squinted through the lens, and whispered a rigger's name as though she had always known it.  The lanterns pulsed once more, and the instant, having held a childhood and a city, did not so much end as stay.
