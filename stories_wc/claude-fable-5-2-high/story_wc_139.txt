At two in the morning the prep room at Halloran's hummed like a kitchen before a feast.  The drains gurgled, the steel table shone, and Dario Kesten lay under my hands, stiff as a locked gate.  You cannot force rigor; forced fingers snap.  You warm the joint, knead, and listen, which is what I call waiting actively: hands busy, attention wide open, letting the body decide when.  I was working his clenched right fist when the loading door banged inward.  Only one man alive would breach a funeral home through the entrance reserved for the dead. 

"Nell," said my father, grinning, rain on his hat, a pale woman behind him.  "Meet Mirela Kesten, the widow."  She looked at her husband and did not look away, and I liked her for it. 

Anton Varga, my father, has sold bridges, miracle tonics, and one cathedral, twice.  I am the child who gave the customers their money back, when I could find them.  I chose this trade because its arithmetic is clean: a casket costs what it costs, and nobody on my table has ever been cheated.  All my life I have been hunting the mathematics of a fair trade, the equation where nobody leaves the room robbed. 

He set a vintage ink pot on my instrument tray, squat green glass with a brass cap, between the forceps and the trocar.  I knew it the way you know a family dog that bites. 

"Her political asylum hearing is at nine," he said.  "Dario was the witness, the one the ministry jailed, and Dario had the bad manners to die of his heart yesterday."  "Without him she is a woman with a sad story, and judges hear forty of those a week."  "So I write one letter on ministry paper, a threat with a stamp, and she is safe for three thousand dollars."  "All I need from my honest daughter is her notary seal on the translation." 

"No," I said, and kept kneading the dead man's fist, because refusing my father is a long job and my hands needed work.  "A lie buys her a year until someone checks the stamp, and then it costs her everything; that is not a trade, that is a loan from a shark." 

He uncapped the pot, and the smell of iron and oak gall rose over the formalin.  "Good ink liquefies rigid thinking," he said, his oldest proverb.  "A clerk sees a confident hand and his rules go soft."  "It worked on the clerk who read your papers, Nell, the winter I carried you across in a bread van." 

The room did not stop humming, but I did.  My honest name, my license, my clean arithmetic: all of it poured from that green pot.  I had kept my ledger balanced by leaving the first entry off the page.  Something stiff in me began to give, the way a joint gives, and I let it, and I kept my thumbs moving along Dario's knuckles.  I did not answer him; I waited the way I work, asking the question with my hands. 

Then the fist opened. 

Mirela made a small sound.  Three fingers had been broken and had healed crooked without a splint, two nails had never grown back, and across the palm ran a row of round burn scars, evenly spaced, like a signature. 

"There is your ministry letter," I said.  "They wrote it themselves, and nobody can call it forged." 

So I committed my own breach of protocol: I capped the embalming tank, because my fluid would bleach what those scars had to say, and I telephoned the county medical examiner's night line to request a forensic examination before dawn.  Then I dipped a pen in my father's ink and wrote what I had seen, measured, and photographed, in plain words, and signed with the name that same ink had once invented. 

"Here is the mathematics," I told him.  "One false page bought my life; one true page may buy hers, Mirela owes the price of a funeral, and you, Papa, drive her to court for nothing." 

My father looked at the open hand, then at me, and for once he did not haggle.  "Keep the pot," he said.  "It writes better for you." 

Mirela laid her living hand over the scarred one and said, in careful English, that she had believed the dead could not testify. 

"In this room," I said, "they do little else." 

The examiner's van arrived at four, headlights sweeping the loading door my father had breached.  The drains sang, the ink dried black on the page, and I stood among the dead, wide awake, feeling the sum come out even at last, or near enough to live on.
