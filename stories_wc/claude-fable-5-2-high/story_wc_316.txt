The dojo sat on a rock that had torn loose from the valley the day history began rewriting itself.  Below it, the Revision rolled through the towns like weather, and Ilse Marrow watched a bridge she had crossed that morning become a bridge nobody had ever built.  She felt nothing about it.  She had felt nothing about anything for three years, which was why she had climbed the chain ladder. 

On the mat between her and her brother Tavin stood a jar, and in the jar floated a brain, and on the jar hung a brass tag stamped DUPLICATE.  The Quiet Clinic had grown it from her cells and imprinted it the night before her treatment, as insurance.  The treatment was meant to dissolve her grief over their mother; it dissolved the griever instead.  Ilse had survived her own ego death and come back with a body, a name, and no weather inside.  The clone in the jar still held everything she had been: the grief, the temper, the love.  Now the clinic was bankrupt, the clone needed a guardian, and both siblings had filed for custody. 

"Paper is useless to me," said Master Oru, kneeling at the head of the mat.  "At dawn the registry named Ilse the original, at noon it named the jar, and by dusk it may say neither of you was ever born."  "That is why courts send such cases up here, where the ground cannot be edited."  "We settle things the old way, through complementary opposites: one presses, one yields, and whatever is false falls out between them." 

Tavin and Ilse stood, crossed wrists, and began pushing hands.  He pressed hard, the way he argued. 

"She is my sister," he said, nodding at the jar.  "She remembers our mother's kitchen, and you look at me like a stranger reading a menu." 

Ilse yielded, let his weight pass, and answered without heat, because she had none.  Losing her self had cost her every comfortable lie, and what remained of her voice sliced through pretense like wire through clay. 

"You signed the consent form, Tavin."  "You do not want to protect her; you want her awake long enough to forgive you." 

His arms went slack, and he stumbled past her to the edge of the mat. 

"Now reverse," said Oru. 

Ilse pressed, and Tavin, eyes red, yielded and found the gap in her. 

"You talk about her rights," he said, "but you do not care about rights."  "You do not care about anything, and you are sick of it, and you think she is the cure." 

"Yes," Ilse said, because it was true.  "I came to take back what I used to feel." 

Oru set down his tea.  "Then you each want to use her, and neither of you has asked what she is."  "She is a past with no present, and you, Ilse, are a present with no past."  "Alone, each of you is half asleep; only together can you vivify one another." 

He drew two copper leads from the jar's base, the clinic's dormant waking circuit, which needed a living pulse to close it.  Ilse took one in each hand. 

At first there was only a hum, and then the kitchen arrived: cardamom, steam, her mother's floury hands.  Grief came behind it like a door blown open, and fury at Tavin, and under the fury a love so stubborn it hurt her teeth.  In the jar, pale light rippled across the folds as her steady, empty calm flowed the other way, giving the frightened thing a now to stand in.  Ilse was crying, clumsily, like someone relearning a language. 

Beneath the rock the Revision swept the valley again, and somewhere a registry changed its mind about which of them was real.  It no longer mattered, because no one could rewrite what she was feeling while she felt it. 

"I withdraw my petition," she said, when she could speak.  "She is not a cure or a confession booth, and she is not mine to own."  "Let her stay on the rock, where nothing can edit her, and let me climb up each day to lend her my hands." 

She held one copper lead out to her brother.  "She is furious with you, Tavin, and she would like to say so herself." 

Tavin took the lead, took her free hand to close the circuit, flinched, and then began, helplessly, to apologize. 

Oru ruled joint guardianship, and wrote it on nothing.  Later Ilse unhooked the brass tag, since the valley could not decide which of them it described, and dropped it off the edge into the changing world.
