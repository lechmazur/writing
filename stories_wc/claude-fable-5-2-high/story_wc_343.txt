The Hesper was the last antique airship still licensed for survey work, and her gasbag creaked like an old chair above the marshes.  Every autumn dusk the starlings rose in murmurations so thick that the captain cut the propellers and let the ship drift, idle, inside the living weather.  Tamsin, the map apprentice, loved those hours and dreaded them.  The crew of nine came from nine ports, and no two shared a mother tongue.  They passed her bread, patted her shoulder, and laughed at jokes she could not follow, so that she was never alone and always by herself. 

Old Master Ilvane had spoken to each of them in their own words.  When the cartographer died in spring, he left Tamsin his unfinished charts and his polyglot's phrasebook, fat as a brick, with exactly one page dog-eared.  She had studied that dog-ear for months.  The folded corner pointed at a single question, copied in Ilvane's hand into every language the crew spoke: "Where does the light wait?"  It was an odd thing to ask directions to, and Tamsin, who wanted more than anything to chart the next great discovery, could not leave an odd thing alone. 

That evening, while the birds poured past the portholes like smoke with opinions, she went down to the hold to fetch chart paper.  Her lantern caught the hopscotch board Ilvane had painted on the planks years ago, for stretching his legs on long flights, everyone said.  Dust lay over it now like felt.  Tamsin knelt, because kneeling to look closer was simply what she did, and began to note what she saw in her pocket ledger. 

Each of the ten squares held a number and, beneath it, one small painted word.  The numbers ran out of order, and the dust did not lie evenly: the squares numbered one through six, scattered across the board, were worn pale where a boot had landed again and again.  She copied the six words in that order.  Three she knew at once, because they sat on the dog-eared page: where, light, waits, each in a different crewmate's tongue.  The other three she could not find, for a phrasebook is not a dictionary, and she did not even know whose languages they were.  There was nothing for it but to ask. 

She climbed to the galley, where the crew sat shoulder to shoulder waiting out the birds, and held up her ledger like a shy lantern.  Okoro the rigger squinted, grinned, and flapped his elbows: birds.  Pilar the cook tapped another word, then walked two fingers across the table after her other hand: follow.  The captain, who had not spoken to Tamsin in a week, pointed through the porthole at the bruised sky going dark and said his word for it, twice, slowly, until she said it back.  Dusk. 

Follow, birds, dusk, where, light, waits.  Tamsin understood then that Ilvane had built a riddle no one could solve alone, and she ran for the deck with half the galley clattering after her. 

The sun was gone.  The murmuration wheeled around the Hesper in vast dark scarves, folding, tearing, mending.  She watched the birds the way she had watched the dust, for the place they left untouched.  To the northeast, low over the reed beds, the flock kept parting around a hollow the size of a barn, and inside the hollow hung a glow.  It was not lamp or moon or marsh fire.  It was a scrap of late afternoon, amber and warm, that the sunset had failed to gather up: stranded luminance, left behind like a tide pool when the day went out. 

The captain engaged one propeller at a whisper and eased them close.  Amber light climbed the antique hull, gilding rivets, ropes, and every astonished face.  Someone said a word, soft as breath; someone else answered with a different one, and Tamsin realized they were each naming the light. 

She unrolled Ilvane's unfinished chart on the rail, took a bearing off the marsh beacon, and inked a small sun where the paper had been blank.  Then she passed the pen along the rail.  One by one they wrote their word for it beside her mark, nine names in nine scripts, and she noted her own beneath them, last and smallest. 

She still could not follow their jokes as they drifted home through the settling birds.  But she folded a new dog-ear into the phrasebook, at the page for "Thank you," and began, diligently, to learn it nine ways.
