Odile filled the kettle to the second rivet, exactly two cups, and set it on the blue ring of flame.  Six minutes to the whistle; she had timed it the week after the funeral, because someone had to.  Six minutes was what she allowed Anselm each evening, no more, and she spent them like wages.  Above her, the skylight of the loft was beaded with rain, each drop holding a rust-orange spark of the streetlamp outside.  Below her lay the old winter quarters, where the cat barn still smelled of straw and iron though the cages had been sold in March.  She had sold them herself, itemized, and filed the receipts under L for lions.  People in town called her practical in the lowered voice they used for ghosts.  They assumed a lion had taken him.  It had been a ladder, a wet rung, and this same skylight, which he had climbed up to caulk and which she had finished caulking nine days later.  It no longer leaked. 

Tonight's six minutes belonged to the last thing on her list: the ash wood carving blank from his workbench.  Anselm had whittled a small lion for every cat he ever worked, eleven in all, and they sat along the beam in order of temper.  The blank was meant to be the twelfth, a pale block the length of her forearm, squared, sanded, and untouched.  For months she had planned to carve his face into it.  That was the sensible thing: contain him in something that would not fall, and set him on the beam with the others. 

She laid out his knife, his gouge, and her ledger, and turned the blank to read the end grain first, the way he had taught her to read a cat before entering.  The kettle ticked as the metal warmed.  The rings were there, dark and fine, each one a year the tree had lived and then made solid.  Time, crystallized; you could put a thumbnail on a summer.  She counted across the end of the block and reached thirty-one, which was also the number of years she had been married.  She did not believe in signs, but she believed in arithmetic. 

So she used the rings as she used the kettle, as a measure, and walked the knife tip across them.  Here was a wide ring, a fat year, the season Sabine dropped twin cubs and the tent sold out through October.  Here was a ring as thin as thread, the winter of the barn fire, when Anselm slept in the straw beside the cats for a month so they would eat.  Thin or fat, every ring had closed.  None of them held the year; they only held where it had been. 

The kettle began its low mutter, and she heard him over it, plain as a receipt.  You never contain a lion, Odile.  You contain yourself, and the lion decides to stay for seven minutes, and then the ring is empty and they sweep it.  Here and gone; that had been the whole act, and he had loved it for exactly that. 

She looked at the eleven lions on the beam, each one carved only after its cat had left the show, not one of them a cage.  A face on that beam would be a thing to dust.  She opened the ledger, in which every evening's boil had to yield one lesson, and wrote the day's harvest: A measure holds only what it is about to give away. 

Then she turned the blank on its side, set the gouge a third of the way down, and pushed.  A pale curl lifted and fell, smelling green even now.  Not a face, then, and not a lion, but a scoop, a long-handled tea scoop with a bowl sized for one spoonful.  She would hollow it across the rings, so that every evening thirty-one years would lift the leaves, tip them into the pot, and come up empty and ready.  It was a useful object, and she knew what the town would say about a widow who measured her tea with her marriage.  She made a second cut, and a third, and the bowl began to show. 

The kettle whistled.  She set the gouge down mid-stroke, because six minutes was six minutes, and the rest could wait for tomorrow's water.  She poured, and the tea came up the color of old cage bars, of manes, of the lamp in the rain overhead.  The steam rose toward the skylight, was there, and was not.  Odile drank while it was hot.
