The harbor called them the noctilucent reckonings, and for thirty years they had been Ines Marrow's to keep.  Each summer, when night-shining clouds hung silver-blue over Pearl Bay, the glazier walked the Prism Atrium and tallied its panes by that cold light, because it showed flaws the sun forgave.  This year the tally mattered more than it ever had.  At noon the council would vote on whether to strip the old weigh hall's roof, four hundred twelve hand-leaded panes, and replace it with one seamless engineered sheet.  Her ledger would be read aloud first. 

She finished counting as the clouds faded and dawn mist came in off the oyster beds, filling the atrium until its angled glass seemed to float.  Nine panes cracked.  Nine, out of four hundred twelve, after a century of salt.  The surveyor's report, folded in her apron, said otherwise in confident print: structural drift, glazing fatigue, removal advised. 

Ines had spent her life reading between lines, the lead ones first.  A window, her teacher Tobiah used to say, is mostly what happens between the cames, and a document is no different.  Between the report's lines she found what it never measured: the ground.  It blamed the glass for a drift it had not bothered to trace. 

So she went to the pond.  It lay at the atrium's center, a round stone basin where the tallymen once rinsed oyster grit from their hands, as plain as a washtub.  Tobiah had taught her its other use on her first reckoning.  Throw a stone dead center, he said, and watch the rings go out and come home; a true basin in a true floor returns them all at once, and a tilted one tells you which way the world is leaning.  He had called it communing, half joking.  She had come to think the joke was the smaller half. 

Ines chose a pebble from the sill, waited for her own breathing to settle, and threw it softly at the center.  The rings opened, thin and exact, each one carrying a faint band of color where the first sun came through the prisms and the mist.  They touched the north rim, the south, the west, and folded back together.  From the east they came home late and crooked, crossing the others in a small, stubborn chop. 

East was the new container pier, where pile drivers had been hammering the seabed since spring.  The glass was not failing; the shore beneath it was being pushed, and the old lead, soft and patient, had been bending to take the strain.  A seamless sheet would not bend.  It would hold perfectly until the day it did not. 

She was writing this down when Councilor Pell came in early, collar up against the damp, already apologizing.  "It's only old windows, Ines," he said.  "Nobody will blame you for signing." 

She understood then what she actually wanted, and it was not to win the vote.  She wanted one person to see an ordinary thing clearly before it was gone: plain panes, soft lead, a washbasin, each doing quiet work that nobody had ever thought to count. 

She held out a pebble.  "Throw it in the middle," she said, "and tell me what you see." 

Pell looked at the stone, at her, at the door.  Then, because the hall was empty and the hour was strange, he threw it.  The rings went out wearing their thin colors.  Three sides answered together.  The east answered late.  He frowned, borrowed a second pebble without asking, and threw again, and the pond told him the same thing in the same even hand. 

"That's the pier side," he said slowly. 

"That is everything the report left out," Ines said.  "The roof has been bending so the rest of us would not have to notice." 

Outside, the tide stood slack, the minute before it turns, and the first cranes were waking on the pier.  By noon something would be decided, and by winter the bay would be a different bay whichever way the hands went up.  Ines knew that.  She opened the ledger anyway and, beneath nine cracked, wrote the rest of the reckoning: four hundred three sound, lead yielding as made, basin true on three sides, ground moving east. 

Pell did not leave.  He stood at the rim with the mist thinning around him, turning a third pebble over in his fingers, watching the water go flat enough to ask again.
