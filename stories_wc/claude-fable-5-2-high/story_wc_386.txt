The shrine was carved into the blue belly of the glacier, and its forty-one candles made the ice glow like starlight on loan.  Ilse Varga knelt at the altar shelf and set down what she had carried up from the wreck of the ferry Solveig: a melted snow globe.  Its dome had slumped like a tired eyelid, and the tiny village inside leaned beneath water gone gray.  Cold seas do not melt plastic.  The inquiry had blamed a rogue wave, yet here was fire, small enough to hold in one hand. 

Ilse dove wrecks for a living, and the dead spoke to her, which was less useful than people imagined.  Down in the Solveig's flooded corridors, the drowned gave her only scraps: a hymn, a held breath, a child's voice counting.  Ghosts, she had learned, cannot hold a whole thought any more than a candle can hold the sun.  They needed windows, brief openings where a living mind stood unguarded, and the widest windows were dreams.  So the dead slipped through as extras, walking on in the background of strangers' sleep. 

The shrine kept a ledger where pilgrims wrote their dreams before lighting a candle, an old custom of the fjord.  All winter Ilse had read it the way she read a debris field, charting what did not belong.  A trucker dreamed of his wedding, and a girl in a yellow raincoat stood behind the cake, shaking a snow globe.  A botanist from another continent dreamed of an exam hall, and the same girl sat in the last row beside a door wrapped in chain.  The baker's dream showed seven links on that chain and a padlock stamped with a small anchor.  Thirty-one dreamers, none of whom had known her, and in every cameo she stood a little closer to that door. 

Ilse pinned the entries to a chart, cross-checked each detail against the ferry's plans, and found the place: the stairwell from the cheap lower deck.  Yesterday she had dived to it and cut free seven links and an anchor-stamped padlock, the mark of Brandt Line.  Halvard Brandt had chained that door so lower-deck passengers could not drift up to the lounge they had not paid for.  Then fire came from the car deck, and forty-one people reached that door and no farther. 

Brandt had also paid for this shrine, and tonight he came to light the memorial candle, as he did before cameras every year.  Beside him walked Sunniva, who swept the ice floor daily and whose daughter Pia had owned a yellow raincoat. 

Ilse had rehearsed this moment like a decompression schedule, because truth brought up too fast can kill.  She could expose Brandt in one sentence, but that sentence would hand a mother her child's last minutes at a locked door.  She would not lie; she had never lied for the dead.  But she had not spent a winter in that wreckage to haul up only blame.  She wanted to salvage the one pure thing down there, and the order of the telling was her only tool. 

"Before you light it," she said, and laid the chain on the altar, link by link, then the padlock, anchor upward.  Brandt's face went the color of the walls. 

"This closed the lower stairwell of the Solveig," Ilse told the cameras.  "This globe was softened by fire the inquiry never mentioned, and thirty-one strangers who never met have dreamed the same door, down to the seventh link."  She opened the ledger to the marked pages and let the reporters read.  Brandt began a sentence about storms, and no one waited for its end. 

Then Ilse turned to Sunniva, who was staring at the ruined dome as though it might breathe.  "She was at that door," Ilse said quietly, giving the hard thread first so the next would hold.  "But in every dream she is shaking this, and beside her is a smaller boy, and he is not crying."  "Down there I still hear her counting, and it is snowflakes she counts, for him."  "She made it snow for him until the end; that is true, and it is the part worth keeping." 

Sunniva wept, but she did not break.  She lifted the globe, carried it to an empty niche in the ice, and set the memorial candle behind it herself.  The warped dome caught the flame and threw it wide across the glacier wall, a crooked little window full of light that was not its own.  Ilse thought of stars, long gone, still lending their shine to whoever looked up.  That night, the ledger says, a pilgrim dreamed of snowfall, and two children in the background, waving.
