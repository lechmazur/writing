The misters hissed off at midnight, and the mist they left hung in the workshop like breath held.  Through the old greenhouse roof, the stars looked rinsed and close.  Wren Halloran sat at her bench among half-built enclosures, a veiled chameleon resting on her wrist, and did not move. 

Three days earlier, the wildlife officer had stood in this doorway and explained the con.  Desmond Pike, who had called himself a sanctuary courier, was no such thing.  For two years Wren had rehabilitated seized reptiles and handed them to him in crates she built herself, believing they were going home to forests.  They had gone to collectors.  He had fled ahead of the warrant, leaving behind only one animal he could not sell. 

The chameleon had arrived dull as ash, wrapped in old skin it had been too dry and too frightened to shed.  A ring of that dead skin had tightened around the end of its tail until the tip turned black.  Wren had done what she always did, which was very little, very patiently.  Warm mist, a soft brush, her wrist offered as a branch.  The animal healed by shedding layers, one papery scrap at a time, and under each scrap it grew quietly vivid, green deepening to gold along the ribs. 

Tonight the last ring had loosened, and the chameleon's tail tip had come away in her palm, dry and curled like a comma.  It would not grow back; chameleons were not lizards of that kind.  She set it in a glass dish on the bench and looked at the returned crates stacked by the door. 

The officer had asked her to reconstruct the routes, every animal, every buyer, and had admitted that without records there was little hope.  But Pike had been lazy in the way of confident men.  He had always sent her crates back for the next batch, each new shipping label pasted over the last.  So she had spent three nights with a steamer and a palette knife, treating paper the way she treated skin.  Each label lifted to show an older one beneath, an address, a date, a name.  Layer by layer the truth came off the wood, and she copied it into a ledger in her small, even hand. 

It was nearly finished when the door latch clicked. 

Pike stood in the mist with his hat in his hands, thinner than she remembered, starlight on his wet shoulders.  The chameleon's eyes swiveled toward him, one and then the other. 

"I came for the crates," he said, and then he saw the ledger. 

Wren's heart went fast, but her wrist stayed steady, because the animal on it needed a branch.  "Sit down, Desmond, you're soaked," she said. 

He sat.  He talked for a long time, softly, the way he always had: debts, a brother in hospital, buyers who truly loved the animals.  "You're kind, Wren," he finished, "and kind people don't send a man to prison over lizards." 

There it was, the place he had always pressed.  Her convictions had never been armor; they were fragile things, thin as shed skin, and for three days she had wondered whether gentleness was only another word for easily fooled. 

She looked at the dish, at the small black comma in it.  Then she looked at the animal that had lost it, bright as wet leaves, gripping her with what remained. 

"You left this one with its tail dying," she said.  "That piece is gone for good, and I can't pretend it back on.  I can't pretend the others home, either." 

She filled the kettle, because he was shivering, and put a blanket over his shoulders, because she was still herself.  Then she picked up the telephone. 

"I'm calling the officer now," she said, "and the ledger goes with her.  You can run, and I won't chase you, or you can drink your tea and tell her the names I couldn't read.  Some of those animals can still be found." 

Pike stared at her as if she had changed color.  She had not; she had only let the dull layer go.  Under the doubt, the old conviction was still there, smaller by a tip, and holding. 

The kettle sang, and she poured.  Pike looked at the door, then at the tea she set before him, and slowly wrapped both hands around the cup. 

Wren dialed, and while the line rang she set the glass dish on the ledger like a paperweight, the tail tip a dark comma over the names.  Overhead the mist thinned, the stars held their places, and on her wrist the chameleon, green and gold, did not let go.
