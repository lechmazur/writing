The first storm let go of the valley all at once, and the hush that followed was so wet and heavy that Ilse could hear the tea steeping.  The ceremony platform, a slab of floating rock moored to nothing but old writing, drifted in the eye of the weather with rain still beading on its low table.  Tea-master Oda poured without a sound and nodded toward the new crack, a pale wound running from the kettle stand to the edge. 

"A breath between storms is all we get," Oda murmured.  "Write it before the second wall arrives, or the rock will forget how to float." 

Ilse knew the law of the place, though she was only a budding rune scholar with one season of ink behind her.  Every storm scarred the platform, and every scar had to be written into a story, rune by rune along the crack, because stone that is merely broken falls, while stone that remembers why it broke holds. 

The trouble was the ink.  Stormglass ink, thinned with the first cup of the ceremony, stayed liquid only for a calm hand.  It crystallized when stressed, and a single tremor in the writer could turn it to splinters before it ever touched stone.  Ilse wore the proof under the glove on her bracing hand: last season her fear had frozen a rune mid-stroke, the steel pen had burst across her palm, and the unwritten corner of the platform had dropped into the valley.  She had not told that story to anyone, least of all to the rock. 

Today she had left the steel pens in their case.  From her sleeve she drew a crow quill, its trimmed tip no wider than a grass seed, cut from a feather she had kept since she was nine.  Oda raised an eyebrow at the unscholarly thing but said nothing, watching the tea instead; when its surface lay perfectly flat, the rock was still enough to write. 

The surface went flat. 

Ilse dipped the quill, drew in the soaked, tea-scented air, and held it. 

Inside the held breath, the world narrowed to the crack and the dark bead trembling at the quill's tip.  Already she felt the old fear climbing her wrist, and already the bead was clouding, tiny needles of glass forming at its heart. 

So she did what no treatise advised and went back to being nine.  The village children had kept rules for weather: never pluck a crow, for only a feather freely shed carries luck; and when a crow crosses overhead, hold your breath until it passes, and the storm will not see you.  She had found this feather on the morning after her first great storm, held her breath over it, and believed with her whole small body that she was invisible to harm.  Scholarship had taught her to be ashamed of that belief.  Now she let it back in, not as truth but as posture, the remembered looseness of a child who has not yet learned what her hands can ruin. 

The needles in the bead dissolved. 

Still holding, she pinned the glove's fingertips beneath her knee and shed it, laying her bare, glass-scarred palm on the wet stone beside the crack.  Scar against scar, the rock felt almost warm. 

The quill touched down, and she wrote the story the crack had been waiting for, beginning not with today's storm but with her own: a scholar was afraid here, and a corner fell, and she came back.  The trimmed tip took the turns of each rune the way a crow takes wind, and the ink ran black and willing down the pale wound toward the edge. 

Her lungs began to burn.  At the rim of the eye the second wall of cloud leaned in, and the tea in Oda's cup shivered.  Ilse did not hurry; a child holding her breath for a crow does not hurry, she only waits for the wings to pass.  She set the last rune, the one that means "and it held," at the very lip of the stone, and lifted the quill. 

Then the wind struck, the platform groaned, and the strain went into the wet line exactly as it was meant to.  Stressed at last by the storm instead of by her, the ink crystallized along the whole crack, a seam of dark glass knitting the rock together, every rune locked inside it like a word in amber.  The platform steadied and rode the gale. 

Ilse let her breath go. 

Oda, rain streaming from her sleeves, slid the second cup across the table and looked from the bare hand to the seam.  "Now it is a story," she said, and inside the roar the hush between them stayed full.
