On the last night before the Vessari collection was split in two, the arcane linguist Maren Okafor broke the first rule of the extinct language archive and filled an artifact with ink.  The glass ink well was nine centuries old, the size of a plum, and blown with two mouths instead of one.  She had tested the glass for cracks all week, mixed the ink from a recipe that would wash out, and brought a facsimile instead of the real vellum; she was, as Tobin used to say, carefully reckless. 

Tobin had not said anything to her in three years.  That was fair, because when the board accused him of inventing a Vessari verb, she had known he was right and had kept quiet to protect her own chair.  Since then they had shared one reading room and no sentences.  Tomorrow the crates would go, half to his new post on the coast and half to remain with her, and the silence would become permanent by simple geography. 

So she carried the ink well down the aisle of dead grammars to his desk and set it between them like a dare.  "I think I can unsnarl the Knot," she said, "but not alone." 

He looked at the ink, then at her, and she watched curiosity fight with injury across his face.  The Knot was the archive's famous failure: a single sheet where lines of Vessari script crossed and recrossed until every scholar had declared it a scribe's ruined draft. 

"Two mouths," Maren said, touching the glass.  "Two pens dipped at once, so it was never one scribe; it is a score, Tobin, with two voices written through each other." 

He leaned in despite himself.  "The chroniclers mention symphonies of truth," he said slowly, his first words to her since the hearing.  "We assumed it was a metaphor." 

"I assumed a lot of convenient things," she said, and let that stand without decoration. 

In Vessari, a promise could not be a whole sentence by itself; its verb stayed open, like a hook, until another speaker's promise closed it.  The Vessari settled feuds that way, via interconnected promises sung together, each vow true only while the others held.  The open verb was the very form Tobin had been accused of inventing. 

"You were right," she said.  "I knew it then, and I have written to the board to say so, but a letter is one voice, and the Vessari knew what one voice is worth." 

Tobin was quiet, and the shelves of extinct tongues seemed to wait with him.  Then he took a pen from his drawer, which was not forgiveness, but was a beginning. 

They dipped together, nibs clicking against the two glass lips, and began tracing the facsimile, she following the strand that ran left, he the strand that ran right.  Where the lines crossed, she read her fragment aloud and stalled on its open verb. 

"I will guard your name," she translated, "for as long as..." 

"...you carry my word," Tobin finished from his own strand, startled, "and I will carry your word for as long as..." 

The sentence closed, and the next one opened, and under their pens the tangle came apart into two clean melodies that leaned on each other like rafters.  Maren laughed, too loudly for an archive, and caught herself, and then laughed again more quietly because Tobin was grinning at the page. 

They read until the windows grayed, trading clauses, their two voices building the old chorale line by line in a room that had held only silence.  It was a treaty between two feuding houses, and it never once said sorry; it only said what each side would do, and made every doing depend on the other's. 

When the last hook closed, the ink well was nearly dry and the movers were knocking at the loading door.  Tobin capped his pen and did not stand. 

"Half the Knot's companion sheets are crated for the coast," he said, "and I cannot read them with one voice." 

Maren's heart leapt, and she made it walk.  "Then I will send you my strand every week," she said in careful Vessari, leaving the verb open. 

"For as long as I send you mine," he answered, "and I will answer for as long as you never again go quiet." 

"I will not go quiet for as long as you answer," she said, and the circle closed like a clasp. 

She rinsed the glass ink well, wrapped it in felt, and placed it in the last crate bound for the coast, a loan he would have to return in person.  Outside, the road forked toward the coast, and Maren, not yet forgiven but no longer silent, allowed herself to hum the first bar.
