Maren kept the dusk ledger at the Cirrus Library, where the shelves flowered and the clouds lay below like a second floor.  Ever since dreams became shareable, people had carried theirs up the cable lifts to donate, and each one took root in the stacks as a pale blossom that opened when someone borrowed it.  Her post was older than the blossoms: sharp-eyed dusk noticer, the one who walked the west terrace each evening and wrote down what the light did.  Up here dusk lasted an hour, and a patient person could learn every shadow by name.  So she knew, the first evening she saw it, that the shadow crossing the reading tables belonged to nothing.  No railing cast it, no reader, no drifting petal.  It moved against the sun, slow as a thought, and paused at the sill where her paperweight sat.  The paperweight was a river stone, gray and thumb-worn, that her brother Anselm had carried up from the valley forty years ago. 

Maren wrote in the ledger: a shadow unbound, terrace four, origin unknown.  Then, because unknown was not a word she tolerated, she went to the light archive.  The library had filmed its own dusk every evening for sixty years, soundless reels kept so gardeners could track how sunlight fed the blossoms.  She ran last week's footage beside older reels, stepping back decade by decade, until the same shadow appeared with a body attached. 

Forty years ago, in silence, Anselm stood on terrace four with his arm held out, offering the stone toward a doorway his sister never came through.  She remembered that evening from the other side of the door: her pride, their quarrel about her leaving, the message she sent that she was busy.  He had set the stone on the sill, donated one dream at the desk, and gone home to the river, where he died this spring.  She had never borrowed the dream, afraid it would be a reproach. 

Now she compared the two screens frame by frame, the way she compared any two dusks.  In the old reel the shadow was crisp: shoulders, elbow, open hand, the stone a dark coin at the end of it.  In the new one the shoulders had gone soft, the elbow was sinking, and the hand had begun to close like a flower at night.  The gardeners had a rule for this: a dream nobody shares has only its dreamer to hold it, and when the dreamer dies, its shadow slips loose and wears away.  Anselm's was wearing away where she could watch, one evening at a time, and what it was losing was the reach.  That outstretched arm was the shape of hope, his hope that she would open a door, and Maren found she could not let it fold. 

She located his blossom in the valley stacks, a tight white bud forty years unopened, and signed it out in her softly deliberate hand.  That night she slept beside it and dreamed what he had dreamed.  There was no reproach in it.  There was the river at evening, two children wading, and a boy holding out the smoothest stone he could find, saying only, take it up with you.  She woke with wet cheeks and forty years of dusks she could have spent differently, all of them lit and none of them returnable. 

Sharing the dream gave it a second holder, but the shadow still wandered, bound to no body.  A shadow wants something standing in the light, and the only thing left here that he had touched was the stone.  At the next dusk she took it from the sill and stood in the doorway she had refused to walk through, the stone on her open palm.  The loose shadow came across the tables against the sun, hesitated at her feet, and climbed to the stone.  She watched it metamorphose: the closing hand opened, the sunk elbow lifted, the shoulders squared, until a river stone was casting the long shadow of a boy's reaching arm.  It reached toward her, as it always had. 

"I'm here," she said, to the terrace, to the valley under the clouds, decades after it could be heard.  She set the stone back on the sill with both hands, and behind her, in the valley stacks, his blossom stood fully open.  In the ledger she wrote: shadow bound, terrace four, origin Anselm; shape intact.  Then she moved his blossom to the front of the stacks, where borrowers would find it, so that other sleepers might help hold the reach.  Every dusk after, the stone on the sill cast an arm far too long for it, and Maren noted it, and stayed until the light was gone.
