Maren made maps for fingers: knotted cord for rivers, beaded wax for ridges, pinpricked vellum for every coast she had ever walked.  She had been blind since a childhood fever and had never needed eyes to know where things were.  Only one region stayed blank on her table: the eleven years since her father walked into the Shrill and did not come out.  She had not wept for him, because she could not find the edges of what she felt, and a mapmaker does not enter country without edges.  Tonight she meant to enter it anyway. 

The Shrill was a forest that sang above hearing.  Its silver-barked trees keened at pitches only bats and dogs could bear, and travelers felt it merely as an ache in the teeth.  The song climbed and fell with the wheeling constellations, never the same from one hour to the next, which was why no one had charted the place.  But twice a year came the Pause, a single night when the summer stars had finished shifting and the winter stars had not begun, and the whole sky hung still.  Her father had gone in on such a night. 

So did she, wearing the only thing he had left her: a platinum ring with a compass no wider than a lentil set in its crown.  He had chosen platinum because it does not ring; iron and brass hummed along with the Shrill until their needles went mad.  The glass was long gone, so the needle lay bare under her thumb, and it had never pointed north.  It shivered with the forest's voice and grew calm only when aimed at quiet. 

At the tree line Maren raised her hand like a hazel rod and began to dowse for silence, sweeping slowly until the trembling eased.  She walked that way until her palm met bark. 

Maren had another gift, one she rarely spoke of: languages soaked into her through her skin.  A handshake with a salt trader had once given her his whole tongue by nightfall, verbs first, like warmth coming back to cold fingers.  Now the tree's speech seeped up her wrist, and she understood that the Shrill was not noise.  The trees were keepers; each kept whatever had been sung against its bark, scattered it as the sky turned, and gave it back true only while the stars stood still. 

This tree held one note, low and patient, in a man's voice.  Her breath stopped.  She knew that voice the way she knew the grain of her own table. 

The ring steadied toward the next trunk, and there a second note waited, a step higher; then a third, falling.  He had walked here humming, touching each tree as he passed, laying a trail of notes the way he once laid survey stakes. 

Deeper in, the needle failed her; quiet pooled in a dozen hollows, and it swung between them like a man asked too many questions.  So she followed the notes alone, palm to bark, tree after tree, holding each in her chest beside the last.  False trunks gave her owl cries or nothing, and she backed up and tried again.  The phrase lengthened, but she could not place it; it was a melody with its face turned away.  Something hot gathered behind her ribs, and for once she did not measure it. 

Then her hand reached for the next tree and found air.  She stepped forward, and the ache in her teeth vanished.  Under her thumb the needle lay utterly still for the first time in her life. 

Here, at the heart of the Shrill, every voice met its opposite and canceled, leaving a clearing of perfect hush: the stillpoint her father had sworn must exist and never found on paper.  In that hush the notes she carried finally had room to join. 

It was the tune he had hummed at her bedside through the fever, the last thing she heard while she could still see his face.  In the trees' language it had words, and her skin gave them to her: I am here, I am here, find me by this. 

He had been coughing blood that autumn; she had touched it on his sleeve and said nothing.  He had not abandoned her to a blank; he had left a map in the one ink only she could read. 

Maren knelt in the moss and wept, soundless and burning, and let the grief show her its edges at last. 

When the ache crept back into her teeth, she knew the stars had begun to move.  She pressed her palm to the nearest trunk and hummed the tune back, so the forest would keep them both. 

At home she tied one knot where the blank had been.
