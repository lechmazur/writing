Tobiah Renn had been dead three harvests, and still he would not rise into the Nebula of Respite.  It hung over the orchard planet like a violet quilt, the cloud that had once hidden seven fleeing ships and softened a cruel sun enough for apple grafts to live.  The settlers said their dead drifted up there to rest.  Tobiah meant to, once the living stopped needing him. 

He remembered when seven crews of strangers had become one family, the winter they shared a single stove and argued their way into loving each other.  That was long settled, which made what came after harder to watch. 

The dry cycle had come.  The floating rock arches that wandered above the rows, sweating nebula dew into the cisterns, now dripped half what they used to, and each household had quietly begun catching water only under whichever arch drifted over its own trees.  Nobody carried a bucket to the butterfly garden anymore.  No arch ever passed above it, so in Tobiah's day he had rung the watering bell each evening and every door had opened, one bucket per house, because without the butterflies no blossom on the planet would set fruit. 

The bell had been silent since his funeral.  People said it had cracked from grief, and they said it the way people say things that excuse them. 

Tobiah could not ring it.  His hands went through bronze, through buckets, through the shoulders he longed to shake.  Only at dawn, in the thin minute when nebula glow and sunrise overlapped, could he grip something as light as chalk.  So he wrote on the deserted sidewalks of the settlement before anyone woke. 

He started small, because a ghost asking for sacrifice gets scrubbed away as a prank.  PIM, THIRD RUNG OF YOUR LADDER IS ROTTEN.  NORTH ARCH CROSSES THE COMMON AT NOON, SET BARRELS.  MARGIT, HE IS SORRY, HE JUST CANNOT SAY IT FIRST. 

The rung snapped under a testing boot, the barrels filled, and Margit's husband wept into his porridge.  Within a month the crooked white letters had become strangely trustworthy, a thing people checked before the weather.  No one knew who wrote them, and no one quite wanted to, the way no one wants to look closely at a kindness. 

Then Tobiah spent his trust.  THE GARDEN IS DYING AND THE ORCHARD WITH IT, he chalked outside the meeting hall.  REEXAMINE THE BELL. 

They stood around the words all morning, arguing as they had not argued since the stove winter.  Pim said the bell was cracked and everyone knew it.  Margit asked who, exactly, had climbed up to look.  Nobody had. 

It was Wren, eleven years old and born under the nebula, who finally pushed into the garden where the bell post stood.  Tobiah drifted beside her, useless and aching.  The milkweed beds were gray, and the last chrysalises hung like dried seeds.  Wild bramble, the one plant that thrived on neglect, had swallowed the post entirely.  Wren parted the canes with her sleeves pulled over her fists and looked for a long time. 

"It is not cracked," she called.  "That dark line is a thorn cane grown right across it, and another has the clapper tied." 

The bell had never broken; they had only stopped looking at it. 

Cutting it free took every household, because the bramble was three harvests thick and fought back.  Pim brought his pruning hooks, Margit brought gloves that did not help, and by dusk there was not an unscratched forearm in the settlement.  Tobiah watched them bleed side by side and thought it the finest thing he had seen since dying. 

Wren rang it. 

The note went out under the drifting arches, plain and a little flat, and Tobiah saw it land in people like a hand on the back.  Nobody gave an order.  They simply went home and came back, one bucket per house, drawn from cisterns they had been guarding from each other. 

It was not enough water, not yet.  Most of the chrysalises were truly dead, and the dry cycle had two seasons left to run.  But Wren found four that twitched when her shadow crossed them, and Pim was already sketching a gutter to lead arch drip from the common to the beds. 

At the next dawn Tobiah wrote his shortest message.  RING IT EVERY EVENING.  He considered signing it and did not. 

Above him the Nebula of Respite brightened, patient as ever, and for the first time he let himself look up at it with something like appetite.  Not today.  There were four chrysalises to see open first, and a family that still needed reminding, now and then, of what it was.
