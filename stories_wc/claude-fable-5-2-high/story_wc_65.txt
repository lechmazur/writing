The nib hovered a finger's width above the vellum, and Ilse Marrow held it there.  One page was all the Charter of Kell allowed its ignorance cartographer each solstice: one sheet, one writing, no revisions.  Whatever she set down, the valley would provision for, build for, and believe, so in Kell the forecast did not describe the coming year so much as write it.  Through the slit window of the obsidian spire, the frozen lake lay white and enormous, stitched with the sled roads that carried every sack of grain the valley ate. 

Her title made strangers laugh.  Other cartographers drew what was known; Ilse charted what was not, inking careful borders around each blank so that no one mistook an unmeasured place for a safe one.  The walls around her were hung with those maps: the lake's unsounded deeps, the passes nobody had wintered in, the years for which no ice record survived. 

The quill in her hand was new.  She had found the feather in autumn, frozen to the shore, blue as the heart of a flame.  Kingfishers hunt only open water, and no kingfisher had been seen in Kell for ninety years.  A bird had come looking, which meant that somewhere on the lake there had been something for it to find.  She had cut the shaft into a nib that night and then gone back to her bead frame. 

The frame filled the spire's lower room: ninety wires, one for each recorded winter, each strung with beads for ice depth, thaw date, and snow load.  Beyond the last wire she had strung forty more, empty, and through the dark months she had filled them again and again, sliding beads forward by rule, changing one assumption at a time.  She ran the model with kind summers and with cruel ones, with her own measurements doubted and with them trusted.  In thirty-seven runs of forty, the sled roads failed within twelve winters.  That much was known.  Which winter, and which road first, lay in the blank. 

Warden Brandt stood behind her now, breathing like a man who had climbed three hundred steps to prevent a sentence.  "Write what your mother wrote," he had said an hour ago.  "The ice holds.  Three words, and everyone sleeps." 

"If I write that, they will build nothing," she had answered.  "And if I write that the ice fails, they will build nothing either, because frightened people only pack." 

She had not argued further.  She had taken him down to the frame, put the bead hook in his thick fingers, and told him to choose the assumptions himself.  He gave Kell the coldest summers on record and slid the beads forward, wire by wire, following her rules aloud like a child at sums.  The ice thinned anyway, a thumb's width each year, until on the nineteenth wire his hook had no bead left to move.  He had stood a long time with the hook hanging.  Then he had asked the question she had been mapping toward all winter: "What do we do?" 

She had prevailed, not by being believed, but by letting him reach the edge of the map on his own feet. 

Now came the harder craft.  One page could not hold a new way of living, and she did not know one anyway.  But one page could hold a border drawn around a blank, and a well-drawn blank was an invitation.  She imagined, as she always did, only outward from what she could measure: twelve winters, a thumb a year, runners that already slid, hulls her grandmother's grandmother had once known how to caulk. 

The forecast assembled itself in her mind, short enough to fit, plain enough to bind.  The ice road has twelve winters, perhaps fewer.  We do not yet know what carries grain over ice at midwinter and over water at midsummer.  This year every workshop in Kell receives timber and one season to answer, and the answers will be drawn onto this map. 

Not a prophecy of ruin, and not a lullaby; a year written so that the valley would spend it inventing.  Kell would stay Kell, she thought, the way a river stays itself by moving. 

Behind her, Brandt cleared his throat.  "My daughter builds sleds," he said quietly.  "She keeps saying the runners waste a good keel." 

Ilse smiled at the blank page, which was already a little less blank.  Out on the lake, the ice groaned once, a long low note, like something turning over in its sleep.  She steadied the kingfisher nib, that small bright proof of open water, and lowered it to the page.
