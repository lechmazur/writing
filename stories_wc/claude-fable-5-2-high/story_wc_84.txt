The pilgrim arrived at dusk, as the caretakers had promised she would, climbing the last switchback with a walking pole and a face like weathered rope.  Ines Varga watched her first through the drone's camera, then with her own sharp eyes, and felt the old jolt.  People said Ines looked perpetually surprised, brows high, mouth a little open, and she never corrected them because it was true.  Raised in transit camps where nothing stayed, she was astonished each time a thing turned up where it was expected.  That astonishment made her a good pilot; she noticed what settled people had stopped seeing. 

For three weeks she had flown her survey drone through the ancient observatory on the ridge, mapping its floor.  The founders, astronomers who also cast type, had sunk their brass matrices into the basalt, each a small mold with one glyph cut into it, scattered like spilled seed.  Nineteen matrices, twenty sockets.  Scholars had read the glyphs left to right, top to bottom, and gotten gravel. 

Ines had not come for scholarship.  Her mother, a daughter of the ridge clans scattered generations ago, had hummed one wavering note at every strange door, and called it the frequency of belonging.  Sound it true, she said, and any ridge household must feed you.  No door ever opened, because she never found the pitch; it was written here, she swore, on the floor nobody could read.  Ines meant to decode it, and learn whether there was anywhere she had been missing from. 

The pilgrim, Marit, entered the dome without greeting, knelt, and took from a cord at her neck a type foundry matrix chip, a brass sliver no bigger than a thumbnail.  She touched it to the stone and began to rise. 

"Wait," Ines said.  "Your people pulled that from this floor." 

Marit's eyes narrowed.  "My grandmother's grandmother pulled it the night the clans scattered, so no stranger could counterfeit the call.  We carry it up each autumn until someone can read the rest.  I know the founders' glyphs, girl, but not their order, and a number out of order is only brass." 

So each of them held half of a thing.  Ines looked up at the shutter, rusted closed across the dome's slit, and at the last violet light leaking around its edges. 

"They were astronomers," she said slowly.  "They would not have written in lines; they would have written the way the sky turns." 

Together they took the shutter chain and pulled, the old woman's weight and the young one's, until the slit groaned open on the first stars.  Ines flew the drone up and set it on the slit's lip, lens to the sky, and let it stare for an hour.  The dusk went down to embers, and the dome filled with that brilliant dimness in which brass shows brighter than it does by day.  On her screen the stars smeared into arcs, long bright trails circling the pole. 

She laid the exposure over her map of the floor, and her brows climbed higher than ever.  Every matrix sat on a trail.  She traced them by following star trails outward from the pole, in the direction the heavens wheel, and the glyphs fell into sequence like beads finding their string.  She called them out by position, and Marit, kneeling, spoke each one aloud. 

"Whoever sounds two," the old woman read, and stopped at the empty socket, where the brightest trail crossed bare stone.  Her hand shook as she pressed the chip home; it seated with a click that both of them felt in their teeth.  "Whoever sounds two, one, six beats to the second is ours," she whispered. 

A number was not a note, but Ines flew a machine that sang.  She lifted the drone into the center of the dome and trimmed its rotors until the blade hum read two hundred sixteen.  The dome, tuned by its builders, took the hum and gave it back enormous, a low gold tone that rolled out through the slit and down the ridge. 

Marit hummed with it, cracked and sure.  Ines opened her mouth and found the note her mother had chased across forty years of closed doors; it had only ever been a little flat, one piece short. 

Far below, in the dark valley, a window lit, and then another. 

"They kept listening," Marit said, "long after they forgot what for." 

Ines stood in the ringing dark, surprised as always, but for once not that something had held together.  She was surprised to be one of the pieces.
