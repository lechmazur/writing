The rain quit at dawn, and Ivory Harbor shone like a wet tooth, every quay of pale limestone rinsed clean.  Sela walked the seawall with her basket, as she had every morning for twelve years.  She was the harbor's ocean-spray archivist, keeper of whatever the sea threw over the stones.  Before the exile that had meant net corks and wreck timber.  After the Admiralty banished the fleet families, it meant messages: carved floats, knotted cords, a child's shoe with a name inked inside.  Someone had to stay and keep the record, so the decree had spared her, and she had never forgiven it for that.  She shelved each fragment by the berth it belonged to.  Sixty-one berths stood empty along the white quays, and her ledger mapped them the way a pilot maps shoals: here the Tarn family, gone; here the Osks, gone.  She had learned to steer by what was missing. 

Sela was no prophet.  Her sight only ever worked in answer: hand her a shard and she saw the whole jar, but ask her about tomorrow and she saw fog.  So she had not foreseen the decree lifting, nor guessed what would follow.  The exile had ended nine days ago, and the boats had come back, and not one had entered.  They hung beyond the breakwater in the thinning mist, sails slack, a ragged line of them. 

Sela understood why.  No keel of the fleet had ever crossed that gap without the herald's trumpet sounding the Homecoming, nine notes that meant the harbor knew you and wanted you.  On the day of banishment, the Admiralty's officer had wrenched the middle valve from that trumpet and flung it off the quay.  The old herald died the second winter.  The gutted horn sat on Sela's shelf, filed under Berth Zero, its other valves seized solid with salt.  To people who had been unwelcome for twelve years, silence at the harbor mouth said only one thing. 

The rain had scoured the tideline deep, and below the herald's steps something winked green and gold in the gravel.  Sela climbed down and dug out a brass cylinder no longer than her thumb, pierced through with ports.  The herald's trumpet valve. 

She ran to the archive, spray drying white on her sleeves.  Oiled, the valve slid home in its casing as if it had only stepped out.  But the horn was still half mute.  With one valve working, she could reach five of the nine notes; the second, fourth, sixth, and eighth were simply not in it. 

She stood on the herald's steps with the horn at her lips and could not blow.  A broken Homecoming might sound like mockery, or like a harbor that had forgotten them. 

Then the mist lifted off the waiting boats, and her sight answered.  She saw the ragged line the way she saw her ledger: not as a fleet with holes in it, but as a chart, each gap a channel someone could sail through.  The missing notes were not hers to supply.  They were berths. 

She played the first note and then waited, holding the silence for exactly the length of the second.  Third note; silence.  Fifth; silence.  Through those staggered intervals the call went out over the water, more door than wall. 

Nothing came back, and she began again, her breath shaking.  On the third round, a ship's bell far out struck once, squarely in the first gap.  Then a voice, cracked and old, sang the fourth note, and a dozen voices found the sixth. 

They remembered.  Twelve years away, and they had carried the half she could not play.  Sela had meant to hearten them, but it was her own chest that loosened first, twelve years of keeping watch alone answered at last. 

The boats came in as the notes had, one, then a pause, then another, each crew singing its part as it crossed the gap.  Sela played until her lips split.  By dusk, forty-four berths held hulls, and the wet white quays were loud with boots.  Seventeen stayed empty; those families had been lost to fever, to storms, to other shores. 

That night she opened the ledger and did not strike the empty berths out.  She wrote the Homecoming across the page instead, five notes in ink and four spaces left open.  For twelve years she had gathered fragments and hoped they would add up to a meaning.  They did, but only once someone answered.  Tomorrow she would play at dawn again, and leave the silences in, in case anyone was still out there listening for their note.
