The palace of Pendeloque hung in the dark like a chandelier, chained to seven giant stalactites above a cavern no one had ever heard the bottom of.  Everything inside it ticked.  Brass roses opened on the hour, clockwork swans circled the fountain in perfect ovals, and the court applauded when the bell told them to. 

Prince Corvin, who could not look at anything without hypothesizing about it, had spent the night climbing the oldest anchor chain just to touch one thing nobody had built.  He came down bedraggled, velvet soaked through with drip water, lace torn on the links, his circlet stuffed in a pocket so it would not fall forever.  He also came down with a question: why did the chain shiver near the stone, alive with the mountain's breathing, and then pass through a fat collar of gray felt before it touched the palace? 

Hypothesis one: the Maker Queen, his ancestor, had hated trembling.  Hypothesis two: she had hated it for a reason, and the reason would be in her workshop, sealed at the root of that same chain. 

He picked the lock with a swan's tail feather, which was only a strip of tin.  The air inside smelled bitterly sweet, old lamp resin and something he had no word for, and on the bench sat the First Bird, the brass swift from which every machine in the kingdom claimed descent.  Children were taught that she built it because living things had failed, and brass would not.  Corvin had always wondered why its breast had a seam. 

His wet fingers shook as he pried, and the little door gave, and where the mainspring should have been lay an egg.  It was small, chalk blue, freckled with rust, and it belonged to no machine.  He knew it from a faded nursery mural: a dripstone swift's egg, the bird extinct since before the palace was hung. 

Beneath it, folded small, was a note in the Maker's hand.  "Forty days kept perfectly warm, perfectly dry, perfectly still, and it will not wake.  Life is unreliable.  I will build what cannot disappoint me." 

Corvin sat down on the floor, because the whole genesis of his world had just turned over in his hands like a coin.  Pendeloque had not begun in triumph over nature.  It had begun in a grieving woman's mistake, and every gear since had been cut to the shape of that flaw. 

But what, exactly, was the mistake?  He hypothesized aloud, the way he had as a boy, teeth chattering.  Perhaps the egg was simply dead.  Perhaps not: the swifts had nested on stalactites, and stalactites went dry for decades, so perhaps their eggs slept like seeds until the stone began to drip and shiver again.  Perhaps perfectly dry and perfectly still was exactly how one told an egg that the world was not ready.  And perhaps she had muffled the chains afterward because every tremor reminded her of what had not hatched. 

It was only a guess, but a guess was the one thing in Pendeloque that had not been wound. 

Under the bench stood her reject bin, full of everything too flawed for a perfect kingdom: warped springs, cracked escapements, a balance wheel that limped.  He began to assemble them into a cradle, choosing each piece for its fault, until the thing rocked the way a heart does when it is afraid, never twice the same.  He set the egg in the ruin of his lace and wrung his sleeve over it, so that cold mineral water ran down the shell. 

Then he carried the cradle up through the roof hatch, hooked it to the chain, and sawed at the felt collar with the tin feather until it dropped away into the dark.  The mountain's shiver came down the links at once.  Below him, for the first time in his life, the palace quaked, faintly, and somewhere a swan missed its oval. 

Corvin clung there dripping, half dreaming, hypothesizing about failure, about treason, about whether hope was only another kind of clockwork. 

Then, against his palm, the egg tapped back.  It was not on the beat. 

He laughed, and the laugh broke, because the knowledge was bitterly sweet: one bird, no mate, a song that would be sung once and then be gone, as nothing brass had ever been allowed to be.  That, he hypothesized, was the part his ancestor had left out. 

A hairline crack ran across the blue, and the prince held still in all that trembling, and watched his world begin again, wrong, and alive.
