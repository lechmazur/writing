At twilight the velvet garden swallowed sound the way moss swallows rain.  Ines Calloway liked it for that reason, and for no other, she told herself.  The Archive had planted its walls with black mallow and plush lichen so that quarantined memories, the ones whose grief was catching, could not echo out into the city.  She had tended them for thirty years without believing a single one was more than vibration trapped in an object. 

Tonight's order was plain: archive item nine, a whispering seashell half, before it decayed past saving.  A memory kept alone too long thinned like a voice in an empty house.  The cure was to give it a body of its own, and the only hour still enough for the sealing came when the last star faded into morning light. 

She unwrapped the shell on the stone table.  It was half a cockle, ridged and pale, its hinge snapped clean.  "A shell only returns the noise you bring it," she said aloud, as she said to every apprentice.  Then she held it to her ear, because procedure required a sample. 

"Inny," the shell whispered.  "Tap twice if you can hear me." 

Nobody had called her that in her whole remembered life.  She set the shell down, checked the garden for pranksters, found only moths, and picked it up again with a smile she did not approve of.  The voice was a boy's, hoarse and cheerful, counting cracks in a ceiling, promising the sea when the fever doors were unsealed.  She did not believe in it, and she listened for an hour. 

Skepticism, at least, knew how to work.  She pulled the item's ledger and read what the Archive had quarantined forty years ago.  Source: female child, seven, sole survivor of the Harrow Street fever house.  Content: her twin brother Tobias, sealed in the next room, speaking to her through a wall with half a cockle pressed to the plaster.  Reason for removal: her mourning spread to every nurse who touched her.  Source's name: Ines Calloway. 

She sat very still among the dark flowers.  So that was the shape of the blank she had built a life around: the solitary rooms, the meals for one, the garden where nothing answered back.  She had called it temperament.  It was a quarantine nobody had ever lifted. 

The method was her own design, and she trusted it more than ghosts.  A memory could be archived through resonance structures, lattices of glass rods each tuned to one frequency of the original, so that no single rod held the memory and the whole lived in the agreement among them.  Built well, such a structure needed no listener to keep it alive.  All night she cut and tuned, striking each rod against the shell's whisper until they hummed as one. 

But the lattice would not hold.  Every phrase Tobias spoke leaned toward a pause, a gap shaped like two small knocks, and into each gap the structure collapsed.  The memory was half a conversation, still waiting for its other half. 

The stars were going out by then, one by one.  She saw two easy endings.  She could press the shell to her ear until the whole grief poured back into her, and carry Tobias as a wound that others would catch.  Or she could let him thin to silence behind these soft walls.  Both endings left someone locked in a room.  What she wanted, she understood with a cold and steady clarity, was to translate isolation into independence, for him and for herself: a memory that could stand without a host, a woman who could stand without a blank. 

So she did the one thing the structure lacked.  She cut a final rod, tuned it to the rap of her own knuckles, and when Tobias whispered, "Tap twice if you can hear me," she struck it twice. 

The answer rang through the lattice and stayed.  The gaps closed.  Glass took up the question and the reply together, and the shell half began to shine, tragically radiant, like a lamp left burning in a house everyone has fled.  Overhead the last star faded into morning light, the air went perfectly still, and she sealed the case. 

Inside, a boy asked and a girl answered, endlessly, needing no one. 

Ines wrote the label in a firm hand: Item nine, archived, quarantine lifted.  She still did not believe in ghosts.  She believed in her brother, which was a different matter, and she knew now exactly what she had lost and that she had answered him.  Then she opened the garden gate, which she had always kept latched, and walked out into the loud, waking city alone and unafraid.
