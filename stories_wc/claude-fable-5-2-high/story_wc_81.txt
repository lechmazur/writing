Ilse Maren indexed pages the way other people breathed, one after another, without favoritism.  The Registry above had sent her down the diving shaft into drowned Veth, to the library everyone had forgotten beneath the harbor, because a logical page indexer would not care whose name stood on which deed.  She cared, and that was why she was careful.  Her grandmother's land claim had been struck out as a forgery, and Ilse wanted to trace honest origins, not flattering ones, even if the honest answer left her family with nothing. 

The rule she had been given was simple.  Every deed in the sealed hall carried the library's accession mark, a wreath around an open book.  Where the wreath was perfect, the deed was genuine; where one leaf was missing, the deed was a later fake, struck from a damaged copy.  For nineteen days, under the hard white lamps the Registry had strung from its surface generator, she sorted four thousand pages into two columns.  Her grandmother's deed, with its missing leaf, went into the second.  She wrote the entry in the same even hand as all the rest. 

On the twentieth day the generator failed.  The lamps died together, and the pumps, which had groaned so constantly that she had stopped hearing them, went quiet.  Ilse did not reach for matches.  The air would keep for hours, the shaft bell would ring when power returned, and panic indexed nothing.  She sat with her hands flat on the table and let the stillness that the machines had taken from this room come back into it. 

Slowly the dark turned out not to be complete.  Moonlight was falling through thirty feet of harbor and slanting in through the tall drowned windows, arriving on the tables as low, wavering, greenish patches that slid across the paper like the shade of leaves.  She watched them without hurry, and because she was watching without hurry, she saw it. 

On the top page of the second column, the sideways light caught a tiny ridge around the wreath.  The seal had bitten into the paper.  She slid a page from the first column beside it, and the perfect wreath lay as flat as a printed picture.  The overhead lamps had flooded every surface evenly and hidden this for nineteen days. 

Her pulse wanted to run ahead, toward her grandmother, and she declined to follow it.  Instead she stated an alternative hypothesis aloud, as plainly as a catalogue heading, so that it could be wrong in the open.  "The true stamp was always chipped, and the perfect wreath belongs to someone who copied the design as it was meant to look." 

A hypothesis owed her predictions, and she collected them by touch and by moonlight.  If she was right, the oldest accession ledger, begun a century before the sea came in, would show the missing leaf on its first page.  She carried it to the window and tilted it into a drifting patch of light, and it did.  And the stamp itself, if it survived, would carry the chip in its own brass.  She found it in the registrar's swollen desk, an antique ink stamp with a pearwood handle, heavier than it looked.  Her thumb read the face of it: book, wreath, and one gap with edges worn as soft as all the rest.  She inked it from her own bottle, pressed it to a blank flyleaf, and laid the fresh mark beside her grandmother's deed.  Same gap, same bite. 

She let herself feel that for exactly one breath.  Then she began to readjust the index, which meant more than swapping two columns.  She would not replace one convenient rule with another, so she checked each page for its own bite in the moving light and set aside every one she could not judge.  The perfect wreaths, she noticed, sat mostly on the grandest claims, the waterfront blocks the Registry's own patrons expected to inherit when Veth was drained.  They would not thank her.  They might say a Maren had gone below and found what a Maren wanted, and they would be right to ask.  So on the index's first leaf she wrote out the method, where to stand, how low to hold a single candle, which ledger to open, so that anyone who doubted her could go down and doubt the paper instead.  Beneath it she pressed the chipped wreath once, as a signature. 

When the shaft bell rang and the lamps snapped back, flattening every page to white, Ilse did not look up at once.  She reached over and switched off the lamp above her table, and went on working in the green, shifting light, unhurried, with the stillness she had reclaimed kept carefully around her.
