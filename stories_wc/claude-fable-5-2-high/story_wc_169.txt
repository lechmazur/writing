Ilse Maren trusted proofs the way other people trusted prayers, which is why she dreamed in them.  Each dawn at low water she climbed down to the tide-polished grotto beneath Carrow Sands, where the sea had rubbed the stone as smooth as the inside of a shell.  The town above her was thinning out, one shuttered net loft at a time, since the herring had gone.  Ilse, who kept the harbor ledgers and knew exactly how fast a place could subtract itself, wanted to puzzle out new equations for hope, because the old ones no longer balanced. 

Her instrument was her grandmother's balloonist's altitude gauge, a brass aneroid dial that had once told a woman in a wicker basket how far she had risen above the fairgrounds.  In the grotto it behaved strangely.  Ilse would stand perfectly still on the wet floor, and the needle would climb and sink anyway, as though the ground itself were being raised and lowered somewhere else.  She reasoned it through over three weeks of tide tables: the grotto was a seam where neighboring timelines pressed close, other Carrow Sands that had made other choices, and the gauge was reading their weather. 

A hypothesis wants a test, so she built trial balloons.  They were small things of tissue and paste, lifted by a candle stub, and beneath each one she hung a proposal for the town, written in the old notation of Lantern Tide.  Before the festival lapsed, families had decorated these walls in patterns that meant things: a spiral of limpet shells for safe return, a ladder of green glass floats for a good season, red ribbon knotted in threes for a child on the way.  Ilse treated the patterns as terms.  She set a full arrangement on the grotto wall, tied its miniature under a balloon, and released it up the blowhole, where the air shimmered like heat over slate and the balloon simply was not there anymore.  Then she sat, lucidly watchful, the gauge open in her palm, and recorded how high the needle said her idea had floated in a world she would never walk. 

Spirals and floats for a rebuilt fleet: four hundred feet, then a slow fall.  Ribbons and mussel shells for an oyster farm: nine hundred, wavering.  She adjusted one decoration at a time, as a careful person changes one variable, and the wall behind her grew into a long glittering derivation. 

The balloons came back on the following ebb, sinking down the blowhole spent and cold, and because she examined everything, she examined them.  The one that had reached three thousand feet did not carry her pattern home.  Someone had added to it: a clumsy fourth knot in the ribbon, a twist of tarred net twine, a gull feather stuck on with what smelled like jam. 

She laid out every returned balloon in order of altitude and felt the floor of her reasoning tilt, quietly, the way the tide turns without a sound.  The low fliers were untouched, exactly as she had made them.  The high ones had all been handled by other hands, a child's, a net mender's, somebody's with paint on the thumbs, in timelines where another Ilse had evidently not worked alone.  The variable she had been hunting was not any pattern.  It was the number of people making one. 

For a logical woman this was an upheaval, and yet it arrived as calmly as a correct answer always does, with a small click and then a great deal of room.  She had been trying to solve Carrow Sands on its behalf, in a cave, before breakfast. 

Ilse took her derivation down from the wall, shell by shell, and carried the decorations up the cliff path in her grandmother's fish basket.  She knocked at the net loft, at the school, at the door of the widower who painted hulls, and at each one she held out a handful of shells and said she meant to revitalize Lantern Tide and could not finish the sum without them. 

They came down at the next low water, eleven of them, doubtful and curious, and the grotto filled with argument about where the floats should go.  A girl knotted the ribbon in fours because she liked fours.  Ilse did not correct her. 

They sent the last trial balloon up together, hung with a pattern no single mind had planned, and it shimmered away toward whichever other Carrow Sands might need it.  In her palm the needle climbed past three thousand feet and kept climbing, and Ilse, boots flat on the polished stone, closed the gauge and looked at the faces around her instead.
