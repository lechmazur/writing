The phoenix had risen an hour ago, and its ashes were still warm in the hollow of the oldest pear tree.  Maren knelt before them in the orchard where the moon never set and the blossoms never became fruit.  She had waited nine years for this night, because only fresh phoenix ash remembers the road back from death.  All her life she had spoken to animals without words, quieting stampedes with a breath, coaxing wolves from snares with her open palms.  Villages called her the beast whisperer, but only one beast had ever whispered back.  Bramble, her old gray lynx, had died in the winter of the long cough, his head heavy across her feet. 

From her coat she took the only thing he had left her, a slip of paper from a fortune cookie.  A spice merchant had tossed them the cookie at their last market together; Bramble had crunched the shell and spat the fortune into her hand like a gift.  It read: The door you open will also open you.  She had laughed then.  She did not laugh now, laying the slip on the ash beside three small pouches bought from that same merchant with everything she owned. 

Beasts do not come to names; they come to smells, and Bramble had known her by the market spices that clung to her sleeves.  So she would conjure him the only way a lynx could be called, through the scent of the rarest of them.  She scattered black cardamom, then grains of paradise, then seven threads of saffron picked on a mountain that had since fallen into the sea.  The ash took them with a sigh.  Smoke rose, sweet and bitter and bright, and wound among the white branches until the whole orchard smelled like the two of them walking home.  Above her the stars leaned close, as if the sky were a face bending over a cradle. 

Then the petals stopped falling.  Between two apple trees stood Bramble, made of moonlight and smoke, his tufted ears turning toward her heartbeat.  She held out her palms, and he came, and his nose passed through her fingers like cold water.  He could not feel her.  He circled, crying the small broken cry he had made as a kitten, and the smoke was already thinning. 

On the ash, the fortune had not burned; its letters glowed like coals.  The door you open will also open you.  Maren understood, and the understanding was a stone in her throat.  The phoenix did not return as the bird it had been; it burned and became, and its ash would bargain no other way.  Bramble could not be dragged backward into fur and blood.  If she wanted the bond reforged, she would have to be the metal that went into the fire. 

She did not want it.  She loved her hands, her gruff voice, the ache in her knees, bread, rain, the plain weight of being a woman in a coat.  She had come to bring him home, not to leave it.  But Bramble cried again, fainter, and she pressed both hands into the warm ash. 

The change began at her fingertips, which paled to silver and lost their edges.  Terror bolted through her like a horse through a burning barn.  And here was the strange thing: the more of her came undone, the steadier she grew.  She knew this fear, having met it in a thousand trapped and shaking creatures, and she did for herself what she had always done for them.  Easy, she whispered to the frightened animal of her own body.  Easy now, I am here, nothing is being taken that you are not giving. 

Her knees stopped aching.  Her coat slid empty into the blossoms.  Her voice went last, and she grieved it for one breath, and then found she no longer needed breath to grieve or to speak.  What rose from the ash was not a woman and not a lynx, only a long pale shape that smelled of cardamom and moonlight. 

Bramble pushed his head against her, and this time there was something to push against.  His purr moved through her the way tides move through the sea, and somewhere very far above, the newborn phoenix crossed the moon like a spark.  The petals began to fall again.  Two shapes walked off between the trees, shoulder to shoulder, unhurried, leaving no prints.  On the cooling ash lay a slip of paper, blank now, its promise kept.
