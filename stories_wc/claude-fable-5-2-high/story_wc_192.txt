The sanctuary had burned down to charcoal eleven winters ago, and Ilse had kept its shadows ever since.  There was no roof and no door now, only black ribs of timber, ash drifted soft as flour, and starlight falling straight in.  The fire had taken the night choir in the middle of a held note, and their shadows stayed scorched on the walls, mouths open, still waiting for air.  That was her whole work: to teach the suffocating to breathe. 

She did it the only way that had ever worked, linking through scars.  Her right palm carried a ridged circle, the brand of the doorknocker ring she had seized that night when the door was already too hot to open.  The walls carried their own scars, pale blisters in the char where each singer had leaned.  She would press wound to wound, breathe slowly, and wait until a shadow's chest remembered the motion, let go its note, and thinned upward into the stars.  Some took a season.  She never hurried them, and she never lit a lamp, because starlight made the gentlest shadows and they had suffered enough from flame. 

For years the valley families had climbed up to mourn, and every wail drew the shadows tight again, back into the last smothered second.  So Ilse had ended it, the way she did everything, with care.  She returned every borrowed pot, wrote each family which of their dead had already gone free, and promised nothing she could not keep.  Then she burned the footbridge over the gorge one plank at a time, working backward toward the sanctuary, dousing each ember before it could fall into the dry pines below.  She kept the nails in a jar.  Since then no voice had crossed, and the silence sat in the ruin like a bowl filled to the brim. 

One shadow remained.  It was small, low on the charred doorpost, a child's hand stretched toward the place where the inside latch had been.  Tobin, the youngest treble, her brother.  Three winters she had pressed her palm to his scar and breathed, and three winters he had not. 

Meanwhile the threshold was fading.  The door itself was long gone; only the frame stood, two posts and a lintel, with the iron ring hung on a nail where the wood had once held it.  Each wind crumbled the posts a little more.  Bracken had begun to encroach across the sill, green fingers in the ash, and Ilse understood what that meant.  A sanctuary is only a sanctuary while there is an inside to leave.  When the frame fell, the hillside would simply take the place, and Tobin would have no door to go out by, only weather, scattering him mid-gasp. 

On a windless night, with the lintel sagging, she knelt and laid her scar against his once more.  She breathed in, and felt, as always, the link close like a clasp.  This time she paid attention to her own chest instead of his.  It was locked.  She took air in sips and gave almost none back, and had done so, she realized, since the moment the ring burned her and she let go.  The others had only needed a living rhythm to borrow.  Tobin was joined to her through that one shared wound, his hand on the inside, hers on the outside, and he was copying her exactly.  She had cut away every mourner who might tighten his breath, and kept the tightest one. 

Ilse stood, lifted the ring from its nail, and stepped out over the bracken to the cold side of the frame.  The iron fit the circle in her palm as if it had been waiting there.  She knocked it against the doorpost, three times, the way anyone does who expects to be answered.  Charcoal sifted down. 

"I let go," she said, the first words she had spoken aloud since the bridge.  "I have been holding my breath for it ever since, and you have been holding yours with me." 

Then she put her palm to the post, scar to scar through the thin black wood, and exhaled all the way down, until her ribs ached and her eyes ran.  Under her hand, something small let out a note eleven years late.  It passed through the doorway as a child passes a held door, without looking back, and rose thin into the starlight.  The lintel came down behind it almost gently, and then there was no inside and no outside, only a hill with ash on it. 

Ilse stood in the emptiness and breathed in it, awkwardly, like someone learning.  In the morning she carried the ring and the jar of nails down to the gorge, and counted how many planks she would need.
