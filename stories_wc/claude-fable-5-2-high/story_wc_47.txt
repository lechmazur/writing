Nine days after her confession went viral, Ines Okoye left her phone buzzing in a drawer and unlocked Hall C.  The data center had been dead for three winters, and she had spent every one of them turning it into a moss garden.  Green cushions swelled over the cable trays; ferns leaned from the vents; the scorched server racks stood in rows like headstones nobody had carved.  Light came down through yellowed skylights the color of weak tea, and the moss drank every sound.  She called it the honeyed hush, a quiet thick and golden enough to hold a person without pressing on the bruise. 

Her old silence had been sour: three years of letting the company blame a power surge for the fire that erased the Keepsake archive, forty thousand saved voicemails from people who would never call again.  The truth, which she had finally typed out and posted, was smaller and worse.  She had been the night operator, a dreamer sketching ferns in the logbook margins, and she had muted a heat alarm that had lied twice that week. 

Four million strangers now knew it.  She had not answered a single comment.  Instead, the morning after posting, she had set a second trowel by the door, propped the gate open, and wired a brass tag to every burnt rack showing which customer blocks it had carried.  She still dreamed, but these days she kept watch while she did it. 

So she saw the old man before he knocked.  He stood in the doorway with a printout of her confession folded in his fist, and his anger looked tired from the long drive. 

"Tobias Reyes," he said.  "Block 14-C.  Eleven voicemails from my wife." 

"Rack nine," Ines said, because she had memorized them all.  "I will not ask you to forgive me.  I can show you where they were." 

Her hand had gone to the amber pendant at her throat, her grandmother's, a moss sprig sealed inside it for forty million years.  She made herself let go of it and walked.  He followed her down the aisle, and the hush closed over his footsteps. 

Rack nine was black, warped, and bare.  Above it, icicles hung from the cracked skylight, thawing in the first mild week of March.  Their drip had stippled the ash on the rack's top with dark wet dots, a ragged line that rose and fell. 

"Moss will not take just anywhere on burnt steel," she said.  "I tried for a year and it all died.  Then I noticed the pattern the icicles leave when they thaw, and I started planting only where the water had already chosen." 

Tobias stared at the dots for a long time.  "It looks like a recording," he said.  "The little line that jumps when someone talks." 

Ines had thought so too, every morning, and had never dared say it to anyone.  She held out the second trowel and a tray of moss plugs she had cut at dawn. 

"I cannot give her voice back.  But this scar is yours, and something living can be grafted onto it." 

Then he took the trowel, and she showed him how to scrape the ash aside and seat a plug in each wet dot, firm but not hard.  They worked along the line without speaking, green following black, peak after valley.  Near the end he said, to the moss more than to her, "She always started the same way.  It's only me." 

The hush took the words and did not give them back as an echo; it simply kept them.  Inside that shelter, Ines felt her own chest open a little, and she let it.  She unclasped the pendant. 

"Amber is what a tree makes when it is cut," she said.  "It bleeds, and the bleeding hardens around whatever was close, and after long enough people call it precious."  She proffered it on her open palm, not as payment, because nothing would pay.  "Hold this until your moss takes," she said.  "It is the only thing I own that I would miss, so you will know I am waiting for you to come back." 

Tobias looked at the stone, at the tiny sprig that had outlasted its own forest.  He did not say he forgave her.  He closed his fingers around the pendant and said, "Six weeks?" 

"Eight, if the thaw is slow." 

"Eight, then." 

After he left, she did not go to the drawer where the phone still buzzed.  She stood at rack nine with her bare throat and watched a drop gather, fall, and darken one spot they had missed.  She pressed the last plug into it, and the hush, sweet and gold, held.
