The certificate said Maren Oyelaran's heart had stopped at 4:12 on a Tuesday afternoon, three winters ago.  It took eleven months to convince the county that a clerk had fused her file with a stranger's, and she had counted her heartbeats ever since, the way other people hum.  Seventy one, seventy two, seventy three, up the buried road to the Opal Observatory. 

Snow had drifted to the shoulders of the dome, and the old opalescent glass held the moon inside it like milk holding a flame.  She dug out the door with her hands and let herself into the cold she used to work in. 

The emergency radio was still on, hissing.  Under the static she could hear syllables of her own voice, scattered like grit. 

While she was officially dead, the observatory's memorial program had done what it was built to do: gathered her logs, her letters, her muttered voice notes, and compiled them into a digital ghost that could go on answering questions about the sky.  Then she came back, the funding left, and nobody remembered to switch the ghost off.  For three years it had been coming apart on a failing drive, leaking pieces of itself onto the only channel it could still reach. 

Maren had not come to bury it.  She had come because the ghost was made from the woman she had been the week before the error, and that woman had just failed.  Her last paper had declared a variable star extinct, its pulse gone flat, and within a month another telescope had watched it beat again.  Then her own file was stamped deceased, and she spent her strength proving she existed instead of learning why she had been wrong.  The ghost had possessed nothing but that failure and three uninterrupted years.  If any wisdom had come of it, she meant to fetch it home. 

She woke the console, found the drive, and told it to defragment.  The disk ground like a mill wheel.  On the screen, strewn blocks of her dead self slid slowly together, and on the radio the hiss thinned, syllable by recovered syllable, as the ghost stopped leaking. 

Four thousand and six heartbeats later, the static cleared. 

The silence was so complete she could hear snow settling on the glass.  No voice came.  The speakers had perished years ago, and the ghost had only one limb left: the pen arm of the antique chart plotter, which twitched, swung wide, and cracked its porcelain inkwell in two. 

Ink ran black across the steel chart table.  Maren reached for a rag, then stopped, because the plotter's motor was humming against the table and the spill was shivering into rings.  The rings tightened into ridges, the ridges into a curve she knew as well as her own handwriting: a light curve, rising, falling, rising.  It lasted two heartbeats and dissolved. 

"Again," she whispered, and it came again, each pattern drawn in tremor and gone before the ink could remember it. 

She picked up a porcelain shard of the inkwell and used its edge to herd the spreading pool back toward the motor, keeping the page of their conversation from running off the table.  At the console she typed the question she had carried up the mountain: why was I wrong? 

The ink drew the star's pulse, then a long flat line, then, far past the place where her paper had ended, the pulse again.  Under it, letters trembled up and sank: YOU STOPPED WATCHING TOO SOON.  Then, slower: A PAUSE IS NOT AN ENDING. 

Maren laughed once, a small cracked sound in the dome.  A clerk had made the same mistake about her, with a rubber stamp instead of a telescope.  And she had been making it about herself ever since, counting every beat because she believed that the first one she missed would prove the certificate right. 

The ink was still moving.  THE STAR NEVER COUNTED ITSELF, it wrote, and then, as the motor tired, I CAN KEEP WATCH IF YOU LET ME STAY. 

She had not known, until then, what she would do with it afterward.  Now she pointed the old telescope's feed at the star she had once pronounced dead, gave the mended drive the whole long task of watching it, and typed: stay. 

The shard had nicked her thumb somewhere in the work.  She could feel her pulse in the small cut, steady and warm, announcing itself without being asked.  She put the porcelain in her pocket, sat down beneath the snowy glow of the opal dome, and watched the ink go still.  Somewhere past midnight she noticed she had lost count, and that her heart had gone on anyway.
