Ilse Marrow read everything the way a prism reads light, splitting one plain line into its several colors.  That habit had made her a slow archivist and, tonight, the only one climbing toward the Serpentine Grotto with rope on her shoulder.  Seventeen iron cabinets stood sealed in the Coil Archive, their dial combinations forgotten since Keeper Aurel died without an apprentice.  In nine days the council would cut them open with torches, and whatever paper lay inside would cook.  Ilse did not argue with the council; she kept her anger banked, like a coal under ash, and went looking for where forgotten combinations hide. 

She found the first hint in Aurel's last ledger: a manuscript margin doodle beside a dull column of candle purchases.  A snake, drawn belly-up, looped seventeen times and swallowed a trail of half-shaded seeds.  Under it he had written, small as a flea, "seeds of vigilance."  Her colleagues saw boredom.  Ilse's mind split it three ways: the snake was a place, the seventeen coils were the cabinets, and the upturned belly was a time.  Once a year, at the Upturn, gravity inverts for an hour over the whole valley, and people lash themselves to their beds and wait. 

She had already visited the grotto once, a month ago, in ordinary weight and with a bright lantern.  She had found a winding tunnel full of mist, a black pool at its end, and iron rings bolted uselessly into the floor.  Nothing else.  It took her three weeks to see the half-shading for what it was.  Aurel hid things at thresholds, at the exact edge where one state tips into another, because only someone watching closely stands at an edge long enough to notice.  So she would reenter the grotto tonight, at the minute the world turned over. 

The mist met her at the mouth and closed behind her like a held breath.  She followed the tunnel's long bends by touch, and at the pool she knotted her harness to two of the floor rings.  Then she waited, lantern shuttered to a single slat, her pulse loud in the wet dark. 

The Upturn came without sound.  For one weightless second she belonged to neither floor nor roof, and then her body fell upward and the rope caught her, hanging her from the ground like a bat.  The pool let go of its bed and fell past her in one shivering sheet, and the mist followed it, settling against the ceiling like a second floor.  The basin lay bare above her head for the first time in a year. 

She opened the lantern wide and saw only slick stone, and the coal in her chest dimmed.  Half-shaded, she reminded herself.  She closed the shutter slat by slat, and at the last sliver, on the border between seeing and not seeing, the basin answered.  Lichen woke along the rock, clandestinely luminous, a green so shy that one more thread of lamplight would have drowned it.  It grew only in small drilled pits, and the pits ran in a spiral of seventeen coils, clustered in groups: four, then nine, then two.  Seeds, planted by a patient man with a hand drill and a year between each visit.  Vigilance, she understood, was what made them sprout. 

Ilse copied them by feel, pencil tied to her wrist, paper pinned against her knee, working inward coil by coil while the hour drained.  At the fifteenth coil the water beneath her boots began to tremble.  She did not hurry; hurrying was how numbers got forgotten in the first place.  She read the sixteenth, then the seventeenth, checked each cluster twice against her thumb, and only then sealed the page in oilskin and wrapped both arms through the rings.  Gravity righted itself, the pool crashed home over her shoulders, and the lichen went dark as if it had never spoken. 

She walked out soaked and shaking, carrying seventeen small fires folded against her ribs.  At dawn the first dial turned under her fingers, four, nine, two, and the cabinet door sighed open on dry, whole paper.  Inside lay the valley's logbooks: a century of river heights, ridge fires, and Upturn minutes, the slow record a town needs when it starts to feel safe. 

Ilse gave the council its numbers and took an apprentice, because Aurel's one mistake had been trusting stone alone.  But lists get lost and apprentices leave, so that evening she opened the current ledger to a dull column of candle purchases.  In the margin she drew a snake, belly-up, swallowing half-shaded seeds, and beside it a tiny lantern with one slat open.  Then she blew out her lamp and sat a while in the dark, the coal in her chest banked low and nowhere near out.
