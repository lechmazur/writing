Nell Varrow kept the light on Sorrow Rock, but what she logged was the dark.  Anyone could count flashes; she counted the nine-second gaps between them, and what the sea did while unobserved.  The ferrymen called her cracked, and she let them, and went on asking her questions in the softly persistent way of water finding a seam. 

Last evening, at nautical twilight, when the horizon was still a drawn line and the first stars stood out for navigators, a stranger had stepped off the mail boat onto the Opal Dock.  She had watched from the gallery, alone above him as he was alone below.  In one flash he stood at the last bollard with something pale at his lapel.  In the next the lapel was bare, the bollard wore the pale thing, and he was straightening as if from a bow.  Then he sat on the bench, and the mist came in and closed the account. 

At dawn the ferrymen found him there, dead, with no name anywhere on him.  The constable said heart, and said the rest was nobody's business.  Nell thought a man's final gesture was exactly somebody's business, and that she was the only somebody who had seen it. 

So now, at nautical twilight again, she went down to investigate.  The dock had been tarred long ago with crushed abalone so boats could find it in fog, and in the mist its planks gave back the turning beam in greens and milky fire.  On the last bollard, pinned into a crack, hung a dried Edelweiss boutonniere bound in grey thread.  She did not touch it yet.  She looked instead at what was missing, which was her method with everything. 

There had been no return ticket in his pockets and no luggage on the boat.  The pinholes in his lapel, the constable admitted, were worn wide as buttonholes, the habit of decades.  And he had made his bow inside the dark interval, facing the tower, where no one watching the light would ever see it.  Either he wanted no witness, or he knew precisely which witness he had. 

The flower troubled her most, because she remembered wearing one.  She remembered a chapel door above the snowline, cold stone under her palm, edelweiss on her breast, and a path up which someone did not come.  Yet Nell had been born on this coast and had never climbed anything taller than her own stairs. 

That was the trouble with borrowed memories.  For forty years she had given stormbound sailors tea and a cot in exchange for one memory each, written into her ledgers under the lender's name, because a life spent on a rock makes few of its own.  She recited them at night for company, and the oldest had worn so smooth they felt like hers. 

She climbed back up and pulled the ledgers down, running her thumb not over the entries but over the lines for names.  Thirty-one winters back, she found the chapel door in her own handwriting, and above it a blank.  The gap gave him back to her: a young deckhand, grey with something worse than seasickness, who would not sign.  He had said, "Keep it for me, because I cannot carry it and work."  She had kept it so faithfully that she forgot it was a loan. 

Now the gesture read plainly.  A man who wants a thing back comes in the flash and knocks.  She must have told him, that long night, what it was she counted.  He had come in the gap, the one place he could be certain she was looking, to hand over the last piece and to thank the stranger who had carried his loneliness while he lived.  Then he had sat down to die where his memory was kept, in sight of the only person who could recognize him. 

Nell went down once more, unpinned the boutonniere, and fixed it in the ledger where his name should have been.  It filled the blank better than a name.  Then she stepped onto the gallery, waited for the beam to swing past, and in the nine seconds of dark she bowed toward the empty, glimmering dock.  No one saw it, which she finally understood was not the same as no one being there.
