Each night the guardian of the Sunken Library curled among the mildewed atlases, and each night sleep took it to the same place.  Through the veil of sleep lay the library's courtyard garden, frozen mid-bloom and drowned in mist.  The guardian knew it was dreaming, the way one knows a word on the tip of the tongue.  It had been a keeper once, with a name and a ledger; now it was claws, matted fur, and a growl for anything it could not catalog. 

Tonight the dream was different, because the guardian had fallen asleep with a stolen thing in its jaws.  That afternoon it had chased a hooded girl through the stacks and torn the pendant from her throat before she vanished among the shelves.  She belonged to the Unshadowed, cultists who preached a realm of endless noon where nothing was ever hidden.  Now the cultist's pendant burned between its teeth, a bead of white glass, and the guardian set it on the ice and stared. 

It knew that light.  It had been hatched in that noon, in a dimension where every object wore its own label and no corner kept a secret.  The first librarian had summoned it across to guard her books, then died, and it had been dimensionally homesick ever since, an ache that soured slowly into ferocity. 

The pendant lit a circle nine paces wide.  Inside it, every frost-furred rose had an edge, a count of thorns, a certainty.  Outside it, the mist began at once, like a page torn mid-sentence.  The guardian paced the boundary of the light, hackles up, as it had paced the library's doors for a hundred years. 

Break me, the glass seemed to hum, and noon will pour through and carry you home.  The guardian raised a paw, and the homesickness rose with it, vast as a missing floor. 

But it noticed what the noon would do.  Light without a boundary leaves no margin, and the library lived in its margins: in the unread, the misfiled, the question a reader carries in from the rain.  Endless noon would bleach every page to an answer nobody had asked for. 

The guardian lowered its paw and remembered, foggily, the first librarian's only lesson.  You cannot guard what stays outside you, she had said, feeding it a poem line by line; take the outside in, and it becomes a room you can live in.  It had interiorized ten thousand books that way, shelving their exteriors behind its ribs.  It had never once tried it with the dark. 

So the guardian, wanting for once to befriend what it could not know, stepped across the edge of the light.  Mist closed over its fur.  It breathed the mist in, cold and unlabeled, and instead of naming it, made a shelf for it and left the shelf blank.  Then it sat down among the frozen lilies to wait. 

Waiting was not pacing; waiting had no perimeter.  Shapes came and went in the gray, a fountain, a sundial, a branch creaking under ice, and the guardian let each one be maybe.  Its growl stayed coiled in its chest, unused, and slowly loosened. 

Then one shape did not go.  The hooded girl stood shivering a few paces off, barefoot on the ice, dreaming her way toward the light she had lost.  Thief or reader, enemy or pilgrim, the guardian could not tell.  A day ago, not telling would have meant teeth.  Now it found, with a lucidity that surprised it, that not knowing her was a kind of company. 

"I wanted to burn it all clear," she whispered, eyes on the pendant.  "I am so tired of being afraid of what I cannot see." 

The guardian, who had not spoken in a century, found a rusted voice.  "So was I," it said.  "Sit.  The mist does not bite, and tonight neither do I." 

It fetched the pendant, gently now, and hung it from a frozen pear branch between them.  Not a door, then; a lamp.  Nine paces of noon, and around it the generous dark where questions could still be asked.  The guardian looked at that small lit circle and understood that home had never needed to be endless.  It took the noon inside, the way it took in books, and gave it a shelf beside the blank one.  The ache did not leave, but it had edges now, and something soft beyond them. 

The veil thinned.  The guardian woke among the atlases with the pendant cool against its paw and, three shelves away, the sound of the girl breathing in her sleep.  It did not growl.  It did not know what she would be when she opened her eyes.  It tucked its claws beneath it and waited to find out.
