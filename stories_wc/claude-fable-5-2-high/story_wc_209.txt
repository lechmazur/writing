Ines Varga had painted forty walls in colors that shouted, and now she stood in the amber pavilion mixing a black.  October pressed through the honey-colored panes, and maple leaves ticked against the roof like slow rain.  On the workbench lay her mother's postcards, forty-one of them, every one a photograph of this same pavilion in autumn.  Her mother had etched and mended glass for the cathedral next door, always at night when the nave stood empty, and had tended the moonflowers that climbed the pavilion's posts.  After Ines left for the city, the cards arrived every autumn, yellowed stock from some forgotten gift shop, each bearing one line.  "The moonflowers opened again tonight, and I still do not know why."  Ines had answered few of them. 

The last card came in January, a week after the funeral, forwarded twice.  "I have put my guess in the Vesper window," it said, "and you will have to wait for the light." 

So she had come home in the last week of winter, to a cathedral banked in old snow.  The Vesper window stood low in the east chapel, a blue saint among lilies, its lower half buried outside by a drift.  By day it showed her nothing but the saint.  The verger, yawning, mentioned that her mother had always worked on it before sunrise, so Ines began arriving in the dark.  She told no one why.  She simply came, morning after morning, with the postcards in her coat and a pencil behind her ear. 

On the first morning, in the hush before the bells, gray light slid under the saint's colors and a second image surfaced.  Her mother had etched it into a sheet of clear glass leaded behind the painted one: the pavilion's roof, fine as frost.  Ines held a postcard to the pane, and the roofline matched exactly.  Then the sun cleared the rooftops, the saint blazed blue, and the etching drowned. 

The rest lay behind the drift, and there was no hurrying a thaw.  She learned to reckon it instead, marking the snowline in pencil on the stone each dawn, counting the minutes the hidden picture lasted.  The drift sank a hand's width, and the etching gave up the pavilion's posts, wound with vines whose flowers were shut.  Lower still, days later, a deeper sheet laid open blossoms over the shut ones, and moths came to them out of the dark.  Ines, who had never trusted a pale color, sat on the cold flagstones and looked until her eyes ached. 

Moths, then; any botanist could have told her mother that.  But the cards had gone on asking for years, and the snow had not finished. 

It finished on a morning of dripping gutters, when the last gray rind slid from the outer sill.  The hush that dawn was complete, the kind that comes before a thing is understood.  In the deepest sheet, at the foot of the window, a path ran from the cathedral door to a small house.  A woman walked it with a lantern.  In the house, one window was lit, and in it stood a child with her hands flat against the glass. 

Ines knew that window.  She had stood there every night until she was twelve, refusing sleep, waiting for the sound of the gate. 

Beneath the path her mother had scratched one line, small as a postmark.  "They open for whoever can only come in the dark." 

The sun rose, and the saint took the window back.  Ines stayed on the flagstones a long while, reckoning what forty-one cards and so few replies came to.  Her mother had never been asking about botany.  She had been marveling, year after year, that anyone had kept a window lit for her. 

She had asked the chapter for the pavilion's back wall and then left it bare all summer, waiting for the right season.  Now the amber light was failing, and she laid the black on in long, unhurried strokes, a whole night's worth.  Into it she set a path, a lantern, and one small square of yellow, the loudest color she allowed herself. 

She pinned the postcards along the workbench and wrote her answer beneath her mother's line on the last.  "I know why now; I was always at the window." 

Outside, dusk settled over the maples, and on the posts the year's last moonflower loosened, white against the dark.  Ines set down her brush and waited up with it.
