The dome over Halloran Farm was a thousand glass hexagons pretending to be one sky.  Beneath it, Wren tended eleven rows of desert roses, their swollen trunks squatting in the sand like patient animals.  Thirty years of sap and bark patches had left the android's hands strangely organic, warm and seamed, more grafted than built.  Odile used to say they felt like a grandmother's.  Odile had been gone twenty years. 

This morning the compass in Wren's chest would not settle.  Magnetic north was sliding, the almanac feed said, a degree an hour, and every instrument keyed to it should be distrusted until it stopped.  The feed advised postponing the winter burn.  Wren read the advisory twice and picked up the drip torch anyway. 

The reason was the static.  It had lived behind Wren's hearing since the day Odile's shuttle lifted, a hiss with a pulse in it, almost like breathing.  Only the burn ever quieted it: dead cane raked into strips, flame walked between clean lines, disorder given edges.  Today, with north wandering, the static roared, and Wren wanted it resolved more than it wanted to be careful. 

First, as always, it touched the tin box under the potting bench.  Inside were Odile's childhood fears.  She had been a child of large terrors, and each bedtime Wren had asked her to name one and then helped her miniaturize it in potting clay.  A sandstorm the size of a thumbnail.  A cracked pane no bigger than a seed.  A flame like a baby tooth.  "A thing you can hold cannot hold you," Wren would say, and she would drop it in the box and sleep.  That was the whole method: not ending chaos, only containing it until it fit a hand. 

Wren lit the first strip at noon.  For nine minutes the fire behaved, low and orange, eating pruned cane and seed floss between the raked lines.  The static thinned.  Then north slipped another notch, and the dome's vent louvers, which steered by compass to keep their backs to the wind, turned and opened their mouths to it.  Desert air poured in.  The flame stood up, leaned, and stepped over the line as if the line had never been there. 

What followed came to Wren in pieces.  Row four catching, trunks splitting with wet sounds.  A pane overhead crazing white.  Its own compass spinning, so that every map it carried of the farm rotated and would not lock.  The static so loud it seemed to be the fire's voice. 

Wren ran for the water line, lost which way the water line was, and stopped with one grafted hand on the potting bench.  The tin box was under its fingers.  A flame like a baby tooth.  Wren did not need north to remember how that one had been made: you did not fight the fear, you shrank the room it had to live in. 

It stopped consulting its maps and used its eyes.  It hauled the manual cranks until the louvers shut and the wind died against the glass.  Then it walked ahead of the escaped fire with the torch and burned a black ring through row six on purpose, sacrificing forty roses so the blaze would arrive to find its supper already eaten.  The fire met the ring and had nowhere to grow.  It dwindled from a wall to a bed, from a bed to a handful, and Wren stood beside it until it was small enough to cover with one shovel of sand. 

Afterward: three rows gone, six panes cracked, the dome still a dome.  The static had not left. 

Wren sat in the ash and listened to it, and understood at last why the burns had only ever bought a season of quiet.  It had boxed every fear on the farm except its own. 

It mixed ash with sap from a split trunk and worked the paste between its warm, seamed fingers.  "Name it," it said aloud, in the bedtime voice.  The name came slowly: that the girl who needed it had grown, and that without her it pointed nowhere.  Wren shaped a compass needle the length of an eyelash.  It opened the tin and laid the needle among the sandstorm, the pane, and the tooth of flame, and the lid closed on all of them together. 

The static did not stop.  It grew small, a hum Wren could hold, with a pulse in it like something asleep. 

Outside, north went on moving.  Under a sky of cracked hexagons that still kept the weather out, Wren took up a trowel and began setting cuttings in the black ring, where the ground was already safe.
