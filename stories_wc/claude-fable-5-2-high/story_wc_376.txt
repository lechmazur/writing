After the oasis dried, the greenhouse city of Verdance learned to speak in rations.  Its glass districts sprawled for miles under a sky the color of old bruises, poison pressing against every pane, and the pump square where people once lingered to talk was chained shut.  Ines Varr still had work, because she was a gilded-leaf calligrapher.  She lettered messages in gold onto living sunfig leaves, and a sunfig wilts near dishonesty, so a leaf that arrived upright proved its sender meant every word.  Lately customers ordered only short things: water owed, water paid. 

The last real question anyone had asked her came from her father, a postal engraver, on the evening the pumps coughed dry.  "Do you resent staying here with me?" he had said.  "No," she answered, and the sunfig on her bench folded like a closing hand.  He saw it, opened his mouth, and then the sirens called every able body to the basin.  The dust took his lungs within a month, and whatever he had meant to say went with him. 

As a child she had asked him where lost letters went, and he had laughed and said, "Same place as lost conversations."  Now she wanted that place the way the city wanted rain. 

She found the first clue while prying up his workshop floorboards to build seedling trays.  Beneath them lay a tin holding a zinc-etched postal plate fragment and a fistful of paper shreds, each strip inked in his careful hand.  One strip read TREASURE, which he had always called the mail. 

For nine nights she pieced the map together on her gilding table, matching torn fibers under the lamp the way she matched strokes of a broken letterform.  Glasshouse by glasshouse the city assembled itself: the orchid wards, the kelp tanks, the long fern arcade, and a dotted line running beneath them all.  The line ended at a hole where one corner had never been paper at all.  She set the zinc fragment into the gap, and its etched grooves carried the dotted line onward to half a postmark: DEAD LETT, BASIN SORTING.  She reread the whole map twice, then once more, because it led under the dried oasis itself. 

At dawn she potted a sunfig sprig, tucked it inside her coat, and followed the service tunnels below the fern arcade.  A warden stopped her at the basin gate and asked her business.  "Pipe inspection," she began, and felt the sprig sag against her ribs.  She breathed out.  "No, my father hid something down there, and I lied to him the last time we spoke, and I need to see it."  The warden studied her, then the small leaf lifting its head at her collar, and unlocked the gate.  "My wife and I haven't said a true thing since the water went," he muttered.  "Take your time." 

The sorting room lay under the cracked basin floor, dry as a kiln.  Racks of canvas sacks lined the walls, each stamped with the full postmark her fragment had been broken from.  So this was his treasure: when the post collapsed, he had not burned the undelivered mail as ordered, but carried it here and shredded the map so no warden could find it on him.  She opened a sack.  Apologies, recipes, a boy asking a girl whether she liked the kelp tanks at night: thousands of conversations stopped mid-breath. 

On top of the last sack sat an envelope addressed to Ines, bench by the window.  "Your leaf wilted," he had written.  "I was glad, because mine would have too if I had claimed I did not mind being the reason you stayed."  "Resent me a little, tell me so over supper, and we will be fine."  "The big speeches can wait; it is the small true things, skipped, that end up down here." 

She reread it until the lamp guttered, and the sprig at her collar stood straight the entire time.  "I did resent it," she told the dry room.  "A little, and I would stay again."  Then she shouldered the first sack. 

At the gate the warden asked if she had found what she needed.  "Yes, and there are forty more sacks, and I am too tired to carry them alone," she said.  He looked at the upright leaf, then fetched a cart. 

By evening they were knocking on doors through the orchid wards, under glass gone amber with the poisoned sunset, handing lost conversations back to the people who had to finish them.  They went nowhere, she decided; they only waited, like seeds in dry ground, for someone to say one small true thing.
