The rain quit just as the lamplighters started downhill, and the terrace above Carrow began to answer itself.  Every drip from the old dancing hall's eaves fell once and echoed twice across the flagstones, soft as April.  Along the terrace's edge ran the parapet of cut-glass prisms, still beaded with rain, laying bands of violet and ember across the wet stone. 

Sefa came up by the servants' stair, as she always did, so that no one could say for certain she had come.  In the desert towns they called her the mirage painter, and they did not say it kindly.  For years she had brushed water onto empty horizons for caravan masters who needed tired people to keep walking.  A mirage is a promise that backs away, and she had learned to back away with it, gone before anyone reached the sand where the lake should be. 

She was finished with lies.  Seven tall mirrors leaned against the hall's outer wall, left from the years of dancing, and tonight she meant to persuade them to show something better than a lie and kinder than a fact: what might yet be. 

Mirrors are stubborn.  They cannot be commanded, only sung to, and the song had been hiding in plain hearing.  Her grandmother had silvered glass in this town, and had rocked her to sleep with a rhyme the lane children still skipped rope to: seven for the sparrows, three for the well, hush for the hour before the bell.  Sefa had been thirty before she understood it was a recipe.  Seven mirrors, three colors drawn from wet glass, and the one quiet in the day when Carrow's ringers climb their towers and the whole town holds its breath. 

That hush had begun. 

She touched her brush to a violet band on the parapet, lifted the color as if it were paint, and sang.  The first mirror fogged like a window breathed on.  She did not curse; she stood and listened, and under her own voice she heard the fault.  Her pulse ran half a beat ahead of the tune, the pulse of a woman already leaving, and the terrace echoed that hurry back at her. 

"You sang it wrong," said a small voice from the servants' stair.  It was Pim, the bell-ringer's daughter, sent home while her father climbed, and too curious to go.  "There's another verse: lend me your drum and I'll lend you my eyes, slow for the sleeper, slow for the skies." 

Sefa looked at the child the way she looked at weather, calmly, noticing everything: the muddy knees, the chin held high over fear, the half-whittled walking stick under one arm.  "Who is the stick for?" she asked. 

"My brother Joss, since the fever took his legs," Pim said.  "Everybody tells him what is, and nobody will say what could be." 

A drum, then; the song wanted a heart that still kept time with tomorrow.  "May I borrow your heartbeat, only until the bells?" Sefa asked. 

Pim held out her wrist.  Sefa laid two fingers there and found it, quick but unhurried, a rhythm with nowhere else it wished to be.  She let her breathing settle onto it, and together they sang the rhyme from sparrows to skies while the dusk went plum over the roofs. 

This time she did not paint the glass.  She graced it, one stroke of violet, ember, or green at the edge of each frame, light as a hand on a sleeper's shoulder. 

The mirrors woke.  In one, a boy crossed a kitchen on two sticks, scowling with effort; in the next he sat high in the bell tower, legs still, hauling a rope with arms like a blacksmith's.  Pim's pulse leapt under Sefa's fingers. 

"They are not promises," Sefa said quietly, because she had noticed the hope before it could harden into certainty.  "They are doors, and somebody still has to do the walking." 

Pim nodded, and her heart steadied. 

Then Sefa saw the seventh mirror.  It held this terrace in plain morning, and a grey-haired woman who had not gone anywhere, showing a ring of children how to lift color from wet glass. 

The bells of Carrow broke the hush, tower after tower, and the echoes folded the visions back into ordinary silver.  Sefa let go of the small wrist, the loan repaid.  Her own heart went on in the borrowed tempo, slow for the sleeper, slow for the skies.  On the parapet the colors were drying to plain glass, but there would be other rains. 

"Show me where the lane children skip," she said, and took the front steps down with Pim, in full view of the town, while the bells and their echoes settled into one long chord.
