After the spirit left Professor Hadley's body, the hovering fruit of the arbor sank a finger's width, and Ines Okoro wrote the time down.  She was a cuneiform translator, not a physicist, but she was kindly methodical, and noting things was how she kept from crying.  Then she folded his blanket, closed his eyes, and thanked each instrument aloud as she copied its final reading. 

The magnetic arbor stood on a steel terrace above the clouds, forty iron-barked trees rooted in lodestone, their pale fruit held near the stems by field instead of fiber.  Nothing here ever quite touched.  Hadley had built the place to catch one instant: the moment a ripe fruit stops belonging to its tree.  He called it the physics of transition, and he had chased it for thirty years without a single clean measurement.  Now his own transition had passed through every sensor on the terrace and left no trace at all.  Only the fruit had noticed. 

By the third morning the sag was worse, and Ines understood that the field had depended on his daily tuning, on a hand that knew it.  If she could not reestablish it, the fruit would drop through the clouds and his life's question would fall with it.  The question was hers now, too.  She had sat at touching distance from his hand at the end, forbidden by his own rule to disturb the readings, and she needed to know what had crossed that small gap, or failed to.  His ambition haunted the terrace like a draft: half-finished equations on the glass, a kettle still set for two, his pencil hovering in its magnetic cradle. 

He had hired her for the one thing he could not do himself.  In a padded case lay a clay tablet from Nippur and, beside it, a black seed the size of a thumbnail, heavy as iron, found sealed inside the tablet's envelope.  The tablet named it the seed of the fruit of knowing, and every tree in the arbor had been grown from its sisters.  This last one, Hadley believed, was the keystone: set correctly at the arbor's heart, it would hold the field steady with no hand to tend it.  But the line of instruction was chipped, and the surviving signs said only that the seed must be placed "in the" something "of the earth."  Hadley had guessed "belly" and buried the sisters, which grew into trees that needed him every day. 

Ines worked as she always did, gently and in order.  She numbered each chip, apologized to the tablet before turning it, and tested forty candidate signs against the broken edge.  None fit, and outside the glass another fruit dipped toward the cloud. 

On the fifth night, to rest her eyes, she pressed a reed stylus into practice clay the way her first teacher had taught her.  She lifted the reed and looked at the wedge it left.  A cuneiform sign, she thought, is not a thing added to clay; it is a gap the clay keeps after the hand has gone.  The comparison opened like a door.  If writing was a kept gap, and the arbor's fruit lived in a kept gap, then perhaps the scribe had meant a gap as well.  She laid the sign for "between" against the chipped edge, and every surviving stroke agreed.  Not in the belly of the earth, but in the between of the earth: the seed was never meant to be buried. 

At dawn she carried it to the arbor's heart, where two great lodestones faced each other across a hand's breadth of air.  Hadley had stood here every morning with his palm in that space, tuning by feel.  Ines opened her fingers, and the seed did not fall.  It hung between the stones, turning slowly, and a hum she had not heard since his death rose through the iron bark.  Tree by tree, the fruit lifted back to its place, a finger's width from every stem. 

She wrote the time down.  Then she wrote, in his notebook, under his last unfinished equation, the only finding she trusted.  Perhaps transition was not a crossing at all, and that was why no sensor caught it.  Perhaps it was a distance that goes on being held after the hand that set it has gone, the way a wedge holds the shape of a reed.  It was not proof, and she labeled it honestly as a hypothesis, with tomorrow's measurements already listed beneath. 

The ambition would go on haunting her; she found she did not mind the company.  Before she left, she held her palm beside the floating seed, close enough to feel the field press back, and did not touch it.  She set the kettle for two.
