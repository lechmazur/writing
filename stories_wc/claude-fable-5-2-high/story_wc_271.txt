The lighthouse keeper left the arboretum his logbooks and nothing else.  Forty years of them, stacked in the propagation shed where Samira worked, on a cliff that the sea was taking back a foot at a time.  After he retired, the last of his kind on that coast, the trust automated the lamp and sent a drone every Thursday to survey the edge he used to walk.  On Thursdays Samira stayed in the shed. 

She had come to the arboretum six years after her sister's wedding, where the sky had hummed and then opened.  She survived with a burst eardrum and a photograph in her fist, an instant print the photographer had handed her seconds before: Laleh laughing under a pomegranate tree.  Her hand had closed on it and never fully let go.  The crumpled photograph lived in her apron pocket, its creases white as scars. 

People told her she was safe now.  She distrusted the sentence, not because it was unkind but because nobody saying it had checked.  She wanted a hope that had done its arithmetic. 

Her grafts were the nearest thing she had.  She was quietly talented at them; she never said so, but Ines, the director, had noticed that the labels in Samira's small square handwriting were the ones still attached to living wood in spring.  Samira could not have explained the method.  Her thumbs warmed when cambium met cambium, and she bound the union there. 

It was the keeper's logs that showed her this was a kind of knowledge.  Reading them one wet Thursday, she found he had forecast through his body: left knee tight, fog by noon; teeth aching, gale from the southwest.  Beside each guess he had written what the weather actually did.  He was right often, wrong sometimes, and he recorded both in the same level hand. 

So Samira began her own column in his last unfinished book.  Thursday, 10:40, hum overhead: wrists cold, jaw locked, breath gone.  Then, underneath, what actually happened: nothing fell.  She also wrote down the one signal she remembered from the wedding itself, the sick drop in her stomach when the doves on the courtyard wall went silent, a full breath before the sound.  That marker had been true.  The cold wrists, forty Thursdays running, had not. 

The entries did not cure anything.  But they accumulated, layer over layer around the same hard grain, the way an oyster answers grit. 

In March, Ines asked her to site the new orchard terrace.  "Nobody else's trees live," Ines said, "so you choose where they stand." 

Samira chose a pomegranate first, a salt-tolerant nursery cultivar, not Laleh's tree and never going to be.  She took the keeper's erosion figures, a foot a year in bad decades, and paced ninety feet back from the edge.  Ninety years, at worst, which was a long life for a pomegranate. 

She was kneeling at the hole, root ball in both hands, when the hum came.  It was Tuesday.  A storm had chewed the path, and the trust had sent the drone early.  It came low over the lighthouse, over the wind-bent pines, a gray seed against gray cloud. 

Samira froze.  Wrists cold, jaw locked, the seedling shaking in hands that would not open.  There was no shed to hide in, only the freeze, and for the first time she looked around inside it.  At its center, where there had always been white noise, she found something small and round and quiet, a pearl of stillness built from forty plain entries.  From there she could read herself the way the keeper read his knee. 

Wrists: cold, the unreliable signal.  Stomach: level.  And in the pines behind her the finches were still arguing, loudly, about nothing.  The true marker was absent. 

She did not tell herself she was safe.  She told herself what the evidence supported: this machine was counting inches of cliff, and she had a tree to plant.  The stillness was hers now, held rather than suffered, and she kept it until the drone finished its line and turned out over the water.  Then her thumbs warmed, and she set the root ball down and firmed the soil. 

That evening she opened the logbook.  Tuesday, 9:15, hum overhead: wrists cold, stomach level, birds loud.  Stayed.  Planted one pomegranate, ninety feet back. 

She took the photograph from her apron and pressed it flat against the page.  The creases stayed, crossing Laleh's shoulder and the trunk of the tree, and Samira did not pretend otherwise.  She taped it in beside the entry, creases and all, where the keeper would have put the weather.  Outside, the automated lamp began its turning, and ninety feet from the edge a small tree stood in the dark with ninety years to use.
