The doctor on the mainland gave Ilse Varga six months, and she thanked him as if he had quoted a ferry schedule.  Then she sailed home to Carrow Island, where she had been caretaker for thirty-one years, and went straight to the temple orchard.  The orchard stood within a ring of living stone pillars, gray lithic creatures that grew a finger's width each decade and breathed slowly through pores the size of poppy seeds.  Between them grew pears, figs, and nine old oaks, and above them all the shearwaters nested in the vaults where stone met stone.  Pilgrims called it a temple.  Ilse had always called it a household. 

Lately the household was losing an argument.  The council wanted wider doors for the summer crowds, and last year its masons had chiseled the east arch square, leaving the pillars there weeping a pale resin that still had not dried.  Now a surveyor named Tobin was coming with plans for mortar, railings, and a paved nave. 

Ilse had six months to answer him, so she began where she began everything, with the botanical diary.  It was a salt-stiff ledger handed down through eleven caretakers, each recording blossom dates, graft unions, and the leaning of pillars measured in hairs.  She had read her own entries for decades but never the first caretaker's, which were written in a cramped hand and an older dialect.  Being gently systematic by nature, she translated one page each evening, no more, with a pear and a cup of tea. 

On the ninth evening the diary gave up its architectural secrets.  Nobody had built the temple.  The founders had wrapped copper wire around oak branches, and the pillars, which tasted metal the way roots taste water, had leaned toward the wire year by year until stone followed wood into arches.  Every vault was a conversation: the oak proposed a curve, the stone agreed slowly, and the birds moved in when the two had finished.  The low doors were deliberate too, the first caretaker wrote, so that people would enter bowing beneath a living limb and remember they were guests. 

Ilse closed the ledger, finished her pear, and decided to remodel the temple herself. 

Tobin arrived on the spring ferry with a brass level and a roll of drawings.  He found her on a ladder in the east arch, winding copper wire around an oak branch in even turns, a thumb's width apart. 

"The council sent me to remodel this place," he said. 

"You are late," Ilse said, not looking down.  "I started in winter." 

He unrolled his plans, and she climbed down, studied them politely, and handed them back. 

"Mortar suffocates the pillars, and your nave ceiling evicts four hundred shearwaters," she said.  "Mine bends this limb across the wound your masons cut, and the stone will follow the copper down until the door is low and round again." 

"How long will that take?" 

"About ninety years." 

Tobin laughed, then saw that she had not.  "The council says you are dying," he said, more gently. 

"By autumn," she agreed, as calmly as she might confirm a tide.  "That is why I am not asking permission.  I was never going to see the end of this work, so six months changes nothing except my manners." 

She showed him the diary instead of arguing.  He read the first caretaker's pages in her translation, then her own from that winter: one branch per day, each numbered, each turn of wire counted, each pillar's lean recorded at dawn.  She had drawn the finished temple as well, with a high nave left to the birds, fruiting aisles left to the bees, and one stooping door for people. 

"Why make us stoop?" Tobin asked. 

"Because respect between species has to be built into the walls, or each generation forgets it," Ilse said.  "Yours forgot in one summer." 

He stayed a week to check her measurements with his brass level, expecting fancy.  On the sixth morning the bubble confirmed what her ledger claimed: since winter the east pillar had leaned one hair toward the wired oak.  Tobin sent his drawings back to the council on the ferry and did not go with them. 

Through summer Ilse taught him the wire, the pruning, and the patience, working fewer branches as her hands thinned.  In autumn she wrote her last entry beneath the shearwaters, noted the fig harvest, and signed the diary over to the twelfth caretaker.  Then she walked out through the east arch, bowing under the copper-bright limb, unhurried, as a guest leaves a house she has loved.
