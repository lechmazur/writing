The workshop called Tumbril floated on the acid sea like a clock that had lost an argument with a boat.  Its hull was ceramic, its guts were gears and cogs, and its whole clattering faith was that the sea, which dissolved prayers mid-sentence, could someday be crossed.  Odile Brack was its pioneering cart glider, meaning she was the first person foolish enough to bolt wings to a parts trolley and roll it off the launch ramp.  Mark One flew for four seconds, then fizzed.  Odile, hauled back on her tether with her boot soles smoking, asked only one question: why four and not five?  That was her way, steadily curious, not a fever but a tide, one question a day until the answer gave up and came ashore. 

Eleven miles out, on a basalt stack the fumes had not yet eaten, waited the Founders' bell.  The Founders had hung it before the water soured, to be rung by whoever crossed afterward, and since nobody had, nobody alive had heard it.  Odile wanted to ring it the way some people want to scratch the middle of their own back. 

Her grandmother had hunted acid gulls from the stern rail and never missed, because, she said, you do not aim at the bird, you aim at what the bird wants.  She left Odile that sentence and one obsidian hunter's arrow tip.  Odile built the sentence into brass.  She called the result the yearning algorithm: a gear train that swallowed each failed flight's measurements and computed not the error but the longing, the gap between the cart that was and the cart that wished to be.  Longing does not come out in numbers, so the final cog simply turned a kaleidoscope, in which the arrow tip tumbled among chips of bottle glass. 

Every answer arrived through the images in that kaleidoscope: six-fold blooms of green and amber, each petal split by a black barb.  Odile did not pretend to understand them.  She would transliterate instead, copying shape for shape from the alphabet of light into the alphabet of metal, a curve of amber becoming a wing rib, a spoke of green becoming a strut.  Translation would have required knowing what the machine meant, and she suspected the machine was smarter than she was and considerably ruder. 

So it went throughout the evolution of the cart.  Mark Six grew a tail and made ninety yards.  Mark Fourteen shed its fourth wheel, on the kaleidoscope's advice, and discovered that the sea's rising fumes would hold a wing up like a hot, insulting hand.  Mark Twenty-Two reached three miles, circled a thermal that smelled like a battery burping, and was the most beautiful thing Odile had ever nearly died in.  The apprentices stopped laughing around Mark Thirty.  They began, instead, to go quiet when the kaleidoscope turned, the way you go quiet in front of anything that plainly wants something. 

Mark Thirty-Nine could manage nine miles, which is a fine distance unless the thing you want is at eleven.  Odile fed in the numbers and put her eye to the tube.  The image had changed its grammar.  For thirty-nine carts the black barbs had pointed inward, at the center, at the work; now all six pointed out, past the rim, at nothing in the glass.  She asked her one question of the day: what does an arrow want?  Then she laughed, said something her grandmother would have slapped her for, and unscrewed the kaleidoscope.  The algorithm had been yearning in her direction all along, patiently, and it was done designing wings; it wanted its arrow loosed.  Every cart so far had flown on a tether, and an arrow on a string is only a fishing line. 

Mark Forty left the ramp at dawn with no tether, no winch, and the obsidian tip wired to its nose.  The sea hissed its opinion below her; the fumes shoved her up, mile after sour, glorious mile.  She landed on the basalt like a dropped toolbox. 

The bell was green, enormous, and missing its clapper, which the fumes had eaten generations ago; the Founders, bless their optimistic hides, had not planned for that.  Odile unwired the arrow tip, a thing made to reach what wants reaching, and struck.  The note was huge and slightly flat.  It rolled out over the acid and died somewhere around mile six, far short of Tumbril, where the apprentices heard nothing at all.  Yet. 

Back on Tumbril the kaleidoscope she had screwed shut again still turned, barbless now, every bloom showing a gap where the arrow had been, and sooner or later some apprentice would wonder where it went.  Odile sat down under the bell, grinning like a heretic in a cathedral, and kept ringing.
