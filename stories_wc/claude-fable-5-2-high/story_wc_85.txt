Tessaly Voss sold ancestors to people who had none.  In the back rooms of the harbor district she forged baptismal ledgers, aged photographs in tea, and, for clients who paid extra, did the thing no other black market genealogist could: she manufactured memories.  She would sit with an orphan for weeks, repeating a grandfather's cough, the smell of his workshop, the tune he whistled, until the client's mind took the story in and swore it had always lived there. 

Eleven years ago she had done this for Maren Aldous.  She gave Maren a watchmaker from Lisbon, a lap to sit in, a ticking lullaby, and one real object to anchor the lie: a slender blued watch hand, supposedly saved from his last unfinished watch. 

What she did not give Maren was the truth she had found while researching the forgery.  Maren had been born in Skerry Hollow, a fishing settlement with no church and no paper, where families cut their lineages into driftwood and left the planks above the tide line.  A living family meant no sale, so Tessaly had closed the file and told herself the shore was surely empty. 

Now Maren had written.  The watchmaker is wearing thin, the letter said; I dream of salt instead of oil, and I need you to come and quell it. 

Tessaly could have done it in an afternoon.  Instead she took a bus to where the road ended and walked north, because after eleven years of poor sleep she wanted to know exactly how much the thing she had kept back weighed. 

Skerry Hollow was gone from every map.  The huts had fallen in, but the planks remained, hundreds of them, heaped silver along the forgotten shore like the ribs of something patient.  For three days she sifted through the driftwood carvings, brushing sand from knife-cut names, reading births and drownings with her fingertips.  Each wave drew back through the shingle and left a sieved hush behind it, a quiet with everything loud strained out, and in that quiet she could not pretend she was only doing research. 

On the third evening she found it.  A gray plank, long as her forearm, bore six generations of names, and at the bottom, in a mother's shallow strokes: Maren, taken inland with the fever cart.  Beneath the name ran a row of notches, one for each year of waiting.  Tessaly counted twenty-three before the wood went smooth.  Maren had been twenty when she came to the back room, which meant the last three notches were cut after Tessaly closed the file.  She sat down in the shingle and held the plank until the light was gone. 

Maren received her on the terrace behind her house, a ledge of cobalt tiles above the harbor, at the hour when dusk turned the glaze nearly black.  The watch hand lay on the table between two cups of tea, and for a long while they sat under the weight of unspoken words. 

"Make him solid again," Maren said at last.  "I can almost hear the ticking, and then it turns into waves." 

Tessaly unwrapped the plank and laid it beside the watch hand.  "There was no watchmaker," she said.  "I built him, and I am good at building, but some part of you has been sanding him down because it remembers a shore." 

She told the rest plainly: the fever cart, the six generations, the file she had closed for the sake of a fee.  Then she guided Maren's thumb along the notches, counting aloud, and after twenty she said, "The last three she cut while I knew where she was." 

Maren lifted the watch hand in one palm and the plank in the other, as if her arms were a scale.  The steel weighed nothing.  The wood pulled her wrist down toward the tiles, and Tessaly, watching, finally had her measurement: three years a mother need not have waited alone, and eleven a daughter had spent mourning a man made of words. 

"I could make you forget this evening," Tessaly said.  "That is the quelling you sent for, but I would rather you hated me accurately." 

Maren said nothing for a time.  Below them the harbor emptied of sound, the same sieved hush as the shore, and Tessaly understood it was not peace, only what remained when the lies were strained away. 

"I don't forgive you," Maren said.  "But you will take me there, and you will read me every name." 

She laid the watch hand in the smooth space after the twenty-third notch, a thin blue tally for the years counted wrong.  Tessaly nodded, and for once invented nothing to say.
