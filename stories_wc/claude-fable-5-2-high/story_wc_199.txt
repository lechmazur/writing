Pim Underleaf sorted seeds the way other people said prayers: slowly, in rows, and with her tongue between her teeth.  Her sorting tray had ninety compartments, and eighty-nine of them were full, labeled, and deeply satisfying.  The ninetieth had been empty since before her grandmother, and it itched at her like a pebble in a slipper. 

She worked in the Cinder Arbor, a grove of ash-gray trees that rode the high winds on a raft of knotted roots, trailing moss like a green beard over the clouds.  The bark was pale as cold hearth ash, the leaves were paler, and everything soft underfoot was moss, which forgave all footsteps. 

Every sky-village along the Updraft told the same tale about the place.  Once a year, when the old wind died and the new wind had not yet drawn breath, there came the hush where past meets prophecy.  In that stillness the arbor let go of its leaves, and the leaves, said the tale, were a message about which seeds to plant for the year ahead.  The tale also said, in every telling, that whatever passes through the hush emerges transformed, always. 

Nobody had read the leaves in three generations.  The old Readers had used a moonstone cabochon, round as a dewdrop, but it had gone through so many hushes that it came out smaller each time: stone, then pebble, then grain, then rumor.  So the planters guessed, the arbor thinned a little every spring, and Pim, who ached to decode those leaves, kept sorting, because sorting was all she knew how to do about it. 

On the eve of the hush, she found a fleck of blue light in compartment ninety.  It was a speck of dust, no bigger than a poppy seed's shadow, and it glowed like milk with the moon in it.  Pim knew what it was: the last of the Readers' cabochon, moonstone dust, come to rest in the one box she had never been able to fill. 

She prodded it with her sorting quill.  It rolled a hair's width and dimmed to grit.  She prodded it the other way, toward the roots of the nearest tree, and it flared so brightly she laughed out loud. 

So Pim did a thing no orderly person should: she carried her tray outside, knelt in the moss, and followed a speck of dust across the arbor, one prod at a time.  It burned brightest at a hollow where, she slowly recalled, a rain-bell had once hung.  Her grandmother's rhyme came back to her, the one children skipped to: leaves fall for the forgotten, so give the forgotten somewhere to land. 

She built a monument there, three acorn caps stacked into a bell no taller than her thumb.  The speck led on, and she built more: a twig arch for the dried well, a husk cairn for the moss-wren nobody had heard sing, a thimble of lichen for the lost verse of a lullaby.  By dusk the moss was dotted with a tiny, crooked city of forgotten things, and Pim, who liked rows, found she did not mind. 

The last flare came at a bare patch between two roots, and she could not remember what belonged there.  Nobody could; that was the point of compartment ninety.  So she built a monument to not knowing, a ring of eighty-nine seeds, one from each full box, with a gap for the ninetieth, and she set the moonstone speck in the gap. 

Then the wind died.  The hush came down like a held breath, the bells of the past gone quiet behind it and the year to come waiting on the other side. 

The leaves let go.  They did not scatter; they fell straight as plumb lines, pale and deliberate, and each one settled on a monument like a hand on a child's head.  Pim finally understood why no one could read them: the leaves were never letters, they were answers, and for three generations nobody had asked the question. 

Where each ashen leaf touched down, it curled, greened, and tightened into a seed.  Bellflower at the acorn bell, wellcress under the arch, wren's-millet on the cairn: the arbor was handing back everything it had been waiting for someone to miss.  In the ring of seeds, the moonstone speck had become one too, small and blue-white, of a kind she had never sorted. 

The tale was right, as mossy old tales tend to be: stone, leaf, and sorter had all come through the hush changed.  Pim gathered the new seeds, wrote READER'S MOON on the ninetieth label, and then, grinning, pressed that seed into the moss instead of the box.  Some things, she decided, were better planted than sorted.
