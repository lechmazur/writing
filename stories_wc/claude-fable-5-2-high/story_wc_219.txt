Three springs after the visitors made first contact and left again, Ione Vasska climbed toward the tower in the rain.  Her hands gave off a faint light, the color of a lamp seen through honey.  Every chemist who had handled the visitors' dust carried that glow under the skin.  Only Ione had been dismissed from the Academy over it.  Her paper had argued that the visitors' single walk on Earth, from their landing field to this abandoned foundry tower, was not a blessing but a reaction.  The Concord Office preferred the blessing.  It had paved a Pilgrimage of Arrival on the far side of the hill, with plaster footprints, recorded chimes, and a fee at the gate.  When the Academy asked her to certify that artificial pilgrimage as authentic, she had refused in a lecture hall, loudly, with slides. 

So she had come to the real route, the old sculpture path that nobody tended.  Bramble and wet fern had closed over the gravel, and the bronze figures stood in it up to their knees, green at the shoulders, saying nothing.  The rain made a low sound on them, like a bell remembered rather than rung. 

The footprints were still there.  The visitors' dust shed water the way a duck's feather does, so three years of storms had only rinsed the prints clean.  They lay pale and dry among the weeds, each one beaded with rain that would not soak in.  Ione knelt, measured the stride, and wrote the number down.  This was her own pilgrimage, and she had built it as artificially as the Concord built theirs: one walker, one sample, no belief permitted. 

The sample hung at her throat.  It was her mother's pendant, a thumb of amber with a fly inside, wings half open, caught forty million years short of wherever it was going.  On contact day a visitor had touched it once, and the dust left there had spent three years creeping to one edge, always the edge that faced this hill.  Amber holds a charge and will not absorb what lands on it, which made the pendant the only honest instrument she owned. 

She wanted to understand the chemistry of homecoming, and she wanted it without incense.  Salmon did it with dissolved minerals, pigeons with iron; she saw no reason the visitors should be granted a soul for it.  She had not been home herself since the funeral, which she considered irrelevant data. 

The prints did not climb straight.  They wandered from bronze to bronze, circling a seated woman, a horse, a child holding a bowl now full of rain.  At each statue the dust lay thicker, pressed into the base like a palm.  Ione mapped every detour, and by the ninth statue her sketch looked less like a walk than like a river system drawn backward.  Many trails, she realized, not one.  The visitors had separated at the gate, each gone to a different figure, and then every trail bent uphill to converge on the foundry tower where all nine bronzes had been poured.  They had not been visiting statues.  They had been walking the metal home. 

Inside, the tower was a tall cold throat of brick, washed by rain through the broken roof.  Around the old casting pit the footprints met in a single pale ring, and the ring was brighter than any dust she had ever catalogued.  Her hands brightened to match it.  She noted the time, the distance, and the fact that her pulse had no business in the notebook. 

Then she lifted the pendant over the pit.  The dust on the amber slid to the lowest point, gathered, and let go, falling as a slow gold thread into the ring.  So that was the mechanism: no map, no memory, only affinity, like leaning toward like, a thing drawn to what it had been made from. 

She had been right, and the Academy had been wrong, and she waited for that to feel like arriving.  It did not.  What she felt instead was the amber in her fist, clear now, and the fly inside it, still unfinished, and a pull that no ring of dust accounted for.  A measured thing, she admitted, was not a lesser thing.  Even a pilgrimage built on purpose, with a notebook and a hypothesis, had walked her somewhere. 

She wrote one last line, affinity confirmed, and under it her mother's address, which she had not needed to look up.  Then she went down the path between the quiet bronzes, her own wet footprints crossing the dry ones, her hands dimming slowly in the rain.
