Three months after the jet stream shifted south and stayed there, Maren Voss stepped off the supply boat onto Halloway Skerry, and the ground took her boot like wet bread.  Everything on the island had gone soft since the sky changed its mind: the peat, the jetty planks, the air itself, which pressed against her face like a damp palm.  The experimental weather station squatted above the landing, its copper wind-harps furred green, its walls sweating.  Her hands shook on the satchel strap, but her voice, when she greeted the keeper, did not.  "I am the map librarian from the Meridian Archive," she said, "and I have come to reverse an exile."  Old Pell looked at her the way one looks at a rising tide. 

A century earlier the Weather Board had banished her great-great-grandmother, Ingrid Voss, to this rock for forecasting exactly this drowned and sagging sky.  The sentence carried a clause the Board thought was a joke: the exile would be reversed if her forecast ever proved true.  Instead, the official record said Ingrid abandoned her lamp during the gale of 1931, and that the packet Corriedale broke on the skerry because of it.  Maren had never believed it, because she believed maps were promises and Ingrid had drawn too many true ones. 

The clue came out of the Corriedale itself: Ingrid's journals, salvaged from the wreck and shelved in the Archive's chart room as water-ruined doggerel.  Maren spent two winters with those swollen pages before she saw that the syllables of each stanza counted out pressures and bearings, and that each stanza ended in a question.  Decoded, the verses said Ingrid had held the lamp all night, had recorded the gale on the station's sealed spools, and had locked the recording engine with a copper pin only she carried. 

Then the jet stream shifted, the rains came, and the flooded churchyard at home gave Ingrid's coffin back to the daylight.  Helping to rebox the bones, Maren found one lid nail unlike the iron others: copper, green, and notched like a key.  Someone had buried the proof with the woman it would have saved.  The Board, embarrassed by the weather, let Maren sail on Ingrid's own terms: come back with evidence, or stay on as keeper. 

Pell led her inside, where the floorboards gave underfoot and the dark smelled of wet wool.  The recording engine filled the back room, a brass loom of dials under a skin of condensation, with one small socket at its heart.  "It has not spoken since my grandfather's day," Pell said.  Maren slid the coffin nail into the socket, and it seated with a click she felt in her teeth. 

She read the stanzas aloud and set each dial to its answer: where the wind stood, how low the glass fell, what hour the lamp was lit.  Six dials turned easily.  The seventh stanza was pulp, all but its last line, which asked who had gone down to the boat.  The seventh dial was ringed with letters instead of numbers, and the room seemed to lean in, soaking up her breath.  Maren's stomach dropped, yet she turned to Pell as if she already knew. 

"Who signed the abandonment report?" she asked. 

"That was long ago." 

"Who was her assistant that night, and why has his family kept this rock for three generations when nobody wants it?" 

Pell's face sagged like the sky.  "Tobias Pell," he said at last.  "He took the boat, she took the lamp, and he wrote the report before she could, then drove that pin into her coffin himself so the engine would keep quiet."  "We stayed because someone had to be sorry here." 

Maren turned the seventh dial to T, then to P.  The engine shuddered, exhaled a century of damp, and began to pay out its spool: a pressure trace falling off a cliff, a lamp log ticked every quarter hour until dawn, two ships noted turning safely away.  In the margin, in Ingrid's hand, ran her forecast of the shifting jet stream, dated a full century early. 

Maren carried the spool to the radio room, her knees uncertain, her finger steady on the key.  She did not plead with the Board; she questioned it.  "By your own clause, the forecast has proved true and the lamp was kept: is Ingrid Voss's exile reversed?" 

Static hissed for a long, sodden minute while the rain leaned on the roof and the walls drank the sound.  "Reversed," the Board answered, "and bring her record home." 

Maren left the copper nail in the engine, where it had always been a key, and offered Pell a seat on the boat.  Outside, the sky still hung swollen and low, exactly as Ingrid had promised.
