Midnight struck eleven chimes over Pellam's Hollow, drew breath for the twelfth, and kept it.  Every clock in town hung with its pendulum tilted, and the dawn that should have followed stayed folded somewhere under the cobbles. 

Tobiah Quill, music box repairman, knew exactly where.  Beneath the square lay the resonance chamber, a copper-ribbed cavern shaped like the inside of a bell, and in it sat the Dawnbox his grandmother Odile had built to wind each morning up out of the dark. 

He went down the spiral stair with his apprentice Wren, a candle stub, and a tool roll that chimed like a pocketful of small bells.  In the chamber, every sound came back larger and truer than it left.  Wren's sneeze returned as a flock of silver pings. 

The Dawnbox stood tall as a haycart, its great pinned cylinder stopped mid-turn, its balance wheel leaning at the top of a swing it could not finish.  Behind its glass heart, a thimbleful of daylight fluttered like a moth in a jar: tomorrow, held hostage by midnight. 

"Everything hangs in the balance," Wren whispered, and the chamber agreed with her eleven times over. 

Tobiah listened along the comb, tooth by tooth.  Nothing was cracked, nothing bent, nothing torn.  The comb shone a flawless, buttery gold. 

"It was steel when I started with you," Wren said. 

"I improved it last spring," Tobiah said. 

The chamber rang his words back, sour as a dropped spoon, and his knees went soft.  That was the trouble with ears tuned to true pitch: Tobiah had always wilted near dishonesty, drooping like a cut tulip whenever a lie was told in his hearing.  He had been wilting for months and blaming his age. 

From his waistcoat he drew a twist of velvet, and from the velvet a chip from the philosopher's stone, red as a robin's breast and no bigger than a lentil.  Odile had left it to him with one instruction: ask it only for what you could say aloud down here. 

"My hands ached," he told Wren, and this time the echo came back clean.  "The stair got longer every month, so I touched the stone to the comb and called it a repair.  Gold never rusts, never needs oiling, never needs me." 

Wren raised the candle to the brass lid, where Odile's engraving curled between stamped forget-me-nots.  "A covenant of rust," she read.  "The box shall be allowed to tarnish, and the Hollow shall be obliged to return." 

Tobiah had read it a thousand times as decoration.  Now he heard the bargain in it: the Dawnbox gave the town its mornings, and the town gave the Dawnbox its hands, its oil, its company on the stair.  Rust was the invitation.  He had perfected the invitation away, and midnight, finding the debt unpaid, had simply kept the light. 

"So we fix it," Wren said.  "But what's broken?" 

"Nothing," said Tobiah, unrolling his tools.  "That is the hard kind of job, mending what isn't torn." 

He knew what would happen when the first note sounded in a room that had held one breath since midnight.  So he took Odile's old winding ribbon, blue silk gone soft as a cat's ear, and used it to tether his wrist to the spindle, and Wren's wrist to his.  Then he laid the red chip against the golden comb and spoke loudly enough for the copper ribs to judge him. 

"Make it steel again.  Make it need me, and whoever comes after me." 

The echo returned sweet as a struck glass. 

Stones like it had turned lead to gold for greedy kings; for one honest sentence this chip did something finer, crumbling into a pinch of red dust that ran along the comb like a blush.  Gold dulled to grey steel, and on the lowest tooth bloomed one freckle of rust. 

The balance wheel fell through the rest of its swing. 

The twelfth chime landed at last, and the chamber took it and made it enormous, a tide of sound that lifted the two of them off their feet and held them bobbing at the end of the blue ribbon like kites.  The cylinder turned, pins plucked teeth, and the old waking tune went plinking up the stair with the freed light flying ahead of it. 

When it set them down, Tobiah stood straighter than he had in months.  He left the ribbon knotted to the spindle and looped its free end around the stair rail, a tether between the box and the town. 

"First of every month," he told Wren, handing her the oil can.  "You carry the tools, and I will carry the complaining." 

Above them, morning opened over Pellam's Hollow, a little late and entirely earned.
