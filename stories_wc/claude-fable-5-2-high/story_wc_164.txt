Mirelle Voss was a sea-foam recollectionist, which meant she spent her mornings kneeling in cold surf, arguing politely with bubbles.  Foam held whatever the sea remembered of the shore, but only until it popped, and the first rule of her trade was to read it before memory fades.  Her tool was a disc of crater glass, fused black-green where a star had once struck the headland, and it had a wedge missing like a slice taken from a pie.  Through the glass, foam showed its feelings as colors, and Mirelle transliterated emotions the way a clerk copies foreign names, shade by shade, into the plain syllables of the village ledger.  She never translated; she would not presume to say what a grief meant, only how it was spelled.  It was gloomy work, and she whistled through most of it. 

What she could not whistle through was the matter of the paths.  Every few years a trail along the coast simply went, grass closing over it like water over a dropped spoon, and nobody could quite say where it had led.  The one her mother used to walk, out to the great geode in the cliff, had vanished the autumn after the funeral.  Mirelle wanted to discover why some paths disappear, partly for the ledger and mostly because a daughter ought to be able to find the place her mother loved.  She liked to conjecture that fog stole them, since fog seemed the type. 

Then, one drizzling October morning, a whimbrel flew over on its way south and something pattered onto her hat.  Seeds: pale crowberry pips, dropped, to put it delicately, in passing.  More lay scattered ahead of her across the dark shingle, a dotted line bending inland where no trail ran.  Mirelle stood very still and let herself conjecture properly.  Whimbrels flew the routes their grandmothers flew, feeding at the same patches, and crowberry had only ever grown along the verges of the geode path.  If she was right, the bird had just drawn her a map in the rudest possible ink. 

She followed the seeds, pip to pip, through gorse that scratched her cheerfully and rain that had clearly cleared its whole day for her.  Where the line had gaps, she guessed, and where she guessed wrong, she fell in a bog and guessed again.  By noon the last seed lay at a crack in the cliff, and beyond the crack the world turned inside out. 

The geode was the size of a chapel, an iridescent cave whose every wall was crystal, violet sliding into green into the pink of a healing scrape.  The tide came in through some low throat of rock and left a pool on the floor, and on the pool sat a raft of old foam, gray and sagging, most of its bubbles already gone.  Sheltered from wind, it had lasted years longer than foam should, but it was popping as she watched, one soft tick at a time. 

Mirelle knelt, raised the crater glass, and began to copy.  The foam held footsteps: her mother's, hundreds of them, and inside each one a color Mirelle knew how to spell.  She had braced for sorrow, but what she set down in the ledger, syllable by syllable, was a small stubborn gladness, the feeling of a woman going somewhere pretty on purpose. 

Then the colors reached the notch in her glass and went blank.  She tilted the disc, and the blank moved with it, and across the pool something winked back from the crystal wall.  It was the missing wedge, black-green among the violet, pressed into a seam at the height of a child's hand. 

Mirelle fitted it to the disc and looked again, quickly, because the foam was down to a few dozen bubbles.  The last footsteps were her own, nine years old, walking out of this cave the week after the funeral, in a color she had declined to spell ever since.  She had snapped the glass herself that day and hidden the piece that could see it.  So that was why some paths disappear: not fog, not thieves, only a last walker who cannot bear the way and a forgetting that kindly finishes the job. 

"Well," Mirelle told the foam, "I owe the fog an apology."  She wrote the color down, all of it, ugly vowels included, and finished the line as the final bubble ticked out. 

Outside, the rain had not improved, but there was a faint crease in the gorse where her boots had been, and seeds lying all along it.  By spring, she conjectured, there would be crowberries, and by autumn, a whimbrel with somewhere to stop.  She walked home whistling, a little off key, on purpose.
