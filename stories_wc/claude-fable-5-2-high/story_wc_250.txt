The rain had stopped an hour ago, and the cobalt dock held its breath.  Every plank was stained blue from forty years of ore sacks, and the wet made the blue deepen until the whole pier looked like a piece of evening that had fallen and stayed.  Maren, guardian of the canal, sat on the lamp house step with her back to the path.  She had not spoken since birth, and the bargemen said the water had borrowed her voice and liked it too much to return it.  In her fingers she turned a dental mirror, her mother's, from the years when her mother pulled teeth for the dock crews.  It was a tool made for seeing the hidden backs of things, where rot begins, and Maren used it for everything: the lamp's burner behind its lens, the undersides of gate hinges, and now the boy coming down the path behind her. 

Teodor was seventeen and walked like someone carrying a full basin.  He stopped two planks away. 

"The night the Wren came in," he said, "the shutters, I was the one who--" 

The sentence stood there with one foot over the edge. 

Maren did not turn.  In the little mirror his face trembled, small as a coin, and behind it the canal lay flat and black and listening. 

She knew the rest.  Six years ago, while she burned with fever, someone had played at the signal lamp, and its three shutters had told the Wren the lock stood open when it was closed.  Her brother Aleks had been at the tiller.  Afterward she had found a child's handprint in the blue dust on the lever, and she had wiped it away with her sleeve before the harbor men climbed the stairs.  She had watched that child grow thin around his silence the way a tree grows around a nail.  She knew silence, and she knew his was heavier than hers, because hers held nothing back. 

Now he waited for her to turn, to strike him, to point toward the harbor office, to do anything that would finish his sentence for him.  The dock ticked with dripping. 

Maren was calm, but it was not a flat calm; it was the calm of the eddy below the sluice, spiralingly calm, a stillness that kept turning down toward one fixed point.  The point was this: punishment would only be more darkness, and darkness learned nothing from darkness.  It had to be taught the value of stars, and the only way to teach that was to make it hold one. 

Far up the canal, a barge lantern appeared, yellow and slow.  The lock was closed. 

Maren stood, pocketed the mirror, and took Teodor by the wrist.  He flinched, then let himself be led up the eleven stairs to the lamp.  She set his hand on the lever, exactly where the small print had been, and laid her own hand over it.  His fingers were cold and shook against the iron.  She did not grip harder; she only matched her breathing to his until his slowed, and he understood that she had always known. 

Together they worked the shutters: left, pause, all, pause, left.  Bars of light and dark went out over the water and began to weave, crossing their own reflections, a cloth of signal that said hold, gate shut, wait. 

The barge lantern did not slow. 

Teodor made a sound in his throat, and Maren lifted her hand away.  He was alone on the lever now, with the dark canal and a boat coming, as he had been at eleven. 

Left, pause, all, pause, left.  Again. 

The pattern steadied under his hands, and out on the water the yellow light dipped twice in answer and drifted to a stop. 

Teodor leaned his forehead against the warm brass of the lamp housing.  Maren took out the dental mirror and tilted it in the beam.  A single point of light jumped from the glass and landed on the black water below the gate, where the Wren had gone down.  She held it there for the length of a breath, one star on all that dark.  Then she turned her wrist and set the star in the middle of his open palm. 

He closed his fingers on it as if it had weight. 

"I was the one who--" he began again, and she laid the mirror's handle in his hand and folded his fingers around that too.  She pointed to the lever, then up the canal, where other lanterns would come tomorrow night, and every night after. 

The sentence never reached its end.  It did not need to; below them the water lay unbroken, and the shutters, under his hands, went on saying the rest.
