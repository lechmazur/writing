Oske leaned in to whisper, and Tovi's whole experiment tilted with him.  Twilight was the only hour the Ember Nexus showed its true colors.  Seven streams of live embers ran down from the seven hill-forges and met in this stone basin, and by day the sun bleached them, and by night they all went a common red.  Only now, in the blue quarter hour, could you tell them apart: the stubborn crimson, the patient amber, the violet that burned for grief refused, the white that burned for promises kept.  Each forge fed its fire on whatever its smiths had sworn, and Oske, who kept the basin, called the result a palette of resolve.  Tovi had come to paint with it. 

She was a pocket-sun alchemist, a maker of small bright spheres that people carried in coats and lunch pails and the cold halves of beds.  Her suns were warm, and they were accurate, and last month a widow had handed one back saying it felt like a lamp left on in an empty house.  Tovi had gone home and done what she always did with heartbreak, which was weigh it.  She set a balance on her kitchen table and logged how the pan seemed to dip when her sister sat down across from her, and she wrote in the margin, in ink, that presence has density and light alone does not.  It was the only science she trusted, the kind that fell in love with what it measured. 

So, to capture that density, she had brought her best vessel to the Nexus: the columella curl of a nautilus shell, the pearl spiral at the heart of it, sawn free of its chambers.  Her plan was written out in eleven steps, seven colors drawn through the curl in order of wavelength.  Three times tonight the light had gone in one end and come out the other exactly as thin as it entered. 

And then Oske, who had watched her fail with the calm of a man who tends fire for a living, put his shoulder against hers and his mouth near her ear. 

"I still carry the first sun you ever made me," he whispered.  "It went out nine winters ago, and I never told you." 

The whisper was not finished, and already she was revising everything.  Because there it was, the thing her balance had only hinted at: his breath warm on her neck, the lean of him, nine winters pressing through a dozen words.  A dead sun, worthless by any ledger she kept, and he had put it in his pocket every morning.  She would have to revalue the whole column of her failures, because the weight had never been in the light.  The weight was in the coming back.  Presence was not something that passed straight through you; it was something that left and returned differently, the way he had returned to that cold sphere every morning as a slightly older man.  And a spiral, she saw, was precisely the shape of that: a path that comes around to the same place one turn higher.  She had been using the curl as a pipe. 

Her hands moved before the eleven steps could object.  She pinched the narrow end shut with her thumb, so that whatever entered would have to turn at the tip and climb back out the way it came.  She raised the open end into the narrow space between his mouth and her ear and caught the tail of the whisper, the breath of it, the word "you."  Then she swept the curl low across the basin, not in order of wavelength but in the order the moment handed her: amber first, for his patience; crimson, for her own mule-headed returning; a thread of the promise-white.  It was improvised, and it was not careless; ten years of practice chose every angle of her wrist. 

The colors wound inward after the breath, met the sealed tip, and came back.  They did not come back as they had gone in.  What rolled out of the shell into her palm was the size of a plum and the color of the sky directly overhead, and it pulled her hand down like a river stone. 

Oske drew back, his secret barely out of his mouth, and stared at what she was holding. 

"Give me the dead one," Tovi said.  "I am not going to replace it." 

He took the dull gray sphere from his coat, and she pressed the new light against it, and the weight went spiraling home into a sun that had waited nine winters to return differently.  When she put it back in his pocket, his coat hung lower on that side, the way a coat does when someone has taken your arm.
