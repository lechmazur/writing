After reality became negotiable, only pressed ink held, so the Curators of the Glass Orchard came to Maren Oost.  She set their Accord in lead forty winters ago: no wind, no decay, no change, forever.  The library had floated perfect ever since, a grove of crystal trees adrift upon a swirling sea of clouds, each leaf a page, each fruit a book that never ripened and never fell.  Nothing rustled.  Nothing was read, either, because reading bends a spine, and bending was change. 

Maren kept one secret from the Accord.  On the day she pulled the final proof, the last wind crossing the orchard had spoken against her ear, and she had caught it in an ink bottle and sealed it with wax.  A bottled whisper, unfiled, uncatalogued, the single thing in the library that had not agreed to hold still.  She knew what it was, because she knew type: one loose sort destabilizes a perfect forme, and one unsigned breath destabilizes a perfect system. 

When Curators asked her questions, she answered with printed slips.  STET, mostly.  Let it stand. 

Only the frost defied the Accord.  The clouds below had signed nothing, and each dawn their cold breath climbed the trunks and wrote white ferns across the glass.  The Curators called it a blemish and waited for the sun to correct it.  Maren called it handwriting.  Every morning for forty years she walked the rows before the light, reading the patterns the way she read a tray of reversed type, and every morning she set what she found.  The ferns were maps of where air would move if air were allowed.  Line by line they became a forme, and the forme, she slowly understood, was a route: every branching led toward one tree at the orchard's heart, the first tree, whose roots held the original Accord. 

Pell, the youngest Curator, found her there on the coldest morning in memory.  Frost covered the first tree completely, and for once the ferns did not branch.  They converged, every white vein running to a single point on the trunk at the height of a woman's hand. 

"You're not permitted past the inner rail," Pell said.  "What are you doing?" 

Maren handed him a slip.  It read: CORRECTING A PROOF. 

"The Accord has no errors." 

She studied him, a boy for forty years who had never heard a leaf move, and did something she had not done since the signing.  She spoke. 

"How many books are in this orchard, Pell?" 

"All of them.  Every book, kept for eternity." 

"And how many have you read?" 

He opened his mouth and found nothing in it. 

"That is the error," she said.  "I printed an eternity nobody can enter.  This morning I mean to press all of it into one moment somebody can." 

She took the bottle from her apron.  Pell saw the wax, the faint gray stirring behind the glass, and understood enough to be afraid. 

"If the Accord breaks, everything it holds lets go.  The trees could shatter.  You have not aged since you printed it.  You would owe forty years at once." 

"Yes," Maren said, and it sounded like relief.  "Glass that cannot ring is only ice.  Stand back or stand with me." 

He looked up at the unread fruit hanging over him, ten thousand closed covers he had dusted and never opened.  He stayed. 

Maren pressed the bottle's neck to the point where the frost converged and drove her bodkin through the wax. 

The rupture was small, a crack no louder than a knuckle.  Then the whisper went out along the frost roads exactly as the ferns had drawn them, and the Accord split in the roots with a sound like a page tearing. 

One leaf touched another. 

It rang, and the ringing ran from tree to tree, row to row, until the whole orchard was rustling, a bright chiming surf above the clouds.  The books woke.  Covers lifted like wings; pages turned in the wind, and every one of them spoke its first line aloud at the same instant, forty years of silence and all the ages shelved inside it poured into a single breath.  Some leaves fell and shattered, which was permitted now. 

Maren felt the years arrive.  Her hair went white as the frost, her knees gave, and Pell caught her, a boy already taller than he had been a minute before.  A ripe book dropped into his free hand.  He opened it, and its spine bent, and he began, haltingly, to read aloud. 

Maren listened with her eyes closed while the sun came up and took the frost away.  She had no slip for this, so she said it. 

"Let it stand."
