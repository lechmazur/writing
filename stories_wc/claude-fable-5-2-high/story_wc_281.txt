The recall notice arrived on a Tuesday, three paragraphs of apology folded around a prepaid envelope.  Concord, the telepathy pill my brother Tobin had swallowed every morning for eight months, was being pulled from every shelf in the country.  The fine print admitted what the commercials never had: after the link dissolved, other people's feelings stayed behind like furniture nobody came back for.  Tobin mailed back his last bottle and came to sit in my light painting studio, because it was the darkest, quietest room either of us knew. 

"I'm grieving a dog," he said from the stool, "and I have never owned a dog." 

I had never believed in Concord.  I had told him so lovingly and often, usually while handing him soup: if you want to know what I'm thinking, ask me, I work cheap.  Being right gave me no pleasure now.  The company's hotline offered hold music and a coupon, and my little brother could not sort his own heart from the strangers still rattling around in it. 

I crossed the wool blackout rug in my socks to squeeze his shoulder, and a spark cracked between my finger and his collarbone.  He yelped, then laughed, the first real laugh in weeks. 

"That one was mine," he said, rubbing the spot.  "Nobody else felt that." 

After the static shock, I stood very still, the way I do when an exposure is about to work.  The pills had tangled his thoughts, but the jolt had arrived through skin, and skin had never been on the network.  I remembered an article about somatic markers, the body's private signatures for feeling: the tight throat, the dropped stomach, the warmth behind the breastbone.  A borrowed grief might live in his head, but would it have a place in his ribs? 

"This is a hypothesis, not a cure," I warned him, already unfolding the tripod. 

"You always say that right before you fix something," he said. 

So I began to devise a method, out loud, so he could argue with it.  I would open the shutter for sixty seconds and name a feeling he believed was his.  If it landed somewhere in his body, he would trace that place with a penlight; if it landed nowhere, he would leave the dark alone. 

The penlight needed softening, so I took our mother's geode from the windowsill and peeled a sliver of muscovite from its silver lining, thin as a held breath.  Taped over the bulb, the mica split the beam into fine, layered threads. 

"I cracked that open when I was nine," Tobin said, and his free hand went to his sternum before I had asked anything. 

"Paint that," I said, and opened the shutter. 

We worked for three hours in the dark.  I named the hammer and the geode, his first drum kit, our father's hospital room, the dog.  For the hospital, the light climbed his throat and shook there.  For the dog, the penlight hung unlit at his side, and he let out a breath like a man setting down someone else's suitcase. 

I did not trust any of it until the images came up on the monitor.  Layered together, the exposures showed a figure made of silver branches, rooted at the breastbone and forking toward throat, belly, and palms.  The pattern was fractally precise: every large fork repeated in smaller forks, and those in smaller ones still, the mica multiplying each tremor of his hand.  Between the branches lay clean gaps of black where the borrowed feelings had found nothing to hold. 

"It looks like a willow," Tobin whispered.  "A willow made of glass." 

"It looks like sixty seconds of you waving a flashlight," I said, because somebody had to, but my voice came out soft.  I could not prove the map was true.  I could only see that the pathways had been there all along, hidden under the noise, and that he had needed a way to illuminate them himself. 

He touched the screen where the light pooled at his sternum.  "So when I can't tell, I check here first." 

"You check there first," I agreed, "and you call me second, and we photograph whatever is left." 

The hope I felt was a thin, bending thing, the kind you can see straight through and still lean on.  It might snap.  I peeled a second sliver for the bulb and pressed the first into his palm, where his fingers closed around it without asking anyone whose it was. 

"One more exposure," he said. 

I reached for the shutter, skeptical as ever, and opened it anyway.
