After the universe contracted, everything that remained hung in one crowded sky above the observatory, a knot of stars close as fruit.  The deck had been sealed in amber resin generations ago, so the light that reached Oriel Vask came honey-colored and slow.  She was the station's lot-caster, appointed because the last forty people alive no longer trusted anyone to choose.  Her lots were a collection of foreign coins, thirty-one of them, each minted on a world the contraction had swallowed.  When rations ran short or a hull seam failed, she shook the pouch, drew one, and tossed it; a dead king's face meant yes.  Nobody could blame a coin, and nobody could blame her.  That was the comfort of it, and lately the ache. 

Tonight the council wanted one last toss.  The knot would not hold forever, and the station could either stay sealed, a gnat in amber outside whatever came next, or crack the deck and fall inward to be remade. 

Oriel had asked for an hour first, because of the static.  It came through the old dish in bursts, hissing like rain on a hot plate, and everyone else heard only the cosmos clearing its throat.  She had spent two years learning to understand it, logging each burst's length and pitch in a notebook she kept squared against the console's edge. 

"You're humming again," said Dov, the dish keeper, from his stool. 

"Am I?"  She was; it was the only way her enthusiasm ever leaked out, a small tune under her breath while her pencil moved.  "Old light is falling back on us, squeezed short, and it lands in reverse order, so whatever it says, it says ending first." 

"A backwards prophecy," Dov said, not kindly.  "Or one lonely woman hearing shapes in noise." 

"Possibly," she admitted, and meant it.  The stars had never once confirmed anything for her; they only crowded the amber, enormous and uncommitted. 

Still, she had distilled four bursts so far, boiling hours of hiss down to a line apiece, throwing out whatever repeated by accident and keeping what repeated on purpose.  The first line received was the last thing that would happen: a new light opens.  Then, stepping backward: strange metals seed the dark; the amber breaks; thirty-one faces are given away.  Each line sat one step nearer to now, and the only step left was this deck, this hour, her hand. 

"What do you want from it?" Dov asked. 

She turned the tuning wheel a hair.  "I have decided everything for this station and chosen nothing.  I want to be on the same wavelength as a reason, just once, the way this dish locks onto a signal and stops wandering." 

The fifth burst arrived as the knot overhead brightened, nine seconds of crackle that raised the hair on her arms.  She played it back slowly, humming, striking out the echoes, matching the surviving pulses against her tables.  What remained, distilled, was short: no coin is in the air. 

Oriel stopped humming. 

The prophecy's beginning, delivered last, was not an event at all but an absence, the exact shape of her habit removed.  Everything after it, the breaking and the seeding and the light, hung on a decision made by a person instead of a king's stamped face. 

"It could still be noise," Dov said, reading over her shoulder, though his voice had gone careful. 

"It could," she said.  "That is rather the point; if I were certain, it would be one more coin deciding for me." 

She emptied the pouch onto the console and looked at the coins properly for the first time in years: copper, nickel, a greenish alloy nobody could name, the whole chemistry of lost places.  Sealed in here they were souvenirs; out there they were ingredients. 

She carried them to the council and gave them away until the pouch hung empty, then told forty frightened people that she, Oriel Vask, chose to open the deck.  Nobody could blame a coin now.  They could blame her, and to her surprise that felt like standing on something. 

Back on the deck, she set her palm against the release and felt the dish's hum settle into her wrist, steady, locked.  Above her the stars pressed close, promising nothing.  She did not know whether a new light would open, or whether she had only taught herself to hear her own wish in the rain.  She pressed down anyway, humming, and the amber began to crack like a morning.
