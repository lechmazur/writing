The wind found the abandoned circus train before Odile did, and the bells on the caboose rail began a single long chime.  Everything that follows happened inside that chime, the way a whole dream can happen inside one breath.  Odile had built the carousel that rode in the seventh car, every horse, every scrolled panel, and the limewood angel that crowned its center pole.  She had not spoken since the morning the owner sold the engine and left the cars to the desert.  Nobody had forbidden her words; she had simply found nothing worth saying over a carousel that would never turn.  For eleven years silence had been a nailed lid to her, and she was tired of living under it. 

In her apron pocket she carried a halo fragment, a curved sliver of gilded wood as long as her hand.  It had snapped off the angel during the last hurried loading, and she had picked it out of the cinders and kept it.  Each spring she walked four days from the rail town to look for the train, and each spring the dunes had hidden the siding.  She was quietly persistent in the way of sandpaper, which never argues with wood and always wins.  This year the dunes had shifted, and the train lay bare, its painted tigers faded to the color of weak tea.  Her plan was small and exact: glue the fragment back, close the circle, and learn from one mended thing how to read silence as something other than an ending. 

She climbed aboard at the caboose while the bells still trembled, and walked forward through the cars, counting.  The cook's car held a drift of sand shaped like a sleeping dog.  The costume car held sequins that winked at her like fish in a dark pond.  At the fifth coupling one car had slumped off the rails against its neighbor, and the gangway split into two torn doorways.  She had lost her count somewhere among the sequins, and she turned left when the carousel car lay to the right. 

The wrong door opened on green.  It was the old water car, slumped across the siding's forgotten well pipe, which it had cracked, and eleven years of slow seep had soaked a floor of blown seed and rotted straw.  Ferns grew from the feed troughs, reeds stood in the broken tank, and a fig tree had put its crown through the roof.  Morning heat lifted the damp into a fine veil, a misting hush that beaded on her eyelashes and softened every edge.  Odile stood very still, because this quiet was nothing like hers.  Hers was a lid; this one was a held breath, crowded with dripping, with leaf against leaf, with things about to begin. 

Then the mist thinned, and a gleam showed among the reeds.  The angel leaned there, shoved into the wrong car by some roustabout in a hurry, her gilding gone soft and whispery under moss.  Odile understood that the right turn would have brought her to an empty center pole and another year of looking.  She waded in, drew out the fragment, and raised it toward the gap in the halo. 

The gap was occupied.  A wren had woven its nest exactly where the missing piece belonged, and three eggs the size of thumbnails lay in the break as if the halo had been opened on purpose to hold them. 

Odile lowered her hand.  She had carried the sliver for eleven years believing a broken circle was a failed circle.  Here was a break that had become a doorway, and a silence that had become a garden simply by being left room.  Perhaps her own quiet had never been a lid at all, only a space nobody had yet planted. 

She set the fragment upright in the moss at the angel's feet, where the wet light ran along its gold.  It was not a missing piece anymore, she decided, but a first piece, the opening curve of something not yet carved. 

Far down the train the bells were thinning toward their last shimmer, and she found that she wanted to answer before they finished.  Her throat was a hinge rusted shut.  She worked at it as she worked at everything, patiently, a little pressure and then a little more.  A scrape came first, then a thread of sound, and then Odile began to sing the waltz her carousel had turned to, low enough not to trouble the nest.  The mist carried the tune out through the fig leaves, and it met the fading chime in the air above the train.  When the bells went still, the silence that followed did not close over her; it waited, gleaming, to hear what she would make next.
