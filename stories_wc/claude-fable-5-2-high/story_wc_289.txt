After the cloud bursts, the lily pond takes an hour to remember it is a mirror.  Marisol Venn sits out that hour beneath the temple's lowest stone petal, her writing desk balanced on her knees, watching the rain's last beads slide down the lotus and split the light into quiet colors.  The temple rises from the middle of the pond like a bud that never quite decided to open, and for eleven years she has worked on its steps, writing love letters for people who cannot find their own words.  The ferryman's proposal was hers.  So were the baker's apology, the widow's late confession to the bell ringer, and both sides of a courtship between two shy clerks who each believe the other is the eloquent one. 

Her letters are inevitably whimsical.  She has tried for solemnity, but a frog in a waistcoat always wanders into the second paragraph, or the moon gets compared to a dropped dumpling, and somehow these are the lines her clients kiss before sealing. 

The work has made her the keeper of every secret in town, and keeping them has made her a stranger.  She cannot sit at the baker's table knowing what he was forgiven for, cannot laugh with the clerks without laughing wrongly, so years ago she stopped crossing the causeway except to buy ink.  Alone on the steps, she has come to suspect that all those letters are one letter, that some single thread runs through every longing she has ever transcribed, and that if she could find it she would understand what she has been guarding. 

Today the storm hands her a clue.  A thrush's nest, knocked from the eaves, has landed on the step beside her, and the rain has loosened it into a spiral of twigs, each one curving a little farther out than the last.  Woven between the twigs are scraps of paper.  Marisol knows the handwriting, because it is hers: discarded drafts, stolen from her wastebasket all spring. 

She follows the spiral with an inky fingernail and reads the fragments in the order the bird chose.  The ferryman's "wait for me" touches the widow's "I waited," which touches a clerk's "I am afraid you will see how ordinary I am."  Stripped of names, the lines do not belong to anyone, and so they belong to everyone.  The thrush, wanting only a soft place for eggs, has built what Marisol could not: the whole town's heart, wound into a single curve. 

There is the thread, she thinks, and it was never hers to hide, only hers to keep unnamed.  A secret, it turns out, is mostly a name; take the name away and what remains is simply true. 

So she builds the nest again, large enough to walk through.  For nine days she gathers willow the storm brought down and floats it out on the monks' flat boats, and with the abbot's amused permission she lays a spiral walkway of branches among the lily pads, beginning at the town end of the causeway and curling inward to the temple steps.  Between the branches she ties ribbons of waxed paper, hundreds of them, each bearing one line from eleven years of letters and not one name.  The frog in the waistcoat is there.  So is the dumpling moon.  She hangs no sign, because the installation is a letter, and she has never signed one. 

On the tenth morning the cloud bursts again, briefly, and when it passes every ribbon holds a row of droplets, and the whole spiral shivers with small rainbows above the green water.  The town comes out to look, as towns do. 

Marisol watches from her petal as they enter the spiral one by one and go silent, reading.  The baker stops at a ribbon and touches his chest.  The two clerks stop at different ribbons, turn, and look at each other for a long time, and whatever they understand, they seem to forgive it by laughing.  Nobody can tell which longing is whose, only that each is standing inside all of them, and that the path leads, turn by narrowing turn, to the woman on the steps. 

The ferryman reaches her first.  "You kept it all," he says, not quite a question. 

"I kept the names," Marisol says, "and I always will, but the rest was never only mine." 

He considers this, then offers his arm as gravely as if a frog in a waistcoat had taught him how.  She sets down her desk, takes his arm, and walks the spiral outward, past every unnamed line, to rejoin the town whose heart she has been carrying.  Behind her, on a high petal, a thrush begins again with a single twig.
