Hail came to Carrow in the last week of August and beat the vineyards flat.  After that the season simply lay there, wounded, with nothing to pick and nothing to press, and the village drifted through its long lull like people waiting in a sickroom. 

Odile Varn, the locksmith, was the only one with work.  The mayor had walked her up to the old quarry, into the marble cavern where the cutters had left a hall as white as bone, and shown her the bronze door at its far end.  Behind it lay the Answering Gallery, where every October the families of Carrow shouted what they had gathered and listened to the stone give it back sevenfold.  They called it the harvest of echoes, and in a year with no other harvest, the mayor wanted it badly.  But the door answered only to its timer, and last year, for the first time in a century, it had stayed shut. 

Odile unscrewed the housing beside the hinge and found the broken timer mechanism: a latch, a counterweight, a cracked bulb of blackened glass, and not one gear.  No spring, no escapement, nothing that counted. 

"Can you force it?" the mayor asked. 

"I could break it," she said, "but then you would have a hole, and what you want is a door." 

She would have to reverse-engineer the thing from its corpse, and she did not yet know what kind of animal it had been. 

Odile had never been good at looking straight at things.  Her master used to say she picked locks with the corner of her eye, attending to the pin she was not touching, and it was true that her best thoughts arrived sideways.  So it was while she frowned at the dead latch on the third afternoon that she noticed, at the edge of her sight, a coin of light sliding across the cavern wall.  It came down the old ventilation shaft, struck a panel of marble polished far smoother than any quarryman needed, and skipped to a second panel across the hall. 

The timer had no gears because the sun was its gear. 

For the rest of that idle month she climbed to the cavern every day with chalk, a plumb line, and a brass protractor, studying the reflection angles at different times: nine o'clock, noon, three, and again a week later when the sun had sunk a finger lower.  She chalked each landing place on the marble until the walls wore a slow white handwriting of arcs.  Extended forward, the arcs would pass a hand's width above the bulb, and there was the fault.  A hairline settling crack ran under the second panel; the mountain had shrugged, the mirror had tilted one degree, and the beam that once warmed the black glass in the third week of October, swelling the air inside until it tripped the latch, now fell on blank stone. 

Odile shimmed the panel with a wedge of lead, fitted a new bulb the glassblower made her, and wrote everything down.  That was her habit and, privately, her creed.  Carrow's cellars would stand empty this winter, but she kept a cellar of another kind, a ledger where every marvel she had ever opened was laid down in angles and measures, in the cool of reason, to keep.  Wonder spoiled if left out in the open, trusted to memory and luck; the dead quarry master had proved that by taking his with him.  Stored properly, it could be brought up later, in some worse year, and still be good. 

On the nineteenth of October the village climbed the quarry road with empty baskets, because that was the custom and no one had the heart to change it.  At eleven minutes past two the coin of light crossed her last chalk mark and settled on the bulb.  Odile was watching the mayor's granddaughter instead, and so she saw the door open the way she saw everything, from the side: a seam of darkness widening in the bronze. 

The families went in and called out what they had: one cask, eleven jars of plums, a goat, a baby.  The gallery took each small thing and returned it seven times, until the meager year sounded, for a minute, like abundance.  People laughed, and some wept, and the stone gave that back too. 

"Does it ruin it," the girl asked her, "knowing how it works?" 

Odile considered the question from the side, as usual. 

"No," she said, and tapped the ledger under her arm.  "It means that the next time the mountain shrugs, someone can go down to the cellar and bring it up again." 

Behind them the last echo thinned along the marble and went out, spent but not lost.
