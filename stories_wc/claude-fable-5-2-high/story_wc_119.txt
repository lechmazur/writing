Corrie rang the brass bell twice, which on Skerry Rock meant last orders and also meant nobody was leaving yet.  The pub filled the ground floor of the lighthouse, a tower that had stopped guiding ships decades ago and still, at closing, turned its lamp for reasons no one admitted to knowing.  Nell Varga sat beneath the stair with a basket of fortune cookies and a stomach full of gulls. 

She wrote the slips for a mainland bakery, eleven words at most, cryptic by contract.  The trouble was that they kept coming true.  She had written Mend the roof before the roof mends you, and a gale stripped the chapel slates that same week.  She had written A patient net catches the moon, and Tam hauled up a silver salver from some drowned yacht.  Accidents, every one, and the whole island knew it. 

That was how Skerry worked: it ran on open secrets, things known by all and spoken by none.  Corrie watered the whisky.  Bess set a place for a son who was never taking the ferry home.  The last keeper, Hale, had spent the war sending somebody something, and his lamp room stayed locked out of manners.  And Nell guessed, yet people planned weddings and sold boats by her guesses, so she had grown afraid of her own pencil. 

Last March she wrote, for no reason, Look for the truth where the sentence stops.  In April she borrowed the lamp room key for somewhere quiet to work, and behind a loose panel she found Hale's biscuit tin.  Inside lay a brass reader the size of a thimble and a typed sheet of spycraft: microdot film specs, giving emulsion, exposure, reduction ratio, and how to seat a page of text beneath a single full stop.  Where the sentence stops.  Even Nell, who knew better, felt the tower lean in to listen. 

She did not want a new life, exactly.  She wanted the same one transposed into a new key: the same small slips, the same short melody of words, but pitched where plain truth could be sung.  So all winter she followed Hale's specs, photographing honest sentences, shrinking them, and gluing one dot at the end of each riddle. 

Tonight she would orchestrate the reading.  Corrie, sworn in and curious, bolted the harbor door and blamed the damp.  "Way out is up and over tonight," Nell called, handing each drinker a cookie, "single file, round the lamp, down the gallery steps."  She worked the crowd the way a sluice works a tide, by directing flow: one body at a time up the narrow spiral, slow enough to read, steady enough that nobody could turn back.  At the top she had clamped the brass reader in the path of the beam, so that a slip held to it bloomed across the lantern glass in letters a foot high. 

Tam went first.  His fortune said The tide forgives what the ledger forgets; his dot said, I cannot see the future, Tam, so please insure the boat.  He laughed until he had to hold the rail.  Corrie's said, We know about the whisky and we come anyway.  Bess stood longest, her face lit white by the words: He writes because he cannot come, so go to him. 

"I knew that," Bess said. 

"Everyone did," said Nell.  "That is the whole trick." 

"Queer, though, seeing it that size," Bess said, and folded the slip into her glove like a ticket. 

One by one the island filed past its own knowledge, familiar as a kitchen and strange as a kitchen at three in the morning.  Nobody was shamed, because nothing was news; they were only relieved of the work of not saying. 

When the stair was empty, Corrie nodded at the basket.  "One left, oracle." 

It was Nell's own, baked with a dot she had never managed to fill, because every sentence she tried about herself came out as a riddle.  The slip, written back in autumn for the bakery, read You will be understood at closing time.  She held it to the reader, and the beam threw a clean, empty circle of light through the glass and out across the black rocks to the water. 

Below, on the gallery steps, the islanders looked up. 

"It says carry on," Tam shouted. 

"It says nothing," Nell shouted back. 

"Aye," said Bess, "and we all read it." 

Nell laughed, accidentally right again, and found she did not mind.  The tune was the old one, riddles on small paper under a turning lamp, but it sat in a key she could finally sing, with the secret kept out in the open, where the sentence stops.
