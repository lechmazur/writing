On the longest night of the year, Wren Halloway climbed the last switchback to Saint Aldous, a wind-scoured cathedral perched so high that the clouds lay below it like a second snowfield.  She was the county's storm siren tester, a job title she always gave with a small apologetic shrug, as though measuring loudness were nothing to boast about.  The siren stood on the bell tower's shoulder, aimed at three valley towns asleep under the cloud deck.  Its annual full-voice test was scheduled for midnight, which the paperwork called zero hour. 

Brother Anselm, the sanctuary's last caretaker, met her at the door with a lantern and bad news.  The cold had split the siren's resin diaphragm clean across.  Wren examined the crack, nodded, and took a tube of epoxy from her kit. 

"Four hours to cure in this cold," she said.  "Nothing sounds until after the glue dries." 

So she waited, and waiting, for Wren, meant listening.  She had taken this work years ago because she believed a warning was only worth making if someone had first paid attention to the sky, and she distrusted any noise that had not earned its place.  Her habit at every site was tracking microclimate variations, the small private weathers that gathered around towers and ridgelines.  She clipped thermometers along the nave, set a barometer on the altar rail, and tied a strip of red ribbon to the cathedral bell rope, which hung down the tower's throat like a plumb line. 

"Does the bell still ring?" she asked. 

"Not in eleven years," Anselm said.  "The towns voted for the siren, and now they sleep through every test." 

Wren asked more questions, and he answered them all, because her curiosity was the credible kind.  She wrote everything down, checked it twice, and never asked about a thing she did not mean to understand.  She learned that the old ringers had used three slow strokes and a pause for wind off the summit, a pattern the valley grandmothers still hummed to restless children. 

The hours passed in a solstice hush, with only the steady summit wind combing the broken arches while the epoxy hardened in its clamp.  At ten, the ribbon on the bell rope changed its mind.  All evening it had streamed east; now it hung limp, then lifted straight up, as if the tower were inhaling. 

Wren walked her thermometers.  The north aisle had warmed six degrees in twenty minutes while the south aisle froze harder, and the barometer was falling like a dropped coin.  She stood very still and let the numbers speak to each other.  Warm air was sliding over the summit and pooling above the cloud deck; when it broke through, a downslope gale would pour into the valleys with no rain or thunder to announce it.  By her reckoning, it would break near midnight. 

"Then your siren is ready just in time," Anselm said, touching the cured seam. 

Wren looked at the control box, at the heavy red switch she had thrown at a hundred sites. 

"It is ready," she said, "and it is useless."  "Every household down there has a notice on the door saying the siren will sound at zero hour and should be ignored."  "If I throw that switch, I will be making the loudest noise in the county, and no one will hear it." 

She felt the smallness of her position, a tester with no authority to cancel anything, and she felt, beneath it, the plain weight of what she had listened to.  Then she made the switch.  She locked the siren's control box, pocketed the key, and went to the tower, where the ribbon now stood rigid as a flame. 

The cathedral bell rope was stiff with frost and older than both of them.  "Show me the rhythm," she said, and Anselm, who had not rung in eleven years, set his hands above hers. 

At zero hour they pulled. 

Three slow strokes and a pause, three slow strokes and a pause, the bronze voice dropping through the clouds like something remembered rather than announced.  Far below, where the cloud deck had begun to tear, lights came on in ones and twos, then in whole streets.  Shutters closed, and animals were led inside.  When the gale finally poured off the mountain, it found the valleys awake and braced. 

Near dawn, Wren filled in her form honestly: siren repaired, test not performed, bell rung instead.  Under remarks she wrote only that the valley still knew how to listen, and that she recommended keeping the rope.  Then she untied her red ribbon, left it on the altar rail, and walked down into the quiet, where the light was already beginning its slow return.
