The rain had passed an hour ago, and the lagoon lay cobalt and still, keeping the sky like a held breath.  Maren sat at her loom on the porch of the net shed, as she did every daybreak, saying nothing.  The villagers called her the shoreline poet, though she spoke her poems to no one and finished none of them. 

Before her hung the woven dawns: a long band of wool begun by her mother, one row for each morning, dyed to match whatever light arrived.  Forty years of sunrises ran down it in rose and ash and gold.  Then came the row her mother had left half made on her last morning, a gray thread stopping in midair.  Maren had woven on beneath it for six years, but she had never closed that gap, and she had never learned to look at it without flinching.  She wanted to; she believed there must be some beauty in a thing left open, if only she could find where it lived. 

This morning she lifted the warp threads with a drake's tail feather, the curled black plume shed by the lame mallard she had been tending since spring.  Out on the sandbar, a boy was flying a red kite into the washed sky. 

Then the string snapped. 

It made a small, bright sound, like a plucked note, and everything that followed seemed to happen inside that one note as it rang.  The kite staggered, folded, and dropped into the lagoon.  The drakes burst up squalling, the boy's cry cracked across the water, and the morning's quiet came apart like a torn seam. 

Maren set the feather behind her ear and waded out, skirts darkening, until the blue closed around her knees.  She lifted the kite gently, the way she lifted the mallard when its bad leg failed.  One spar was broken, the tail was gone, and a length of wet string trailed from the bridle. 

The boy met her at the shallows, his fist still gripping the other end.  "It's ruined," he said.  "My father made it, and he's gone with the boats till winter, and now it's only half of a kite." 

Maren did not hurry to answer.  She crouched to his height and listened, warmly attentive, the way she listened to tides, until his breathing slowed and he had said all of it.  Half of a kite, she thought, and felt the gray thread on her loom tug at her. 

"Come," she said.  "Let us tend it and see what it wants to be." 

On the porch she splinted the spar with a reed and knotted a tail from loom ends, tipping it with the drake's curled feather for a rudder.  It looked wrong: black plume, red paper, green reed, a patchwork of things that had never meant to meet. 

The boy tied his spool to the mended bridle, then held up the wet length she had cut away.  "What about the broken piece?" 

Maren looked at the red string, and then at the gray thread halted on her loom.  Her mother had taught her that a weaver's whole craft was harmonizing discord, laying the colors that quarreled side by side until each made the other sing.  She had done it with wool for six years and never once with grief. 

Now she worked the red string into the unfinished row with her fingers, starting from the far edge and coming back toward the gray.  The string ran out a hand's width short.  The row stayed open, yet it no longer looked like a wound; gray and red leaned toward each other across the gap like two notes waiting on a third.  Through the bare warp between them, the cobalt lagoon showed, bright as a dyed thread no one could have spun.  So that was where the beauty lived: not in the thread, but in the place the thread left for the morning to come through. 

The boy ran to the sandbar and gave the kite to the wind.  It climbed crookedly, splint and plume and all, wobbling like the lame mallard, and it was lovelier for the wobble.  The drakes settled back onto the water, and the hush returned, deeper now, polished by what had broken it. 

Maren sat down to tend the day's own row, and a poem rose in her, three lines and the start of a fourth.  For the first time, she spoke it aloud to the lagoon, and when the words ran out she did not flinch.  She left the last line open, the way the sky stays open above a kite.
