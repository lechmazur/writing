Ilsa Venn was paid by the Registry of Charted Wonders to visit fantasy islands and decide, with calipers and a straight face, which parts of them were real.  Her ledger had three columns: Verified, Unverified, and Delightful Anyway, and she was strict about all three.  She reached the island of Sorrel Tam nine days before the monsoon, when the ancient ridge above the village was in bloom from spine to shoulder, white heather and red torchflower crowding stones older than any map. 

The Registry's question was simple.  The islanders claimed that everything they knew, every boat pattern, lullaby, and boundary quarrel, was kept in a tree whose fruit let one mouth taste what every mouth had ever said.  If the claim failed inspection, Sorrel Tam would be moved to the Annex of Fables, and its history would be shelved beside mermaids.  Ilsa had seen what that did to a people: within a generation, their grandchildren quoted the Registry instead of their grandmothers.  It had happened to her own island, which was why she had taken the job, and why she refused to do it carelessly in either direction. 

The tree, however, was a stump.  Lightning had taken it two monsoons ago, the keeper Maro told her, holding out a seed the size of a thumb joint, dark and ridged like a walnut that had been thinking. 

"This is the last knowledge fruit seed," he said.  "It sleeps.  Put it in the ground asleep and the monsoon rots it.  It must waken first, and it wakens only when it is told what it has forgotten." 

"Told how?" Ilsa asked, pencil ready. 

"We sleep on the ridge among the flowers," Maro said.  "In the morning we tell our dreams, and someone interprets them until we agree what they remember.  The tree always did the interpreting.  Now there is no tree, and nobody trusts their own reading." 

Ilsa weighed the seed on her brass scale: eleven grams, cool, inert.  She wrote it under Unverified, then, because the ridges on its shell resembled a frowning owl, added a small note under Delightful Anyway.  Then she proposed an experiment, because a reality checker who cannot test a thing has no business doubting it. 

Each night, forty villagers would sleep on the ridge.  Each dawn, before anyone spoke to anyone else, each would whisper a dream to Ilsa alone, and she would record it, weigh the seed, and take its temperature with a thermometer she kept in a knitted sleeve. 

The first morning gave her forty scraps of nonsense.  A goat wearing a net.  A door in the tide.  Seven knots tied by a woman with no face.  But when she sorted the scraps on a flat stone, nine dreamers had counted seven knots, and none of them had ever been taught the old net pattern that the eldest fisherman, squinting at her notes, recognized as his grandmother's.  Ilsa read the interpretation aloud to the seed, feeling ridiculous, and afterward the thermometer read half a degree warmer than the stone it sat on. 

Sunlight, she wrote.  Probably sunlight. 

So she moved the seed into shade and kept going.  For six mornings the dreams overlapped like fish scales: a lullaby with a missing verse that three neighbors each dreamed a line of, a boundary stone buried under torchflower exactly where a child said a blue dog had been digging.  The villagers argued over every reading, loudly, and Ilsa noticed that the arguing was itself the remembering, that no single sleeper held the verse but the ridge full of them did.  The seed gained four tenths of a gram. 

Humidity, she wrote, since the air was thickening and the southern sky had gone the color of a bruise.  Probably humidity. 

On the last dry night Maro asked her to sleep on the ridge too.  "An inspector must not contaminate the sample," she said, and then lay down anyway among the heather, because forty data points deserved one more. 

She dreamed of her own island's harbor song, the one the Registry had filed under Fables, sung by voices she did not know in an accent that was not hers.  At dawn she told it to the whole circle, and they interpreted it for her, gently, as they had for each other: nothing shelved is gone while someone still argues over it. 

When she picked up the seed, there was a hairline split along the owl's frown, and inside it something pale, either a root tip or the grain of the shell.  Her lens could not settle it. 

They planted it beside the stump as the first warm drops struck the blossoms.  In her ledger Ilsa wrote Unverified, pending one monsoon, and left Sorrel Tam on the map, glad for once not to know.
