Odile Marsh worked the odd hours at the Abara Library of Scientific Poetry, eleven at night until five, when the only patrons were people who could not sleep.  Tonight there were three: a retired chemist frowning at Lucretius, a night nurse reading odes to the mitochondrion, and a student asleep over a villanelle about entropy.  Each sat alone inside a cone of lamplight, and Odile watched them the way one watches snow, without wishing to interrupt it. 

They thought her eccentric, but every oddity had a reason.  She shelved in wool socks because shoes echoed under the dome; she whispered call numbers aloud because the ear catches errors the eye forgives; and she wore, in every season, a tattered silk scarf, because Mrs. Abara had left it to her with instructions. 

Mrs. Abara, a geometer before she was a librarian, had founded the place and kept its odd hours for forty years.  When Odile was nine and a patron's bored daughter, she had asked the old woman where stories went when a book was closed.  "They live in this building," Mrs. Abara had said, "and when you are ready, walk straight and you will find the place."  Odile had spent twenty years growing up, taking the job, and walking straight into walls.  The instructions pinned to the scarf said only: Straight is not what you think here. 

At three, mopping a coffee ring from the floor mosaic, she noticed for the first time that its interlocking fish were the fish on her scarf.  She unwound the silk and spread it across the desk.  Printed on it was a disk of fish, large at the center and shrinking toward the hem until they dissolved into fraying thread.  She knew the picture from Mrs. Abara's lectures: a map of the hyperbolic plane, where every fish is truly the same size and the rim is infinitely far away, so there is always more room toward the edge.  In that geometry a straight line looks like an arc, and it must meet the rim at a right angle.  One arc was stitched in red from a fish near the center, the fish the desk stood on, but moths had eaten its far end along with the hem. 

The tatters did not matter.  An arc that has to strike a circle squarely can be finished by anyone with a compass, and Odile, who kept one in the drawer for patrons checking sonnets about Euclid, finished it on tracing paper.  Then she walked it. 

In her socks she followed the curve across the mosaic, counting fish, bending past the nurse, who did not look up, and past the sleeping student, whose breath stirred a page.  The chemist watched her go by, a woman pacing an invisible line at three in the morning, and returned to his atoms without comment.  Near the wall the tiles shrank to the size of thumbnails and the shelves narrowed to hold only pamphlets, as if the room itself were thinning toward infinity.  Her arc met the wall squarely at a bay of elegies for extinct stars.  Behind the lowest shelf, a panel gave under her hand. 

Inside sat a reel-to-reel recorder under a dust cloth, and beside it forty years of analog memory wound in flat tins, each labeled with a date and an odd hour.  Odile wiped the heads with the clean corner of the scarf, threaded the oldest reel, and pressed the heavy key to reawaken the machine.  The needles twitched, the reels dragged, then turned, and a hiss like distant rain came from the small speaker, too soft to carry. 

"Three in the morning," said Mrs. Abara, young, unhurried.  "A welder is reading a sonnet about entropy under his breath, and he believes nobody hears him."  Then there was only the welder's murmur, a page turning, a chair's creak, magnetized and kept. 

Odile tried a later tin, dated the winter she turned nine.  "A child has just asked me where stories go," the voice said, older now.  "They go here, into whoever reads alone and is quietly witnessed; the edge has room for all of them, and someday for her." 

Odile sat a while among the thumbnail fish, twenty years of walls behind her.  Then she threaded a blank reel, wrote the date and the hour on its tin, and set the microphone on the pamphlet shelf, facing the room. 

"Three in the morning," she whispered.  "A chemist is arguing with Lucretius, a nurse is resting inside a cell, a student is dreaming in nineteen lines, and none of them knows they are a story." 

Across the room, the chemist glanced up at the librarian kneeling by the wall, alone and plainly content, and he turned his page.
