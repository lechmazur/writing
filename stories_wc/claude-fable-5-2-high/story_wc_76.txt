At dusk the tide went out of Skarrow Rock like a breath no one expected to be drawn again.  Odile Varn stood at the lighthouse door and watched the sea's slow exhale uncover the hunger stone.  It surfaced only at the lowest ebb, a black slab chained to the ledge, carved by keepers long dead.  IF YOU SEE ME, WEEP, said the first line.  The last line, in her grandfather's chisel hand, said THE LIGHT DIES WITH THE LAST VARN.  She was the last Varn, and the light had been dead since her grandfather's day.  Without it no boat would risk the reef twice, and her shelves held nine days of oatmeal. 

On the mainland she had been laughed at as the counter clockwise clock maker.  Her clocks ran backward on purpose, hands sweeping leftward toward the hour they began, because Odile believed anything built could be read back to its first intention.  A prophecy was only another mechanism, and she had come home to take it apart. 

The lamp room had defeated her for a month.  There was no wick, no oil cistern, no burner, only a great lens of smoky glass on a spindle, and a chain that dropped through the tower's hollow core into the dark.  Her father, who had kept a dark tower all his life, had left no instructions.  He had left, in a tin beneath his cot, a yoyo. 

It was ancient, older than the tower's mortar: two discs of the same smoky glass, a whalebone axle, a cord of braided gut.  Thrown the ordinary way, it dropped and hung dead.  A puzzle, then, and puzzles were her trade. 

She was neatly inventive by nature; now she laid a white cloth on the workbench, unwound the cord, and set each part in a row, numbered in chalk, last piece first.  Reading it backward, she found what a forward mind would miss.  The cord was not tied to the axle but looped, so the discs could spin freely at the bottom of the fall.  The axle's groove was cut as a left-hand spiral, so the toy would only bite and climb if it were wound counter clockwise. 

She reassembled it, wound it her own way, and threw.  At the end of the cord the discs spun, singing, and the smoky glass woke.  Light poured out of it, hoarded daylight, centuries of noons the glass had drunk and kept.  A latent radiance, patient as a seed, released only by speed.  Then the spin caught the spiral and the yoyo climbed back into her palm, dark again and warm. 

Odile laughed once, a cracked sound in the empty tower.  The lighthouse was the toy made enormous and turned upside down.  The lens was the disc, the chain was the cord, and the falling would be done by whatever weight hung at the chain's far end. 

In the lamp room the chain lay wrapped clockwise around the spindle, the ordinary way, the dead way.  She rewound it leftward, link by oiled link, until her palms bled.  Then she followed the chain down the hollow core with a lantern, out through a sea-gate at the tower's foot, across the dripping ledge. 

It ended at the hunger stone. 

The stone had never been a gravestone for the light; it was the light's weight, pinned to the ledge by one rusted iron cotter, poised above a cleft that only the lowest ebb emptied.  Her grandfather had wound it wrong, watched the light fail, and carved his grief onto an engine. 

Already the sea was turning; she could hear the exhale end.  There would be no ebb this low for another month, and she had nine days.  If the old chain snapped, she would have thrown her only chance into the sea with her own hands.  The thought should have frozen her; instead it made her blood sing.  Fate had carved her a last line, and she meant to initiate the one motion it had not foreseen. 

She set her chisel against the cotter and struck. 

The hunger stone tipped, hesitated like a held breath, and fell.  The chain screamed past her out of the tower, and far overhead the lens began to turn, leftward, faster, until the glass remembered every noon it had ever swallowed.  Light broke over Skarrow Rock, white and merciless, flinging her shadow across the reef and miles out over the black water. 

No boat answered; perhaps none would.  But when the spin caught, the chain would climb, hauling the stone up to fall again and again, with or without a Varn to watch.  Odile stood hungry in the borrowed daylight, on the far side of her written ending, and wound the yoyo for another throw.
