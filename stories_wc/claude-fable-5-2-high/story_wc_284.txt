Ilse Marrow climbed Zephyr Bluff in mist so thick that the sea below was only a sound.  It was the week the earth tilts toward spring, when winter loosens its grip one drip at a time.  Three years earlier the Service had burned her: file sealed, name struck, pension voided, every door politely shut.  A spy who waits for permission while a courier is dragged back from a border fence is, they said, broken equipment.  She had not disagreed. 

Then, in February, a parcel arrived from the estate of Tobias Wren, her old handler, who had died keeping the weather station on this bluff.  Inside lay the pillbox he had confiscated the night she was burned, the silver one issued to hold her last-resort capsule.  Its swivel lid was engraved with a ring of stars and one small arrow, its base with a ring of letters, and the pivot pin was snapped through.  His note read: "Mend it first, then come when the bluff thaws, and I will vouchsafe you the place where your unused courage waits." 

She had spent two evenings with a jeweler's file and a sliver of brass, fitting a new pin until the lid turned again, star by clicking star.  It was the first thing she had repaired since being declared beyond repair herself. 

Now the mist lay on the bluff like breath on a mirror, silver and without edges, and the old station stood shuttered inside it.  Ilse had chosen the hour with care.  The Service still kept a watcher in the village, a bored man with good binoculars, and mist was the one coat he could not see through.  Prudently daring, Wren had written in her first assessment, back when it was true.  After the fence she had kept the prudence and mislaid the rest. 

She found his message along the lee of the drystone wall, exactly where no one would think to look for writing.  All winter, after each snowfall, Wren had walked this line and pressed his cane's ferrule into the fresh layer, small clusters of holes that the next fall buried.  To the watcher's binoculars it had been an old man's constitutional across a blank white page.  But packed snow outlasts loose snow, and now, as the drift sank in the thaw, every pressed mark stood up as a little pillar of ice.  The accumulated winter was giving up its code all at once, and by noon the sun would take it back for good. 

Ilse knelt, the damp soaking her knees, and counted the first cluster: four pillars.  She turned the mended lid four stars and read the letter under the arrow.  With the pin broken the lid would have slid anywhere and said anything; fixed, it could only tell the truth.  Cluster by cluster she worked down the wall, writing nothing, holding each letter in her head the way she had been trained.  The mist muffled even her breathing. 

The message was short, because a dying man has only so many snowfalls.  It read: PELL ALIVE KESTREL MILL EAST BANK STILL WAITING FOR YOU. 

Ilse sat back on her heels.  Pell was the courier, the one she had watched dragged from the wire while she waited for a voice in her ear to say go.  She had come up here half hoping Wren would clear her name, hand her some proof that would mend her standing with the Service.  He had vouchsafed her something harder and better: not a pardon, but the unfinished job itself.  Her courage had never been lost, only unspent, and it had been waiting three years in a mill across a river, with a man who still expected her.  The broken thing was not her reputation.  It was a promise, and a promise could be fixed the way the pillbox had been, by one small true part put back where it belonged. 

She did not run for the border.  First she walked the wall once more and swept each cluster flat with her sleeve, so the watcher would find only slush.  She would need river charts, a boat no one would miss, and the next night of mist, which this season offered generously.  No voice would say go this time, and she found she no longer needed one. 

At the cliff edge a zephyr came up off the water, the soft west wind the bluff was named for, and opened the mist like a hand.  Far to the east, a thin bright line of river showed, then closed.  Ilse turned the lid of the pillbox until the arrow pointed that way, pocketed it, and went down through the silver quiet to begin.
