The Cobalt Observatory kept its silence the way a bell keeps a note, returning it a little late from the blue-tiled dome.  Autumn came in through the open shutter, and larch needles drifted across the floor where Ilse Maren sat embroidering galaxies.  The great chart, silver silk on indigo wool and older than her grandmother, hung in the hall below, and the captains of the seven watchtowers read their lamp-hours from its rising stars. 

Everyone at the observatory knew Ilse was simple.  She hummed to her needles, asked the astronomers whether stars got lonely, and laughed at her own echo.  She had built that foolishness stitch by stitch, because people say everything in front of a fool. 

What Ilse wanted, more than silk or wages, was to hear the stories no one else stayed to hear.  Her grandmother had taught her that every chart leaves stars out, and that the gaps are where the telling lives.  So she listened to the wick-trimmer, old Benno, who tended the eternal candle at the top of the dome and talked because nobody answered.  From him she learned a law so old the court had forgotten it: a flame taken from the eternal candle may be put out by no hand but the Queen's.  She kept it the way she kept odd lengths of thread. 

Nine days before the equinox, Castellan Drey, Warden of Hours, brought her a list of corrections.  Seven stars on the great chart were to be moved, each by the width of a single thread. 

"Do they itch, sir, that they need to shuffle?" she asked, and he smiled the way people smile at dogs. 

"By the equinox, girl," he said, and his boots echoed down the stair long after he was gone. 

Ilse stitched as she was told, and while she stitched she thought the way a needle thinks.  A needle never leaps; it can only enter one of the holes beside the last, so every pattern is found by trying the neighbors.  She did not ask what the corrections meant; she asked what each one sat next to.  They were not better astronomy, so perhaps vanity; if not vanity, a message; if not a message, a schedule.  Each moved star belonged to a tower, and each would now rise a little later than the one before.  On equinox night, when the captains came to synchronize their lamps to the chart, a slow gap of darkness would walk the ridge from the larch wood to the Queen's lodge.  Armed men could walk inside it. 

She listened harder that week, sweeping thread ends near doorways, and the unheard stories arrived: a cook feeding forty strangers in the wood, a page carrying sealed hours to the Chancellor.  No one would believe the fool against the Warden of Hours.  But a fool could be indulged. 

That equinox afternoon she climbed to Benno with a horn lantern and came down cradling a flame from the eternal candle, giggling loudly enough for the dome to giggle back.  "My stars want to meet their cousins," she told each tower captain, and each, laughing, let her light his watch-lamp from it.  Then she recited Benno's law in a singsong, and each captain, frowning, found it in his dusty book of orders.  No hand but the Queen's could darken those lamps now, whatever the chart said. 

At dusk the seven towers stood synchronized, not to Drey's shifted stars, but to one flame.  From the dome Ilse watched the ridge burn steady and unbroken, and watched the larch wood wait.  Nothing came out of it.  The silence held so long that it seemed to echo, the sound of forty men deciding to go home. 

Drey came up the stair near midnight, his face the color of old wax.  "Who told you to carry that flame?" 

"The stars did, sir," Ilse said, threading a needle badly on purpose.  "They itch." 

He stared at her for a long moment, found only a fool, and went back down into a court where his hour had passed without him. 

Before dawn she took her smallest scissors to the great chart and unpicked his seven stars.  Under the fourth, the wool showed older needle-holes, a thread's width the other way.  Someone had moved this star before, in some earlier autumn, for some earlier plot that no one had ever told.  Drey's night was only the first knot; the coup was still coming, and now she held its thread.  Ilse hummed, for anyone listening, and pulled the loose end gently.  Above her the eternal candle burned, the dome gave back her small tune a little late, and she leaned in to hear what the silence would say next.
