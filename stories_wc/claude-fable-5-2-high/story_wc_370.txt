At eleven minutes to midnight, Harrow Street Station breathed out its last commuters and settled like a lake.  On the mezzanine, behind glass anyone could see through, stood a room labeled Lost Property: Books.  Thousands passed it daily and saw nothing worth wanting, which was exactly how the Society of the Margin kept its library secret. 

Odile Marsh had been its keeper for nineteen years, and had not left the room for most of them.  She was not cold; she was the opposite.  She felt other people the way a tuning fork feels a struck bell, so a crowded platform rang through her until she could not find her own note.  So she loved the world from behind glass: mending the umbrellas left in the returns bin, setting a cup of tea outside the door each night for the cleaner, then slipping into the stacks to hide before he came. 

Tonight the returns bin held one book, a slim treatise on paradox, and when she opened it a crumpled invitation fell into her palm.  She knew the handwriting because it was hers. 

The Society invites Mr. T. Wren to be received at midnight. 

Nineteen years ago, in her first week, she had meant to write Renn, the celebrated logician, and her pen had slipped one letter toward the man who mopped the concourse.  Tobias Wren had arrived in a pressed shirt, holding the card like a ticket to somewhere better.  The members had been kind in the way that is worse than cruelty, and he had crushed the invitation in his fist, apologized for existing, and gone.  Odile had felt his shame land in her own chest like a swallowed stone.  She had hidden that night, and in a sense had never come out. 

Now she smoothed the card on the desk, expecting only the old ache.  Instead she found pencil on the back: a column of shelf marks in a careful, unschooled hand.  A mistake, she thought, is only a sentence nobody has finished reading.  She took her lamp and followed the list. 

The Society's single rule was that every reader must answer the previous reader in the margin, so each book was a conversation folded inside a conversation.  At every shelf mark, beneath the confident ink of scholars, she found the same soft pencil.  Wren had kept his cleaner's key.  For nineteen years he had walked out of this room each night and never once left it, reading while the floors dried, answering philosophers who would have laughed at his shoes.  His notes were better than theirs: plainer, kinder, and more often right. 

Odile stood among the shelves and let the paradoxes nestle into one another like bowls.  A library concealed by being seen held a recluse who felt everyone, who held an error that had chosen correctly, which held a man who stayed by leaving. 

The final shelf mark led back to the treatise itself.  On its last page he had written, dated that morning: Retiring tonight at twelve.  Returning what was never mine.  Thank you for the tea. 

The station clock showed four minutes to midnight.  Out on the mezzanine a mop bucket squeaked closer, and her body turned toward the stacks from long habit.  Then she understood what his margins had been teaching her.  She did not have to choose between the crowd and the cave; she could leave her hiding and still stay in her quiet, the way he had left the Society and stayed in its books. 

She opened the door. 

Tobias Wren, gray now, stopped with his hand halfway to the teacup.  She braced for the bell of another person and it came, but it was not shame she felt in him; it was a wide, settled calm, and it steadied her like a hand. 

"I wrote the wrong name," she said, holding out the creased card.  "It took me nineteen years to read my own mistake, Mr. Wren, and it says I invited the right man." 

He looked at the card, then at her, without hurry.  "I'm finished here at midnight," he said. 

"Then leave the way you always have," Odile said, and pressed the key back into his palm when he offered it. 

The clock began to strike.  He drank the tea standing in the doorway, neither in nor out, and she stood beside him in plain sight, and the empty station seemed to grow larger around them without growing lonelier.  Afterward she would hide only one thing: the crumpled invitation, slipped back into the treatise, waiting for the next mistake wise enough to find it.
