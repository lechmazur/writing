Ilse went down into the catacomb orchard with a burned tarot card against her ribs and no plan beyond arriving.  That was her way.  At nineteen she had held her own card, the Tower, over a candle because she believed no one should be born owing a fate to anybody.  She still believed it.  But the flame had eaten three holes through the pasteboard, and for eleven years she had felt three matching drafts blowing through her, cold places where purpose should have caught and held. 

Beneath the city, pear trees grew pale in the ossuary dark.  Their roots snaked between femurs and through the eye sockets of stacked skulls, drinking whatever the dead had left unfinished.  From every branch hung tarot cards on red thread, the unlived destinies of those shelved below, and everyone above knew you could barter for one. 

The keeper met her at the first arch, a broad old woman called Maud with pruning scars on her hands.  She was lighting every second lamp along the corridor and leaving the rest dark. 

"Oil is dear," Maud said.  "Trades hold only while these burn, so do your choosing within the glow." 

Ilse showed her the card.  Maud turned it over, looked through its holes at the lamp, and handed it back the way one returns a hurt bird. 

"Nobody living will take this, girl." 

"Then I'll trade with somebody dead." 

"You can."  Maud's voice was fiercely gentle, like a hand closing over a wrist near a fire.  "The dead cannot refuse, and that is exactly why you should think hard about it." 

Ilse walked the rows in the half-light.  She was an inlayer by trade, and by compulsion she inlaid meaning everywhere she went.  Above ground she had pressed shell into cracked doorsills for luck and copper into a bridge rail where a friend once jumped, filling every gap but her own.  Even here her fingers itched at each chipped skull. 

Then she saw the Star.  It hung from a young tree whose roots cradled a small skeleton, a child's, the card crisp and whole and shining faintly.  A whole life of hope and healing, never spent.  Her drafts howled for it. 

She had the red thread half untied before she understood what the trade would do.  Her ruined Tower would hang here in its place, and those roots would drink it, and a child who could not say no would lie tangled forever in her collapse.  She had burned her card so that no fate could trammel her.  She would not trammel someone smaller with the ashes. 

She retied the thread, and her hands shook, and down the corridor the first lamp guttered out. 

Maud was watching from the arch, pruning hook on her shoulder.  "Most take it," she said. 

"I came to patch holes, not to dig one in a child."  Ilse sat down among the roots and opened her pouch of shell, copper, and fish glue.  "Is there another kind of barter, where the dead get something they would want?" 

Maud considered her for the length of a held breath.  "They want what anyone wants, to have meant something.  Give them that, fairly, and the orchard may give back." 

So Ilse worked while the lamps died one by one.  She set a chip of shell into the child's cracked brow and said aloud that it meant someone had stopped here and stayed.  She wound copper around a split root so it meant mending, and around a stranger's finger bone so it meant a promise kept.  It was the same reckless thing she had always done, spending herself on gaps that were not hers, only now she asked for something in return. 

The orchard answered in its slow way.  A flake of bark loosened under her palm.  A pear seed dropped into her lap, and from the child's tree a single thread of red came free without taking the Star with it. 

She laid the burned card on her knee and inlaid the three holes: bark in the first, the seed in the second, and across the third a lattice of red thread and copper that held nothing but the light passing through.  The Tower still fell on its scorched face.  But now it fell toward a root, a seed, and a window.  The drafts in her did not vanish; they narrowed to something she could breathe around. 

"That is no destiny I know," Maud said, holding the last lit lamp. 

"No," Ilse said, "but it is paid for, and nobody else is carrying it." 

She pocketed the card against her ribs and promised the child she would return with more shell.  Then she climbed toward the evening, before the glow went out.
