Odile had not slept properly since the smog thickened, so she painted at night in the rooftop greenhouse, where the filters hummed and the saffron crocuses kept their violet mouths shut.  Eleven floors below, the city coughed under its brown lid.  Tonight a waxing moon, thin as a fingernail paring, found a gap in the haze and laid one pale stripe across her worktable.  The stripe landed on the calligrapher's brush. 

It was a slender bamboo thing, its dark bristles ending in a crescent of white hair, as if it had been dipped once in the moon and never rinsed.  Its owner had left it behind last winter.  On the worst-air night of that year, Odile had found an old woman wheezing in the stairwell, helped her up to the filtered warmth, and steeped three red threads of saffron in hot water for her.  They had shared no language.  The woman had only drunk, breathed, and then written nine swift characters on the fogged glass with her wet brush before slipping away at dawn.  By noon the condensation had run, and the words were gone. 

A librarian later squinted at Odile's clumsy sketch and guessed Sarrowic, a marsh tongue drowned with its valley when the reservoir came, one of a dozen lost languages the city had paved over without noticing.  No living speakers were registered.  Odile suspected the registry did not count kitchens. 

She needed those nine characters back, because the tenants' committee would vote at the full moon on whether to lock the roof door on bad-air nights.  "You let strangers breathe our air, and not one has ever come back to say thanks," Mr. Hale had told her.  "Strangers forget."  Odile believed otherwise, and she meant to prove that strangers remember kindness the only way a painter could: by putting the old woman's words where the whole district could see them. 

The trouble was that her waking mind could not recall a single stroke.  But insomnia had taught her about the country between awake and asleep, and when moonlight touched the white tip of the brush, she let herself drift there.  The daydream came the color of steeped saffron: the old woman's wrist turning, a hook, a long falling line, a dot like a seed.  Odile surfaced, and her hand still knew the motion.  She painted the first character in gold ochre on the first pane of the south wall, then wrote in her ledger: Pane one, night one, eight remaining.  That was how she hoped, methodically, the way she primed a canvas. 

Each night the moon fattened and pushed a little more light through the smog, and each night the daydream lasted one character longer.  When clouds or soot swallowed the moon, she did not guess; she marked the ledger No light, watered the crocuses, and waited.  A guessed word would be a forgery, and she was trying to rouse a language, not invent one. 

On the eve of the vote, under a moon one sliver short of round, she painted the ninth character and hung a lamp behind the glass.  The sentence glowed amber over the district, unreadable to her, addressed to anyone.  Nothing answered.  The towers stood in their haze like unlit candles, and Odile sat among the crocuses with the brush in her lap, too tired to sleep and too stubborn to stop looking. 

Near three, a window lit on the ninth floor of the block across the avenue.  Then another, lower down, and a third far to the east, each one small and yellow, each one staying on. 

A little before dawn someone knocked on the roof door, which was not locked.  A young man stood there in a courier's jacket, a filter mask pushed up on his forehead, holding a paper bag in both hands.  "My grandmother has described this greenhouse since winter, but in the smog she never knew which roof," he said.  "Tonight she looked out and read her own handwriting." 

He read the panes aloud, and the lost syllables were soft and reedy, like water moving through grass.  "It says, 'Here a stranger gave me breath; I will not forget.'"  He nodded toward the lit windows.  "Those are the ones who can still read it; she woke them all." 

In the bag were a jar of honey and a note of thanks in two languages, which Odile decided Mr. Hale should see before the vote.  She held out the moon-tipped brush, but he shook his head.  "She says keep it, and she will come teach you what you wrote." 

Odile put the kettle on and counted six red threads into two cups.  Behind her the crocuses were opening, and for the first time in months she thought she might sleep.
