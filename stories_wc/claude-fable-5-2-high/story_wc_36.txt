The guardianship ended the night Tavin vanished, and the order took Isme's title before the thunder had finished echoing.  They left the relic with her only because no one else would climb the Thunder-echo range to fetch it.  For nineteen years she had kept the gilded reed flute locked in its cedar case on the Keeper's Ledge, unplayed, as the rule demanded.  The flute could weave a path between this world and the one beneath its echoes, and the order feared that path more than it understood it.  Tavin, her apprentice, had been young enough to want understanding.  He had taken the flute while she slept, played it on the ledge into a breaking storm, and stepped into the sound.  She found the flute on the stone and nothing else. 

Now, with no title left to protect, Isme wanted only one thing from that failure: what it could teach her.  Grief was a closed case; a lesson might be a door. 

She began with the driftwood.  It had always turned up in the high canyons after storms, pale and salt-cured, though no sea lay within a hundred leagues.  As guardian she had swept it off the ledge like litter.  Now she gathered every piece, because wood from another shore meant the seam between worlds tore open somewhere without any flute at all. 

Her method was the plain, slow one range folk used to mark trails in fog.  Whenever thunder rolled through the range, she stood still, traced the echo with her ear, and laid a length of driftwood on the ground pointing toward the place where the sound died.  Then she walked to that place and waited for the next storm.  Marker by marker, ridge by ridge, a line of bleached arrows grew across the mountains.  She spoke to no one, and the canyons kept their long hush around her work. 

By autumn the arrows had stopped wandering.  From every ridge they pointed inward to a single slot canyon, a full day's walk from the Keeper's Ledge. 

Isme stood at its mouth during the next storm and felt the first insight land like a stone in her chest.  The thunder entered the canyon and did not come back.  What followed was avalanchingly quiet, a silence that came down the walls all at once and buried every smaller sound beneath it.  Driftwood lay heaped on the canyon floor, still damp.  The seam was here, and it opened in the quiet, not the noise.  Tavin had played in the wrong place at the wrong moment, into thunder that shredded his note and wove him only halfway through. 

The second insight was harder.  He had erred because she had taught him nothing of the flute, and she had taught him nothing because she had guarded it for nineteen years without once asking what it was for.  The order had mistaken silence for safety, and so had she. 

Isme knelt on the canyon floor and set the cedar case before her.  Her hands, which had latched it ten thousand times, had to learn the opposite motion to uncase the flute.  The gilding had worn thin at the finger holes, rubbed away by players older than any rule.  It had been made to be used. 

She waited through one more roll of thunder, and when the great quiet fell, she breathed a single low note into it.  The note did not echo.  It ran forward like a thread through cloth, and the canyon walls thinned around it until she saw both worlds at once: red stone, and behind the stone a grey shore under a windless sky.  She stepped along the note, weaving between them, sand and gravel alternating under her boots. 

On the grey sand lay a row of driftwood she had not arranged.  Each piece pointed down the shore, one after another, the way range folk mark a trail in fog.  Tavin had lived, and walked on, marking his road so that he could be found. 

Isme took a length of driftwood from the tide line and laid it at her feet, pointing back toward the seam, so the way home would be marked for two.  Then she raised the flute, no longer its keeper but its student, and followed his arrows into the quiet.
