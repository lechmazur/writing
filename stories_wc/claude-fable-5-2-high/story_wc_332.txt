On the Murmurs, seven islands where wind combs through sea-drilled basalt and makes the whole archipelago whisper, the library opened at midnight and closed at dawn.  Maren kept those odd hours for the night-fishing crews, the lighthouse relief, and the sleepless, and lately for herself.  Since Sola died in the spring, daylight had felt like a room someone had just left.  Maren slept through it, and every sleep delivered the same dream: a doorway made of morning, and Sola in it, turning to speak.  Every waking, the dream bolted before the face arrived. 

Sola had chased something like it for years, a painting of all seven island dawns at once, which she called her runaway dream because it escaped every sheet of paper she set in front of it.  Now it ran through Maren instead, and she wanted, with a librarian's stubbornness about lost things, to catch it and shelve it where it belonged.  All she had to work with was Sola's watercolor palette, which sat on the returns desk like an overdue book.  Seven pans, cracked dry as tide flats, and Maren could not paint. 

One night in October, at the slack hour past four when no one came, she lifted the palette to dust beneath it and a pan of cobalt fell out.  In the tin well underneath, scratched with a pin, was a number: 551.5.  Anyone else would have seen a batch code.  Maren saw a call number, and under the other six pans she found six more. 

She walked the stacks with a lamp while the islands whispered at the windows.  Each number led to a book Sola had borrowed in her last winter, a tide atlas, a field guide to terns, a history of the lights, and in each margin, in soft pencil, Sola had written an island, a bearing, and a few words.  Kittering, east by north, where you laughed at my hat.  Sallow Rock, due east, where I should have said it sooner.  It was a cipher of longing, keyed to the one reader who would be awake to break it, and the seventh margin held the instruction.  I could never hold all seven mornings on one page, but you paint with light, my love, so put them on one frame, and then wet my colors. 

Maren had photographed sunrise after every closing for twenty years, her walk home doubling as her only art, and Sola had always refused to call it anything less than painting.  So she began, which seemed a foolish thing for a widow of fifty-eight with bad knees and a borrowed skiff.  On seven clear mornings that autumn, she locked the library, rowed out through the last of the dark, and set her tripod on the bearing Sola had given.  She never advanced the film.  Each dawn went onto the same frame, a short breath of exposure, one horizon laid over another the way Sola laid wash over wash.  Those minutes before the sun were the quietest she had ever stood in, a hush of improbable beginnings, when even the basalt held its whisper as if listening for her shutter.  At each island she also filled a small vial with rainwater pooled in the rock at her feet. 

After the seventh morning she developed the frame in the library's map-room sink, with the blinds down and the islands murmuring outside.  The print came up slowly in the tray.  Seven horizons had braided into a single threshold, a doorway made of morning, exactly as she dreamed it.  In the middle, where seven suns had stacked their light, the paper had stayed pure white, a brightness roughly the height of a woman, with no face in it at all. 

Maren waited for the old grief to tell her she had failed.  Instead she heard Sola, brush in her teeth, explaining for the hundredth time that the brightest part of any watercolor is the paper you have the nerve to leave alone. 

She opened the vials and let one drop of island rain fall into each cracked pan, and the pigments stirred, darkened, and woke.  It took so little to vitalize what she had assumed was finished.  With Sola's smallest brush and no skill whatsoever, she tinted the seven dawns, cobalt for Kittering, rose for Sallow Rock, working inward until the color reached the edge of the white.  There she stopped. 

The dream had not been running from her; it had been running here, to a shape she was never meant to fill in.  She hung the print by the east window, gave it a call number of its own, and walked home through the whispering dawn to sleep, for once, without chasing anything.
