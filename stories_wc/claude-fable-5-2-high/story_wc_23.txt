The hydraulics failed on the ninth of October, with a sigh like a held breath finally given up.  The ram bled its last oil down the mast, and the great signal lamp of Cape Varrow sank into its well and stayed there.  Ines stood on the gallery under a sky crowded with stars and understood that no one was coming to fix it.  The station had been struck from the lists eleven years ago, the autumn the fish died and the whole coast packed its boats and fled south.  She had come back alone, because her brother Tomas had gone into the water off this reef during that flight, and she could not live anywhere that did not know it. 

In the camps they had called her the patchwork navigator, since nothing she steered by was whole.  Her chart was sailcloth stitched from the scraps of other pilots' charts, and her star lore was half her father's, half a drowned mate's.  Where a village had been erased, she had sewn the hole shut and written its name on the seam. 

The phone rang at midnight, three days after the lamp went down.  It was the old coastguard line, a cable that ran through every dead harbor to the south, and it had not rung in eleven years. 

"Where the cannery burned," a woman said through the static.  "Six hulls, going up." 

Then the line died. 

Ines found the cannery's seam on her chart and sat a long time with her finger on it.  Going up meant north.  That same week the geese had passed over the cape heading north too, into the teeth of autumn, a reverse migration her father would have called madness and she now suspected was homesickness with wings. 

The calls kept coming, always at midnight, when the old schedule had required every harbor to report.  "Below the widow's light," a man said one night, and on another a child whispered, "The school with no roof."  They gave no names and no coordinates, only the landmarks they had left, which were the things that were gone.  Ines pinned each one to a seam and watched the pins creep toward her, harbor by harbor, up the geography of loss she had spent a decade learning to read. 

The line's strength fluctuated with the phases of the moon.  At the quarters, when the tides were lazy, the voices came clear enough to hear breathing; at full and new, the springs flooded the cable junction on the flats and drowned every word.  She did the arithmetic twice.  The boats would reach Varrow reef at the dark of the moon, on the blackest night, with the phone dead and the lamp in its well.  It was the reef that had taken Tomas. 

So she began to build. 

She cut the dead ram out of the mast and dragged a spar up from the boathouse to make a lever arm, lamp at one end, a net of beach stones at the other.  The arm needed a pivot that would not seize in salt air, and the station's steel was all rust.  She stood a while before the shelf where she kept the one thing she had carried both ways: a lion's paw of bronze, green with patina, the broken foot of her mother's censer.  It had snapped off in her fist the night their boat rolled, while Tomas, clutching the bowl, went under with the rest.  Its socket, where the leg had joined the bowl, was nearly wide enough for a pivot pin, and she filed the pin to fit. 

"You were made to hold fire up," she told it, and bolted it into the mast head.  Her hands bled; she cried once, hard, with her forehead against the cold iron, and then kept working. 

On the last clear night of the waning quarter the phone rang, and the woman's voice said, "Next is the reef where we lost them." 

"Look for a low star that does not move," Ines said, the first words the line had ever let her send back. 

At the dark of the moon she leaned on the spar, and the stones dropped, and the lamp rose on its green bronze foot into a sky so full of stars it seemed to be joining them.  She waited through the black hours with the dead phone in her lap.  Near dawn, six small lights answered from beyond the reef, turned, and came through the gap one by one, north, the wrong way, the way home. 

Tomas was not among them, and never would be.  But the water that held him had a light over it now, and Ines, for the first time, stitched onto her chart a place that was still there.
