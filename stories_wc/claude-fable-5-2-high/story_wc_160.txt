The amphitheater inside Mount Sorrel breathed twice a day, and Odile Varn had learned to work between its breaths.  At dusk the dormant volcano drew cool air down through the tiers, and the old stone shrank until every joint clenched shut.  At dawn the deep heat pushed back, the building exhaled, and seams opened wide enough to swallow a coin.  Above the highest crumbling row stood her floodgates, six iron leaves holding the crater lake off the stage.  She had rebuilt their gearing from clock parts and a wrecked tram, and the valley called her ingenious when it wanted something.  This week it wanted two things. 

The farmers below wanted the gates opened before their terraces turned to dust.  The Keepers wanted them shut forever, because a full release would scour the amphitheater, the last thing their ancestors had built, into gravel.  Both sides were right, which was the trouble, and both expected her answer by morning. 

Her old master, Brann, would have known what to do, but Brann had died in spring, leaving her an opened envelope and an antique surveyor's chain.  The council clerk had opened the envelope first, found a parts invoice inside, and handed it over with a shrug.  Odile had been angry about that invoice for months.  Thirty years of gruff corrections, and the man could not leave her one kind sentence. 

Tonight, with no answer and no sleep, she turned the envelope under her lamp and finally looked where a machinist should have looked first: at the join.  The leftover adhesive on the flap was wrong.  Brann had not licked a gummed edge; he had sealed it with pine pitch in separate dabs, and through her loupe each dab glittered with brass filings.  She scraped one free, warmed it, and smelled the chain's own polish.  Then she measured the gaps between dabs against the flap's length and felt her hands tremble.  The flap was a hundred millimeters long, the chain a hundred links, and the pitch sat at seventeen, forty-two, and eighty-eight.  He had known a clerk would read any letter, so he had written in glue, in a language only his apprentice would bother to read.  A plain answer would have been seized by one faction and smashed by the other. 

She carried the chain down to the stage an hour before dawn.  Moonlight fell through the broken crater rim and the ferns rooted there, spotting the tiers with silver and shadow, so that nothing looked entirely ruined or entirely whole.  She pinned the first brass handle at the stage's center stone, where Brann used to stand and criticize her welds, and walked the chain straight up the middle stair.  At link seventeen she found only a worn step, sealed tight.  She almost quit, then understood, and sat down to wait for the building to breathe. 

Dawn grayed the rim.  Warmth rose through the rock, the tiers ticked and sighed, and as the amphitheater exhaled, the seam at link seventeen opened around a bronze plate no wider than her palm.  Behind it was a sluice throat, ancient, clean, angled beneath the seats.  There was another at forty-two and a third at eighty-eight, just under her gates.  The ancestors had never meant the lake to stay out; they had built channels to carry it under the audience, across the stage in a shallow mirror, and on to the valley.  Preserving the place and using it had once been the same act.  Brann had found the plates, kept the secret from zealots on both sides, and trusted her to finish the arithmetic. 

By midmorning the sun would swell the upper stone and pinch every seam closed again.  She pried the three plates, wedged them with tram bolts, then climbed to the gallery and cracked a single gate by one hand's width.  The iron began to tremble, and so did she, because if she was wrong the lake would take the stairs, the stage, and Brann's name with them. 

The water vanished into the top sluice instead.  She heard it running under the tiers like applause in another room, and then it slid out across the stage, ankle deep, and poured through the old archway toward the terraces.  Not one stone moved. 

When the farmers and Keepers arrived to shout at her, they stood on opposite sides of the mirror and found nothing to shout.  Odile wound up the chain, and on the back of its last brass tally she found fresh scratches in Brann's cramped hand: I KNEW YOU WOULD CHECK THE JOIN.  It was the kindest sentence he had ever written, and he had hidden it where only work could find it.  She laughed, wiped her eyes, and went to oil the gate.
