In Sorrel Cove, they called Wren Calloway the morning fog saxophonist, because nobody had ever heard her play in clear weather.  Nobody knew why, either, and that ignorance was the whole point of her life.  Before dawn she carried her father's tenor down the cliff stairs to the grotto under the headland, where the fog always arrived first.  The case still smelled of his cork grease and peppermints, a smell that could make her nine years old without warning. 

The grotto was radiant even in mist, because at its heart the roof had fallen in centuries ago and left an atrium walled with quartz.  Thousands of natural prisms leaned from the walls, and the first gray light broke on them and bled pale color into the fog.  Her grandfather, the harbor telegraph operator, had named it the prism atrium, and he had been the first to understand what it said. 

Because it did speak, every foggy dawn, as dewdrops formed on the prisms and fell ringing onto the hollow stone below.  Short drops, long drops, pauses: Morse, steady as a patient hand on a key.  He had copied eleven words before he stopped and burned the page: WHEN ONE LISTENER HEARS THIS TO THE END THE SEA TAKES.  A prophecy spoken in code, addressed to whoever was clever and lonely enough to finish it.  And fog carries sound, straight up through the open roof and over a town full of retired radio men and curious children. 

So the family took on what they called the vested hush, a silence deeded to them like land and owed like taxes.  Her father had taught her the method on this same wet ledge, his hand between her shoulder blades.  You could not drown the code out, because the atrium answered loud playing by ringing louder.  You had to work through negative space, he said, the way you hide a drawing by shading in all the paper around it.  The meaning lived in the gaps between drops, so you whispered through the horn, breath-soft subtones laid exactly into each pause, until dots and dashes blurred into plain rain.  He had called her tone quietly vivid, color with the volume turned all the way down, and said the grotto would like her. 

He had been dead since February. 

This morning the fog was thick as wool, and the dew gathered fast.  Wren wet the reed, set her feet, and waited for the first drop.  It fell, and then the next, and her breath stopped in her chest.  Every operator has a fist, a rhythm as personal as handwriting, and the atrium's had always been a stranger's.  Today it was her father's, with the small hesitation before each dash that had lived in his playing too. 

She lowered the horn. 

Three letters rang out unhushed into the colored mist: W, R, E.  It was not beginning with WHEN anymore; it was beginning with her.  The ache that rose in her was sweet and terrible, like finding his voice on an old answering machine.  She could stand here and let him talk, just once, all the way to the end. 

All the way to the end. 

The phrase turned cold in her, and she understood how the thing worked: it learned its keepers, and it baited the hook with whatever they missed most.  Her grandfather must have heard someone, too, and burned the page anyway.  Above her, a gull cried, and somewhere up in the town a kettle would be starting, a window opening onto the fog. 

Wren raised the tenor and whispered.  She did not fight his rhythm; she knew it better than her own pulse, and she slid her breath into every hesitation, every gap, filling the silences he was using to reach her.  The letters lost their edges.  Her name dissolved into a soft, senseless patter, and over it floated the lullaby he used to play her, so quiet it was mostly air.  And there, in the rests of that old tune, she found the real shape of him: not a voice in the dripping, but the places where he had taught her to breathe. 

She played until the sun cleared the headland, the prisms blazed, and the last dew dried without a word.  The fog thinned to nothing, and the atrium was only stone and light again.  Up in Sorrel Cove, people would remember hearing a saxophone in their sleep and feel homesick for no reason.  Wren packed the horn into its peppermint dark and climbed the stairs, already listening for tomorrow's weather.
