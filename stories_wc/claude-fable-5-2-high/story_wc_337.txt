"Before you ring the bell, Warden, let me tell you a secret," Odile said, not turning from the net.  Hesper stood in the doorway of the prism sanctuary with her hand on the bell rope and did not pull.  Above them the dome of cut glass took the starlight apart and laid it across the floor in thin, exact colors.  The centuries-old fisherman's net hung in the middle of that light, gray and heavy, every knot of it a small closed fist. 

"You know what I do here," Odile said.  "I restore the death masks of the drowned when they break, and for eleven years they have not stopped breaking." 

Along the walls the masks looked down from their pegs, pale clay faces seamed with her glue and gold.  One of them ticked, the way cooling pottery ticks, though the night was not cold. 

"They crack from the inside," she said.  "That is the first part of the secret." 

She slid a tapestry needle into a knot near the hem, its blunt tip parting the strands without cutting a fiber. 

Hesper stepped forward.  "The widows tied those knots so the dead would rest." 

"The widows tied those knots so they would not have to listen," Odile said.  "Every one binds a last word that somebody on shore could not bear to hear.  That is why this place accumulates silence the way a cellar accumulates damp, and why you call it peace." 

The knot gave.  A sound came out of it, small and perfectly clear, a man's voice saying a woman's name once.  A band of green starlight on the floor brightened, then sank into the flagstones like water into sand.  On the wall, one mask stopped ticking. 

Hesper's hand fell from the rope.  "What did you do?" 

"I let him finish," Odile said.  "A spirit cannot go to ground while its last word is held in the air.  It paces.  It pushes at the only face it has left." 

She worked on, and the telling and the unpicking kept the same slow pace.  A knot opened on a curse, another on a laugh, another on a boy's plain request for his mother.  Each time a color flared and went down into the stone, and each time the sanctuary grew quieter in a different way, lighter, like a room after a guest has finally said goodbye.  Hesper understood, with a clarity that frightened her, that she had never heard quiet before, only the interlaced hush of the net, hundreds of held breaths woven tight. 

"You said the first part," she managed. 

Odile's needle paused over a knot paler than the rest, the twine barely weathered.  Her hands, steady through a hundred mended faces, were not steady now. 

"This one is mine.  Eleven years ago my brother Ansel went over the side in fog, forty yards off the breakwater.  I was on the stones, and I heard him call, and I knew that if I answered I would have to go in after him.  So I stood still and told everyone the fog had been silent.  Then I came here at night and tied what I had heard into your net, as the widows taught.  His mask broke within the month.  I asked for the restorer's bench so I could keep gluing it shut." 

Hesper looked at the mask nearest the bench, the one with the most gold in it, a young face split and rejoined so often it was more seam than clay.  It was ticking fast. 

"I thought I was mending them," Odile said.  "I was only burying the same sound deeper, and tonight I mean to exhume it.  Ring the bell now if you want the hush back." 

Hesper thought of her own mother's fingers on this net, of a knot somewhere in it that she had never dared to look for.  She crossed the floor and held the net taut with both hands. 

Odile set the needle and eased the pale knot apart.  The voice that came out was not a scream.  It was her name, called the way he used to call her in for supper, a little impatient, sure of being answered. 

"I'm here," Odile said, eleven years late.  "I heard you, Ansel.  I heard you then." 

Violet light flared at her feet and went down into the stone, slowly, like someone lowering himself into a chair after a long walk.  The gold-seamed mask went still. 

The secret was told, and the net between the two women was looser by a dozen knots, with most of the night left. 

"Show me how to hold the needle," Hesper said, and through the open door, for the first time, they could both hear the sea.
