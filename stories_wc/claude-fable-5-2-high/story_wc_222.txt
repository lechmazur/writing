On the third morning that the sun rose green in the north, Wren Halloway climbed out of the clouds with a stolen lid buttoned against her heart.  Above her the spire of Saint Gnomon stood sheathed in ice, a white needle the old keepers had raised to read the hours for every valley below.  Now the sky was wrong, the shadows fell sideways, and the valley bells rang noon at three different times. 

Wren was a thief with manners.  She took only from those who could spare it, and in every emptied place she left a chess piece carved by her own knife, hollow, with one true secret rolled inside.  A truth for a treasure seemed only courteous. 

She had broken that rule once.  At fourteen, clumsy and hungry, she had dipped into a pilgrim's pocket in the valley market, and the lid of a brass pocket sundial had come away in her fingers.  She ran, and left nothing.  Engraved inside the lid was a table of tiny numbers for nudging a shadow toward the true hour, and under it four words: amend daily, marvel always.  For eleven years those words had itched like an unpaid debt.  When rumor said the keepers were reckoning blind because their master table had been lost long ago, she knew whose pocket it had been, and she started climbing. 

She meant to leave the lid and vanish.  But the chapel she crept into at midnight held no altar, only a chessboard on a plinth of blue ice and a card lettered in a cheerful hand.  The Order plays against the Unknown, it read, and the Unknown is winning, praise be.  Wren laughed before she could stop herself, and the sound skittered up the frozen walls.  These people prayed by playing; they were sacredly playful, and a gift dropped in the dark would be a poor move against them. 

So she did what she did best.  She lifted the Unknown's black knight from its square and set her own carved knight in its place, with a secret inside: I stole from one of you, I am sorry, and I am afraid to say so to your face.  Then she hid in a storeroom among coils of plumb line, one hand on her rope, ready to run. 

The next midnight her knight was gone.  In its square stood a white bishop of walrus ivory, plainly hand-carved, with a seam at its base.  The paper inside said: A thief who apologizes is rare weather, and so is this sky, and both frighten me in the good way; signed, Oda, Keeper of the Dial.  Wren read it four times. 

For three nights they traded pieces instead of moves, and the board filled with confessions.  Her rook admitted the market, the fumble, the eleven years.  Oda's pawn admitted that she had kept the lidless dial anyway, and had wondered ever since what sort of pickpocket takes half a thing.  On the fourth night Wren hollowed her broadest queen, fitted the brass lid inside, and wrote: Here is your table, so amend your hours and forgive me. 

She should have left then.  Instead she waited, heart knocking, for one more piece. 

It was a white king, and the note in it was long.  The table will not mend anything, Oda had written, because it was cut for the old sky and this is a new one.  I do not need my lid back.  I need steady hands to hold the line while I measure a sun nobody has met.  Come to the roof at dawn, and bring your knife. 

Wren checked her rope twice, as any sensible thief would.  Then she left it coiled in the storeroom and took the stairs up. 

The roof was a disc of ice above an ocean of cloud, and a small old woman in a patched habit stood at its center, grinning at the green north.  "You are taller than your handwriting," Oda said, and handed her the lid and a plumb line. 

The sun came up where no sun belonged, and the weighted string threw a shadow thin as a hair across the ice.  Oda called out a number, laughing at how wrong it was.  Wren turned the lid to the light, scratched through the first figure of the old table, and with her carving knife cut the new one beside it. 

"One amended," said Oda, "and three hundred sixty-four mornings to go, if the sky behaves, which I hope it will not." 

"What does that make me?" Wren asked. 

"An apprentice of wonder," said Oda, "and the pay is terrible." 

Wren spread her arms to keep her balance on the ice, and found she was holding them open to the whole strange sky.
