The door of the radar dome was still shuddering when Ansel Voss set down his pen.  Mirela had not left; she had only slammed it, and now she stood with her back to him among the hanging puppets, breathing hard.  The argument had been loud enough to wake the pigeons in the dead antenna housing, and the hush it left behind felt scooped out, like the dome itself.  The air force had decommissioned the station thirty years ago, and the troupe had rented its shell for almost nothing, because nobody else wanted a room built for listening to the sky. 

Ansel had been a calligrapher of the urbane sort, a man who lettered diplomas and embassy menus in a pressed collar, before Mirela recruited him to write for her puppets.  He still wore the collar.  He was still, even now, uncommonly serene, and that was half of what she had been shouting about. 

The other half was Dunmore. 

For two years their shows had toured the seediest bars along the river, places with sticky floors and no stage, where a cardboard mayor could be hanged nightly and no licensing board would ever hear of it.  The puppets said what the newspapers would not: who had signed off on the cracked levee, whose cousin held the dredging contract.  Ansel had believed that was the whole of ethical storytelling, to aim upward and never miss. 

Then, last night at the Drowned Rat, he had given the levee inspector a puppet of his own, a sodden little man named Dunmore with a bottle sewn to his hand.  The real Dunmore had been sitting at the bar.  He had not shouted.  He had watched himself sign the false report, watched himself drink, and then paid for his beer and walked out past his laughing neighbors. 

"You wrote his shame in your prettiest hand," Mirela had said, "and you never once asked him what happened."  Ansel had answered, calmly, that the report was public record, and the calm had been the match. 

Now, in the hush, he looked for something to do with his hands and found the story stick.  It was a length of peeled ash from Mirela's grandmother's campfires, where whoever held it told the tale and everyone else kept quiet until it came around.  The troupe used it as a prop and a ledger; Ansel had inked along its grain, in fine copperplate, the name of every person their puppets had ever spoken for.  Dunmore was the newest, the ink barely dry. 

One panel of the dome had fallen out years ago, and through the gap the August sky was shedding meteors, thin and soundless.  Each streak lit the stick for less than a breath.  In one flash he saw the names; in the next he saw that every one of them was in his handwriting. 

Understanding came as the light did, in half-lit pieces, never staying long enough to be comfortable.  The stick had been made to travel around a fire, and he had turned it into a pen.  His serenity, which he had taken for fairness, was only the steadiness of a hand that never trembled because it never touched anything alive.  Another meteor, and he saw the rest, or half of it: the dome around him had spent its working life sending signals out and waiting for them to come back changed.  He had only ever sent. 

"Mirela," he said, and his voice was not quite steady, which made her turn.  "I want to reverse the show." 

He held the stick out to her, inked end toward himself, bare end toward her hand.  "Tomorrow the puppets sit in the audience, and the stick goes to the bar.  Whoever holds it tells the levee story as they lived it, and our mayor and our dredgers have to listen with their little felt mouths shut." 

"And Dunmore?" 

"I walk to his house first and offer it to him, and if he shuts the door, his puppet stays in the trunk." 

Mirela studied him as a meteor crossed the gap and went out.  "That is a worse show," she said.  "Sloppier, slower, and the drunks will ramble." 

"Yes," Ansel said.  "It may also be the first honest one." 

She took the bare end of the stick, and for a moment they both held it, neither one the teller.  Then he capped his ink, leaving a hand's width of ash blank beneath Dunmore's name for whatever the man might choose to say.  The hush in the dome was no longer the hollow after shouting; it was the quiet of an old ear turned toward the sky, serene at last for the right reason, waiting for something to come back.
