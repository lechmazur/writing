Tamsin Vale climbed the last stair into the stargazer's open tower with a clipboard and a kindly doubt.  The Harbor Trust had sent her to decide whether old Corwin could keep living here, roofless, above the sea.  They had chosen her because his late wife, Maren, was her aunt, and because Tamsin believed nothing she could not measure.  Below the headland, gray whales were migrating south, their breath rising in pale columns under the early stars. 

Corwin sat wrapped in blankets beside the square Maren had set into the floor: nine broad tiles numbered so every row, column, and diagonal summed to fifteen.  Only eight remained in place.  Years ago Maren had pried out the center tile, the five, and filled the hollow with rainwater, so the stars could be studied lying at one's feet.  "To sculpt space for reflection," she used to say, meaning the pool and meaning more. 

Corwin held the lifted tile in his lap now, its corner chipped.  "It hums when they pass," he said, offering it.  "Maren's magic square tile.  It keeps her counting.  Hold it, and you will hear the whole music." 

Tamsin held it.  It was cold clay, and silent, and she felt her answer gather weight.  The easy thing was to nod; the Trust's thing was to write unfit and go home. 

"I hear nothing, Corwin," she said gently.  "I don't think clay remembers.  But I would like to understand what you hear." 

He did not flinch; he looked relieved, as though truth were a blanket too.  "Then transcribe for me," he said, showing his shaking hands.  "Every migration Maren wrote the passage down in numbers.  I have forgotten her way, and the whales will not wait." 

He pointed to a shelf of notebooks, forty winters of digits, one through nine, in no order Tamsin could audit.  She meant to say it was impossible.  Instead, pacing to keep warm, she found her feet doing something her mind had mislaid.  Four, nine, two; three, skip the water, seven; eight, one, six.  It was the hopping game from the single summer she had spent here at eight years old, a pattern thirty years forgotten.  One hop for every whale's breath; where a whale sounded and its flukes went under, you called out the tile beneath you.  The notebooks were not sums at all.  They were a score, paced out on a floor, of a music that moved below hearing. 

"I taught you that game," Corwin said, delighted.  "And I chipped the five, carrying it up these stairs.  Maren never scolded me." 

Tamsin stopped on the seven.  She remembered Maren's hands teaching her the hops, and she remembered, with a child's hot shame, the tile slipping from her own fingers.  Yet she also remembered, impossibly, how the stair looked from a tall man's height, and the sound Maren's laugh made when it was meant for a husband.  Thirty years of his telling and one summer of her living had grown together like roots, and no audit would ever separate whose was whose.  Entangled memories, she thought; not magic, only love keeping poor records. 

She could have corrected him.  She could have lied and agreed.  "I remember dropping it too," she said instead, and her voice shook, because it was a confession and a gift at once.  "Maybe we both did.  Maybe that is why she never scolded anyone." 

Corwin laughed until he wept, and then a spout rose silver off the point, and there was work to do.  He called the breaths; she hopped the old path around the pool and wrote each number where a whale went down.  Hour after hour the page filled: nine, two, two, seven, a long rest, one.  She heard no music, and still she felt it, the way one feels a cello through a wall, in the breastbone rather than the ear. 

Near dawn, Corwin asked whether she would set the five back in its place, now that she knew the pattern whole.  Tamsin looked at the hollow, where the last stars floated beside her own tired face. 

"No," she said.  "She carved that room on purpose, and I intend to keep it open." 

On her clipboard, beneath the Trust's question, she wrote that the keeper forgot things, as everyone did, that his archive required a scribe each migration, and that she volunteered.  She still did not believe the tile hummed.  But she laid it back in Corwin's hands, warm now from her own, and below them the whales went on south, unheard, and written down anyway.
