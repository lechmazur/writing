Orla Venn designed hedge mazes she could never quite see.  Since the morning Linn fell from the bridge, Orla had been retrocognitively trapped: whatever she looked at showed her only what it had once been.  A clipped yew appeared as last year's sprawl, a rose as its bud, her own spotted hands as the smooth ones Linn used to hold.  She pruned by touch and trust, a blind woman in a bright yesterday. 

Worse, the yesterday kept deepening.  Each season her sight sank further back, and the proof lay in the tin box of photographs she carried in her apron.  To everyone else the prints were sharp; to Orla they were fading, Linn's face paling like an image undeveloping in its tray, as her vision waded back toward the hour before each shutter clicked.  Soon she would see blank paper, and then nothing that had ever known Linn at all. 

So she needed to discover the texture of time.  If time was smooth, she would slide down it forever; if it had grain, there might be something to grip. 

The garden where she worked grew on the bridge itself, a span of fig roots Linn had coaxed across the gorge strand by strand until it could carry soil, box hedges, and the two of them.  The bridge was alive and still thickening, people said; Orla saw a thin green rope trembling over the drop, and walked on it anyway. 

Only one object refused her backward sight.  It was a halo fragment, a gilded arc no longer than her palm, snapped from the shrine saint Linn had been regilding when she slipped.  It looked to Orla exactly as it felt, as though it had no before. 

One sleepless night, out of a designer's habit, she traced its curve on paper to complete the circle, and the circle would not close.  The arc promised more rim than any flat radius could afford.  She knew that extravagance from kale leaves and coral: hyperbolic geometry, the shape of surfaces with too much room in them, which must ruffle to fit the world.  If the one thing immune to the past was curved that way, perhaps time was too. 

A maze was the only instrument she owned, so she built one to test it.  Through hyperbolic geometry she replanted the bridge: five paths meeting at every crossing where four should fit, each ring of hedge holding more turnings than the ring before.  No dead structure could have borne it, but the living roots obliged, frilling and buckling beneath her hands to make the room her drawings demanded.  She worked by fingertip for many seasons while Linn's face thinned to a stain. 

On the day the last print showed her only white, she took the tin box and the halo fragment and walked in. 

The bridge quaked gently, as it always did, like something breathing in its sleep.  At the first crossing her sight, which had only ever fallen straight back, hesitated.  At the second it spread.  Every leaf showed her not one yesterday but a crowd of them, bud beside bud beside bud, each moment ringed by more neighbors than a straight line could ever hold.  She reached into the shimmering air and, trembling, felt it at last: time was not a thread or a slope but a ruffle, dense and crimped as kale, with grain enough to climb. 

She understood, then, why she had been falling.  She had been clutching flat things, and nothing flat can lie along a ruffle; it can only slip. 

At the maze's heart she knelt, set the halo fragment on the frilled roots, where its curve finally lay easy, and opened the tin.  Her hands shook so hard the lid rang.  She pressed the photographs, brittle from years of handling, into the wet roots and let them disintegrate, flake by flake, into the weave Linn had braided.  Then she closed her empty fingers on the grain of the air and pulled. 

The green rope beneath her thickened.  Roots swelled into limbs, hedges surged up around her in ruffled tiers, seasons passing through her eyes like wind through leaves, until the rushing stopped at a single wet leaf, quivering, entirely present. 

Orla looked at her hands, and they were old, and they were hers.  She looked at the bridge, enormous now, every knot and crossing of it a decision Linn had made and the fig was still keeping.  No photograph had ever held that face so well.  The halo fragment gleamed at the center, a piece of a ring too wide to finish, and Orla, still shaking, began to learn the garden of today.
