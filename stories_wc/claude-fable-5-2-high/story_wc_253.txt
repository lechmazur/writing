After the happiness mandate, grief was audited like water usage, and Oda held a county license to reduce it.  Her face had stopped moving at her sister's funeral, the week before smiling became law.  The examiners classified the blankness as deadpan, a performance style, and exempted her.  She did not correct them. 

The Brandt farmhouse sat beneath three giant wind turbines that the grid had planted in its barley.  Their blade shadows crossed the kitchen every four seconds, like a hand passing over a face.  Halvor Brandt had been flagged for eleven months of unsmiling since his wife, Marit, died.  An inspector would come Friday.  If Halvor could not demonstrate contentment, he would be moved to a wellness residence and the farm reassigned. 

He let Oda in without speaking.  Silence was the only mourning the mandate could not fine, and he had filled the house with it.  On the table stood a dried flower bouquet in a jar, cornflowers and yarrow gone the color of paper.  Oda recognized a funeral arrangement.  Regulation required her to confiscate sorrow objects on sight.  She wrote "table decoration" on her form and sat down. 

Her method was dramatic narration of mundane tasks, delivered with no expression at all.  Halvor stood to fill the kettle. 

"The man approaches the tap," Oda said.  "He has done this ten thousand times, yet experts agree it could go either way." 

He looked at her.  Her face gave him nothing, which was the act and also the truth. 

"Water enters the kettle.  The nation holds its breath." 

He set the kettle down and went out to the hens.  She followed and narrated the hens.  That was Tuesday. 

On Wednesday a cornflower head dropped from the bouquet, and Halvor held it until his tea went cold.  The bouquet would not last the winter.  Oda walked to the field where a cracked blade lay retired in the stubble and cut a block of balsa from its core.  That evening she began to whittle. 

"The woman removes everything that is not a flower," she said.  "It is slow work, and she is not qualified." 

Shavings collected on the floor.  Halvor watched the knife.  Outside, the turbines made their long sound, a breath that never finished. 

Oda understood that she was paid per documented laugh.  She understood that the county wanted grief removed, not lightened, and that her reports made no distinction.  Her sister used to announce the washing-up like a boxing match.  That was where the method came from, and Oda had sold it to the people who outlawed missing her.  She did not know what that made her.  She kept whittling. 

By Thursday night there were three wooden cornflowers, rough and pale.  She pushed their stems in among the dried ones. 

"The bouquet accepts new members," she said.  "There is no ceremony.  The old flowers do not comment." 

Halvor made a sound through his nose.  It was small and dry and it surprised him.  Oda marked one laugh on her form.  Then she spent an hour teaching him to hold a smile long enough for an inspector, which was fraud. 

On Friday the inspector stood in the flickering kitchen with her tablet.  She asked about the bouquet. 

"Handicraft," Oda said.  "Mixed media." 

The inspector asked Halvor whether he was content.  Halvor held his mouth the way he had practiced. 

"The man considers the question," he said, and his voice was rusted from disuse.  "He decides the hens need him." 

The inspector laughed, logged the household as compliant, and drove away. 

Oda signed the certification that Halvor Brandt was happy.  He was not.  Neither was he what he had been on Tuesday. 

She packed her knife.  At the door she listened to the house.  The hush was still there, but it had been remade.  Before, it had been a sealed room.  Now it was the pause a person leaves before the next line, with space in it for a kettle, a hen, a wooden flower.  Marit was still in it. 

Oda had wanted that for him: not less grief, only less weight.  She had also earned a fee, falsified a record, and trained a mourner to pass.  The county would call it a success and send her to the next farm. 

Behind her Halvor said, to no one, "The man fills the kettle." 

Oda walked out under the turbines.  A blade shadow crossed her face and found nothing to change.  "The woman leaves," she said, and waited to feel something, and started the car.
