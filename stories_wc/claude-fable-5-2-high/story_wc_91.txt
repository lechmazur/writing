Ilse Marrow had given away three shipping lines, a cannery, and a house with forty windows, and now she owned nine jars.  She slept under a driftwood lean-to on the black sand beach below Kehu, the volcano that breathed orange over the village every night.  The villagers called her the poorest generous woman alive, and they were right on both counts. 

With no money left to give, she gave coincidences.  Each dusk she walked the cane fields behind the dunes and coaxed lightning bugs into her jars, and each night she set those jars where they would bend someone's path.  A green glow by the well made the shy baker linger until the widowed net-mender arrived.  A glimmer on the jetty drew a boy with a cracked flute toward the one fisherman who knew how to mend it.  Nobody suspected her; people simply said the beach was lucky. 

Ilse did not do it only for them.  Her own life had turned in a single afternoon of signatures, too fast to feel, and ever since she had wanted to know what change was like against the skin, whether it was smooth or rough, sudden or slow.  So she arranged small turnings in other lives and watched them closely, the way she watched lava cool into the sand beneath her feet. 

That week the sky was turning too.  Two bright planets drifted toward each other night by night, and the full moon was sliding, patient as a tide, toward the shadow of the earth.  The old women hummed a rhyme about it while they salted fish: when the moon wears red and stars walk the black shore, the mountain opens a door, and all pass through unburned.  Ilse had heard it a hundred times and thought it pretty nonsense. 

Her plan for eclipse night was modest.  Old Tavo, the boatwright, walked the cliff road home every evening, and his daughter Mirelle, who had not spoken to him in six years, would be mending nets at the far cove.  A detour was all they needed.  Ilse carried her whole collection down the beach and planted the jars one by one in the black sand, a dotted line of living light leading from the foot of the cliff road, along the water, to the cove.  Tavo could never resist a curiosity. 

The moon began to rust at its edge.  The surf drew back over the sand with its long whispering hush, and under that sound Ilse noticed another, a deeper quiet, as if Kehu were holding its breath. 

Then the mountain exhaled. 

There was no roar, only a seam of gold unzipping high on the slope and a slow bright river feeling its way downhill toward the cliff road, the only road out of the village.  Bells rang.  People spilled from their doors with children and chickens and stopped, because the way they had always fled was already glowing shut. 

It was Tavo, first to reach the foot of the road, who saw the jars. 

"Stars," he said, almost laughing, "walking the black shore." 

The rhyme passed from mouth to mouth in whispers, and the whole village turned as one and followed the green lights along the tide line, under a moon gone the color of embers.  Ilse stood at the last jar, stunned, counting heads as they passed.  She had meant to nudge one stubborn man toward one stubborn daughter, and instead she had laid out, jar by jar, the door the old women sang about. 

Behind them the lava reached the sea and the water answered with a vast soft hiss, steam rising white against the red moon.  On the headland past the cove, Mirelle found her father in the crowd and took his arm without a word.  Ilse almost missed it. 

Later, as the moon began to clear, she walked back toward the new shore, close enough to feel its heat on her face, and watched molten rock shatter in the waves into glittering black grit.  She scooped up a handful that the tide had already cooled.  It was warm, coarse, and a little sharp, and it ran through her fingers like something that had not finished deciding what to be. 

So that was the texture: not one great breaking, but ten thousand small ones, each still holding a trace of the fire it came from.  Her own turning had felt like nothing because she had only looked at the signature, never at the years of small givings that came after. 

She walked back along the line and opened the jars, one by one.  The lightning bugs lifted over the black sand, the planets leaned close above the smoking mountain, and the beach kept its bright, whispering hush.
