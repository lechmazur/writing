After the Bureau learned to measure luck, every acre in the valley was priced by a trembling needle.  Odalys, vine dresser of the Verrin garden maze, was by then as withered as the old wood she pruned each winter.  She had outlived two masters, and now she was outliving her only lie. 

Thirty years ago, when the first assessors came, she had borrowed a gauge at dusk and watched its needle lie dead over the maze.  Unlucky land was grubbed up and sold for gravel.  So she had been wisely pragmatic: she bought forty marbles from the luckiest shooter in the county, each one humming with won fortune, and buried them at the roots.  The needle sang, the Verrins boasted, and the vines were left in her hands.  It was a small deception, planted to protect a larger truth she could not then prove: that gardens know their gardeners. 

This spring, kneeling to tie in a cane, she turned up a marble and found it crazed through like river ice.  She dug up the rest of the collection by lantern, and every one was cracked, its luck long since bled into the frost.  The reassessment was nine days off. 

She sat among the cracked marbles and did her sums the way she thinned a crop, without sentiment.  New marbles would cost a year's wages, and they would crack too, and in ten more winters there would be no Odalys to bury the next batch.  A lie that needed replanting every generation was a poor trellis.  Better to mend it into something that would bear weight.  And here the gauge itself, her old enemy, might serve, because a thing that measures luck also measures its absence. 

Odalys had always commemorated her small victories elaborately, since nobody else would: a rung bell, a thimble of last year's wine, a red ribbon, an entry in the ledger.  The graft that took in the drought year; the blight turned back at the third alley; the dead corner that budded after she sat with it all through one February.  Now, for nine nights, she polished cracked marbles on her apron until their fractures caught the lantern like trapped frost, then hung each in a twist of wire at the exact spot where a victory had happened.  She rang the bell thirty-nine times, drank thirty-nine thimbles, and her knees complained of every one.  The fortieth she kept in her pocket, for a victory not yet won.  The marbles that had once lied underground now told the truth in the open. 

Assessor Pell arrived on the tenth morning, young and courteous, and was frowning at his gauge before he reached the first turning. 

"Zero," he said.  "The register says sixty." 

"The register was me," said Odalys, and she told him about the shooter, the burying, and the frost. 

"That is fraud," Pell said, not unkindly. 

"It was," she agreed.  "Now look up from your needle and tell me what zero means." 

Around them the vines were breaking leaf a fortnight early, green flames along gray wood, and the cracked marbles winked at every bend. 

"It means nothing here happened by chance," he said slowly. 

"Then ask what did." 

She sent him in alone, and for an hour the maze gave him dead ends and snagged sleeves.  Then she walked in after him, slow on her stick, and he watched tendrils along both walls turn toward her the way they turn toward a south window.  He timed it twice with his watch, and the needle never stirred from zero.  She put the ledger in his hands: thirty years of dated victories, each one now answered by a marble he could go and find. 

"You must write something in your column," she said.  "Write what is there." 

Pell wrote for a long while.  His report, when the Verrins read it, said that the maze held no luck and therefore could lose none, and that its yield was attributable to its keeper.  Land that could never turn unlucky was worth more than gravel, and the Verrins kept it. 

That evening Odalys polished the last cracked marble, the first one she had turned up, and hung it over the gate.  She rang the bell, drank the thimble, tied the ribbon, and wrote in the ledger: one lie, made honest.  Her hand shook on the pen; she was withering, and she knew it as plainly as she knew pruning weather.  Tomorrow she would ask the Verrins for an apprentice, because a garden that knows its gardener must be given time to learn another.  Above her, in the cracked glass, the last light bloomed, and a new tendril, uncurling, found her wrist and held on.
