Maren Oszkar had taught choirs in eleven countries before she came home to Velden, and the traveling had left a stillness in her like the hush after a train departs.  She rehearsed in the velvet library, a reading room whose walls a long-dead merchant had lined in green velvet so that no page-turn would disturb another reader.  That autumn the velvet smelled of woodsmoke and wet leaves, and it swallowed every sound that was not meant. 

It was the only room in Velden where the hill families and the river families would both sit, because neither side had ever owned it.  Thirty years earlier a dam had been opened in the night, a harvest had drowned, and each side still sang its own version of whose hand was on the wheel.  Maren wanted one thing before she was too old to want it: to braid those voices into a single harmony. 

At the first rehearsal, sixteen singers arrived and split down the aisle like a parted seam.  Ilse Brandt of the hills sang sharp and loud, a blade of a soprano; Dov Marek of the river answered with a bass that leaned on every note like a shoulder against a door.  They were not singing the hymn; they were singing the dam. 

Maren did not scold.  She set a small wooden disc on the reading table, a zither soundhole rosette carved from pearwood, its pierced strands looping over and under one another like plaited hair. 

"Old Tobias Kern cut this," she said.  "He was the last rosette carver in the valley, and he made zithers for hill weddings and river funerals alike.  When he died in spring, the craft went into the ground with him." 

Dov turned the rosette over in his thick fingers.  "It is mostly holes," he said. 

"Yes," said Maren.  "Tobias did not add anything to make this; he took wood away until the sound had somewhere to come out.  We will learn this hymn the same way, by subtracting noise." 

She handed out scores on which she had drawn small open circles above certain bars.  "These circles denote a rosette," she told them.  "Where you see one, you stop singing and listen to whoever is left." 

Week after week, as the lindens outside went yellow and then bare, she cut things away.  First the volume, then the vibrato people wore like armor, then the little held notes that were really grudges.  The circles were placed so that Ilse had to go quiet while Dov carried the line, and Dov had to go quiet beneath her.  Hatred, Maren had learned in other broken towns, is not unlearned in a speech; it is unlearned the way an accent is, by long listening. 

In October Ilse walked out, saying she would not hold her tongue for a Marek.  Maren let her go and left her chair where it was.  She was expectantly resigned in those weeks, as keepers of vanishing arts must be: certain that most of what she loved would disappear, and still setting out the empty chair as if for a guest. 

Ilse came back in November with rain on her coat.  She had stood a while in the doorway first, hearing the hymn go on with a hole in it exactly her shape. 

"Nobody took my line," she said. 

"It was not ours to fill," said Dov, not looking up, and he slid the rosette along the table toward her chair. 

Ilse touched the carved strands.  "My mother's wedding zither has this same rose," she said. 

"So did the one we played at my father's burial," said Dov. 

Neither of them said anything about the dam, and that silence, too, was a kind of circle on the score. 

They sang the hymn on the last evening before the first snow, with no audience but the books.  The velvet took away every echo, so that nothing stood in the room except what sixteen people chose to give.  At the circled bar Dov stopped, and Ilse's soprano came through the gap, no longer a blade, only a thread; then she stopped, and his bass rose under the altos like a floor.  Over and under, over and under, the voices crossed the way the pearwood strands did. 

Maren lowered her hands and let the last chord end without her.  She knew the choir might not outlast the winter, that hymns vanish as surely as carvers do.  But Dov was holding the door for Ilse, and outside the last leaves were coming down quietly, one less noise in the world.  Maren put the rosette in her pocket and waited, as she always had, for whatever would come through the open place.
