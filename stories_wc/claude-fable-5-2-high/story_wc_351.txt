The dam had been gone eleven weeks, and the canyon was still learning how to be a canyon again.  Ines Calloway walked its floor under a moon so bright that the drying silt shone like poured tin.  She had engineered the drawdown herself, forty years of reservoir released in a schedule her colleagues called chaotically precise: scribbled on napkins, taped to windows, and correct to the centimeter.  That was how her mind worked, in helices.  She never traveled straight toward an answer; she circled it, climbing, passing the same point again and again from a little higher up.  Tonight the point she kept passing was a house. 

The town of Vesper had drowned when she was nine, and now its foundations rose from the mud like the ribs of something patient.  She found her own doorstep by counting paces from the chapel well, and her hands were shaking before she knelt.  It was where memory said it would be, under the collapsed porch: a tin tube gone to lace, and inside it, the broken periscope mirror. 

She and her twin, Mara, had built the periscope the summer before the flood, because the canyon walls were too high and they wanted to see what lay beyond the rim.  On evacuation night, one of them dropped it.  For forty years Ines had remembered the tube slipping from Mara's fingers.  For forty years Mara had remembered it slipping from Ines's, and she had said so, gently, even in the hospice last spring. 

Their memories had always been entangled that way, two strands wound around one axis, each twin holding scenes that could only have been witnessed through the other's eyes.  Ines remembered the taste of a plum only Mara had eaten.  Mara had remembered the sting of a wasp that marked only Ines's wrist.  Now one strand was gone, and the other was unspooling in a canyon that was itself becoming something else, and Ines could feel her whole life loosening with the silt. 

She laid the shards on her jacket and fitted them together by moonlight, seven pieces, the way she would read a failed weld.  Fractures keep honest records.  The cracks radiated from two points, not one, on opposite edges of the glass, where the tin had been wrenched in two directions before it fell.  Two girls, each pulling, each refusing to leave it behind.  Neither memory was true, and both were. 

Ines sat back on her heels and trembled, and the canyon seemed to tremble with her, small stones ticking down the walls as the long-soaked rock dried and shifted.  She knew the science of what she was about to do, because she had read it at Mara's bedside when reading was all she could manage.  A memory is not a photograph in a drawer; every recall softens it, and it sets again in a slightly new shape.  Through synaptic plasticity, the brain rebuilds what it revisits.  For forty years she had been reinforcing a blame without meaning to, one remembering at a time.  So she would modify it on purpose, the way she would modify any flawed design: not by erasing the old drawing, but by revising it against the evidence. 

She held the largest shard, closed her eyes, and summoned the night of the flood sirens.  This time she let herself feel her own hands on the tin, pulling, and Mara's pulling back, and the terrible bright crack between them.  She ran it again, and again, spiraling, each pass a little higher, until the new version no longer felt like a correction and began to feel like a place she had been. 

When she opened her eyes, the moon had moved a hand's width along the rim, and she understood what she had actually come down here to do.  The county wanted her to stay on and certify the canyon as safe, then move to the next dam.  But all around her lay what four hundred families had dropped in one night: a half-dug cellar, a trellis for vines never planted, a child's instrument for seeing over walls.  Somebody should dig for those, not the objects but the intentions folded inside them.  She would become the archeologist of abandoned dreams, and she would begin with her own. 

She wedged the seven shards back into the corroded tube with river clay, not mending them, only setting them at the old angle.  Then she raised the periscope above her head, as two girls once had, and looked.  Seven moons quaked in the glass, and beyond them, in every fragment, the rim of the canyon and the dark open country past it. 

"There," she whispered, to both of them.  "We finally see over."
