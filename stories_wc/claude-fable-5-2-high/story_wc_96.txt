The library had no roof anymore, only iron stacks rusting under the stars like the ribs of something that had once breathed stories.  Wren sat between two leaning shelves with a dented coffee tin in her lap, watching the eastern rooftops for the moon.  She had perhaps ten minutes left of being herself. 

Every month, as the moon cleared the skyline, Wren molted a personality the way a snake leaves a skin on a fence.  The memories stayed, but the caring did not always carry over; last month's brawler had become this month's gentle counter of petals, and nobody, least of all Wren, could say who would arrive next.  It was a ruinous way to live, which was why she sold poems on street corners for a penny apiece and slept wherever the Wardens were not.  It was also, Odalys liked to say, the only honest anarchy left in the city. 

They had met eleven years ago in the library at Harrow Prison, two inmates assigned to sort a truckload of donated books dumped on the floor like rubble.  Odalys shelved by subject; Wren, depending on the month, shelved by color, by grief, by the sound of first lines.  Instead of fighting, they argued, and the arguing became a friendship, and the friendship became a habit: each time Wren molted, Odalys introduced herself again, patiently, across a cart of paperbacks. 

Then they were released into a city that had decided books were clutter.  The Wardens had stripped this library's roof for scrap, pulped what they could carry, and left the stacks to the rain.  What they could not carry, the neighborhood had hidden in attics and bread boxes, loose pages scattered through a thousand frightened homes.  So Wren's penny poems carried a second meaning for those who knew to look: come when the moon clears the skyline, and bring what you saved. 

Boots crunched on the broken tile.  A Warden swung his lantern over her, took the tin, and shook it beside his ear.  Pennies rattled.  He pried the lid, saw copper, snorted, and tossed it back.  "Rich night, poet," he said, and his light went wobbling away down the avenue. 

Wren breathed out, poured the pennies into her pocket, and pressed her thumbnail into the seam at the tin's floor.  The false bottom lifted.  Underneath lay a twist of moonflower seeds and a stack of catalog slips, one from each self she had been since the roof came down, each bearing a single line in a different slant of the same hand. 

Odalys emerged from behind the atlas stacks, where she had been holding her breath and a crate of salvaged pages.  She wrote tonight's line quickly, because her edges were already loosening. 

"What if the next one doesn't care?" she asked. 

"Then I will introduce myself," Odalys said, "like always." 

The moon cleared the skyline.  It felt, as it always did, like a pane of glass cracking with a lamp behind it: every line of the break filled with light, and then a stranger was looking out through her eyes. 

The new Wren was sharp and tired.  She remembered everything the way one remembers a stranger's diary, facts without warmth, and she looked at the rust, the wet tile, the absurd tin, and thought: scrap. 

"Odalys," said the woman with the crate, offering her hand.  "We sort books together." 

"There are no books," Wren said. 

"Read your slips." 

Wren almost refused, but the slips were in her hand, and a poet, even a cold one, cannot leave lines unread.  The brawler had written, Break the lock, not the spine.  A coward had written, I was afraid and came anyway.  The petal-counter had written, tonight, Whoever you are, we are a library too.  Slip after slip, the scattered selves stacked into one poem, wild and misshelved and somehow whole, and on the back of each, in every slant, was the same word: rebuild. 

Around her, the moonflowers her earlier selves had planted along the stacks were opening, white throats unfurling up the rusted iron as if the shelves had decided to bloom rather than fall.  And through the gaps in the walls came the resistance itself, neighbors quiet as moths, carrying pages in bread boxes and coat linings. 

Something cold in the new Wren cracked, and what came through the crack was not warmth exactly, but purpose.  She had always wanted, in every skin, to forge beauty from anarchy; here was the anarchy, arriving by the armful.  She pressed a seed into the dirt at the foot of the nearest shelf, took the first torn page from a child's hand, and turned to Odalys. 

"By subject," she said, "or by the sound of first lines?" 

Odalys laughed, and under the stars they began to argue.
