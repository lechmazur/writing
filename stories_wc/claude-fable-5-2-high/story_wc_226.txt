The towers of Ossuary Reach were carved from the ribs of something that died before language, and the canyon around them never forgot a sound.  A cough from some grandmother's girlhood still wandered the lower ledges.  So the people had forged a hush, hammering it into every child the way iron is hammered, until the silence rang like a held bell. 

Maro Varr, glasscutter, had never taken the hammering well.  As a girl she had slipped prisms into the Wardens' lamps so their stern white light fell in ribbons across their robes, and she had meant no harm; she only thought white looked lonely.  Tonight, the night before the ritual, she carried a satchel of those same prisms across the rope bridges, and her grin had gone thin. 

At dawn her sister Ilse would be forgiven.  Warden Oshe would climb the Confessor's Spire, the tallest rib, pale and forever wrapped in river mist, and speak one sentence into the canyon.  The Spire's hollow would throw it the whole length of the Reach, and it would echo until the bones fell.  Ilse Varr broke the hush, and eleven died.  That was the price: Ilse could come home, but only as that sentence, and every other telling would be forbidden. 

"It is true," Ilse had whispered through the cell grate.  "I rang the storm bell."  "The Ninth Tower fell." 

"You rang it because the river was rising," Maro whispered back.  "Forty people climbed out of the low houses." 

"One line is cheaper than the whole of it."  "Let them have it." 

"Cheaper for whom?" Maro said, and left before her sister could answer. 

She could not unsay the Warden's truth, and would not have wanted to; eleven had died.  But a single truth, like white light, was only many truths held too tightly together, and Maro knew how to refract it. 

On the bridge below the Fourth Tower, where lamplighters ate supper, she hung a prism on the lamp, and color fell across their startled hands.  Then, in the lowest whisper the canyon would carry, she spoke a line.  "The bell was cold, and the water was colder." 

Nobody breathed.  Poetry was permitted in the Reach only at funerals, written beforehand and approved. 

"Your turn," Maro breathed to a wide-eyed lamp boy.  "Only what you saw; it need not rhyme, though I will think less of you." 

The boy swallowed a laugh.  "My mother carried me up the stair, and the stair was already wet." 

His whisper slid off the bridge and went wandering down the canyon, small and permanent.  An old man added a line about his brother, who had been in the Ninth Tower, and it was not a kind line.  Maro nodded and let it go out with the others, because it was true too. 

She did this all night, tower by tower: a prism, a first line, then waiting until someone could not bear to leave it unanswered.  Weavers gave lines, a gravedigger gave three, and a widow gave only a name and then, after a long while, the word "warned."  By the grey hour the canyon was murmuring, a hundred whispers crossing and recrossing, grief beside gratitude, neither drowning the other. 

She saved her largest prism for the foot of the Spire, where the first sun would find it.  Warden Oshe was waiting there in the mist. 

"I have been listening," the Warden said, very low.  "My son was in the Ninth Tower, and I wrote that sentence so I could say it once and be done." 

"I know," said Maro. 

"You have made forgiving her cost more." 

"It always cost this much; you were only going to let Ilse pay all of it, by being nothing else."  "Now you give up the clean line, she gives up hiding inside it, and the rest of us must hear everything, forever." 

Somewhere down the canyon the lamp boy's wet stair came around again, and behind it the old man's bitterness, and behind that, "warned." 

"And you?" Oshe asked.  "What do you pay?" 

"My line went out first, and the canyon keeps my voice on it while the bones stand," Maro said.  "If this is ever called a crime, it is signed." 

The sun reached the rim.  The mist around the pale Spire took the light and broke it gently, and the prism laid a faint band of every color across the Warden's grey sleeve.  Oshe looked at it the way she must have years ago, with a child grinning somewhere behind a pillar. 

"The rite requires one sentence," she said at last.  "It does not require an empty canyon." 

Then she started up the stair, and Maro stood in the forged hush, listening for the eleven to land among the forty.
