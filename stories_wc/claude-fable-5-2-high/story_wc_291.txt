Three days after the Calloway evidence box vanished from the county locker, Odile Farrow still had the ribbon.  It was a thumb-length fragment of black satin from a masquerade mask, signed out weeks earlier so she could match its pigment.  Now it was all that remained of Wren Calloway, nineteen, found at the bottom of Harrow Quarry in 1991. 

Detective Pruitt had shrugged when the loss was logged.  "Cold cases go colder," he said, "and nobody in this town has lost sleep over her in thirty years." 

That shrug bothered Odile more than the theft.  She painted the unit's forgotten dead for exactly one reason: to awaken whatever empathy had gone dormant in the people who walked past their names.  It was strange work for a woman who could not recognize a face, not even her own in a window.  Faces reached her as loose parts, so she built them the way a surveyor builds a map, with calipers, grids, and ratios checked three times.  People she knew by other things: a gait, a cough, the habits of their hands. 

So she gave the ribbon the same method.  Under the lamp she measured it, sketched it, and slowly diagrammed the knot still cinched at its torn end.  It was not a bow.  It was a doubled hitch with a backward tuck, the kind the old quarrymen used on blasting lines, and Wren could not have tied it behind her own head.  Someone had fastened her mask for her that night. 

Odile had seen that tuck before, every week, on the string ties of the evidence envelopes Pruitt handed her.  She could not have picked his face from a lineup of two, but she knew his hands. 

A knot was not proof, and the proof was gone.  Only a confession could reopen the case, and a confession required a man who could still feel.  So she finished the portrait first, and she finished it differently.  Then she asked Pruitt to drive her to the quarry at dawn, to check the light, she said. 

Mist filled Harrow Quarry like breath held in a cupped hand.  The cut walls stood in their own shadow, and the flooded floor showed nothing, not even sky.  Their boots on the gravel were the only sound, and both of them walked as if it might wake someone. 

Odile set her easel near the rim and uncovered the canvas.  The left half of the face was Wren at nineteen, masked in black satin, her mouth caught mid-laugh.  The right half was Wren at fifty-two, bare and lined, aged by careful arithmetic from her mother's cheekbones and her sister's jaw.  Masked and unmasked, dead and unlived, the girl he remembered and the woman no one had been allowed to meet, joined along a seam Odile had measured to the millimeter.  Unifying those opposites was the only way she knew to make one face hold both the loss and the person lost. 

Pruitt's breathing changed.  His face told her little, as faces did, so she watched his hands, and they had gone still at his sides. 

She held out the ribbon fragment on her palm, its knot uppermost.  "I measured this forty times," she said quietly.  "I don't know your face, Dale, but I know how your hands finish a knot." 

She was not setting a trap.  She meant to lance something, the way a doctor opens a wound that has sealed over rot, so that what was trapped could finally drain. 

For a long while he did not move.  Then his thumb found the backward tuck and pressed it, the way a man tests an old bruise. 

"I worked the lines here that summer," he said.  "She asked me to fix her mask at the party, and later we came up here and argued about her leaving town."  "I grabbed the ribbon to make her stay, it tore, and she went backward into the dark."  His voice was flat from thirty years of being kept that way.  "I ran, and then I spent a career making sure I never felt it." 

He looked at the older half of the painting, the half that had never happened, and his shoulders folded.  "She'd have had gray in her hair," he whispered, as though that were the crime. 

Odile said nothing and let the quiet do its slow work. 

At last he walked to the car and opened the trunk.  The evidence box sat inside, taped shut, undestroyed.  "I couldn't burn it," he said.  "I think I was waiting for someone to make me look at her." 

Odile laid the ribbon on the box and wrote down the time.  Behind them the mist was thinning, and both halves of Wren's face took the light.
