After the river took the bridges, help reached Calder Hollow the way gossip does: sideways, and ahead of the facts.  Someone heard from a cousin's dentist that Fenwick Halt, the railway station shut for thirty years and upholstered in ivy, had water filters for anyone who brought blankets.  Someone else heard that it had blankets for anyone who brought diesel.  Neither was true until both rumors arrived, carrying their goods, and made each other honest. 

This was how Marit Voss worked.  She had run disaster-relief logistics for agencies with letterheads, and had learned that forms moved slower than floodwater while rumors outran it.  So she requisitioned nothing; she whispered one improbable thing to a ferryman, another to a choir, and let chains of hearsay haul what no truck could. 

Nobody in Calder Hollow had met her.  They knew her only by the chalk on the departures board, in handwriting that slanted like a person leaning away. 

"The 9:15 to Anywhere is delayed eleven thousand days.  We apologize for the convenience." 

"Please loot responsibly.  Queue to the left.  Thank you for your disobedience." 

And beneath, underlined twice, the only rule: nobody arrives empty-handed, nobody leaves empty-handed. 

No one was in charge, and everyone said please.  People left eggs and took antibiotics, left a song and took a tarp, and went home owing nothing, which Marit, watching through the ivy from the signal box, considered elegant.  She kept the ledger in her head, and her own column was empty, because taking meant standing in the ticket hall and being looked at. 

Her theory, never chalked, was that somewhere in the arithmetic of other people's needs lay the cure for loneliness, waiting in the remainder.  Eleven years of disasters, and the remainder was always zero.  So the jokes got louder as the nights got quieter. 

On the ninth day a boy named Tobin came for his grandmother's insulin with nothing to trade, the flood having taken their house down to the doorstep.  He stood a long time under the rule.  Then he set on the ticket counter a gray chip sealed in cracked acrylic: the town museum's fragment of moon rock, a goodwill gift from some forgotten president, dug out of the silt where the museum used to be.  He took the insulin, thanked the empty hall, and left. 

Marit came down after dark and held the chip to her lantern.  It was the loneliest object she had ever seen: a crumb of a place with no weather and no neighbors, carried four hundred thousand kilometers to sit behind glass.  It was also worth wildly more than insulin, and by her own arithmetic the station was in debt.  She could not send change through hearsay. 

She chalked, a little frantically, "Found: one moon, small.  Owner may reclaim it.  We swear this was not a holdup.  Ha." 

But Tobin had told his grandmother, who told the pump queue, and the fragment began to catalyze something Marit had never seeded.  That was when the story changed.  Her rumors had always been about things; this one was about her. 

At the pump it went: the station took a moon and gave no change.  At the ferry: the station has never taken anything for itself.  By the choir: a woman in the signal box has fed four valleys, is owed by all of them, and is too polite, or too frightened, to collect. 

In a town bound by reciprocity, that was an intolerable thing to know. 

They came at dusk on the eleventh day, leaderless, an orderly mob.  They brought soup, a mended chair, and a fiddle, and queued to the left of the signal-box stairs while the grandmother knocked. 

"We have read your rule," she called.  "You have left here empty-handed every night, and we would like to lodge a complaint, please." 

Marit, behind the door, tried a joke: "The complaints office closed in 1987."  Her voice cracked in the middle of the year. 

"Then we will wait," said the grandmother, and the mob, courteously, sat down on the platform. 

Marit looked at the fragment and finally read the remainder: the cure was never in balancing the ledger but in being on it, owed and owing, with a door someone could knock on. 

She opened the door. 

"I owe you," she said, and had to start again, "I owe you change for a moon, and I have nothing small enough." 

"Keep it," said Tobin.  "Come down and eat, and then we can all owe each other for ages." 

She came down into the lantern light, took a bowl, and was looked at. 

In the morning the departures board read, in handwriting that stood nearly upright, "The 9:15 to Anywhere is cancelled.  Staff are staying."
