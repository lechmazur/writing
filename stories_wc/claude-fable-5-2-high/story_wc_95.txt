The Twelfth Disputation was set for noon, and at noon Halvard Brix was not there.  So Tamsin Vole, dueling philosopher, stood waiting in the glass atrium beside the quiet brook while her mechanical arm ticked like a patient clock.  Around her, the Guild did what the Guild did with any delay: it argued.  Three elders in ceremonial gray debated whether lateness was a forfeit or merely a premise.  The string quartet began the overture, stopped, apologized, and began again.  A heron walked in from the brook, was offered tea, and declined. 

Tamsin kept her foil sheathed and her face composed.  She had lost her right arm to Halvard in their third disputation and had spent eight more refusing to lose the argument.  He held that a question was a room to pace in forever; she held that a question was a road, and that a philosopher's whole business was to turn questions into destinations.  Eleven bouts had settled nothing. 

"Madam Vole," said the umpire, "a parcel, delivered by a boy who ran away with great formality." 

It was a Danish biscuit tin, and it held what such tins always hold instead of biscuits: sewing things, or rather one sewing thing, a single card of needles.  The needles were not ranked in tidy rows.  Eight of them had been thrust through the paper at eight deliberate slants, and the ninth pointed straight off the edge.  No note.  Halvard's hand, though; she knew how he pinned things, having once been pinned. 

Her gaze went to the sundial he had donated to the atrium that spring and never finished.  Its morning half was painted with eight hour lines in oxblood red; its afternoon half was bare stone.  The Guild had mocked those lines for months, because every one sat at the wrong angle for telling time. 

Tamsin laid the card on the dial and turned the degree ring on her brass wrist, measuring needle against line.  They matched, slant for slant.  They were wrong for hours and exactly right for something else: the eight guard positions of the foil, prime through octave, each blade angle every duelist learns before learning to think.  The card, read left to right, gave the order. 

"Forgive me," she told the elders, drawing her foil, "I must fence your sundial." 

"There is no precedent," said the eldest. 

"Then kindly witness one," said Tamsin, with a bow so courteous it left no room to refuse.  She was diplomatically fierce in this as in all things; she apologized before every touch and had never yet missed one.  The elders rearranged their chairs to argue about it from a better view. 

Her arm clicked through the angles in the needles' order, blade hovering above each red line in turn.  By the fourth position her body knew it before her mind did.  This was no attack, and no defense anyone would use against an opponent.  It was half of the mirror drill, the old form two fencers perform shoulder to shoulder, and it was the half that guards a partner's right side.  Her brass side. 

Half a form, on half a dial, from a man forbidden by Guild law and his own pride to offer terms before a bout.  He had hidden his overture where only waiting would reveal it, then made her wait.  The quartet, sensing something, finally got past the opening bars. 

There remained the ninth needle.  Tamsin set the card back on the dial, matched to its lines, and sighted along the needle that pointed off the edge.  It aimed across the bare afternoon stone, through the glass, to the brook.  A question, then, with a direction attached.  She found that her brass fingers were trembling, which they were not built to do. 

"The disputation is not forfeit," she announced, "merely relocated; do bring the tea." 

She walked out through the glass doors, and the Guild followed in procession, elders, umpire, quartet still playing, heron last. 

Halvard Brix sat on the bank with a pot of oxblood paint, a brush, and the gray look of a man who had been waiting far longer than an hour. 

"You said a question should lead somewhere," he said, rising.  "I paced in mine for years, Tamsin, and it only ever led to your arm."  He held out the brush, handle first, the way one offers a blade.  "The afternoon half is yours, if you will have it." 

Tamsin considered him as she would any thesis, thoroughly and without mercy. 

"I apologize in advance," she said, "for how exacting my angles will be." 

She took the brush in her brass hand, and together they walked back toward the glass, the Guild parting politely, the question arriving at last.
