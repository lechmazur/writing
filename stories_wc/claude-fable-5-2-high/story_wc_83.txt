The breakers tripped at 0314, and the Halcyon Observatory went dark thirty kilometers above the sleeping earth.  Samira Qadir stood in the humming afterimage of her screens and did not curse.  She had done this herself, switching off every noise filter and feeding the raw sky to the processors until the old thermal breakers gave up.  They would not reset until they cooled.  Twenty minutes, the manual said, of nothing but balloon fabric creaking overhead and the stratosphere pressing its cold against the hull. 

She had taken this posting because it floated above the ceiling where drones fly.  Nobody on the hiring board had asked, and she had not said. 

In her pocket, as always, sat half of a geode crystal.  Tariq, a geologist with dust permanently in his eyebrows, had given it to her whole on their wedding morning, a gray fist of rock they were to crack open on their tenth anniversary.  "Ten years of not knowing what is inside," he had said.  "That is the gift."  The drone cracked it that afternoon instead, along with the courtyard, the musicians, and sixty-one people whom a distant screen had mistaken for a convoy.  She had dug one half out of the rubble with her hennaed hands; the other half she never found. 

Since then she had been on a hunt that she described to no one.  Officially, she studied faint wobbles in the orbits of icy bodies beyond Neptune, working through perturbation theory: take a solved system, add a small disturbance, and compute what unseen thing must be doing the tugging.  Privately, she was looking for where unused potential pools.  Sixty-one futures had been cancelled in one afternoon, and nothing in physics is simply cancelled.  The children the guests would have raised, the bridges her cousin would have built, Tariq's ten patient years: all that energy had to have gone somewhere, and wherever it gathered it would have weight, and weight leaves wobbles.  She knew it was not science.  She hunted anyway, night after night, because the alternative was believing it had all just stopped. 

Tonight, for one second before the dark, the unfiltered data had shown her something.  A residual, a slow rhythm the filters had been scrubbing away for three years as instrument noise.  Eleven seconds, peak to peak. 

Unable to sit, she paced the gondola, from the cold telescope mount to the hatch and back.  The floor answered.  It was the faintest lean, the whole floating observatory swaying beneath its balloon to balance one small woman walking.  She stopped, then walked again, counting.  Mount to hatch and back: eleven seconds. 

The laugh that came out of her was an ugly, astonished sound.  For three years she had paced through every sleepless shift, and for three years the software had politely erased her from her own sky.  The unseen mass disturbing the system was Samira Qadir, fifty-four kilograms of survivor. 

She took out the geode and turned it in her fingers, feeling the crystals she could not see.  Tariq had explained it once: a geode begins as a hollow, a bubble where something was lost, and mineral water pools in the emptiness and slowly grows teeth of light.  Potential did not pool in some far dark between planets.  It pooled in whatever had been hollowed out and left standing. 

The thought was merciless, because it meant no one was waiting anywhere, and every unlived year from that courtyard had only her to live in.  It was also the first thing in three years that made her heart pound. 

She climbed to the dome and cranked the shutter open by hand.  No screens, no corrections, no software deciding what counted: the stars above the weather did not twinkle, and they stared back hard and exact.  Starlight caught in the broken amethyst, and she felt the dead the way she had felt the floor lean, intangibly tangible, a pull with no hand behind it, yet real enough to tilt a ship. 

Below, a breaker ticked as it cooled.  She went down, set her palm on the lever, and threw it.  The screens woke, asking whether to restore the filters.  She pressed no. 

Then she opened the model and, for the first time, entered the observer as a term: her mass, her eleven-second stride, her name.  Under it she typed sixty-one more names, terms she intended to carry into every calculation of what one hollowed woman might yet do.  Then she paced, on purpose, and watched the sky wobble with her.
