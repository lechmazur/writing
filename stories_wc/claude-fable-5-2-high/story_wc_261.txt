Maren woke when the anesthesia wore off, and the first voice she heard was not human.  It was the ivy.  It pressed against the window of the recovery tower, a hundred years of green climbing the old observatory stone, and it was thinking about light.  She understood the thought the way she understood thirst. 

Dawn had not quite arrived, and the tower held the hush that follows a meteor, when everyone who saw it is still deciding whether to speak.  Last night, before they wheeled her under, she had watched one fall past this same window and had spent it on her brother.  Let the kidney take, she had asked.  Let Teo keep what I give him. 

Now her side burned under its dressing, Teo lay somewhere on the floor below, and a vine was murmuring in her skull.  Miracles, she thought, must be like meteors: aimed at one field, landing in another. 

On the sill stood a frosted hourglass, the ward's only rule.  The night nurse had turned it and said to lie flat until the sand ran out, or the stitches would not hold.  But the glass was etched cloudy and the cold had furred it white, so Maren could not see how much time remained. 

Not seeing was the thing she had never been able to bear.  She was the one who asked their mother, at the funeral, why Father had really left.  She was the one who pressed Teo, every birthday, about the pale seam through his lip that he would never explain.  She called it wanting closure.  Teo called it picking scabs, and once, gently, told her that some people could read a scar without tearing it open, and she should learn.  She had wanted to learn ever since, and had never found a teacher. 

The surgeon's notes lay on the bedside table, and she turned her head to them, because paper was at least something she could read.  The handwriting defeated her, but the coffee rings did not.  Three dark rings overlapped near the margin, quick and close, a cup lifted and set down by a man keeping himself awake at her bedside.  A fourth ring was wide and pale, the mark of a cup left to go cold.  The fifth was only half a circle, smeared toward the door, with one long drip trailing after it.  He had sat with her, then been called away fast enough to spill. 

There was only one other patient in the tower. 

Sixty stairs down, she would know, and she got one elbow under her and felt the stitches begin to give. 

Down, offered the ivy, or something shaped like the word.  It had its own way of going there. 

Maren lay back, closed her eyes, and let her attention tunnel with the vine.  They went through the window frame's rot, into mortar a century soft, along rootlets that had tunneled grain by grain through the tower's skin.  On the way she felt the ivy's own scars.  Here a gardener's shears had taken a limb in some forgotten spring; here lightning; here a ladder.  The vine did not remember the pain of any of them.  It remembered only which way it had turned afterward, and every knot was a sentence that said: from here, I went toward the light instead. 

So that was how a scar was read.  Not backward, into the cut, but forward, along the growth. 

One floor down, the ivy's newest leaves lay against a window propped open a finger's width.  Through them Maren tasted breath: warm, slow, even, the sleeping air of a man whose blood was being cleaned at last.  A second breath kept time beside it, quicker, bitter with coffee, the surgeon in a chair, called down not to a disaster but to watch a kidney wake. 

Teo had not needed her miracle.  Ordinary hands and her ordinary kidney had been enough, so the wish had drifted one floor up and settled in the person who did need it.  It had given the woman who tore things open a way to know without tearing. 

Maren came back to her own bed and laid her palm flat over the dressing, lightly, not lifting its edge.  Someday this would be a seam like the one through Teo's lip, and she thought she would finally stop asking about his.  She could read where he had gone from it: toward her, every birthday, anyway. 

The sun cleared the hills and the frost on the hourglass began to run.  She did not wipe the glass to check the sand.  She lay flat in the hush, listening to the ivy think about light, and let the time finish falling on its own.
