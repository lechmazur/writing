After the last resource war, there was nothing left to fight over, and the quiet that followed had teeth.  Gantry, the scrapyard settlement, lay across the cracked wasteland like a rusted net, its bus hulls and crane arms bridging fissures that breathed cold dust.  Mercy slept under the axle of a dead harvester and did not work anymore.  She was a bloodhound, and for six years her work had been finding bodies in the flats for the burial crews.  Her nose was intuitively descriptive; every scent arrived already telling its own story, how old, how afraid, how far.  That was the gift: she could translate distance into closeness, one breath at a time.  But every closeness she had ever made ended at something cold, and one morning she lay down on the trail and would not rise.  Ansel, her handler, had knelt beside her, smelling of lamp oil and sorrow, and said, "Enough, then." 

Three months ago he had walked east, chasing a rumor of living trees, leaving Mercy with his daughter Pim, a hand-cranked phonograph, and one wax cylinder.  Pim played it every night.  It held only his voice, scratchy and patient, and all the voice did was riddle.  "I am written where the needle cannot sing; read me with what found the dead, and find the living."  Pim, who was eleven and smelled of boiled rainwater and worry, could not solve it.  Neither could the salvage bosses, who had borrowed the cylinder twice, hoping for a map to anything worth taking. 

Then a salt trader came in off the flats with a parcel marked for the girl.  Inside lay an autumn leaf preserved in wax, red as a struck match, every vein sealed under an amber skin.  Nobody in Gantry had seen a leaf since before the war, and the whole yard went still around it.  Pim carried it to the harvester and held it down to Mercy without a word. 

Mercy did not want to smell it.  She breathed anyway, because the girl's hand was shaking.  Beeswax, tinted with rust; under it, sap, wet and green and impossible; and pressed into the wax, a thumbprint of lamp oil.  Ansel, alive when he sealed it. 

She stood, joints cracking, and went to the phonograph.  She knew that wax; the cylinder wore the same rust tint, and she had refused to read anything since the day she lay down.  Now she laid her nose along the grooves.  Past the voice-track, where the needle never traveled, the wax ran in faint colored bands, and each band was a text written for her alone.  The first groove held iron and old rain.  She looked east and whined toward the water tower that had fallen in the war. 

Pim tilted the cylinder to the lamp.  "It's red here," she whispered, "then yellow, then gray, then green."  Yellow was sulfur, the stinking spring two days out.  Gray was ash and bitter sage, country Mercy had never walked.  Green, the last and thinnest band, was the same wet sap that slept inside the leaf. 

Pim saw the colors and Mercy read the scents, and between them the riddle came apart like a knot in warm water.  He had hidden the road where the bosses would never look, in the one sense they could not borrow.  He had waited to send the key until the trees were true.  And he had trusted a dog who had quit to want one more trail, if only it ended warm. 

They left before dawn, while Gantry creaked in its sleep.  Pim wore the cylinder against her chest and the leaf inside her cap.  The wasteland opened ahead of them, cracked to the horizon, holding its breath the way a wild thing does before it bolts. 

At the fallen tower, Mercy lowered her head to the red-streaked iron.  Lamp oil, three months thin, and beside it a smear of rust-tinted wax where he had marked the leg for her.  No fear in it.  No cold. 

For six years her nose had told her how far away the dead were.  Now it told her how near the living could become, and the telling felt like a color she had never been shown.  Pim's hand found the loose skin of her neck.  "Yellow next," the girl said into the hush.  Mercy put her nose to the wind and began, breath by breath, to make the distance small.
