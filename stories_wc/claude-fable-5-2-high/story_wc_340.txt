Tovah Brill had scrubbed the boiler pits of Hallowmere Spire for nine years, and this morning she was promoted to carrying a dead man's things. 

The spire stood icebound above the clouds, a needle of frosted iron where airships once moored, and its only elevator was a brass cage that climbed ten floors in about the time it takes to regret something. 

She stepped in at the sculleries holding a tray: Captain Aurel Venn's goggles and Captain Aurel Venn's diary, bound for his daughter in the observatory. 

The steward had told her not to look at either. 

Tovah, who was warmly inquisitive in the way a kettle is warmly inclined to boil, had looked at both before the gate finished closing. 

The cage shuddered past the first floor, and she opened the diary. 

Everyone below stairs knew what it held, because the steward had read it aloud with relish: three years of misery, begun the day the captain's lungs failed and the physicians grounded him in the spire for good. 

"I am nailed to an icicle," ran a typical entry, "and the sky goes on without me." 

Miss Ilse Venn had read that far and no further, and had not come down from the observatory in the forty days since the funeral. 

Tovah felt sorry for her at a sensible distance, the way one feels sorry for weather. 

But by the second floor she was squinting, because her mother had stoked these furnaces for thirty years, humming, and had taught her that every groan in the pipes has a tune beneath it if you listen sideways. 

So Tovah read sideways. 

Between the bitter lines ran rows of pale scratches, too orderly for moths and too broken for words. 

At the third floor the shaft's windows began, thick with rime, and the morning sun came through them shattered into a thousand drifting sparks. 

One spark crossed the goggles on the tray, a smear of violet slid over the page, and where it passed the scratches joined hands into a word: laughed. 

Tovah said something her mother would never have hummed. 

By the fourth floor she had the goggles on, smelling of oil and old leather, their lenses cut in facets, and she held the diary up to the frosted glass. 

Read plainly, by lamp, the cipher was nothing; read through scattered light, each facet gathering a different broken ray, it stood up and spoke. 

The fifth floor said: "Ilse laughed today when the barometer fell on my foot; worth the toe." 

The sixth said: "Taught her to read wind off the rime today, and she is better than I was, which I shall not tell her yet." 

Tovah turned a page and found it folded; she let it unfold, and unfold again, into a chart as long as her arm, drawn in the same hidden strokes: a small airship, sketched rib by rib, and christened Ilse's Turn. 

Somewhere around the seventh floor she understood, with the pleasant chill of a puzzle closing, what the old man had done. 

He had kept his anguish in plain ink, where physicians and stewards could admire it, because anguish was what a grounded captain was expected to produce. 

His joy he had encrypted, because it embarrassed him, and because he meant it as a gift that could only be opened here, in the light of the very ice he cursed. 

Then he had died before explaining the trick, which was, Tovah reflected, extremely like a captain. 

At the eighth floor she considered folding the chart, removing the goggles, and delivering the tray as silently as furniture. 

That was the job, and she had waited nine years for it. 

But she had spent those years seeking out the joy beneath anguish, not from saintliness, only because the boiler pits were unbearable otherwise, and here was the largest tune she had ever found, and upstairs a woman who had heard only the groan. 

Passing the ninth, she did not expect thanks; grief is seldom polite, and servants who read diaries are seldom kept. 

She found she minded this less than she should. 

The cage rose into the observatory's glare and stopped with a sigh, and the gate rattled open on a room of glass and frost where a thin woman sat with her back to the sun. 

"Leave it," said Ilse Venn. 

"I will, miss," said Tovah, "but first put these on and hold any page to the window." 

Ilse took the goggles, more to end the conversation than to begin one, and lifted the diary toward the rimed panes. 

"Worth the toe," she read, and made a sound that was not yet a laugh but was plainly related to one. 

Tovah stayed at the top, humming.
