Dawn had lasted nine days over the sapphire viaduct when Nell Corrow carried the broken ship's bell up its stairs.  Everyone in Marrowquay knew why the sun sat stuck on the horizon like a coin nobody would pick up.  For three hundred years the bell had rung once for every fish landed, so the sea could count what it lent and the moon could repay it in tides.  Then old Hask hauled up the last fish, rang for it, and the bell split in the middle of the note.  The count never closed.  The tide stopped at half, the sun stopped at rising, and the world waited on an unfinished sum. 

Nell was a lukewarm mage, which was the academy's kind word for it.  She could not boil or freeze; she could only bring a thing to the temperature of a held hand and keep it there.  She had also joined three orders and left each one the week her enthusiasm cracked.  But she believed, with her whole uncomplicated heart, that a mended bell would finish its note and the sea would send the fish back.  Hask gave her the bell because nobody else had asked.  He followed her up because he had no nets to mend. 

The viaduct carried the River Sele across the bay on forty arches of blue stone, and the river had been dissolving it, very slightly, since the day it was built.  Every arch leaked, and every leak grew a sapphire icicle beneath it, drop by drop.  In the stalled dawn light they glowed like the inside of a wave. 

"The crack runs lip to crown, and a piece went overboard," Hask said. 

"Then I will rebuild the piece," said Nell, and wedged the bell under the fastest drip she could find.  It pattered like rain, and within what would have been two days the gap had filled with bright blue glass.  She rang it with all her strength.  The patch burst into glitter, and the old crack ran a finger longer. 

Hask did not laugh.  "That is how we broke it," he said.  "Every year the nets came up thinner, and every year we rang harder, as if loud were the same as faithful." 

Nell looked at the glitter on her sleeves and recognized it.  It was what her devotion had always been made of: quick, bright, and gone at the first hard knock. 

So she stopped hurrying and started counting.  She walked all forty arches with a finger on her pulse, timing each leak and scratching the blue it left behind.  Fast drips left glass that crumbled under a thumbnail.  Slower drips left stone that held a knife's edge.  The slowest, under the seventh arch, fell once every forty heartbeats and left nothing at all, because the water there ran too cold to give up what it carried.  A proper mage would have boiled it and gotten glass again.  Nell cupped the bell in both hands and made it lukewarm.  Each cold drop landed on bronze as warm as skin, lingered, and left behind a grain of blue too small to see.  Forty heartbeats, one drop.  It was the only spell she had, and for the first time it was exactly the right one. 

She kept at it for sixty thousand drops, dozing with her hands on the bronze, while Hask brought bread and the sun did not move.  Somewhere past the halfway mark, he told her gently that the fish were not coming back. 

"The last one was the last one," he said.  "A bell cannot ring a thing out of nothing." 

Once, that would have been the knock that shattered her.  She waited forty heartbeats, and found she was still holding the bell. 

"Then it is not a request," she said.  "It is a receipt.  The sea lent everything it had, and somebody should finish saying so." 

When the last grain settled, the seam was dull, dense, and the blue of deep water.  Nell hung the bell from the seventh arch and struck it once, no harder than a knock on a friend's door. 

The note was not loud.  It was level and patient, and it went on exactly as long as the half it had been missing.  Below them the tide let out its breath and began, at last, to ebb.  The sun came unstuck and climbed, and the icicles of the viaduct turned from sapphire to plain morning blue.  The mudflats came up wet, shining, and empty. 

Nell looked at them for a long while, and did not crack.  "I will come back tomorrow and ring it again," she said, "softly, for whatever the sea decides to lend next." 

Hask, who had not expected a tomorrow, nodded.
