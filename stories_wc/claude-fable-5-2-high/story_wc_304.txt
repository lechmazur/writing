The grotto hung in the high cold like a held breath, a hollow of floating stone moored to nothing, its mouth open to the aurora. 
Green light poured across the ceiling in slow curtains, and Maren worked beneath it with her combs laid out like a surgeon's knives. 
She was the grotto's beautician mortician, and tonight the body on the slab was Osk, who had taught her both halves of the trade. 
The first half was easy: wash the face, oil the beard, braid the grey hair the way he had worn it. 
The second half had always been his. 
Every body kept one last spark, Osk said, a final flicker that lingered for a single night before oblivion took it. 
Caught in an emerald chip split from the grotto's green seam, it would glow in a family's palm for generations. 
Missed, it simply went out, and the dead were only dead. 
Osk had caught hundreds. 
Maren, whose hands were never idle, had never caught one. 
Now there was no one left to do it for her, and no family below waiting for him except, she supposed, herself. 
She set the receiver on the slab beside his shoulder. 
It was a crude thing, a coil of copper and a brass horn, and it had no stations to find. 
It drank the aurora and gave back static, a dry hiss like sand poured on glass. 
The spark hides in the noise, Osk used to say, with his eyes closed and his big hands flat on his knees. 
You find the pattern, and the pattern tells you when. 
Maren listened and heard only hiss. 
She adjusted the coil. 
She smoothed a stray hair at his temple, straightened his collar, moved the lamp, and turned the horn a finger's width toward the sky. 
The hiss crackled and jumped with every touch, and still it told her nothing. 
Outside, the aurora was thinning at its eastern edge, where a grey seam of morning had begun to show. 
When the green went, the night went, and Osk's spark with it. 
Her fingers reached for the comb again, and she stopped them in the air. 
The static had flinched before she even touched him. 
She moved her hand left, and the hiss leaned left. 
She breathed out hard, and it rippled. 
All night she had been hunting a pattern in the noise, and most of the noise was her. 
She understood then why he had sat like a stone, and what he had meant by the phrase she had always taken for an old man's poetry. 
There is a threshold of stillness, girl, and the dead are already across it. 
You have to go and meet them there. 
Maren sat down on the cold stone and laid her hands flat on her knees. 
It was the hardest work she had ever done. 
Her fingers wanted the comb, her eyes wanted the dawn, and her chest wanted to grieve aloud. 
She let each wanting pass through her like wind through the grotto's mouth, and she did not follow any of them. 
She did not sleep or drift; she stayed lucidly watchful, awake in every nerve and moving in none. 
The static settled like silt in a jar. 
Under it, faint as a pulse felt through a glove, something repeated. 
Three soft ticks, a pause, two more. 
She knew it before she could name it: the rhythm Osk tapped on the slab's edge whenever he braided, three and two, three and two. 
The ticks were slowing, and the pauses were growing longer. 
The pattern was telling her when. 
She took the emerald chip from her apron and, in one unhurried motion, pressed it to the center of his brow. 
On the last tick she let her own held breath go, as he had taught her, to infuse the stone with whatever came. 
The receiver went silent. 
For a moment the chip was only a cold green splinter, and the grey in the east was winning. 
Then a point of light woke inside it, small and steady, warmer than the aurora and nothing like it. 
It pulsed three times, then twice, and held. 
Maren lifted the chip and closed her hand around it. 
Above her the last green curtain folded itself away, and morning came into the grotto without a sound. 
She did not reach for the comb. 
His hair was finished, and so, at last, was her apprenticeship. 
She sat a while longer beside him, perfectly still, with his light going three and two against her palm.
