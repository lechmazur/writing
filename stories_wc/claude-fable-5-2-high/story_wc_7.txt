Odile Marsh could taste memory in anything a hand had touched.  Bread carried the baker's quarrels; honey carried summers; a stolen plum carried guilt, sweet and a little rotten.  That was why she noticed, before anyone else in Fennick, what the Clearing had done. 

The Harmony Board had called it mercy: a painless procedure to prune grief from the brain, one clinic visit per citizen.  But grief grows tangled with everything else, and the clinics cut wide.  Now the food her neighbors cooked tasted of nothing at all, like stew with holes where the grandmothers should be.  Odile had skipped her own appointment and kept on skipping it; nobody on the Board thought to chase a soup cook. 

The seed came from Ines Vane, the botanist who had run the research greenhouse on the hill until the Board padlocked it.  The night before her appointment, Ines had pressed it into Odile's palm, black and ridged as a peach stone, and said only, "Wait for the sky."  Afterward Ines smiled at Odile in the market the way one smiles at a pleasant stranger. 

So Odile did what she did with any ingredient she didn't understand: she touched it to her tongue.  Ines's memories flooded in, tasting of chalk dust and cold coffee.  A knowledge fruit seed, the last one, saved from a tree that Ines's great-grandmother had coaxed to fruit exactly once.  The name was an old joke, because the fruit taught nobody anything.  It worked through synaptic plasticity: for a few hours it made a brain as supple as a child's, so that severed pathways could bud again from their stumps.  The seed would wake only under the Threefold Crossing, when the moon and two planets stood in a single line, once every ninety-one years.  The next Crossing was eleven days off and would last seven nights. 

"Well," Odile told the seed, "you can't rush a stock, but you can at least light the stove." 

She climbed the hill that evening and broke the rusted padlock with a meat mallet.  The greenhouse was a ribcage of rusted iron, half its panes fallen, dead vines hanging like burnt noodles from the rafters.  She licked rust from her thumb and tasted decades of patient work, abandoned mid-sentence.  A ruin, then, but she had made good suppers out of worse leftovers.  For eleven days she swept glass, hauled compost up from her kitchen, and patched the roof's biggest wounds with oiled canvas, leaving one pane open to the sky. 

On the first night of the Crossing, three lights threaded themselves like beads above the hill, and the seed split with a sound like a knuckle cracking.  It grew the way things grow in dreams, a hand's width each hour, throwing out shoots in every direction, greedy and frantic.  By the fourth night Odile understood it would spend itself on leaves and never fruit before the lights drifted apart. 

She would have to prune it, and her hands shook on the paring knife, because pruning was the Board's word.  Then she remembered her grandmother at the tomato stakes: you cut so the plant can reach, girl, never so it can't.  The Board had cut people to make them quiet.  Odile cut toward the light, taking the crossed and crowded shoots, sparing every branch that leaned at the open pane. 

On the seventh night the tree hung with fruit, small and gold, glowing faintly against the rusted ribs like lamps in a ruined church. 

She did not post notices or make speeches.  She baked forty small tarts, opened her counter as usual, and charged nothing extra.  A softened brain still needs something to grow toward, so each tart held a flavor she had tasted for years in one neighbor's cooking, before the holes. 

Ines Vane came in at noon and got the one that tasted of chalk dust and cold coffee.  She chewed, frowned, and set down her fork very carefully. 

"Odile," she said slowly, as if finding the name in an old coat pocket, "you waited for the sky." 

Then she wept, which nobody in Fennick had done since spring, and Odile came around the counter and held her. 

"Grief's just the salt," Odile said.  "Nobody wants a plate of it, but take it out and nothing tastes like anything." 

By dusk the street was loud with neighbors crying and laughing over crumbs, and no rule in the Board's books forbade dessert.  That night, as the three lights slid apart, two women climbed to the rusted greenhouse, where the tree still glowed, and saved every seed for whoever would be standing there in ninety-one years.
