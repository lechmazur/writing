The storm had blown itself out, and the lamp of Scar Light turned its copper hood over a sea gone quiet as poured oil.  Ansel stood on the gallery of the lighthouse prison and looked down at the jagged reef, where two warders lay among the rocks like dropped coats.  He had warned them.  He had told Brandt and Holloway that the seventh swell came clean over the west landing.  They had laughed, gone down to save the skiff, locked the landing gate behind them out of habit, and taken the key.  "Wolf's whelp," Holloway had said, not unkindly.  Everyone knew whose son he was.  His father had cried wolf three times across a valley, and the valley had never again spent belief on that bloodline.  Ansel was here, the last prisoner of a prison being forgotten, trusted with the lamp but never with his word, because he had once reported a ship foundering in fog and no wreck was ever found.  False alarm, the magistrate said, and it runs in the family. 

Since then he had been chasing hush.  Not silence, which was only the absence of his father's voice, but the particular hush in which a listener stops weighing the speaker and simply hears.  He had never once caught it. 

Now the relief cutter was due at dawn.  Its crew would land at the east steps, climb to the gallery, and look down; then they would find him, unlocked and alone, and whatever he cried after that would be wolf.  So the truth had to reach them before they found the bodies, and it could not come as a cry. 

He went down the spiral stair into the warren beneath the tower, cells and cisterns cut into the reef rock in no order any relief crew had ever learned.  At its heart stood the chapel cell, and the chapel's narrow door, rusted ajar for decades at the width of one sideways man.  Ansel raised his candle to it.  The oak was scuffed pale at shoulder height, hip height, wrist height, each mark a prisoner who had turned himself thin to pass.  No crowd had ever come through that gap.  The scuffs said so: one body at a time, lantern set down, companions left behind, and nobody on the far side to laugh with.  A valley had jeered his father; a door like this would sift any valley into single listeners. 

Inside, the confessional had rotted to a frame, but its brass lattice was whole, worn bright where foreheads had rested.  Men had told this lattice things no court would credit, and it had kept every one, the way a web holds dew that ought to fall.  A lattice sustains impossibilities because it never asks who is speaking.  He lifted it free and wedged it upright in the gap of the door, at the height of a face. 

Then he took off his jersey, gray wool from the flock the wolf finally took, his only inheritance besides the name, and began to unravel it.  He tied the first end to the ring of the east door, where the cutter's crew would enter before they had seen anything at all.  He walked the yarn through the warren the way the night had gone.  Past the hook where the landing key should hang and did not.  Twice around the bars of the landing gate, locked from the seaward side, where no prisoner's hand could have turned it.  All his life he had been lost inside his father's story; tonight he would thread the labyrinth with his own.  People walk a thread; they do not weigh it. 

The last of the wool ran past the scuffs, through one diamond of the lattice, and ended in his fist.  He sat down inside, on what was now the penitent's side, and blew out the candle.  High above, the lamp went on turning, and at each pass a little copper light came down the stairwell and rested on the brass.  For the first time since boyhood he was unhurried. 

Toward dawn he heard oars, the east door, a voice calling the warders' names and getting no answer.  Then the yarn drew taut in his hand, slackened, drew taut again, as someone followed it by the fingertips.  It paused a long while at the gate.  At last a lantern stopped outside the narrow door, and a shadow came to hover at the lattice, not entering, not leaving. 

"Is someone there?" a woman asked, low, the way people speak in chapels. 

Ansel did not cry out.  "They are on the west reef," he said through the brass, "and I will tell you once how it happened." 

The shadow stayed, and there it was: the hush.
