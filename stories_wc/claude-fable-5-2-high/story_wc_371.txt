The last geese had dragged their ragged line south over the city three days ago, and Odile Brandt had waited for exactly that.  With the birds gone, seed could lie on open ground overnight and still be there in the morning.  So last night she had sown the rubble lot on Kessel Street for the third autumn running, and today she carried what the second sowing had given her through a gap in the palace wall. 

The courtyard beyond had been abandoned so slowly that no one could name the year it happened.  Odile could, almost: she saw where ivy had taken one more windowsill since spring, where a fig had lifted another paving stone the width of a thumb.  Noticing such things was her affliction.  For nineteen years in the permits office she had watched the city subtract itself by amounts too small to protest: a bench unbolted, a streetlamp left dark, a bakery's hours trimmed by thirty minutes, then an hour, then a day.  She had noticed every step, and she had stamped every form. 

The demolition order for Tomas Vesna's bakery had been one of forty on her desk one Tuesday.  Within a year the developer had gone bankrupt, the old baker had died, and the neighbors walked past the fenced rubble as if nothing but rubble had ever stood there.  That forgetting frightened her more than the wrecking crew had.  She had let something holy go because it looked like a shop that sold rolls, and she wanted, late as it was, to learn to see the sacred inside ordinary things while some were still left. 

So she resigned, cut a flap in the fence, and began to garden land that was not hers.  She knew nothing about growing, so she worked by rules of thumb, and the rules made her gentle: never pull what you cannot name, move a root only at dusk with its own soil around it, ask the ground once and then wait.  Winter rye came up between the broken bricks where the ovens had been.  This July she had cut it with kitchen scissors, threshed it in a pillowcase, and ground it in a coffee mill until she had two kilos of gray, fragrant flour. 

The tea house hid in the palace's old orangery, behind wisteria thick as a wall, with no sign and no license.  Odile had found it last winter by following a smell she thought the city had lost.  Mirela Vesna, the baker's daughter, served six mismatched tables there, and with every pot of tea she set down a slice of dark sour bread raised from her father's starter, which she had carried out of the bakery in a jam jar the night before the machines came.  Bread from a bakery that no longer existed; Odile had eaten it weekly for a year and had never once said who she was. 

Today the room was empty, the stove ticking, the eaves silent where swallows had nested.  Mirela brought the tea and the slice.  Odile set her bundle on the table and unwrapped the cloth from the flour sack. 

"Rye," she said.  "Grown on Kessel Street, where your father's ovens stood." 

Mirela's hand stopped above the sack.  "I saw green through that fence in May," she said, "and thought I was imagining it." 

Odile had uncloaked the field; now she had to uncloak herself.  "I worked in permits," she said.  "The order that took the bakery has my stamp on it, and I knew what it was when I stamped it, and I told myself it was only one more small thing." 

The stove ticked.  Mirela looked at her for a long time, the way one looks at a stranger who turns out to have been in every photograph. 

"He kept baking the last morning," she said finally.  "Forty loaves, with the crew waiting outside, because people would want breakfast." 

"I bought one," Odile said.  "I did not understand what I was holding." 

Mirela did not say she forgave her.  She took the jam jar from its shelf by the stove, spooned a little of Odile's flour into the starter, added water, and stirred until the gray paste loosened and breathed. 

"It may refuse strange grain," she said.  "Come back Thursday and we will see whether it has risen; I am promising nothing past Thursday." 

It was not pardon.  It was a jar with a cloth over it, alive or not, and Odile, who had learned to ask the ground once and then wait, found that it was enough to stand on.  She ate her slice slowly, noticing everything: the sour, the crust, the steam, and, on the jar's glass, the first small bubble climbing.
