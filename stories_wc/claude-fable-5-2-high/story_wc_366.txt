The Garrow light flickered out at dusk, while Ines Calder was still a mile off with the week's supplies.  She did not swear or pray; she simply opened the throttle, because talking had never moved a boat.  For nineteen years she had run oil, flour, and mail to the four lights of the Skerry chain, and in that time she had said perhaps a hundred words to each keeper.  She noticed them instead.  She knew which keeper's hands shook pouring tea, which one saved his crusts for the gulls, and that old Pell on Garrow hummed whenever the fog came down so the dark would know someone was home. 

The keepers spoke to her in stones.  At every landing stood a stack: three flat stones meant all well, a fourth meant send more oil, a toppled stack meant come quickly.  Pell always crowned his with a piece of green sea glass, which meant the kettle was on. 

They spoke to one another through her, too, in the letter chain.  Nobody admitted starting it: an oilskin packet that traveled light to light in her mail locker, each keeper adding one unsigned page before she carried it on.  No names, no replies owed, only a page of whatever a person could say to no one in particular.  Ines had never read a line of it.  She only knew that men walked lighter after they had held it, and she guarded that packet more carefully than the oil. 

Tonight the stack at Garrow landing stood three stones high, with no glass on top.  All well, and no kettle. 

She climbed the hundred and twelve steps with the packet inside her coat.  Every keeper on the chain said Garrow's lantern room was haunted, that the ones who had tended it never quite went off watch.  Ines had felt it before as a courteous chill by the gallery door, a sense of being counted.  Now, with the lamp dead, the room held a faint glow of its own, as though the great lens remembered light the way a shell remembers the sea. 

Pell sat in his chair beneath it, hands folded, finished.  Ines stood a long moment and let herself see him properly: the darned cuff, the mug gone cold, the small smile of a man who had heard an engine coming.  At his feet he had built one last stack, five stones, a height their code had never used.  The green glass sat on top, and under the bottom stone lay a folded page.  He had left it under stones, and stones were how keepers spoke to her, so for the first time she read. 

"I started the chain," the page said.  "Forty years ago a girl drifted past this rock in a dory, in fog, and through my spyglass I saw her face when the beam found her.  I have wanted ever since to bottle that look, that feeling of safety, and hand it to the men who make it and never get to feel it.  Letters were the nearest bottle I could find.  Five stones means: keep it going, whoever you are." 

Ines sat down on the cold iron floor.  She had been that girl, and she had wanted the very same thing ever since: to bottle the safety of that beam and never be without it again.  It was why she kept close to the lights and far from people.  She turned the sea glass in her fingers, a bottle once, broken and worn soft, and understood what the sea had been telling her.  Nothing that mattered stayed corked; safety was not a thing you kept but a thing you passed along, beam by beam, page by page. 

Out past the reef, a trawler's lantern was wandering toward the rocks.  She rose to protect it, and found the wick already trimmed, the reservoir full, the match tin waiting open on the sill where no living hand had set it.  The chill at the gallery door had company now, something warmer, humming. 

"I see you," Ines said aloud, which was more than she usually managed, and struck the match.  The lens caught and threw the light out over the water, and the trawler turned. 

She kept the watch till dawn, and in the small hours she wrote her first page, unsigned, in a hand the other keepers would not know.  She told them what their lights looked like from a small boat in fog.  She folded it into the oilskin beside his, so the chain would hold and its maker stay as nameless as he had wished.  At sunrise she rebuilt the landing stack, five stones, and set the green glass on top.
