In the Silver House, Ilvane sold everything except touch.  Patrons paid to watch her body speak: a wrist turned like a line break, a spine that held a stanza, a heel that landed where a rhyme should.  They called her the chaste courtesan and meant it as a joke, but her refusal was the only thing she owned.  Lately, even that was softening. 

The cause stood in the silver courtyard under snow: the prism sanctuary, a glass chapel no taller than a well house, which caught starlight every clear night and sang.  Inside hung the harvest of echoes.  Each autumn the Wardens walked the city with spindles, winding up whatever people had said against the grain: every no, every why, every not like that.  The words left the air as pale threads, and the threads were hung in the prism, where starlight plucked them into a lullaby. 

It was a beautiful song.  It had two phrases, a long falling sigh and a rising smile, and it made the whole city agree.  Nobody quarreled in the markets anymore.  Nobody asked Ilvane what her dances meant; they nodded along, serene as dolls, and went home to sleep at the same hour.  The peace frightened her more than any riot could have.  What frightened her most was the evening she caught her own hand rising to accept a patron's, gently, as if it were someone else's idea.  Her no was in the prism with all the others.  She meant to have it back. 

The sanctuary door had no keyhole.  It was bound shut by a snarl of the oldest threads, forty knots deep, drawn so tight by the song that fingers slid off them like rain off glass.  But a dancer hears where a phrase breathes, and Ilvane had heard it: between the sigh and the smile the lullaby rested for one beat, and in that beat the threads went slack.  One beat was enough for one knot, if the hands were quick and the body knew the meter. 

So for thirty-nine nights she had crossed the courtyard while the house slept, knelt in the snow at the door, and waited.  Each knot was an echo, and it would not loosen until she had felt its shape.  A fishwife's why should I came undone with a shrug of the shoulders.  A boy's that is not fair needed a stamped heel.  She untangled the threads the way she danced, by letting her body say the line the knot was holding, and each freed strand drifted off over the rooftops toward whoever had first spoken it.  Thirty-nine people in the city had begun, quietly, to frown again. 

On the fortieth night, Madam Oriel was waiting by the sanctuary with a lantern and a fur over her arm.  She kept the house and, in autumn, carried a spindle.  She did not shout. 

"You will catch your death, dear," she said, in the voice of someone who has not disagreed with anything for years.  "Come in and be warm." 

"One knot left," Ilvane said. 

"I know," said Madam Oriel, and held out her hand, kind and unhurried, because she did not need to hurry. 

Overhead the stars cleared the chimney line, the prism filled with light, and the lullaby began its sigh.  It poured through Ilvane like warm milk.  Her shoulders dropped; her knees wanted to stand, her head wanted to nod, and her hand, traitor, lifted an inch toward the offered one.  How easy it would be, and how pleasant, and how like everyone. 

The sigh ended. 

In the rest, the last knot slackened under her fingers, and she felt whose it was.  It was small and plain, a girl's voice on her first night in the Silver House, saying no.  This knot would not open for a shrug or a stamp.  Her body had to mean the word, here, with the song in her blood and a kind hand waiting. 

Ilvane turned her lifted palm outward, a single clean gesture, the shortest poem she had ever danced.  With her other hand she drew the thread free, and the sanctuary unlocked with a sound like ice letting go of a pond. 

The harvest came out all at once, ten thousand pale threads streaming up into the starlight and away over the walls, each one hunting its own throat.  The prism stood empty and bright, and the smile it owed the city never came.  Somewhere beyond the gate a shutter banged, and a man's voice said, loudly and with great surprise, "No." 

Madam Oriel lowered her hand and looked at it, frowning, as though trying to remember an old argument.  Ilvane rose from the snow, and the smile that finished the song was her own.
