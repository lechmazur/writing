The plague ended on a Tuesday according to the Wardens and on a Thursday according to the Harbor Guild, which tells you most of what you need to know about Vess. 

I live in the upper collector sewer beneath the old customs quarter.  The city sank; the sewer, built by better engineers, sank less.  At low tide the roofs of Vess stand out of the lagoon like bad teeth, and my tunnel is dry to the knee. 

I call myself a philosopher.  I am a philosopher the way a man with a bucket is a navy.  I have a treatise of three chapters, no readers, and one real skill, which I did not invent.  It is silt-reading, the method of the old tide clerks, who logged every flood of this city by the mud it left behind.  The plague took the clerks and left their manual, half rotted, and me, who had nothing else to read in quarantine. 

So when the survivors came back to argue over the ruins, both sides came down my ladder. 

The Guild's story: the fever walked in overland from the hill farms, and then the sea rose by itself and finished the job.  Nobody's fault, so salvage goes to whoever held the docks. 

The Wardens' story: the Guild let a fever ship, the Patient Margaret, dock for her cargo, then broke the sea gate to wash away the proof.  Murder, so salvage goes to the families of the drowned. 

Each side offered me a year of bread to confirm it.  I told them the mud does not eat bread. 

"Rude, for a drain rat," said the Guild factor. 

"Accurate, for a drain rat," I said. 

Then I cut the section.  The method is simple and nobody likes it: you do not dig for what you want.  You cut one clean wall straight down through the sedimentary layers of the settling basin, and you read from the bottom up, the order in which it was written.  It took four days with a mason's trowel. 

At the bottom, black muck: the ordinary life of a city.  Above it, a white band thick as my hand: quicklime, washed down from the streets where they limed the dead.  Above the lime, gray lagoon silt, not in one slab but in eleven thin bands, each capped with a hair of winter weed.  Eleven slow years.  No gate was broken; the sea came in the way damp comes into a wall. 

And inside the lime, not above it, sat a brass cog.  A seafarer's chronometer cog, cut finer than any town clock, stamped on its rim: P. MARGARET.  The ship docked, somebody stripped her instruments to sell while her crew was still warm, and one cog went down a drain with the lime. 

So the Guild lied about the ship and the Wardens lied about the gate. 

I agonized over it for two tides, quietly, because the tunnel is too damp to echo.  If I announced a verdict, each side would keep the half it liked and call me bought for the rest.  Worse, I would become an oracle, and oracles die, and then there is nothing left but stories again.  The clerks did not leave Vess a verdict; they left a method, and I had nearly traded it for the pleasure of being right in public. 

So I rebelled the only way I know, which is to reason it through and then be unpleasant about it.  I sent word that no finding would be issued, and that each side should send one person, in boots, at low tide. 

They came angry: the factor, and a Warden girl who had lost a street of cousins.  I gave them a candle each and the first page of the manual. 

"Bottom up," I said.  "Do not tell me what it means; tell me what it is." 

"White," said the factor.  "Lime." 

"Below the gray, or above?" 

"Below." 

The girl counted the gray bands twice, got eleven both times, and went quiet.  Then the factor found the cog, which I had left in the wall, and read its rim aloud, and he went quiet too. 

"You could have just told us," he said. 

"You would have just not believed me," I said.  "Now you have to disbelieve your own thumbs." 

They did not reconcile; I will not insult you.  They climbed my ladder still arguing, but arguing about bands and lime, which is a better class of argument. 

The girl came back at the next low tide and asked what the second page said.  She holds the candle badly.  The cog stays in the wall, and I have started chapter four.
