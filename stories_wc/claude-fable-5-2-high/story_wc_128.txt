The last chord of the harbor symphony dissolved into wind, and the applause rose toward the conductor like gulls toward a fishing boat.  Marta Quill, cargo broker of Tern Wharf, did not clap yet.  She was counting, as she always counted.  On the roof terrace of the Quayside Library, where shelves stood open to the stars under canvas sails and the breeze turned pages nobody was reading, she counted the people no one was applauding.  The page-turner in mismatched shoes.  The two dockhands who had hauled the piano up four flights and now leaned by the atlases, pretending their backs did not hurt.  The girl who had weighted every music stand with beach stones so the wind could not steal a single note.  Marta noticed such things the way other brokers noticed tariffs, with a whimsical attentiveness her rivals mistook for distraction until she outbid them.  She knew which gull robbed which crate, and she had privately named every crane on the wharf. 

Six months earlier, she would not have been on this roof at all.  Then an envelope meant for 14 Tern Row arrived at 14 Tern Wharf, her office, one smudged word astray.  A shrewd broker opens nothing that is not hers, but the flap had split, and a tarnished locket slid onto her ledger.  Inside was a photo no bigger than a thumbnail: a woman on a breakwater, holding up a lantern with a dented brass hood.  Marta looked from the photo to the wall above her desk, where that same lantern, dent and all, had hung since her father died. 

She carried the envelope to Tern Row herself.  The night librarian, Odalys Finch, received it with ink on her knuckles and suspicion in her eyebrows, because brokers did not climb library stairs without wanting something. 

"My grandmother," Odalys said, opening the locket.  "She walked the breakwater every foggy night, lighting boats home, and the harbor never paid her or wrote down her name." 

"She once pulled a half-drowned stowaway out of a storm," Marta said slowly, "fed him, and sent him off with her own lantern." 

Odalys stared. 

"That boy was my father, and he built a brokerage by its light without ever learning whom to thank." 

So a friendship began over cold tea and a wrong address, the broker shelving returns on slow nights, the librarian teaching her that a catalog and a manifest are cousins.  Both, Odalys said, were lists of what the world carried and who carried it, though neither usually bothered with the second part.  That sentence had lodged in Marta like a splinter, and she had spent the spring doing sums. 

Now, as the applause thinned and the conductor took his third bow, she felt the sums trembling in her coat pocket.  One part in a hundred of every fee her brokerage earned was not much.  It might embarrass the people it meant to honor, or dry up in a bad season, or be laughed off the wharf by Friday. 

Odalys, beside her, pressed the locket into her palm and said, "Go on, before you audit the joy out of it." 

Marta stood, and the dented lantern she had carried up from her office swung lit in her other hand.  "Before anyone goes home," she called, her voice smaller than she liked, "I wish to initiate something, and I may be doing it badly." 

She opened a new ledger on the lectern, its first page blank except for one name: Ines Finch, lamp-walker.  "This is the Lantern Ledger, and it will live in this library, where names are kept properly."  "Each season it will record, and pay, the hands this harbor leans on and forgets." 

Then she read them out: the page-turner, the stone-gathering girl, the dockhands by the atlases, the night loaders who had sent the orchestra's crates up dry.  She got one name wrong, was corrected, and wrote the correction down with care. 

The silence that followed was the kind she feared.  Then the conductor set down his baton and began to clap toward the atlases, and the roof took it up, ragged and astonished and loud.  One dockhand wiped his face with his cap.  The stone girl asked, very seriously, whether she might add her brother, who mended the nets nobody saw mended. 

"That," Marta said, handing her the pen, "is exactly how it is supposed to work." 

She did not let herself grin fully; one percent was still one percent, and winter was coming.  But the breeze lifted the ledger's first page as if to read it, and under the stars the lantern that had saved a stowaway and raised a broker burned steadily, waiting for whoever would carry it next.
