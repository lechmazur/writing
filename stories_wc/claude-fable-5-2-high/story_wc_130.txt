The dripping stopped at midnight, as it always did when frost sealed the seep above the cavern.  Ilse waited among her pale pear trees until the last stalactite let go its last drop and the orchard grew silent beneath the moon, which leaned through the broken roof like a neighbor at a fence.  The villagers who bought her fruit believed the mineral water did all the growing.  She let them believe it, and she conjured only in this hour, when nobody climbed down to watch. 

Tonight she carried a small tin that rattled.  Inside lay a pair of glass eyes, amber with black pupils, which Tobin had made for an owl he never finished mounting.  Her husband had been a taxidermist, a patient man who liked to say that the eyes decided whether a creature looked dead or merely paused.  Eleven years ago he had walked into the lower galleries with a lantern to look for a new spring, and the galleries had kept him, or he had kept walking.  No body, no letter, no lantern. 

Ilse had buried her mother and felt that grief shrink by honest fractions, year over year, the way a sum is paid down.  This one did not shrink.  It swung between mourning and expecting him for supper, and she wanted to understand the mathematics of that, what rule governed a feeling missing one of its terms. 

She knelt at the stepping stone where the orchard path met the mouth of the lower galleries.  Tobin had laid it their first spring, a mosaic of blue and white tile set in a ring, and a stalactite had been dripping on it ever since.  Every drop was recorded there, the ones that fell while he was home and the ones that fell after, chipped into the glaze as flecks and crescents.  It was the only ledger of her waiting that she had not written herself, and so the only one she trusted. 

A harsher conjurer would have cracked the stone open and demanded an answer.  Ilse's craft had always been gently inventive, the same small coaxing that taught pear roots to drink limewater, and she saw no reason to shout at a thing Tobin had made.  She breathed on the glass eyes, whispered to each a different instruction, and held one before each of her own. 

Through the left, the stone showed only what was gone: the chips glowed like a scatter of cold stars, and the tile between them went dark.  Through the right, it showed only what remained: the ring of blue, stubborn and nearly whole.  Neither picture meant anything alone.  She had to dovetail them, the way Tobin joined the corners of his specimen cases, loss cut to fit into remainder. 

For an hour she tried to make the two images close flush, to force the chips to total something final, a number she could carve on a marker and be done.  They slid apart every time, and her eyes ached, and the moon moved a hand's width across the roof. 

Then she remembered his cases properly.  A dovetail does not hold because the wood is solid; it holds because half of each board has been cut away to receive the other.  She stopped forcing, let the dark places in one picture stay open, and the bright places of the other slid into them with a click she felt in her teeth. 

The joined pattern was a curve.  It began at the center of the stone, where the earliest chips clustered, and wound outward toward the rim, drawing nearer the edge with every turn and never reaching it.  Ilse knew the shape from her girlhood sums.  It was a line that approaches its limit forever, and it told her nothing about whether Tobin lived.  It told her instead why her grief would not pay down.  Grief was not a debt in her case but a distance, always closing, never closed, and a thing that never closes is not broken.  It is only unfinished, like an owl waiting for its eyes. 

She sat back on her heels and cried a little, which she had not allowed herself in years, never knowing the correct amount.  She set one glass eye on the stone facing the galleries and the other facing the orchard, so that something of his would keep both watches while she slept.  From her apron she took a pear seed and pressed it into the wet silt beside the path, just where a man coming up from below would have to see it grown.  She covered it with her thumb and told nobody. 

Near dawn the frost loosened, and the first drop fell and struck the stone between the two amber eyes.
