The ferry capsized on the same night that noise became impossible, so Maren never heard her brother drown.  She remembered mouths open in the black water, shaping screams that went nowhere, and the tuning fork case bumping against her chest like a small wooden raft.  She had clung to it until dawn. 

Two winters later, the world was still mute, and the village, having no use for a piano tuner, had given Maren the keys to the winterglass conservatory on the headland.  Its panes had been grown from brine frost rather than blown, and they kept warmth the way wet sand keeps a footprint.  A palm laid on winterglass left an amber print that lingered for an hour; a sigh left a rose-colored cloud.  Inside, the orchids went on blooming as though nothing had happened to the air.  Maren called them her unshaken marvels, and she kept a list of such things chalked on a slate by the door: orchids, tides, bread rising, the warmth of a hand. 

The forks were not on the list.  Struck against her knee, they only shivered, eight steel tongues with nothing left to say.  Then, one frozen morning, she set a fork warmed in her pocket against a pane, and a thin gold line bloomed and faded like a held note.  A hotter fork made a brighter, longer line.  Maren stood very still, understanding that pitch could become temperature, that duration could become fading, and that silence itself might be translated into music if she could only learn what it was trying to say. 

So she began heat-mapping emotions.  Villagers came in from the cold, leaned against the glass, and thought of weddings, debts, dead dogs, first kisses.  Maren charted what their bodies left behind: joy flared in the chest and fingertips, shame burned the face and starved the hands, longing pooled low and steady as banked coals.  She assigned each fork a temperature, heated them over a brazier, and played the maps back onto the great south wall, where chords opened in color and decayed in rhythm.  People wept without a sound and came back the next week. 

Her largest composition was the capsize.  She had mapped it from her own body, pressing herself to the glass and remembering, and every measure came out blue-black, because that was what she believed the night had been: cold water and a brother who let go without a sign.  Tobin had been beside her at the rail, and then he had simply not been. 

She performed the piece on the anniversary, laying barely warmed forks to the wall, and the panes stayed dark as a bruise.  The audience shifted on the benches.  It was accurate, she told herself, and it was dead. 

Old Oskar, the ferry's deckhand, stood up in the back.  He had always been silently expressive, even before the Hush, a man who argued with his eyebrows and apologized with his shoulders, and now his whole frame said no.  He walked to the wall, found the measure where the hull rolled, and pressed both palms flat to the glass.  Gold blazed under his hands. 

Then he turned and acted it out for her: a man in the water, an arm hooked through a floating case, a sister thrashing an arm's length away.  Oskar's hands shoved the invisible case forward, hard, and then opened, empty, and sank. 

Maren looked at the tuning fork case on her table, at the scuffed corner she had never explained.  It had not drifted to her.  It had been given, in the one language left, and she had scored that gift as nothing.  Her face burned; she knew exactly how it would map. 

She could not rectify the night, but she could rectify the score, and the brazier was still lit.  She took the A fork, Tobin's favorite, the one he used to hum against, and held it in the coals with tongs until the tines glowed.  Oskar stepped aside. 

She began the piece again from the rolling hull, blue, blue, blue-black, and at the measure where her brother vanished she set the hot fork to the glass.  A single line of gold climbed the wall, branched through the frost-grain of the pane, and hung there, shining, long after it should have faded.  Nobody could gasp aloud, but forty breaths bloomed rose on the panes at once. 

When the last color had gone, Maren walked to the slate by the door and chalked one more line beneath the orchids and the tides.  The list of unshaken marvels now ended with a brother's last push, and with eight steel forks that could sing after all.
