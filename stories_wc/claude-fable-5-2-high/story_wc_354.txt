The courier reached the Nexus at twilight, when the lodestone ridges tightened their pull and every compass needle spun like a dizzy child.  Vesna Aldric, exiled chess tutor to the heir of Carrow, took the sealed slip with two careful fingers.  For eleven years the slips had come, each carrying one move in the game she and Oriel had never finished.  This one was blank. 

"I ride back before the next phase of the moon," the courier said, eyeing the ridges uneasily.  "Three nights, then your answer or none."  By the rule they had sworn, an unanswered move forfeited everything. 

She carried the slip to the slate table outside her hut, where juniper pieces stood exactly centered on their squares; iron ones would have crept at dusk.  Vesna was thoughtfully precise in everything, which was why the regent had banished her.  He had wanted a tutor who let the child win.  She had wanted an heir who could sit inside a hard position until it showed its beauty.  So she and eight-year-old Oriel had made a covenant over the board: never the easy move when a true one exists, and never abandon a game begun.  The regent's decree had eclipsed that covenant, throwing its long shadow across a border and a decade.  But an eclipse, she reminded herself, is a shadow passing, not a light going out. 

From her pocket she drew the worry stone Oriel had pressed into her hand at the gate.  It was polished basalt, black as a king, with a dent a small thumb had worn during every difficult position.  Eleven years of her own thumb had widened it, so that now the dent fit neither of them and both.  She set it on the slate, and it turned slowly, as it did every twilight here, answering a north that no needle could find.  She had chosen this unmapped crossing of ridges for exactly that reason: where nothing pointed the way, direction had to be earned. 

A blank slip could mean resignation, and the thought sat in her chest like a swallowed pawn.  Yet she had spent her life translating silence; a board says nothing aloud, and every empty square still means something.  So she lit a lamp, laid out all the old slips by date, and began to correlate the length of each pause with the move that had followed it.  The work took two nights and was not easy, which pleased her. 

The pattern, when it came, was clean as a well-played endgame.  Oriel's blunders had always arrived quickly, dashed off in fear or temper.  The long silences, without exception, had come before the finest moves: the quiet rook lift at fourteen, the patient knight retreat at seventeen.  Silence was not Oriel giving up; silence was Oriel looking at something hard until it turned beautiful.  And this silence, five months long, was the longest of all. 

On the third twilight she studied the position itself.  Oriel's black king stood hemmed behind its own pawns, safe and slowly suffocating, much as the heir sat hemmed in the regent's court.  Every easy move lost.  Only one true move existed, and it was terrifying: the king must leave its shelter and walk across the open board, straight toward her side. 

Vesna laughed once, softly, and her eyes stung.  The slip was blank because this move could not be written; it could only be made.  Somewhere on the border road, she believed, a young heir was walking out from behind the pawns. 

She might be wrong; she was precise enough to admit that.  Eleven years had taken things that no reunion would return, and the child who wore that small dent was gone for good.  Still, the covenant asked for the true move, not the certain one. 

When the courier returned, the moon not yet turned, she handed him nothing. 

"No reply?" he asked. 

"My answer is a lamp," she said, "because needles fail here, and someone will need a light to steer by." 

She hung the lamp on the highest ridge post, carried a second stool to the table, and set the basalt stone on the square where the black king would arrive.  The stone turned in the gathering pull and settled, its dent facing the road. 

Twilight thinned to violet, and the first stars came out above ridges that had never pointed anyone home.  Far down the dark slope, something small and yellow flickered, perhaps a lantern, perhaps only a low star.  Vesna straightened one juniper pawn exactly on its square, folded her hands, and waited for the beautiful, difficult move to finish crossing the board.
