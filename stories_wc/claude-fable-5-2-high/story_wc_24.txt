After blowing the whistle, Marit Aune heard the fjord take its quiet back.  It had been the long blast, the one the signal code reserved for a single meaning: do not trust this station's time.  The echo went up between the cliffs, came down as nothing, and the fishing boats turned their lamps toward Skarvnes Horizon Measurement Station and waited. 

That was three weeks ago.  Since then the director had sailed south to explain himself, the two observers had gone with him, and Marit remained, because she was the hydraulic clock shepherd and a flock cannot be left. 

Her flock was twelve water clocks in a stone hall, each a copper cistern spilling over a weir filed to a razor edge, so that the thinnest possible sheet of water fell into the counting vessel below.  They wandered like sheep.  Cold thickened them, thaw hurried them, and every dawn she walked the row with a float gauge, nudging stragglers back toward agreement.  Their work was to time the instant the sun's lower rim touched the sea, the figure from which the Directorate printed the tables every pilot on the coast steered by. 

But in autumn the Directorate had sent a mantle clock from the capital, walnut and brass, with orders to log the sunset by its hands and trim the flock to match.  The mantle clock agreed beautifully with the printed tables and disagreed with the sun by as much as forty seconds, which at sea is a reef.  So she had pulled the whistle cord, and written to the Admiralty, and kept the mantle clock key in her apron pocket ever since. 

The reply came by mail boat on a windless morning.  Your conduct is eminently peculiar, it read; an examiner arrives at midwinter, and you will demonstrate to him which clock is true. 

Marit read it twice beside the cisterns and found, under her anger, an honest hole.  She knew the mantle clock was wrong.  She could not say what her own clocks were right about, since they drifted with every frost, and she had never once asked what a clock really measures.  Before she accused anyone again, she wanted to understand that. 

So each evening she turned the question around.  Instead of reading the vessels that filled, she read the cisterns that emptied, counting backwards from the brim, mark by mark, toward the touch.  She did the same with the mantle clock, whose spring took fourteen half-turns of the key; each evening the turns it accepted told her how many it had left.  Then she went up to the observation slit, where the snow lay unmarked on the sill, and squinted along the dip-sight at the sea. 

The fjord below was so still that the mountains stood in it twice.  Beyond the mouth, the horizon ran hard and thin as the lip of a weir. 

Nine nights she counted down, and nine nights no clock reached zero when the sun touched.  The water clocks ran slow by exactly the depth of the frost; the mantle clock ran fast by exactly the tightness of a spring tempered in a warm workshop far away. 

On the tenth night, with the numbers falling in her mouth, she understood.  Counted forward, a clock seems to gather time; counted backward, it shows what it is, a thing spending itself.  Water measures its own emptying, a spring its own loosening, and neither has ever touched the sun.  Time was not in the cisterns at all.  It was out there on the razor edge, one instant wide, and the only true thing in the station was the walk she made each dawn to bring the wanderers back to it.  The Directorate's lie had never been a wrong clock, since every clock is wrong.  The lie was the order to stop looking. 

At midwinter the examiner climbed from the boat, a neat man with a ledger, and asked which clock was true.  Marit put the mantle clock key in his glove and led him up to the slit. 

"Squint," she said, "and count down with me." 

They counted backwards together, his breath and hers, while below them twelve cisterns and one spring ran out at thirteen different speeds.  The sun touched the edge between two of their numbers, belonging to neither. 

The examiner said nothing for a while, which in that place was easy.  "Then none is true," he said at last, "and you must come up here every evening." 

"Every evening," said Marit. 

He gave back the key, and she hung it on the nail beside her float gauge, one more straggler to mind.  Then she went down and blew the whistle once, short, the signal for time checked, and the fjord took its quiet back.
