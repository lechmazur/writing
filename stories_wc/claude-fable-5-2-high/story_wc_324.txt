At Saint Alder's Hospice, the dead called once, at twilight, and Ines Varga's job was making sure someone answered.  Her badge said contact tracer for hearts, which made new nurses smile until they watched her work.  When a heart stopped, she traced everyone it had touched, the estranged son, the chess partner, the woman at the bakery, and gathered them before dusk.  Because the calls came thin as breath on glass, and a call nobody received simply went out, like a pilot light. 

The loud dead, the well-loved ones, never worried her.  She had taken the job for the quiet ones, the voices with no one listed, because her own grandmother had called into an empty room.  All Ines had of that call was the instrument that should have caught it: her grandmother's hearing aid, a beige crescent forty years old.  Its old telecoil picked up what modern devices filtered away, and the dead arrived in it as faint pulses, each one a knock from far off. 

Tobias Wren had died at noon with a blank contact sheet.  No family, the chart said, no visitors, no one to notify.  Ines did not believe in blank sheets.  She had spent the afternoon asking the same question of orderlies, cooks, and the man who fixed the lifts, long after her supervisor told her to file it and go home.  What she had to show for it was his last photograph, taken by a volunteer a week before.  Tobias sat in his wheelchair among the atrium ferns, almost smiling, and on his shoulder rested a hand cropped off at the wrist.  The hand wore a green gardening glove. 

Whose hand?  That was all she wanted to know, and it itched at her like a splinter. 

So at twilight she carried the photograph down to the atrium, where the misters had been running for the ferns and the evening chill had turned their spray to fog.  The glass roof held the last violet of the sky.  Mist stood between the planters in slow white rooms, and every sound she made came back to her smaller.  She fitted the hearing aid into her ear and waited. 

The call came the way they always did, like stepping off a stair that was not there.  A pulse, a pause, a pulse, and inside them a man's voice worn down to its grain, saying one word over and over: Marguerite. 

"I hear you, Mr. Wren," Ines whispered.  "I'll find her." 

But there was a second rhythm under his, and that stopped her.  It was fainter, ragged, stumbling where the dead were steady, and no call she had ever traced had sounded afraid.  A sensible tracer would have logged interference.  Ines turned her head slowly, the way her grandmother used to when hunting a voice across a crowded kitchen, and the stumbling pulse sharpened toward the east wall. 

She followed it through the fog, three steps, listen, three steps, listen, past the fountain, past the tree ferns, the ticking growing louder and worse.  Behind the last planter lay an overturned watering can, and beside it a small old woman in a volunteer's apron, grey as the mist, one green glove on.  The pulse in the hearing aid was not a call at all; it was her pacemaker, failing, and no one came to the atrium after dark. 

Ines hit the emergency pull beside the door so hard she split a knuckle, then knelt in the wet and found a wrist.  "Marguerite?" 

The woman's eyelids moved.  "He stopped ringing for his tea," she breathed.  "I knew.  I just wanted to finish his ferns." 

"He's calling you," Ines said, and because it was the only useful thing she owned, she slipped the hearing aid into Marguerite's ear. 

She could no longer hear him herself.  She only watched the old face listen, watched it fold and then open, while alarms carried softly down the corridors and running feet came on. 

"Tell him I'm here," Marguerite said. 

"He knows," Ines assured her, holding the gloved hand.  "That's how it works.  You answered, so it counts, and now you have to stay and remember it for him." 

The crash team took her up into the light.  Later a doctor would say ten more minutes in that cold and there would have been two blank sheets, and someone would use the word hero.  Ines would shake her head, still dizzy, because she had not gone down there to save anyone.  She had only wanted to know whose hand. 

Alone in the thinning mist, she wrote one name on Tobias Wren's contact sheet and propped his last photograph among the ferns, where the glove had been.
