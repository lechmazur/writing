The Vessant Canal had been drained to its knees for dredging, and in the gray twilight before sunrise it lay open like a book left face-up.  Ines Marrow stood on the bow of the salvage barge Patient Heron with her father's antique pocket sundial closed in her fist.  It was useless at this hour, a brass promise waiting for a light that had not arrived.  On the wheelhouse shelf, the skipper's radio murmured the canal board's morning notices.  "When that goes quiet, they open the sluices," said Odalys Teague, pouring tea as if nothing ever hurried.  "Whatever you mean to salvage, have it by the silence." 

Ines nodded.  Twelve years ago she had walked out of a surveyor's office and never gone back, an errant seeker of legends who measured stories instead of land.  She had chased drowned bells, walking saints, and a fox that paid tolls, and every one had dissolved under her hands.  But she had not been hunting marvels.  She wanted to understand the architecture of lies: which timbers were ornament, and which one held the roof. 

So she had trained her instincts the way a diver trains breath.  In forty market towns she had listened to horse traders and widowers and wagered against herself on the single true detail, then checked parish books until her gut stopped guessing wrong.  Every lie, she learned, leans on one true thing, and the liar's voice slows down as it passes. 

All that practice had been for one story, the first she ever heard.  Her father, a bargeman, used to tell her of the Meridian, a boat that sank under Saint Brannoch's Bridge carrying a clockmaker's fortune and a woman who kept the hours for the whole canal.  He said the sundial had washed up from the wreck, and that the C.M. on its lid stood for the clockmaker.  He also said her mother had gone away to the sea towns, and he said it quickly. 

"The bridge is a half mile back," Odalys said. 

"I know," Ines answered, thoughtfully clear, the way water is once the silt has settled.  "The bridge was decoration."  "He always slowed at the lock with the green door." 

The green door stood above them now, flaking, and below it the canal bed shone with wet clay and the quiet litter of a century.  Ines climbed down the ladder in borrowed waders and let her feet choose.  The radio voice drifted after her, reading depths and closures.  Her instinct pulled left, toward a rib of black oak rising from the mud beside the lock wall, and she did not argue with it.  She dug with a trowel, then with her hands. 

There was no fortune.  There was a small hull, a tiller, and a brass plate that she rinsed in a puddle until the letters came up.  It read: Meridian, Calla Marrow, Keeper of Hours, Vessant Lock. 

Ines opened her fist and looked at the initials on the lid, C.M., and felt the whole tale turn over as quietly as a sleeper.  Hindsight did not strike her; it cleared, like a pane wiped with a sleeve.  There had never been a clockmaker.  The woman who kept the hours had been the lock-keeper, his wife, and she had not gone to the sea towns.  She had gone down here, in her own skiff, a few yards from her own green door.  The fortune, the bridge, the faraway towns were walls he had raised around the one room he could not enter, so his daughter might grow up beside a wonder instead of a grave.  That was the architecture: not a trap, but a shelter, and she had spent twelve years tapping its walls from the outside.  Everything she believed about him had come down, and nothing in her was broken. 

She carried the plate up the ladder against her chest.  Odalys read it, read Ines's face, and set a second cup on the rail without a word.  Then the announcer wished the waterway a good day, and the radio went silent. 

In that silence the sluices groaned open upstream, and water came walking back along the canal bed, unhurried, covering the oak rib, the lock wall, the place.  The sun cleared the poplars.  Ines opened the sundial on the rail, raised its little gnomon, and watched the first shadow fall across the hours.  A sundial, she thought, only ever tells you where the light has already been. 

"Where to?" Odalys asked. 

"Nowhere yet," said Ines, who had run out of legends and found, at last, a story she could tell truthfully.  Below them the water lay level and bright over the room her father had built, and she let it rest.
