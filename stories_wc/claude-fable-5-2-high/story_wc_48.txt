Maren Voss carried two endings to Three Mouths, one in each coat pocket, and the mist took her in without a sound.  Here two rivers and a tidal channel knotted together among collapsed locks, and since the keepers left, the place had gone feral: willows through the gatehouse roof, otters in the sluice, brambles swallowing the towpath signs.  Every water that passed through chose a direction here, which was why she had set her last chapter on this bank, and why her brother had launched from it twenty years ago and been carried down to a sea that kept him. 

She sat on the lock beam and opened the manuscript to its final unwritten page.  In the left-pocket ending, her ferrywoman Isa shipped her oars and let the river have the boat, as the river had once had her husband.  In the right-pocket ending, Isa turned the bow and rowed against the current toward the landing where people still needed carrying across.  Her editor wanted the drift; readers trust sorrow, he had written, and Maren, who had trusted little else since Anders, half agreed. 

The other half was the trouble.  For two decades she had asked strangers one question, what do you hope for, because she could no longer feel the answer in herself and wanted to collect the shapes of different hopes, the way a locksmith collects keys to learn a lock she cannot see.  She paid each stranger with a single sentence in a novel, a walk-on, a face at a window, gone before the paragraph ended.  Seven books held hundreds of such passings, and not one of them was hers. 

So she did what she knew how to do: she became lucidly watchful, emptied of opinion, clear as the inch of air between the mist and the water.  The mist turned slowly on itself, and figures began to cross the far towpath, one at a time.  Whether they were dawn walkers or her own pages given gray bodies, she did not decide, because deciding is not watching. 

An old man tested a rusted lock wheel with his palm, as the keeper in her second novel had, the one who hoped someone would need the gates again.  A girl ran by with a kite and no wind, the shape of a hope that only asked for weather.  A woman set two cups on the sluice wall, poured one, and waited beside the other.  Each stayed for the length of a sentence and was folded back into the white. 

Maren saw then what her collection had been telling her for years: not one of those hopes described an arrival.  They were all directions, a hand on a wheel, a string held ready, a cup left empty on purpose. 

Below her boots the water hesitated.  The tide was coming in from the estuary eleven miles off, a low gray shoulder pushing upriver, and for a few minutes the surface of Three Mouths ran backward, against the current, without any noise at all.  It laid something pale on the silt beside her. 

She picked up a spiral seashell fragment, a whelk broken lengthwise so that its inner stair stood open, each turn wider than the last.  It had come from the sea that kept Anders, eleven miles the wrong way, and it had needed to be broken to show how it was made.  Here was the shape she had never been able to collect, because she had been standing inside it.  Asking a thousand strangers the same question, year after year, with no answer of her own, had not been the absence of hope; it had been rowing. 

She folded the left-pocket ending into a boat and gave it to the tide, which, in passing, carried even the drift upstream.  Then she wrote the last page: Isa turning the bow, Isa pulling, the landing lamp small and not getting nearer and not going out.  On the bank she placed, for one sentence only, a gray-haired woman with a piece of shell closed in her fist, who watched the ferry go by and was not named. 

An ending is a vow, and a vow wants a witness, so Maren stood to solemnize it.  She read the final line aloud to the three waters, pressed the fragment to the page until its spiral left a faint wet seal, and said her brother's name once, plainly, as an officiant says amen.  Nothing answered except the mist, circling. 

She closed the manuscript, and the pages settled against one another with a whisper that seemed to hold the whole morning inside it.  Then she put the shell in her right pocket and walked home along the towpath, upstream.
