After the astronomers left and before anyone thought to return, there was an era no chronicle bothered to name.  Through its hush, Oru kept the shooting star tracking station on the basalt plateau of Kess.  The old ledger listed her as custodian of cosmic debris; the departed crew had simply called her the cosmic janitor.  Each dawn she climbed the three great dishes with a horsehair broom and swept away the night's fine black fall, the iron dust of stars that had ended overhead.  The dishes heard nothing now.  Their wires had gone to rust, their dials to silence, and still she swept, because the sweeping was the only prayer she trusted. 

She belonged to no temple.  She was simply curious about the spirit of things, the way a child is curious about the inside of a seed.  Did a falling star know it was falling?  Did it grieve, or did it sing?  She asked the sky aloud, and wrote its silences down as carefully as answers. 

With the instruments dead, she tracked the only way left to her.  She owned one pane of yellow lantern glass, the last survivor of the station's signal lamp, and each night she held it against the dark and traced the path of every falling star across it with a fingertip dipped in ash.  By morning the pane was a map of endings, which she copied into the ledger and then wiped clean.  The glass had a hairline crack from corner to corner.  For that reason she never set it back in the lamp, never risked it near flame, and the station had stood unlit for longer than she could count. 

Then, in her fortieth winter on the plateau, her right hand began to shake.  It started as a flutter and became a weather of its own.  The broom skittered; the ash lines wandered.  Oru bound her wrist in leather, braced her elbow against the railing, and admitted nothing, though there was no one to hide it from except herself.  A keeper who could not keep a line straight was, she believed, already half swept away.  She scrubbed harder at the lichen that kept creeping over the dishes, as if tidiness could stand in for steadiness. 

On the longest night of that winter the sky came apart.  Stars fell by the hundred, white and green and the red of opened fruit, too many to trace with a braced arm.  Oru tore the leather from her wrist, raised the yellow glass, and let her hand do what it would. 

Her finger shuddered across the pane.  Behind the pane, so did the star. 

She went still, watching.  The next one fell, and through the amber she saw what forty years of steady lines had hidden from her: nothing falls straight.  Each star trembled as the air took hold of it, flaring, faltering, shedding itself in sparks, and her trembling ash line lay over its path more truly than any stroke she had ever drawn. 

For the first time she did not merely record the falling.  She felt it in her own wrist: the long cold crossing as a dark, unnoticed grain, and then the moment of meeting something larger and being unable to hold together.  That moment, and no other, was when a star gave light.  Its breaking was not its failure.  Its breaking was its whole radiance. 

Near dawn one fragment outlasted its own fire and struck the plateau below the dishes.  Oru walked out barefoot over the frost and found it resting in a bed of lichen, black, thumb-sized, still warm.  She held it in her shaking palm and understood that it had not survived by resisting the sky.  It was what remained after everything that could be given had been given. 

She carried it inside, slid the cracked pane into the signal lamp, and before fear could advise her, struck a flame.  The glass ticked once and held.  Yellow light poured across the plateau, and the crack burned in it like a second, brighter path. 

By that light she finally saw the lichen she had scrubbed at for years.  It was flourishing, rust and gold and sea-green, rooted in the very dust she swept, feeding slowly on fallen stars.  The stillness of the forgotten station had never been empty.  It had been growing the whole time, patient as stone, and she had mistaken it for neglect. 

After that Oru swept the dishes gently and carried the star dust down to the lichen beds.  She left her wrist unbound.  Each night she lit the cracked lamp, traced the falling with an honest, wavering hand, and sat inside the quiet as it flowered around her.  No one was watching, and the light crossed the dark anyway.
