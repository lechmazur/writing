After the Butterfly Effect, the sky kept its pieces.  That was the name survivors gave the afternoon one startled wingbeat in a Kansas field tipped the weather past mending, and the air broke into drifting terraces of prism glass.  Tamsin had been nine, sitting on the entry bench of her mother's corn maze, when one of those terraces formed beneath her and lifted.  She was twelve now. 

The terrace still drifted, a slab of faceted light the size of a barn floor, and every sound on it arrived in splinters: half a breath, a third of a footstep, quiet with cracks in it.  She lived on rain pooled in the facets and on corn she grew from the clod of soil that had risen with her. 

She owned two things.  The first was the bench, oak, built by her mother, who designed mazes the way other people wrote letters.  The second was a pilot's logbook that had fluttered down in her first winter aloft, its cover stamped with the name of Hobb, the crop duster who used to photograph each season's maze from above.  Every pass over the farm ended with a weather code in his square hand, and every code said the same thing: 27015KT, wind from the west, fifteen knots.  She had read it a thousand times without knowing what it was for. 

What she wanted to know was smaller and stranger than the way home.  At the maze entrance, visitors had always stopped talking, as if the gap in the corn were a church door.  The terrace had a place like that, the eastern edge where the glass thinned to nothing, and each time she neared it the same hush climbed her spine.  Why did some thresholds feel sacred and others feel like nothing at all?  Her mother would have known, and her mother's only remaining sentence was the bench. 

So Tamsin studied it, featherly determined, which was her mother's phrase for her: light enough to be blown off course, stubborn enough to keep landing on the same spot.  Each morning she knelt and followed the grain of the seat with one fingertip.  Grain is weather's diary, her mother used to say, drawing next year's maze straight from a tabletop.  Wide bands were wet years, tight bands were drought, and the spaces between were the part that mattered.  Leave the necessary distance between paths, she would murmur over her plans: too near and nobody gets lost, too far and nobody gets found. 

In the third spring, Tamsin's fingertip noticed what her eyes had missed.  The bands were lopsided, crowded on one side and generous on the other, every ring of that farm oak leaning away from the same steady shove.  A west wind, the same one Hobb had copied down pass after pass.  The code was not a record of weather; it was the farm's signature, the one wind that lived there and nowhere else she had drifted. 

She had no instruments, so she learned to intuit it instead, pressing her palm to the grain until she knew that pressure the way the oak had known it, then lifting the same palm into the air.  For forty-one days the air answered wrong.  On the forty-second it leaned on her hand from the west, even and patient, and something in her chest said fifteen knots before she could doubt it. 

She walked to the eastern edge.  The cloud below had torn open, and there was the field, close enough to smell, the terrace riding as low as the day it formed.  Cut into the corn was a maze she already knew by touch: the bench's grain, enlarged to forty acres, every lopsided band a path.  Her mother had kept the sister plank, then, and had been planting it into the ground for three years, a letter addressed to the sky. 

A small figure stood at the entrance gap and did not run. 

Tamsin stopped at the edge of the glass, and the hush came, and this time she understood it.  A threshold felt sacred when it was the last necessary distance, the one gap nobody else could close for you.  Her mother was not running because she knew it too: a found child has to do the last of the finding herself. 

Tamsin tucked the logbook under her arm, pressed her palm once to the bench in thanks, and stepped off the light into the center of the maze.  Behind her the terrace drifted on, emptier, scattering its broken colors over the stalks.  She did not hurry.  She followed the grain outward, band by band, toward the gap where the quiet was waiting to be finished.
