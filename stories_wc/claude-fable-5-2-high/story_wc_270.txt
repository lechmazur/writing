The ferns told Oda Maren about the kite before any person did.  She had never asked to hear them.  In the first winter of the uprising, she hid in the steam grottoes of the Caldera with her wounds packed in living moss, and when the scars closed, something green stayed inside, listening.  Now, during the reconstruction, she raked silt from the volcanic hot springs while the village rebuilt its bathhouses around her, and the plants murmured like neighbors. 

The council had bigger worries than an old insurgent who answered the reeds.  In the ruins of the Lineage Office, masons had found the last embryo in amber, a honey-colored stone the size of a plum with a curled shadow at its heart.  Everyone knew what the Office had done to such children.  Before sealing them, its engineers wrote a destiny into their cells, soldier or miner or obedient governor, then flew a kite at each naming with the chosen fate inked along its tail.  Oda had spent twenty years fighting that custom, and her own daughter, Ilse, had spent them serving it as an engineer.  Ilse died in the final shelling, and they had not spoken since the day she took the Office's coat. 

Half the council wanted the amber smashed; the other half wanted it hatched and obeyed, whatever ruler slept inside.  Nobody could do either, because the stone turned hammers and ignored fire. 

Then the ferns pressed an image into Oda's mind: a pine above the gray pool, bent by decades of steam, with a rag of red silk caught in its crown.  She climbed, cursing her knees, and brought down an abandoned kite, its frame broken, its long tail crowded with marks no naming scribe ever made.  The pine remembered the hands that had tied it there, and the memory smelled of Ilse's soap. 

Oda sat on the warm rocks all night with the tail across her lap.  The script looked like Lineage glyphs, but it read as nonsense, a fate of scattered syllables.  Near dawn she noticed the knots between the glyphs, long and short, long and short, and her breath stopped.  It was the insurgent drum cipher, the one she had tapped on the kitchen table when Ilse was small, never guessing the girl was learning it.  The knots told her which glyphs to keep and which to throw away.  What remained was a letter. 

"Mother, the child in the amber is mine, and I could not stop them from demanding a destiny, so I chose the one they would never check.  I did not write her a throne.  I wrote her your ears, the green listening you came home with, because a people rebuilding should have someone who hears what grows.  Soak the stone in the gray pool, then the white, then the red, at sunrise, and it will unseal.  Fly her kite, but let her write the tail." 

Oda read it three times while the horsetails hummed at her ankles.  The insurgent in her still wanted every implanted fate burned out of the world, this one included.  But her own gift had been an accident, a wound's leftover, and it had fed the grottoes' fugitives all winter by telling her which roots were safe.  Ilse had taken that accident and made it a choice.  Maybe that was the difference between a chain and an inheritance: whether the hand that passed it down meant to open a door or lock one. 

She carried the letter to the council and read it aloud, cipher and all, so no one could say she had invented it.  The smashers shouted that a destiny was a destiny.  The hatchers complained that a listener was no governor. 

"Then you are both disappointed, which is how I know it is fair," Oda said.  "We will keep the kites, and we will keep the naming, but from now on the tail goes up blank, and the child fills it when she is ready." 

At sunrise the whole village stood in the steam.  Oda lowered the amber into the gray pool, where it clouded, then the white, where it softened like wax, then the red, where it opened without a sound.  The midwives lifted the tiny quickening thing into the cradle basin, where the springs would finish what a womb had begun.  Around the pool, every fern leaned inward, and Oda felt them greet a second listener, faint as a pulse. 

She mended the red kite that week, folded Ilse's letter away, and sewed on a new tail, long and bare.
