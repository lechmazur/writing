Three weeks after the judge signed her emancipation, Dessa Marlow came down to the county foster care file room and asked for her own life.  She was seventeen, legally her own guardian now, and she held the request form like a ticket she expected to be refused. 

Ines Calloway, the archivist, had worked the basement for nineteen years and had been broken-hearted for six of them.  She had fostered a boy named Tobias for eleven months, and when he was moved, the file summed up all of it in two words: placement ended.  No line for the pancakes shaped like planets, or the night-light, or the way he had finally laughed.  Since then she had made a private rule of her public job: to magnify small kindnesses wherever the record had shrunk them. 

Dessa's folder was thin.  It held eleven placements, nine incident reports, and black bars where names should be. 

"That's it?" Dessa said.  "Everything they wrote is what I broke." 

"Files are written by tired people on bad days," Ines said.  "The good days rarely required a form." 

She turned the pages slowly, examining what was absent as carefully as what was there.  Between ages three and four, the record went quiet for fourteen months: one address, no incidents, and a penciled note in the margin.  Effects envelope, see overflow. 

"What's overflow?" 

"A place things go when nobody decides about them," Ines said.  "I can't promise you there is anything, but a quiet year in a file like this is an unknown worth examining." 

She led Dessa past the humming dehumidifier to the back range, where unlabeled boxes sat under a single flickering tube.  There was no index for unknown histories.  There was only dust, and Ines had learned to read it the way other people read rings in a stump. 

"Dust falls at about the same rate down here," she said, tilting her flashlight so the beam skimmed the lids.  "A thin gray film means five years, and a felt you could write your name in means fifteen." 

Dessa had been three fourteen winters ago, so Ines looked for felt.  Ines walked the row, reading the accumulated patterns: clean rectangles where boxes had been borrowed, finger trails from an old audit, a drift against the wall undisturbed since the flood year.  On the third shelf, one box wore a thick gray felt on its lid, but its sides were clean, and the shelf beneath it showed a pale scrape. 

"This one was slid here from somewhere else and never opened," Ines said.  "Moved, but not looked at." 

"Story of my life," Dessa said, but she leaned closer. 

Inside, under expired bus passes and a single mitten, lay an envelope with her case number and a roll of paper bound by a rubber band gone brittle.  Ines set both on the reading table and stepped back.  "They're yours to open." 

Dessa tipped the envelope, and a brass button rolled into her palm, domed and dull, stamped with crossed rifles. 

"An infantryman's button," Ines said quietly.  "From a dress uniform." 

The rubber band crumbled, and Dessa began to unfurl the paper across the table, a strip as long as a door.  It was a height chart, ruled in pencil, with fourteen small marks climbing it and a man's square handwriting beside each one.  Dess, tall as the doorknob.  Dess, ate two eggs, sang to the dog.  At the top, a note to whoever came next: she sleeps if she holds the button off my old tunic, so please let it travel with her. 

It had not traveled.  Someone had sealed it in an envelope, and the envelope in a box, and the dust had kept the only honest account. 

Dessa closed her fist around the brass.  "I don't remember him," she said.  "I used to think I couldn't sleep without something cold in my hand, and I never knew why." 

"Your hand remembered," Ines said.  She did not say that he was surely alive, or that he would surely want a letter, because she could not know either thing.  "His name is sealed, and I can't unseal it," she said.  "But I can forward a letter through the agency, and people who measure a child fourteen times tend to keep wondering how tall she got." 

Dessa almost smiled. 

While she wrote, Ines took a blank addendum sheet and entered, in ink, what the county had left out: fourteen quiet months, a height chart, a button, a request ignored.  She thought of Tobias, and of whatever unknown clerk might someday read the dust on his box. 

When Dessa left, button in her pocket, her handprint remained in the gray felt of the lid.  Ines did not wipe it away.
