Three years after the last human dream was dreamt, Ottoline Marsh still catalogued walls.  She was a historian by training and an oddity by reputation, a woman who pressed a brass grain probe against plaster the way doctors once pressed stethoscopes against chests.  Her library filled the old grain silo at the edge of Vell, reclaimed from rust and pigeons, its shelves climbing the curved concrete in a slow spiral.  She had chosen it because a round wall hears everything twice.  The shelves held no novels, only her transcripts: what a bakery wall remembered of quarrels, what a nursery wall had kept of lullabies.  People in Vell slept like stones now, and woke with nothing, and they thought her collecting was a harmless madness.  Ottoline thought it was the only work left.  If the nights no longer made stories, someone had to gather the ones already soaked into the world. 

One object in the silo resisted her.  It was a fragment of a medieval tapestry, no bigger than a door, that she had bought at the estate sale of Hesper Lund, the miller's widow next door.  Two dragons, one russet and one green, curved across the wool with their jaws open and their bodies bent like parentheses.  They were plainly circling something, yet whatever they circled lay beyond the cut edge, so the picture turned around a center it did not contain.  A displaced axis, she wrote in her ledger, and underlined it twice.  The edge had been cut with scissors, not frayed by centuries, and Ottoline was too sensitively perceptive to mistake a wound for an accident.  She could feel grief in a room the way others felt a draft. 

So on a windless October evening she carried her probe into the empty Lund house, where the tapestry had hung.  The bedroom wall told her little, only a pale rectangle and the old murmur of a woman talking in her sleep.  The fireplace told her more.  When she slid the probe into the cold ash, as the silo men had once sampled wheat, it struck something that was not cinder.  She dug out three diaries, half burned, their spines black and their inner pages the color of toast.  Hesper had tried to destroy them, and a damp chimney had refused. 

Ottoline read them at her desk in the silo, under one lamp, listening for the missing half of each scorched sentence the way she listened to plaster.  The dragons, Hesper wrote, had been in her family for twenty generations, and they had always coiled around a golden pole, the pin on which the old weavers believed the sleeping world turned.  She had dreamed beneath them every night of her life. 

Then came the entry Ottoline read three times.  "Last night the dragons let go of the pole and flew out my window," Hesper had written.  "They wound themselves around the silo, and its wall began to," and there the fire had taken the line.  The next legible entry was shakier: "I woke knowing it was the last dream anyone would have, so I cut the pole out, because it holds nothing now." 

For three years she had believed, like everyone, that dreaming had simply stopped, a spindle snapped.  But Hesper had not dreamed of a breaking.  She had dreamed of a moving: the axis was not gone, only displaced, out of the sleepers and into the walls that had listened to them for so long. 

Ottoline looked up the hollow height of her library, at the one shape in Vell built entirely around its own center.  She took the fragment from its drawer and pinned it to the concrete, and the curve of the wall bent both dragons around the empty middle of the silo, so that at last they had something to circle.  Then she did what she had done ten thousand times, and laid the probe against the wall, and her ear against the probe.  She had always listened for what walls had heard.  She had never once asked what they might be making of it. 

The silo was quiet in the way a bell is quiet after ringing, a bronze quiet, full and still warm.  Inside it, faint as breath in a brass tube, something was not remembering but inventing: a baker forgiving a quarrel that had never ended, a lullaby growing a verse no mother had sung.  The walls were dreaming. 

Ottoline opened a fresh ledger and wrote until the lamp guttered.  In the morning she would unlock the silo doors and invite the dreamless of Vell to come and listen, each with an ear to the wall.  Tonight she only listened, while two woolen dragons turned slowly around the place where the world had put its center.
