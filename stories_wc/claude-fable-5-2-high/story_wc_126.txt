After the rainforest burned, the ash came down the coast for forty days and settled on Echo Harbor like gray snow.  The harbor had been named for its cliffs, which gave every sound back twice.  Now they gave back nothing.  People spoke in whispers, then in gestures, then not at all, and the hush became chronic, a thing the town carried the way an old sailor carries a limp. 

Maren was the harbor's violinist, and she was born with an ear that missed nothing.  She could hear which plank on the quay was rotting and which gull was lying about fish.  So she could hear that this hush was not empty.  It was full, like a held breath, every voice in town bound tight around something it could not finish saying.  The forest had been cut off mid-sentence, and no one knew how to speak after that. 

She still played on the quay each morning with her grandmother's bowl at her feet.  It was a mendicant's bowl, beaten brass, carried by her family since they were wandering tellers who begged supper for stories.  Once, coins had rung in it like small bells.  Now she kept it bound in a wool scarf, because its ringing made people flinch.  Nobody stopped, and she never played past the first phrase.  Every tune she knew had an ending, and endings felt like lies.  She wanted, badly, to find something beautiful in a thing left unfinished, and could not. 

On the forty-first day she walked out at low tide to the sunken orchard.  It had been a greenhouse once, long as a church, built for pears before the marsh swallowed its floor.  Storms had since thrown eleven ships around it, and their hulls leaned inward in a ring like elders listening.  Half the glass was gone, and the pear trees stood knee-deep in brackish water, some dead, some budding. 

Maren drew one note and stopped.  The note went out to the wrecks, touched their hollow ribs, and came back.  It came back changed, lower, unfinished, as if the hulls had asked a question. 

Then she remembered how her grandmother told stories.  Every summer the family built a bonfire on the shingle, and the old woman would tell a tale up to its best moment and then close her mouth.  "The rest belongs to whoever is still awake," she would say.  A cousin would take it up, then an uncle, then a child, and no story was ever ended, only handed on.  As a girl Maren had hated that, because she wanted to know how things came out.  Standing among drowned pear trees, she understood that the old woman had not been withholding endings.  She had been making room at the fire. 

Maren gathered broken timber from the wrecks and stacked it on the one dry rise of the orchard floor.  Lighting it frightened her, for everyone feared fire now.  But this was a small fire, a fire with a ring of stones and a purpose, and she struck the match. 

By dusk the glow in the old glass could be seen from town.  They came slowly along the tide path, the baker, the net-mender, the pilot's twin boys, expecting disaster and finding a woman with a violin.  No one spoke. 

Maren set the bowl on a stone beside the fire and began to unbind it, turn by turn, until the scarf fell away and the brass took the light.  Then she played.  It was a tune she had been making since the ash began, and it had no ending because she had not found one.  She played it to its highest step, where the next note leaned out over nothing, and she lifted her bow. 

The wrecks returned the phrase, lower, asking.  The bowl hummed with it, a thin gold thread of sound. 

"I am not begging coins tonight," Maren said, and her voice cracked from disuse.  "I am begging the rest, from whoever is still awake." 

The fire snapped.  Then the net-mender, who had lost a brother to the burning, hummed one note.  It was not the note Maren would have chosen.  It was better, because it was his. 

The bowl rang to it, and one of the twins added another, and the baker laughed aloud, and the laugh went round the ring of ships and came home twice.  The hush did not break; it came unbound, the way a knot loosens once the first strand gives. 

They did not finish the tune that night.  They never have.  Upriver, they say, green is coming through the black, with no plan and no ending.  Maren plays up to the edge each evening and leaves the door open.
