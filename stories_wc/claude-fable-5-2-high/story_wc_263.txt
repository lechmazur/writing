In Varn, the almanac went blank when the sun did.  Oona's father had kept it all his life, ruling each daylit date in ink, yet he always left the seven weeks of polar night as empty pages, an unwritten calendar the village simply endured.  He died on the last written day, and he left her his brass astrolabe and one word he never managed to speak. 

Oona heard it anyway.  She had always heard unspoken words, the ones that gathered behind people's teeth, which was why Varn called her a moonstruck dreamer and let her wander.  Through his failing breath she caught it, blurred by her own grief: finish. 

So she did not sleep. 

That winter the village was loud with silence.  Passing the fiddler's door she heard the reel he had not written; at the weaver's window, a pattern knotted somewhere short of the loom; in the schoolroom, thirty stories pressed flat under sums.  Everyone in Varn fought the dark by working through it, lamps burning, shutters barred, and everything they might have made sat locked inside them like water under ice.  Oona's own locked thing was the worst: she had once painted the sky on every scrap of birchbark she found, and since the funeral her brushes had stiffened in their jar.  She could not bear all that unmade beauty pressing at her ears.  She meant to free it, and she believed the blank pages were where her father had hidden the key. 

On the first unwritten night a star fell, and in its wake she heard a syllable, bright and unfinished, like a word the sky had decided against.  She raised the astrolabe, sighted where the streak began and where it died, and ruled its path across the empty page where a date should have been.  Night after dateless night she stood in the snow, measuring, her fingers splitting on the cold brass.  Each falling star gave her one line and one syllable.  By what might have been the fortieth night, the lines had become a fan, and every one of them, carried on down the page, met at a single point on the ridge above Varn. 

She knew what stood there: the archway, last rib of a chapel older than the village, where nobody went because the aurora hung so low over it. 

She climbed, dizzy with sleeplessness, the astrolabe knocking against her ribs.  The arch stood black against curtains of green fire, and the light poured through it and stained the snow the color of deep water.  Beneath it the ground was not white but faintly silver, drifted with a dust finer than frost.  This was where the falling stars came down, what was left of them, and it was speaking. 

Not aloud; nothing that mattered in Oona's life had ever been said aloud.  The dust remembered.  It remembered being fire, and before that being the hearts of suns, and it remembered, with a longing that made her knees give, that it was meant to be made into something again.  A reel, a pattern, a painted sky. 

Every syllable she had gathered joined here into one word, and it was not finish.  It was the word her father had truly failed to say, the one her grief had bent into a command: rest. 

She understood then, and the understanding frightened her.  Dust settles only on what keeps still.  Varn, forever working behind its shutters, had shaken the stars off for years; her father had left the dark pages empty not as a riddle but as an invitation nobody accepted.  To rest meant letting the dark in, and the dark held him. 

Oona set the astrolabe in the snow.  She lay down under the archway, on the silver drift, and for the first time since the funeral she stopped holding herself shut.  Grief came first, enormous and cold.  Then the aurora leaned down through the arch, the dust lifted in its light, and it settled on her eyelids, her split fingers, her open mouth, remembering itself into her.  She wept and shone and slept. 

She woke unfrozen, with colors crowding behind her eyes.  Back in Varn she softened her brushes and painted that night's sky across a blank page of the almanac, then carried the book from door to door.  She only told each neighbor the thing she had heard them not say, and asked them to put down their work and come sit under the arch awhile.  The fiddler came first, grumbling, and sat still until the dust found him. 

Before the sun returned to give the days their numbers, the dark pages were full: a reel in crooked notation, a weaver's diagram, thirty small stories, and one painted sky with a man's shadow resting in its corner.
