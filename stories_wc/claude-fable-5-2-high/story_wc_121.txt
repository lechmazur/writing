The rain had stopped, and the old viaduct glowed.  Wet lichen in its forty arches held the last light of evening and gave it back green-gold, so bright the puddles below looked like windows.  Mara Venn stood a hand's width above the stones, as she had stood above everything since she was nine.  The townspeople called her the gravity defier and brought their children to watch her walk on air over the gorge.  They never asked what it cost to refuse the ground. 

From the keystone above her, one drop let go.  Mara lifted the ancient clay tablet from her satchel and pressed her thumb to its runes, and the drop stopped falling.  This was the tablet's only gift: between the drop and the ripple, it opened an eternity.  She was periodically eternal, forever for the length of one falling drop, then mortal again until the next rain.  She had spent eleven wet seasons of such eternities inside the thing the runes built. 

They built it now.  The arches multiplied, branching off the viaduct in impossible directions, each one glowing with a different evening.  Through one she saw herself as a mason with mortar under her nails.  Through another she saw herself old, grounded, laughing at a kitchen table.  Through a third there was no Mara at all, only a small grave beside a larger one.  These were her fractured timelines, every life that had splintered away from the one she was living, and together they made the labyrinth. 

Her father had dug the tablet out of the viaduct's footing the week before he died, and he had read her the only line he could translate.  "Whoever conquers the labyrinth of self walks out whole."  She wanted that more than she wanted the sky.  She was tired of being a splinter. 

For years she had tried to map the maze, chalking arrows on glowing piers, and for years the map had failed.  Last season she finally understood why.  The runes were not a picture; they were rules.  The tablet did not store a labyrinth but grew one through procedural generation, laying each new arch according to the turn she had just taken, starting from a single seed.  Every corridor was her own last choice, repeated and elaborated.  And she always chose up.  At every fork she drifted toward the higher arch, the lighter air, and the rules obediently generated more height, more brightness, more places with no floor.  She had been building her own exile. 

So tonight she would not explore.  Tonight she would retrace. 

She turned around and followed her own rule backward, arch by arch, taking at every fork the lower way, the way she had come from.  The lives thinned as she went.  The old woman at the table flickered out, then the mason, and each time an arch closed, the glow around her darkened by one shade.  It frightened her how beautiful the brightness had been, and how much of it was made of people she would never be. 

At last a single arch remained, and under it a single evening.  It had rained that evening too.  Her father stood on the scaffold with his trowel, forty feet above the gorge, and a girl of nine stood beside him holding the lantern.  Mara watched the plank tilt.  She watched him fall, and she watched the girl lunge after him and then, in terror of the ground rushing up to take them both, refuse it. 

That was the seed.  It was never flight, only a child deciding that weight was the thing that killed, and that she would carry none of it. 

Mara stepped into the arch and knelt, on nothing, beside the girl.  "He falls in every timeline," she said.  "I have looked."  "But you do not have to keep falling upward to get away from it."  The girl looked at her with her own eyes. 

Then Mara did the one thing the rules had never been given.  She chose down.  She let the weight come: her father's hand slipping, the eleven seasons, the grave she had never once stood on.  It poured into her like water into a footprint, and she sank through the last arch as it went dark. 

The drop struck the puddle.  The ripple spread and broke against her boots, which were on the stones, and wet.  The viaduct's glow was fading to ordinary dusk: one viaduct, forty arches, one evening.  In her hands the tablet showed a single rune where there had been hundreds.  Her knees ached, her satchel dragged at her shoulder, and she weighed exactly as much as a woman who had lost her father.  Mara walked home along the ground, heavily, through the dimming light, whole.
