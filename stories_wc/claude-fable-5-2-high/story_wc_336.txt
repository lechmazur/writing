Oriel had pruned the terrace orchard for sixty springs, and she knew the sound of this one before it arrived.  It was a hush, the held breath of branches a day before their buds break, when hope is still small enough to hide in a fist. 

The orchard grew on a mossy parapet beside the waterfall, a green ledge of old stone where ferns drank the spray and the light came through the mist in slow gold sheets.  At the ledge's end stood a gray stump.  That had been the Wonder Tree, whose blossoms wore a color the valley had no name for, a color that made carters stop their mules and children ask questions nobody could answer.  It had died nine winters ago.  Since then the carters hurried, the questions thinned, and her apprentice Tam, who was eight, had never once seen the color at all. 

What remained was in Oriel's apron pocket: one heirloom seed, dark as a plum stone, handed from pruner to pruner for seven generations against exactly this grief.  She had planted it once already, in the best soil on the parapet, and it had slept like a pebble.  The old pruners' rhyme explained why: the first tree was not of this world.  Its color had been carried through the waterfall from the country on the other side, and a seed of it would not wake until it tasted that color again. 

So all winter Oriel had gone weaving between worlds.  Behind the falls there was a gap in the rock, and beyond the gap lay a meadow where everything, even the shadows, glowed with the nameless color.  She passed back and forth like a shuttle through a loom of water, and each time she tried a new way to bring a thread of it home.  Oriel was insatiably creative; she had once grafted a pear to bloom twice, and built shears that hummed when a cut was wrong, and she did not run out of ideas.  She soaked wool in the meadow's dew.  She sealed petals in wax, trapped light in a jar of honey, pressed glowing moss between slates.  Forty crossings, forty inventions, and every time the falls rinsed her cargo to the gray of wet ash. 

Now, in the hush, she was teaching Tam to prune, because the trees could not wait on her sorrow. 

"Why cut so much?" he asked, frowning at the pile of twigs. 

"Because a tree is not its wood," she said, as she had said to a dozen apprentices.  "You take away everything that is only stuff, and what is left is the essence, the shape it means to be." 

Her shears stopped in the air. 

All winter she had been hauling wood: wool, wax, honey, slate.  She had tried to carry the color's body through the water, and the water had kept it, as water should.  But what was that color with everything pruned away?  Not pigment.  It was the stopped mule, the unanswerable question, the caught breath.  It was wonder, and wonder weighed nothing a waterfall could wash out. 

She also knew, being honest, that her own wonder had been worn smooth by forty crossings.  She looked at the boy who had never seen it. 

"Tam," she said, "put down the twigs and hold out your hand." 

She laid the heirloom seed in his palm, folded his fingers over it, and led him along the wet ledge behind the falls.  The roar closed over them, then opened. 

The meadow lay shining, every blade lit from within.  Tam did not speak.  His mouth opened, his breath went in and stayed, and in his fist the seed grew warm as a wren's egg, capturing the essence of that one long, astonished silence.  This was her last invention: no jar, no wax, only a first look and a seed to drink it. 

"What is it called?" he whispered at last. 

"Nobody knows," said Oriel, and found her own breath caught beside his. 

They walked home through the water with his fist held tight.  The falls took nothing, because there was nothing to take. 

On the parapet she knelt by the gray stump and let Tam press the seed into the dark soil, and she cupped earth around it to succor it through the last cold nights. 

Three mornings later, when the orchard buds broke, a pale shoot stood there with one folded leaf.  Along its seam ran a thread of the color that had no name.  On the road below, a carter stopped his mule.  Oriel kept her shears quiet in her lap, and Tam, beside her, began to ask a question.
