Every afternoon, when the giant Brum lay down beside his pump, the rooftop canals of Vael went still.  The water that he drove up the towers all day stopped roaring through the aqueducts, and for the length of his nap the city could hear itself.  Ilse Maren, natural history illustrator to the Canal Guild, spent each of those silences listening for one sound. 

Her father had been a lock singer, one of the men whose songs opened the old gates between towers, each gate keyed to the last note of its own tune.  Eleven years ago his heart had stopped one note short of an ending.  The gate stayed shut, and his interrupted cadence still haunted the city at every nap, a phrase climbing toward home and never arriving.  Lately it had grown faint. 

Ilse had drawn every living thing in Vael from life, the gutter herons, the eels, the moss that flowered on slate, and she believed that echoes were living too.  They returned like swifts, so somewhere they must hatch.  If she could find the place where echoes are born, she might learn why his would not finish, before it faded for good. 

But the sound always led upstream toward the Shell, the stairless headwater tower where he had died, and by day the current ran too hard to pole against.  Even in still water, no skiff could cover that distance across a giant's nap.  When Brum woke, the roar and the current returned together. 

So today she wore her father's pendant, a brass infinity symbol rubbed smooth by his thumb.  Lock singers used such pendants to hold a breath past its natural end.  "It sculpts time itself, like a thumb in wet clay," he had told her.  "Press one loop and the other swells, but the clay is your own, and whatever you add to this hour comes out of your last ones." 

She pushed off from the Guild roof as the pump's thunder died.  The canals lay like poured glass, threading from parapet to parapet on stone arches, with washing lines and chimney smoke reflected under her hull and the streets a rumor far below.  In the hush she heard it: her father's voice, thin as thread, rising and breaking off.  She poled after it past the tannery tower, over the long aqueduct where herons stood asleep, farther than she had ever gone. 

Then, far beneath her, Brum's long breathing hitched toward waking.  The Shell was still six towers off. 

Ilse closed her fingers on the pendant and squeezed one loop until it bit.  The hour bulged.  A drop falling from her pole slowed until she could have sketched it, and the giant's waking breath stretched into one long inhale.  She felt the price leave her, somewhere ahead, some winter evening she would now never see.  She poled on through borrowed minutes and reached the Shell. 

Inside, the tower was hollow and spiraled like a whelk, and at its heart stood his gate, green with age, still shut.  Behind its bars, in the spiral's throat, the air shivered like heat over slate.  She watched with a draftsman's patience, as she would watch a nesting bird, and saw how it worked.  Each sound in Vael drifted up here, touched its own ending against the curved wall, and at that touch turned and flew home as an echo.  Echoes were born at endings.  His phrase had none to touch, so it beat round and round behind the bars, leaking thinner each year, caged by the very gate its last note should have opened. 

Ilse was no singer, but she had heard that climb four thousand times and knew where home was.  She knew, too, that to uncage him was to let him end.  The old gates wanted the note held for a count of sixty, and her stretched hour was already thinning; below, the long inhale had begun to turn.  She pressed the loop again, harder, and paid without counting.  Then she waited for his phrase to climb, and where it broke she sang the missing note and held it on borrowed breath until her ribs ached. 

The gate groaned open.  The whole phrase swept past her, finished at last, touched the curved wall, and flew out over Vael as an echo, once, complete.  Then it faded, as finished things do. 

Below, Brum exhaled and woke, and the returning current carried her skiff home.  That night she found a streak of white in her hair and one loop of the pendant worn thin as wire.  The last plate in her field guide shows an open gate and an empty spiral, and beneath it, in her small hand, every note of his song, ending included.
