The reaper carried the wax cylinders in his coat for nine days before he dared climb back to the observatory.  He had found them beneath the floorboards of her cottage, labeled in her slanting hand, and he had known at once whose voice slept inside.  Dusk was settling over the headland when he pushed open the bronze door.  The dome stood as old as the first charts, its great lens cracked by some forgotten storm, so that the last sunlight entered whole and landed in pieces.  Amber and violet lay scattered across the floor, the brass, the desk where Ilse had worked.  He had stood in this room every evening of her last winter, and she had never once looked at him.  That was the shape of it: a stair's length between them, always, and him loving her across it. 

"Mind the third step," he murmured, and flinched.  It was not his phrase.  It belonged to a lamplighter dead two centuries, whose last words no living mouth remembered.  This was the reaper's affliction: everything the world forgot of the dying stayed in him, and rose to his lips unbidden, like an echo that had lost its canyon.  He carried ten thousand farewells and not one word of his own for what had hollowed him since he took her. 

Her phonograph still sat beside the telescope.  He fitted the first cylinder and turned the crank with fingers that had closed so many eyes. 

"Plate nine," said Ilse, thin and bright through the hiss.  "The serpent coiled off the northern reef I have named Old Hunger."  "Sailors will not steer around a blank, but they will steer around a beast with a name." 

The page itself lay pinned to her desk, a single leaf from the atlas she had never finished.  Sea monsters crowded its waters: the serpent on the reef, a many-armed thing where the fog bred, a whale with a lantern jaw guarding the whirlpool.  Each was a fear she had caught, inked, and christened so that others could pass it by. 

He played the cylinders in order while the light slid lower.  On the sixth, her voice had the fever in it. 

"The village has gone inland from the sickness, but the fleet is still out, and someone must keep the signal lamp and finish the soundings."  "I know what staying costs."  "I have seen him at the foot of the stairs." 

The reaper stopped turning the crank.  The dome held its breath with him, and far below, the sea went on saying the only thing it knew.  He turned it again. 

"You are very patient," she said, and she was speaking to him, across the years, as she had never spoken across the room.  "I did not look at you because I had work to finish, not because I was afraid."  "I named you months ago, in the margin: the Ferryman of Small Hours."  "But there is one empty sea left on my page, and I think it is yours."  "Whatever frightens the one who waits, give it a name, and it will let you pass." 

The cylinder ran out into hiss. 

He bent over the atlas page and found the place, a pale oval of unmarked water in the southwest corner, and beside it, tiny, his name in her hand.  He had no ink.  He had only what reapers have instead of tears, a cold brine that had never fallen before tonight.  It fell now, into the empty sea, and the broken lens threw its last scattered light across the wet paper.  Where each shard of amber touched, the brine began to crystallize, salt blooming grain by grain along the paths the light chose.  He watched a shape assemble itself: not a serpent, not a beast with teeth, but a small curled creature with its mouth open and nothing coming out. 

He understood it then.  He did not fear the taking; he feared the silence afterward, the day her last words would become one more forgotten phrase rattling in him, stripped of her. 

"The Quiet After," he said, and for the first time the words were his own. 

The salt creature glittered and held still, a thing that could now be steered around. 

Out on the darkening water, a fishing boat rounded the northern reef wide, as boats had done every evening since her soundings reached the harbor towns.  None of the crew knew her name, yet her dying was still at work in every safe turn of the tiller, an echo that needed no canyon.  The reaper climbed to the signal lamp, struck a light, and kept it burning for her until the stars took over.
