The sun's rim returned like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath, and Halla pulled her veil back down.  The eclipse had given her two unburned hours to cross the salt pan, the only daylight her white skin and pale eyes could bear uncovered.  Now it was over, and the encampment stood before her, empty. 

The Lullkeepers had pitched nothing; they had simply moved into what the wind had already made.  Centuries of gusts had scooped the sandstone into alcoves, sleeping shelves, and fluted corridors that hummed like blown bottles.  Hearths were banked, not kicked apart.  Sixty people had disappeared during the darkness, calmly, while the Survey's watchers on the ridge stared up at the sky. 

Above her shoulder, the hummingbird ticked.  It was a Survey machine, brass-throated and glass-eyed, with wings that blurred at eighty beats a second, and it recorded everything she looked at.  That was the contract: she would find the Lullkeepers, and the drone would carry home their secret.  The Survey wanted it for soldiers. 

Halla wanted it for a boy.  Eleven years ago her brother had gone through river ice, and time had turned to honey.  She had seen each crack travel, each bubble rise, had been handed what felt like an hour, and had not moved.  The Lullkeepers were said to know why time slows in crisis, and she had tracked that rumor across three deserts, because a gift that useless demanded an explanation.  She was angry about it the way the desert is hot: completely, and without raising its voice. 

Halla had not been born wild.  She had been raised among ledgers in the Survey's orphan house, and she had chosen wildness the way others choose a trade, practicing it on purpose: sleeping without walls, owning nothing she could not carry, trusting her own eyes over any record.  The drone was the one leash she had accepted, and she felt it at her neck. 

She read the camp.  The wind had already erased every footprint from the sand.  But in the largest alcove, on a tilted slab the gusts had grooved like a washboard, a jar lay on its side, and ink ran from it in dark threads. 

The Lullkeepers wrote everything in disappearing ink, letters and lessons and debts, and it faded as it dried, so that a thing could be read once, wholly, or not at all.  This spill was no accident.  A panicked spill splashes; this had been poured from knee height, slowly, and the grooves had carried it into a branching pattern she recognized as the slot canyons to the east.  One thread ran thicker than the rest, and where it ended someone had pressed a single thumbprint.  A map, left for anyone who came on foot and paid attention. 

Then the returning sun slid across the slab, and the outermost threads began to pale.  The hummingbird dropped past her ear to drink the pattern with its glass eye. 

And time turned to honey. 

She saw each wingbeat separately, eighty patient strokes to the second.  She saw the ink retreating grain by grain, the side branches going first, the thick thread and its thumbprint holding.  And inside that enormous quiet she finally noticed what was missing: herself.  No part of her was composing a report, rehearsing a memory, or bracing for what came after.  Time had not slowed; she had simply stopped spending it on copies.  A crisis burned away the keeping, and what remained was the whole of a moment, which is longer than anyone expects.  That was why the Lullkeepers wrote in ink that would not stay.  That was the lesson on the slab, and it could not be recorded, only undergone. 

On the river she had been nine, and had only stood inside that wholeness, marveling.  This time she moved. 

Her white hand closed around the hummingbird between one wingbeat and the next, gently, the way one catches a moth.  She slid the memory reed from its breast and ground it under her heel.  Her anger had never been a fire; it was a level, and everything she did sat true against it. 

She dipped a finger in the last wet ink and wrote her report on the Survey's own form: found them, found it, come and learn it on foot.  She folded it into the drone's message clasp, rewound the spring, and let the bird go west.  By the time it reached the ridge, the page would be clean. 

On the slab, the thumbprint faded last.  Halla had already read it, once and wholly, and she walked east into the canyons as the wind closed over her tracks, a pale woman disappearing by choice into the full length of the afternoon.
