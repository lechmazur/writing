Oriel had inhabited Chime Hollow for thirty years, and in all that time no one had ever surprised her.  The cave sang around her constantly, because water fell from the roof onto ten thousand crystals and every crystal answered with its own note.  The drips were steadily unpredictable, never stopping and never repeating, so the song had gone on for centuries without once becoming a tune. 

Her sight worked through overlapping shadows.  When a pilgrim climbed the passage with a lantern, the pilgrim's shadow ran ahead and crossed the shadows of the crystals, and in that doubled dark Oriel saw what was coming.  She saw their questions before they asked them.  They wanted crowns, deaths, and buried gold.  She gave them smaller truths instead, because her whole purpose was to magnify everyday magic: the bread that would rise on Thursday, the neighbor who would wave, the frost that would spare the beans. 

To prove that small things mattered, she showed each visitor the cave pearl.  It lay in a shallow pool beneath the oldest drip, a grain of sand that falling water had turned and coated, turned and coated, until it shone like a little moon.  Most pilgrims glanced at it, sighed, and asked again about the gold.  Lately Oriel had begun to sigh with them, and she had quietly decided that this season in the Hollow would be her last. 

Then, one afternoon, a voice said "Good day" from three steps behind her, and Oriel dropped her ladle. 

A woman stood at the mouth of the passage with a walking stick, dusty boots, and no lantern at all.  Her eyes were pale and still.  She had come up through the dark casting no shadow ahead of her, and so the seer of Chime Hollow had not seen her coming. 

"I am Wen," the pilgrim said.  "I followed the music from the valley, and it took me four days." 

Oriel recovered her ladle and her dignity, raised her lamp, and let Wen's shadow fall across the shadow of a tall quartz spire.  She looked into the doubled dark and found, for the first time in her life, no question waiting there. 

"What do you want to know?" she asked, and felt strange asking. 

"Nothing about tomorrow," Wen said.  "I only want to meet whatever is making that sound." 

So Oriel, out of habit more than hope, led her to the pool and guided her hand into the cold water.  Wen's fingers found the pearl and went still.  A drip struck, the pearl rocked against her fingertip, and a crystal overhead rang like a struck glass. 

"Something is turning it," Wen whispered.  "Something I cannot find is keeping it from ever settling down and sticking to the floor." 

"Water," said Oriel.  "Weight, and time, and the slope of the stone." 

"Unseen forces," Wen said, laughing, "which is to say, the only kind I have ever known.  The wind that tells me where a wall ends, the warmth that tells me a door is open, the pull in my feet that tells me which way is down.  I trust them every hour, and they made this, and you get to live beside it." 

Oriel opened her mouth and found that her usual speech was not needed.  For thirty years she had held the pearl up to others like a lens, and no one had ever held it up to her. 

"Put out your lamp," Wen said gently.  "I can smell the oil, and I think you have been listening with your eyes." 

Oriel hesitated, because the lamp was her whole craft, and then she pinched the wick. 

The dark came down complete.  It was not empty.  Darkness, she understood, was simply every shadow overlapping at once, and inside it she saw more than she had ever seen in any sliver.  She saw no future, only the present, enormous and ringing: each drip a bright point of sound, each crystal a held breath, the pearl turning in its pool like a small patient world.  The blackness glittered in her ears. 

She had spent three decades telling strangers that ordinary things were wonders, and she had stopped believing it at roughly the same rate the pearl had grown.  Now she believed it again, all at once, and she laughed until the crystals laughed back. 

Wen stayed three days, then went home down the mountain as she had come, by ear.  Oriel stayed too, though not as before.  When the next pilgrim arrived asking about gold, she took his lantern, blew it out, and put his hand in the water. 

"Wait," she told him, as the dark began to shine.
