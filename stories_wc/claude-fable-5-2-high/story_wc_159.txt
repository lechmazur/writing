The opal library floated a mile above the clouds, and nobody had climbed to it in nineteen years.  Maren, its glass-orchid caretaker, still unlocked the doors each dusk, because deserted marvels were marvels all the same.  Her orchids stood in a single row down the longest hall, the Museum of Extinct Feelings, one blown-glass bloom beside each exhibit.  Every bloom held a name the world below had stopped saying.  Lit at the right moment, an orchid would glow and speak its name aloud once, and so the name would last another night.  The right moment was set by the wind chime on the east balcony.  Its five bamboo tubes played a song so old that no one living knew the words, and the opal walls caught that song and carried its echo slowly westward down the hall.  An orchid would only take flame while its own note of the echo was passing through it.  So Maren's whole work was to walk across the museum at the pace of an echo, taper in hand, and light. 

Tonight she paused first at the chime and touched the fifth tube, the cracked one.  Seven winters ago her teacher, Tamsin, had died, and Maren had blown an orchid for her and set it last in the row, tuned to the fifth note.  The first night it spoke, the name sounded so small in the empty hall that Maren ran back and seized the fifth tube to silence it.  The frozen bamboo split in her fist.  Since then the tube had only rattled, the song had limped on four notes, and every fifth orchid had stayed dark.  Forty names had gone unspoken because she could not bear to hear one.  And glass that stays unlit for seven winters clouds to milk and never speaks again.  This was the last night of the seventh. 

There was no bamboo above the clouds to cut a new tube from, so she unhooked the cracked one, hoping her own breath might draw from it what the wind could not.  The wind rose, the four whole tubes sang, and the echo started down the hall.  Maren followed it as she always did, gently systematic, dusting each stem with her sleeve before she touched the taper to it, skipping nothing.  Beside the case labeled The Patience of Lamplighters, the first orchid flared amber and said, "Ansel Roe."  Beside The Joy of Being Expected Home, the second said, "Lidia Marsh."  Third, fourth; then the fifth, already gray at the petal tips. 

She blew across the cracked tube as the echo arrived, but the bamboo gave only a dry buzz, and the taper's flame slid off the glass like water.  The echo lingered over each bloom for only a few breaths.  The exhibit beside this orchid was one she had hurried past for seven years: a pressed purple spire under glass, labeled Foxglove Regret.  Taken in small measure, the card read, it steadies the heart; taken whole, it stops it.  Maren had taken hers whole.  She had silenced an entire note to avoid one name, and then let shame keep her from ever listening for what was missing. 

She listened now.  The echo hung above the gray petals, four notes and a gap, and the gap had a shape, the way an empty chair does.  Tamsin used to hum that fifth note over the furnace while the glass cooled.  Maren waited for the gap and sang into it.  Her voice cracked worse than the bamboo, but the opal took the note, rounded it, and handed it back whole.  The flame caught.  The gray orchid went violet to the root and said, "Corwin Hale," a name she had not heard in seven winters. 

She did not let herself weep yet, because thirty-nine more were waiting and the echo would not.  So she crossed the museum as she had always crossed it, case by case, four blooms lit in silence and the fifth lit singing, past The Awe of First Snow and The Loyalty of Ferrymen.  Behind her the hall filled with colored light and with names, each spoken once to no audience but her. 

At the west door stood the last orchid, the one she had blown with her own breath.  Her throat was raw and the echo was thin by then, nearly spent.  She sang the fifth note anyway, set the taper to the stem, and stayed.  "Tamsin Vey," said the glass, small in the enormous hall, exactly as small as before.  This time Maren said it back. 

Then she walked the lit hall eastward and hung the cracked tube on its hook again, unmended, so the song would keep its gap and she would have to fill it every night.  A small measure, taken nightly, steadies the heart.
