Ilse Varga made paper that vanished.  For twenty years she had steeped tissue in acids, dried it, and sold it to conjurers who wanted a flash and no ash.  She was tired of making nothing last.  Her mother, an engraver, had left her one thing that did: a copper storybook illustration plate, palm-sized, showing an orchard in a crater with stars hanging in the branches like fruit.  Beneath it ran a caption in mirrored letters: Where the light is kept.  As a child Ilse had listened to that story with her ear on her mother's chest, and her mother had said that every heartbeat was pointing somewhere, if only someone would draw the map.  The heart had stopped before Ilse learned how. 

So she carried the plate north into the subarctic, past the last road, and climbed down into the crater in the final hours of its long winter night.  The orchard was there, as engraved, but withered: black apple trees bent like old handwriting, bark split, not one bud.  Around the trunks lay what the vanished keepers had thrown away, half sunk in snow: jar lids, corks, lantern glass, the bone handles of pruning knives.  The story said the keepers had sealed starlight in the heartwood each winter so the trees could bloom in the dark. 

Ilse trusted stories less than chemistry, and her chemistry was simple.  Flash paper is the most eager substance there is; if any light still pulsed under that bark, a slip of it would know.  She took off her glove, laid a slip against the nearest trunk, and held it there with her bare palm.  A tremble would ignite it by friction, so she did not tremble.  She knelt in the snow and slowed her breath until frost furred her sleeve and stitched her lashes together.  Her knees went numb, then her wrist, and still she kept her hand flat, the way she had once kept her head on a chest.  After most of an hour the slip went up in a small white gasp.  One beat.  The tree was not dead; it was only very slow. 

She pried a jar lid from the snow and set it at the roots, then moved to the next tree and knelt again.  Here the flare came sooner and brighter, so she laid down two lids and a cork, pointing the cork toward the stronger pulse.  Tree by tree, through hours no clock could divide, she wrote the orchard's heartbeat on the snow in other people's rubbish: lids for strength, corks for direction, shards of lantern glass where a beat faltered.  When she finally stood back, stiff as the trees, the scattered marks had become a spiral.  It was the same spiral her mother had engraved in the border of the plate, the one Ilse had always taken for decoration.  It was a map, and it wound inward to the oldest tree. 

There, at the height of her heart, she found a hollow plugged with gray wax.  The wax had cracked, and through the crack a thread of silver light was leaking into the dark, thin as breath.  That was why the orchard had withered: its safekept starlight was draining away, one winter at a time. 

Above the crater rim the last stars were thinning, and the sky had gone the color of unprinted paper.  The wind stopped.  Snow hung where it was, and nothing in the crater moved, as if the world were holding its breath between the long night and the first sunrise. 

Ilse understood that she could widen the crack and take the light home in a jar, the only lasting thing she would ever own.  She also understood what her mother had meant: a map is for arriving, not for taking.  No flame of hers lasted long enough to melt wax, so she warmed a stub of bottle wax in her fist against her chest, which took a long time, and she let it.  Then she pressed the copper plate over the crack, engraved side inward, and worked the wax around its edge with her thumbs until the silver thread went out.  The seal held.  The orchard on the plate now faced the light it had been made to describe. 

She had one slip of flash paper left.  She laid it on the old trunk, flattened her numb palm over it, and waited without counting.  It flared within minutes, bright against her skin, and behind it she felt the wood knock once, like someone answering a door.  On the snow the spiral of lids and corks lay waiting, a map for whoever came next to keep the place.  A rim of sun touched the crater's edge, and the world let its breath go.
