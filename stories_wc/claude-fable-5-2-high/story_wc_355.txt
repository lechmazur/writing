Tobias Quill had rolled back the workshop roof so the stars could attend, and he bowed to them before snuffing the last lamp.  "Honored guests," he told the sky, "tonight we open a door without knocking it down."  Shadow settled over the benches, over zithers, gutted radios, clock escapements, and a glass harmonica he had built from jam jars.  He wore a conductor's tailcoat over gardening boots, because he believed every craft deserved a little ceremony. 

On the center bench lay his sister's war diary, shut by the brass clasp he had made for her nine years ago.  It was a resonance lock, his proudest trick: a reed inside the clasp released only when it heard one exact pitch.  Maren had chosen the pitch in secret, the morning she enlisted in the artillery and he refused to see her off.  She came home a pyrotechnician, set the diary on his bench without a word, and had said little since. 

A hacksaw would have settled the matter in a minute, but Tobias did not break things in order to know them.  That was his whole creed, applied to locks, to wasps, to nations, and it had cost him a sister. 

Beside the diary sat his life's work, a ledger titled A Catalog of the Varieties of Silence.  Its entries were intuitively descriptive, named by feel rather than measurement: snow deciding; a coat still warm; the breath a choir takes together.  Two hundred and twelve silences, and not one from a battlefield, because he would not go where guns were.  Maren had gone, and whatever quiet she had found there he hoped to recover from behind that brass. 

Tonight offered his one chance, for the reed was shy and needed a stillness the valley produced only once a year.  Across the dark field the whole town stood hushed around the launch racks, where Maren always let the hush ripen before lighting the Armistice fireworks.  No dog barked, no cart rolled; even the crickets seemed to be holding their programs in their laps. 

Tobias switched on his salvaged oscillator, laid two fingers on the dial, and began tuning frequencies as gently as a man waking a child.  He offered the clasp the note of their mother's kettle, pure and bright.  Nothing.  He offered the church bell, then the whistle of the noon train, then, wincing, the low growl he imagined a cannon made.  The brass sat unmoved, and across the field a single taper flared at the racks. 

"I have been asking what I would have chosen," he confessed to the stars, hand on his heart.  "The question, ladies and gentlemen, is what she would have carried." 

So he closed his eyes and stood, for once, where Maren had stood: nineteen, frightened, proud, leaving a brother who would not come to the door.  What had she heard through that door?  Him, of course, humming over his bench as he always did, reaching for concert A and landing a hair flat.  She had teased him about that flatness all through their childhood. 

Tobias found the true A, then, swallowing his pride, eased the dial downward until the tone sagged into his own imperfect voice.  The reed shivered, and the clasp opened with a click as small as a forgiven debt. 

He had expected accusations, and by starlight he found a catalog instead.  Page after page, in her blunt hand, Maren had gathered the silences he could never collect himself.  "After the barrage: not peace, only everyone listening for an answer."  "A field kitchen when the list of names is read."  "The gun crew at dawn, each of us pretending the others are asleep." 

The last entry was dated the day she came home.  "I will make loud things that hurt no one, Toby, and before each I will hold one silence that ends in color instead of grief; that one is yours, when you can hear me." 

Tobias pressed the diary to his tailcoat and understood that she had gone to war carrying his flat little hum, and had been cataloging for him ever since.  He had kept his hands clean of every violence except the refusal to listen.  Nine years could not be recovered, but a sister might be, if the stillness held a moment longer. 

He ran to the open doorway, cupped his hands, and sang his flat, familiar note across the field with his whole chest.  Far off, the taper paused, dipped once like a bow, and touched the fuse.  In the last dark second Tobias wrote by feel: "Silence two hundred thirteen: my sister, waiting for me to arrive."  Then the sky applauded.
