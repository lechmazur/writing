At midnight the arbor held still above the clouds, a roof of old pines that generations of watchers had trained into one living lattice.  Ines sat on the plank deck beneath it and looked for patterns, because that was the job.  The valley below was cut off every winter, and couriers jumped from the summit ledge with medicine only when the watcher said the cloud would open. 

She had made the work her own.  Silk ribbons hung at nine heights in the branches to show her each layer of wind, and a row of shaving mirrors caught the moon so she could watch the cloud sea without turning her head.  Even the shutter latch was her invention, an old parachute buckle from her rigging years, screwed to the frame where her hand would find it in the dark. 

At eleven she had told Dario the east gap would hold until one.  Just before midnight he had answered, "Your word is my weather," and stepped off the ledge with the serum strapped to his chest.  Then ice in the cloud tops filled the radio with static. 

In that hiss, the lowest three ribbons turned and pointed the wrong way.  In the mirrors the east gap folded shut like a hand.  Nobody else on the mountain saw it.  That was the watcher's burden, the part no training named: to know first, alone, and to own what came of the knowing. 

The static cleared all at once, and the quiet it left seemed to carry the shape of every word she had not yet said. 

"Canopy open," Dario said, calm as a man reading a list.  "Still good, Ines?" 

She could say yes.  He was six hundred feet above her in the dark, and doubt was a weight he did not need.  But trust kept alive by a comfortable lie would die on the cliffs under that cloud, and him with it. 

"No," she said.  "I am revising my call."  "The east gap closed behind the static, and I was wrong about one o'clock."  "There is no valley tonight."  "The only ground you can see is me." 

A breath passed over the radio.  "The deck is forty feet wide," he said.  "The wind will keep the canopy flying and drag me off the far edge unless I cut it loose the instant I touch."  "And I took the old harness off the hut wall tonight, Ines."  "Mine is in for stitching, and I never learned these releases." 

She knew them.  She had packed and proofed that pattern for eleven years, but when she reached for the steps in words, nothing came.  The knowledge had never lived in words.  So she did what she always did with a problem: she looked for where the pattern was actually kept. 

She crossed to the shutter, backed out the two screws with her pocketknife, and closed the parachute buckle in her hands.  Then she shut her eyes and let her fingers go where they had gone ten thousand times.  They moved, and she only reported them. 

"I have the twin of your shoulder buckles in my hands," she said.  "Thumb under the cover plate and fold it down."  "Now push in before you pull; it will not give unless you push first."  "Two fingers through the wire, a quarter turn toward your heart, and it lets go." 

"Plate down, push, turn," he repeated, and then, "Got it." 

"Come in over the ribbons," she said.  "The top ones are honest tonight." 

He came out of the dark like a held breath, a pale wing sliding over the pines without a sound.  His boots struck the planks.  The canopy heaved toward the far edge and took him three stumbling steps with it.  Her own hands were still making the motions on the buckle when his finished them.  The silk tore free of his shoulders, sighed across the deck, and wrapped itself harmlessly around a trunk. 

Dario knelt where he had stopped, a yard from the drop, one palm flat on the serum case. 

"You said one o'clock," he said at last. 

"I did, and then I said otherwise." 

"That is why I jump on your word," he said.  "It is the only one up here that changes when the sky does." 

She opened the log and did not strike out the eleven o'clock forecast.  Beneath it she wrote: Revised at midnight; gap closed; courier down safe on the arbor; will try again at first opening.  Then she screwed the buckle back onto the shutter frame, where her hand would find it. 

Below them the clouds lay closed and silent, and she went on watching, listening to the quiet repeat what had been said.
