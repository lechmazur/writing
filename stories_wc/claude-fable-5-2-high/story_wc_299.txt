Three weeks after June Okafor called a counterfeit amplifier genuine on live television, the only client who still phoned was a dead man's son.  The studio audience had applauded her verdict, and then a collector in the third row stood up with a blacklight and took the applause away.  Now she drove a switchback road into Brenner Granite, where the pit had filled with green water and the office trailer leaned on its cinder blocks like a tired drummer.  She knew the place.  Nineteen years ago she had been the roadie for the Lowland Hours, and the band had played its last show on a flatbed stage at the bottom of this quarry, six weeks before Cass Whitlow, its singer, died on a wet highway. 

Teo Brenner met her at the trailer door with a clipboard and an apology for the smell of mildew.  "The buyers take possession Friday," he said.  "Dad kept everything, and I need to know what matters before the dumpster comes."  "I saw the broadcast," he added, not unkindly. 

"Then you know my rate dropped," June said, and went to work. 

Facts were her packing foam.  She told Teo the wall clock was a 1962 Seth Thomas, forty dollars; the blasting ledgers were worth more to a historical society than to a dealer; the oak swivel chair was Milwaukee-built, 1948, two hundred if the casters held.  Each number was a wall she could stand behind, and she did not look out the window at the pit. 

The bottom drawer of the desk had rusted shut through nineteen winters of damp.  June braced a boot against the frame, the way she used to brace against a jammed truck gate, and had to wrest it open an inch at a time.  Inside lay one envelope, and inside the envelope lay a scrap of speaker grille cloth no bigger than a playing card. 

Her mouth started before her heart could.  "Oxblood weave with a gold stripe, vinyl-coated paper fiber, used by one Chicago supplier between 1960 and 1963," she said.  "Reproductions use polyester, and the stripe spacing runs a millimeter wide."  "That is exactly what I missed on camera." 

Teo turned the envelope over so she could read his father's pencil: Night of the show, torn on the load-out, the girl said keep it. 

The facts ran out like cable off a dropped spool.  She remembered it then: Cass's amplifier catching the trailer hitch at two in the morning, the cloth ripping, and old Walt Brenner holding a flashlight for her because everyone else had gone to the party. 

"Dad talked about that night for years," Teo said.  "Not the music so much."  "He said after the applause the walls kept clapping for a long while, softer each time, and one person stayed down there coiling cable until the quarry was quiet."  "He said that was the part worth seeing." 

June held the scrap to the window.  For nineteen years she had believed her usefulness ended when Cass did, and that appraising was only a way to stand near old things without being asked what they meant.  On television she had wanted the applause back so badly that she handed the crowd a certainty she did not own. 

"So what is it worth?" Teo asked. 

"I can't prove this came off Cass Whitlow's amp," she said slowly.  "The weave is right, the period is right, your father's note is right, and my memory agrees, but memory is the worst witness in my trade."  "Call it seventy percent, and I am completely sure about the missing thirty." 

Teo laughed, a short bark the trailer walls gave back.  "Confidently uncertain," he said.  "Dad would have hired you on the spot." 

She wrote it on the form exactly that way: provenance probable, value undetermined, appraiser certain of both.  It was the first honest line she had signed since the broadcast, and it steadied her hand like a road case set down level.  Teo pressed the envelope back into her palm and said the girl who told Walt to keep it should decide where it went next. 

Outside, the evening had turned the pit water the color of old brass.  June walked to the rim above the drowned place where the flatbed had stood, and understood what her work had always been.  Someone had to stay after the crowd left, handle what remained, and say truthfully how much was known. 

She clapped once.  The quarry answered, then answered again, fainter, as if Cass were counting her in from the far wall.  June stood and listened until the last echo was gone, and then she carried the scrap back to her truck.
