The 7:10 commuter ferry Marigold vanished mid-channel on a Tuesday, though from her deck it seemed the city vanished instead.  Wren Calloway, deckhand, was coiling the stern line when the fog folded shut and opened again on a grotto.  Spectral limestone curved overhead like a ribcage, glowing faintly, and through its broken roof poured starlight, though it had been morning.  Two hundred commuters looked up from their phones.  Wren loved this job the way some people love a church, so she did what she always did: she smiled and checked the rail gates.  "Minor delay, folks," she called, "but what a ceiling." 

In the wheelhouse, Captain Oduya stared at the clock.  Its hands no longer circled; they slid sideways across the face, left to right, like wipers clearing nothing.  Beside it stood the hourglass he turned at every departure, a habit older than Wren.  It was empty, both bulbs, the sand not fallen but gone. 

"We have no time," Oduya said, "and I mean that literally." 

"Then we can't be late," Wren said, and picked up the handset to radio the harbor office. 

Static answered, then a voice she knew from voicemail greetings: her own.  "Marigold to harbor," the other Wren was saying, "we never left the dock; the fog cancelled us, and the same minute keeps arriving." 

They compared notes quickly, two deckhands with one name.  On the dock side, the hourglass was full in both bulbs and would not run; the clock there slid right to left.  One morning had split at the fog into a ferry that left without arriving and a ferry that stayed without leaving.  Around Wren, the commuters were thinning; starlight had begun to pass through the man who always bought two coffees. 

"We have to make them one morning again," she said into the handset.  Her other self asked how. 

Wren looked out the wheelhouse glass, because looking was the part of the job she was best at.  The stars reflected in the black water were not scattered; they sat in pairs, red-tinged and green-tinged, exactly where the channel buoys sat on the chart.  The sideways clock swept once for every swell against the hull.  Even the passengers held their pattern: the knitter at the port window, the students on the stairs, the two-coffee man facing the bow.  The grotto was not a place; it was the route with the morning peeled off, every pattern still linked to every other. 

"We do the run," Wren said, "both of us, stitch for stitch: you do every departure step, I do every arrival step, and we meet in the middle like a zipper." 

Oduya, given a task, became a captain again.  He steered between the paired stars while Wren radioed each mark across the split.  On the dock, the other Wren rang three bells; in the grotto, Wren answered with three.  The other cast off the bow line in figure eights; Wren flaked hers down ready for a cleat she could not yet see.  She walked the cabin asking her regulars to do exactly what they did every day, and they did, gratefully, because routine was a thing they knew how to do while frightened.  The knitter counted rows aloud.  The two-coffee man, half transparent, handed Wren the second cup as he had every Tuesday for a year. 

"Still warm," she told him, delighted, and it was. 

With each matched step, the clock hands tilted a little off sideways, hunting for round.  Over the radio the two voices drifted toward unison until Wren could not tell which of her was calling the marks.  At the last pair of stars, Oduya turned the empty hourglass, out of habit, out of faith.  Sand fell from nowhere into the lower bulb. 

The limestone thinned to fog, the starlight to an ordinary gray, and the city dock slid out of it with a cleat exactly where Wren's line was waiting.  She made fast and found she remembered the morning twice, once from the water and once from the dock, both of them true. 

Two hundred solid commuters filed past her, already forgetting, already looking at their phones.  Wren did not mind.  That was the bargain of a ferry: nobody stayed, everybody returned, and the leaving was the one thing that never left. 

The Marigold still vanishes now and then, for a breath, on foggy Tuesdays between the third and fourth buoys.  Wren keeps the hourglass by the helm, drinks the coffee she is handed, and radios ahead that they are right on time.
