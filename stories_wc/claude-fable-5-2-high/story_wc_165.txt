The Tidal Arcade at Sallow Quay had thirty-one arches, no roof, and a floor the sea borrowed twice a day.  At low water, under the stars, the Brine Players staged their melodrama on the wet flagstones, and between acts the audience belonged to Wren Calloway. 

She had been an actuary once, pricing other people's sameness into tables.  Now she carried a concertina and a notebook, and her act was simple: she let the crowd shout numbers, any numbers, then played them back as a tune.  Ordering randomness, she called it, though never aloud.  Monotony, she had decided, did not need defeating; it needed serenading, and creativity was the only instrument that carried. 

Tonight she had twenty minutes, a turning tide, and a black bishop in her coat pocket. 

She had found the chess piece three weeks ago in the wrack line, boxwood stained dark, its mitre chipped.  It took her one evening to learn whose it was.  At arch nineteen, old Mr. Abbas played both sides of the same game every night, a grey pebble standing where his queen's bishop should be.  It took her longer to notice Dolores, who tore tickets at arch seven with the same wrist, the same sigh, four hundred times a show.  During intermissions Dolores solved chess problems from a folded newspaper and looked at no one. 

Two solitudes, twelve arches apart, on the same diagonal. 

Wren could have walked the bishop over and handed it back.  But a thing handed over is a transaction, and a thing that happens is a story, and she knew which one people kept.  So for nineteen nights she chalked where the first tongue of the flood came in, and her notebook showed what the flagstones already knew: the arcade tilted, and the water always ran slantwise, from the seaward lip at arch seven to the back wall at arch nineteen.  The sea moved like a bishop.  All she had to do was put the piece in its path at the right minute and keep everyone else's eyes busy. 

The interval bell rang.  The heroine froze mid-swoon, the audience stretched, and beyond the arches the tide began to come back.  Wren stooped by arch seven as if to tie a bootlace, stood the bishop in the dry runnel, and walked on to her crate at arch thirteen. 

"Numbers," she called, opening the concertina like a lung. 

They gave her a birthday, a bus route, the price of eels.  She pinned each to a note and played the sequence back, and the nonsense came out as a reel, lopsided and alive.  She watched the way she always watched, lightly attentive, her notice resting on the crowd like an open hand under a bird.  At the edge of that attention, water silvered the runnel, lifted the bishop, and carried it past Dolores's boots.  Dolores looked up from her newspaper, saw a bishop travelling a diagonal, and stood. 

Exactly as charted. 

Then, three yards short of Wren's crate, the piece nosed into a crack her chalk had never found and stopped dead. 

Her fingers stumbled on the price of eels.  Every table in her old life said the same thing: intervene, correct, close the gap.  One step off the crate and a nudge of her boot would do it.  It would also make her the author, visibly, and then nothing would have happened at all. 

She kept playing.  She took the stumble, repeated it on purpose, and turned the mistake into the reel's refrain. 

At arch nineteen, Mr. Abbas lifted his head at the hiccup in the music.  He saw the ticket girl crouched over something small and dark in the shallows, and for the first time in three weeks he left his board. 

They met in the middle, ankle-deep, under more stars than either had looked at lately. 

"Yours?" Dolores asked, holding up the bishop. 

"Mine," he said.  "You play?" 

"Only against the newspaper." 

"The newspaper cannot gloat," said Mr. Abbas.  "Come and be gloated at." 

Wren had planned an arrival.  She had not planned a meeting halfway, or the joke, and those were the best parts.  Order randomness too tightly, she thought, and it was only a table again; leave it a crack, and it sang. 

The bell rang for the second act, and the crowd dropped coins in her hat and drifted back toward the heroine.  Mr. Abbas came last, and set the grey pebble among the coins. 

"For the understudy's retirement," he said.  "Also, the sea does not use chalk." 

Wren pocketed the pebble and said nothing, which was its own kind of confession.  At arch nineteen Dolores opened with a pawn, and the tide came on slantwise, as if it had thought of everything itself.
