The clinic promised a clean slate and delivered a blank one. 

After the procedure I knew my name, Calder Voss, and that I had paid to forget eleven years of collecting debts for men who never wrote anything down. 

The surgeon explained, kindly, that guilt and empathy grow from one root, so both had come out together. 

I watched a nurse cry over a patient down the hall and noted only that it was 4:10. 

My old self had left me a shoebox the screeners let through: three newspapers and a baseball. 

Notes were forbidden, so nothing was circled, nothing underlined. 

The ball was inked in a steady adult hand: "For Calder, who went left at every fork; Ines Marrow, October 9, 2061." 

That date was thirty years away, which made the signer a time traveler or a liar. 

I assumed liar, then read the papers the way I must once have read ledgers, for what was missing. 

The oldest carried a business brief: Marrow Hardware, foreclosed by a private lender who declined comment. 

The second held an obituary: Dale Marrow, dead "suddenly," survived by his daughter Ines, nine, a pitcher, whom he loved taking to see the glowing lake under Harrow Ridge. 

The third was that morning's: search suspended for a missing girl, every mapped passage of the Harrow caverns checked twice. 

Between those lines sat everything nobody had printed. 

"Suddenly" meant a man who ran out of ways to pay me. 

"Mapped" meant the lake was not on the map, because the obituary knew about it and the searchers did not. 

And the ball meant that some version of me had already made this journey, or that somebody wanted me to think so. 

I felt nothing about the girl. 

That was precisely why I went: I wanted to stand near a grief I had caused and see whether anything in me would catch. 

I signed myself out, bought a flashlight, and took a bus to Harrow Ridge. 

The search tape hung slack across the cave mouth, already abandoned. 

Past the last painted survey arrow, the passage split. 

The right-hand way was wide, dry, and sloped up toward air; the left was a wet crack that asked me to crawl. 

It was like that at every fork, seven in all, a labyrinth of choices in which the easy branch always led back to being the man the clinic had made. 

The ball in my pocket insisted I had gone left each time, but a ball cannot crawl for you. 

At the fifth fork I sat for a long time, knowing I could leave and feel fine about it forever. 

Feeling fine forever was the problem, so I went left. 

The seventh crack opened onto the lake. 

I switched off the flashlight and the dark did not arrive. 

The water held a faint blue of its own, and threads of living light hung from the ceiling like a sky someone had pawned and hidden down here. 

I had been told I was past wonder, and I mostly was, but I stood there anyway. 

A pebble struck the water, and a ring of brightness bloomed where it landed and slowly faded. 

The girl sat on the far shelf with a swollen ankle and a dead lantern, throwing stones to keep the lake lit. 

"It only glows where you bother it," she said, not surprised to see me, as if men walked out of walls daily. 

"Dad said it's been doing that for ten thousand years." 

Delicately robust, I thought: a light a breath could scatter, outlasting every empire above it. 

She was built the same way, nine years old and three days underground without crying. 

"I knew your father," I said. 

"I am the reason he was frightened at the end." 

I had no memory to confess, only arithmetic from newsprint, and I gave her that. 

She looked at me for a while, then cried at last, hard, and the sound landed in me like her pebble. 

Something small lit where it struck. 

It hurt, which I recognized, with tired astonishment, as the thing I had come for. 

I gave her the ball to hold while I lifted her onto my back. 

She read her own name in the blue light. 

"That's not how I make my M." 

"Give it thirty years," I said. 

"Is this a trick?" 

"Probably," I said, "but somebody went to enormous trouble, and I have been fooled by far less." 

"Keep it; apparently you return it someday." 

Then we started up through the forks, her weight on my shoulders, my slate no cleaner but finally worth keeping, and I chose each turn myself.
