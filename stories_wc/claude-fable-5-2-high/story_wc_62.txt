The council wanted one stone for forty-one drowned, and Ines Varga, who had carved the village's dead for twenty years, did not know what shape it should take.  One grief she understood: it was the width of a name.  But the ferry Marigold had gone down in March with half the harbor aboard, and this grief belonged to everyone and fit no slab she owned. 

So at dusk she walked up to the ancient greenhouse, as she did whenever a stone refused her.  Her great-grandfather had raised it, pane by salvaged pane, and four generations of Vargas had eaten supper there among the fig trees, at a long table scarred by hot dishes.  Tonight the cracked glass was orange with more than sunset.  The monarchs had arrived, thousands folding themselves onto the iron ribs to rest on their way south. 

And with them, as in every autumn since his funeral, came her grandfather.  Tobias Varga stood at the head of the table, gray as slate dust, visible only where the wings stirred the light.  In life he had taught her the chisel, and he taught her still, for exactly as long as the butterflies stayed. 

"Forty-one," she said.  "I cannot find the shape." 

Far out over the water, thunder rolled, slow and unhurried, and nothing in the greenhouse flinched.  He never spoke much, dead or living.  He pointed to the shelf above the table, where her grandmother's woven ribbon roll sat fat as a loaf. 

Ines knew it the way one knows a doorframe.  The family custom was older than the ribbon: at supper, each Varga brought a favorite poem and traded it away, so that you went to bed carrying someone else's beloved words.  After every trade her grandmother wove one finger of ribbon, a colored thread for each person at the table. 

"Retrace it," Tobias said, his voice like a mallet wrapped in wool. 

She unrolled the ribbon down the length of the table and went at it as she went at granite, fiercely meditative, her whole attention narrowed to her fingertips.  Years passed under her hands.  Here was her mother's green, bright through a thousand suppers, and here was the winter it vanished from the pattern.  Ines had always believed the thread was cut. 

Tobias turned his hand over, and she turned the ribbon.  On the underside the green ran on, hidden, still pulled tight with every pass, holding the other colors in their places.  Her grandmother had never woven a death as an ending, only as a thread gone beneath. 

Ines sat very still while the thunder walked closer and the calm in her deepened to meet it.  Collective grief was not a bigger stone.  It was a weave, and a weave had to be made at a table. 

So across the three weeks of the migration, she opened the greenhouse every dusk and fed the bereaved, a few families at a time.  She asked each to bring the poem their dead had loved best, and to trade it.  A net-mender's widow went home with a drowned boy's counting rhyme.  The boy's father left carrying a hymn the ferry captain used to hum.  Nobody was consoled, exactly, but people began nodding to one another on the quay like kin. 

Tobias watched from the fig shadows, and afterward he stood at her shoulder while she cut.  She carved forty-one narrow slates, each with one name on its face and, beneath it, a line from the poem that name's family had carried home.  When her chisel hurried, his cold hand lowered her wrist, and she retraced the letter until it was true.  The slates would be set edge to edge in a winding band along the quay path, so that no one could reach their own dead without walking past everyone's.  A ribbon of stone, forty-one threads gone beneath and still holding the harbor together. 

On the last dusk the storm finally stood overhead, and the monarchs lifted through the broken panes ahead of it, a slow orange exhalation.  Tobias thinned as the wings thinned. 

"What poem do you trade me?" she asked, because it was supper, and that was the rule. 

He laid his hand on the nearest slate, on a stranger's borrowed line, and was gone. 

Thunder broke over the glass like a great door closing gently.  Ines took down her grandmother's shuttle, found the loose end of the ribbon, and wove one finger more, running his gray thread underneath, where it would pull everything tight.
