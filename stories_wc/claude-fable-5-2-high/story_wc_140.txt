The city killed me with a typing error.  While I sweated through a fever in the charity ward, a stranger died in the container yard wearing my stolen coat, and the registry filed him under my name.  By the time I could stand, I had been buried, my room re-let, my accounts closed, my signature retired.  The clerk explained that a correction required a living applicant, and on paper I was not one. 

So I went to the place where I had officially died.  The port stacks its containers nine high, rust red and salt blue, in canyons that shift every time a ship unloads, so that no path through them survives a week.  Deep in that labyrinth I found the box with a chalk cross on its door.  He had crept inside for warmth, not knowing it had been fumigated that afternoon.  Tucked into a seam of the corrugated wall was a label from the poison bottle, peeled off whole and pressed flat: skull, crossed bones, a warning in three languages.  On the back, in pencil, someone had written: tell her the lemons came in. 

I should explain how I take in the world now.  Since the fever, or since the certificate, everything reaches me as texture first.  A lie is slick as fish skin; a foreman's shout is gravel; rain on steel is burlap dragged across my arms.  My own memories, though, had gone smooth as sea glass.  I could still see my mother's kitchen, but it had no grain, as if being struck from the record had sanded it.  Those six penciled words were different.  They felt like dry rope, rough and alive, a memory still belonging to someone.  I wanted to learn that texture, and the only man who could teach me was lying under my name. 

So I began to keep time by the tides.  On the flood, ships berthed and the gangs poured in, and I sat in the gaps between stacks, listening.  Nobody told me anything, because nobody tells a dead man anything, but talk drifts in a steel canyon.  A crane driver recalled a quiet fellow who slept beside the refrigerated boxes for the hum of their motors.  A lasher said he hummed back at them, always the same four notes.  The tea woman said he paid in foreign coins and asked, every week, which fruit ships were due.  Nobody had ever asked his name, and the registry had settled the question by giving him mine. 

On the ebb, when the yard emptied and the mud flats stank, I went back to the chalked box.  I wrote each overheard fragment on whatever paper the wind gave me, torn manifests and tally sheets, and pasted it to the wall around the label.  Tide after tide I layered scrap over scrap, until the steel wore a skin of other people's half-sentences.  When I ran my palm across it, I felt grain: rope, wool, lemon rind.  A man was returning, and he was not me. 

On a spring tide, the highest of the month, a fruit ship came in riding low and yellow-scented.  Its cook stood at the tea stall asking whether anyone remembered her father, who had gone ashore here and stopped writing.  He wrote on anything, she said, wrappers, labels, because paper cost money.  Her voice had the exact texture of the pencil. 

I had not spoken to a living person in forty tides, but I stood up and said, I can show you.  She followed me through the canyons to the chalked box and read the wall by lantern, layer by layer, her fingers moving over the hum, the coins, the fruit ships.  At the center she found the label, turned it over, and made a sound like cloth tearing.  I told her which grave, and whose name was on the stone, and why.  She said she would have it recut with his. 

Then she asked what no clerk had bothered to: and you, what do I call you?  I reached for my name and found it had gone smooth, a pebble that could have been anyone's.  But behind it, my mother's kitchen had roughened: flour on the table, a chipped blue bowl, grain everywhere.  So that was the texture I had been hunting: a memory gets its grain from being handed across, one voice layered on another. 

Call me whoever told you, I said.  She nodded, as though that were a name, and pocketed the label. 

When the tide turned, her ship went out with it.  I stayed in the box, facing the bare opposite wall, and on the next ebb I pasted up one scrap: flour, a blue bowl.  I did not sign it.
