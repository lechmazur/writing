Pell's hands shook the way they always shook, a fine tremor that made tea-sellers along the ridgeway pour her cups only half full.  She was a relic courier, the jittery one, and the job was mostly walking.  Twelve days north along the spine of the mountains with a sealed thing on her back, twelve days south with a receipt.  Overhead the aurora did what it did every night, green curtains dragging across the dark, and nobody on the ridgeway looked up anymore. 

Oda had looked up.  Oda had trained her, walked this route for forty years, and then one spring simply had not been at the Southgate when Pell arrived.  No note, no body, only her pendant hung on the hook where the courier satchel waited: a single dragonfly wing pressed between two slivers of glass.  That was nine years ago. 

Pell wore the pendant and kept Oda's one firm rule, which was never to let a handoff take less than an hour.  "A route is just a rut," Oda used to say, "until you stay long enough for somebody to surprise you."  So Pell lingered at every waystation, asking one more question, accepting one more half cup, stretching each brief meeting until it had room for a story in it. 

Lately the stories were about Oda, and they were wrong.  At Cinder Halt, a boy filling her cup told her that the First Courier had been nine feet tall and had outrun the aurora itself.  "She was shorter than me," Pell said, "and slow, because she stopped to talk to everyone."  The boy smiled politely, the way people smile at someone spoiling a good tale.  His grandmother, who had actually known Oda, had died that winter.  Pell understood then that she was walking through the years when a person stops being remembered and starts being told, and that she could not hold the door open alone. 

This trip's cargo was a clay casket of seeds from the drowned gardens of Ell, three centuries dormant, bound for the Northhold vault.  The vault was where relics went to be labeled, shelved, and never thought about again.  Pell had carried a hundred wonders there and watched each one become an entry in a ledger. 

On the ninth night, as she crossed the high saddle beyond Cinder Halt, the aurora came down low and loud.  It did that sometimes, a hiss like sand poured on silk, and the pendant against her breastbone began to shiver in answer.  She had always taken that for her own trembling passed along the cord.  Tonight, because Oda would have, she hung it from a trail marker and stepped back, and the wing kept humming without her.  It was not shaking with her; it was vibrating sympathetically with the sky, a thin membrane tuned to the same note. 

Then something on her back joined in.  She set the satchel down and laid her palm on the casket, and felt the clay purring, felt a faint dry ticking inside like rain starting.  Seed coats were splitting.  Somebody in Ell had bred them to germinate when the sky sang, and Pell, standing still under it long enough, had let them hear.  She laughed out loud on the empty ridge, and her hands shook, and for the first time she thought of the shaking as a kind of listening. 

Sprouted seeds would be dead before the vault clerk finished the label.  So Pell turned around, which no courier had ever done, and was knocking at Cinder Halt by dawn.  She woke the boy, put three split seeds in his palm, and showed him the white root tips already reaching.  She stayed four hours instead of one, helping him dig a bed in the lee of the tea stall, telling him how Oda hummed off-key and cheated at dice.  He could keep his nine-foot giant, she decided, as long as something true was planted next to it. 

Then she walked the whole route, slowly, leaving seeds and long conversations at every halt, the casket lighter at each one.  The Northhold got an empty clay pot and a receipt that read, in an unsteady hand, Delivered. 

By the next spring, pale flowers stood along the ridgeway, and on loud nights their petals trembled in time with the pendant and with her.  People came out of the tea stalls to watch, cups forgotten, faces green with light.  They said the First Courier had sown them, and Pell, lingering over a full cup someone had finally trusted her with, let that one stand.  Oda was still gone, but up and down the ridge, people were looking up.
