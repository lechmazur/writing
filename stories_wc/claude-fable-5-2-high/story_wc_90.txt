The Lullaby had been three warships once, and its welded ribs still remembered.  Sela Marrow walked its corridors in heavy boots she did not need, for sorrow kept her pinned well enough.  As a girl she had been a wonder: gravity loosened its grip on her whenever she was happy, and she had drifted laughing over her mother's garden.  She had not left a deck in thirty-one years. 

Thirty-one years ago the fold-cannon in this very hull had fired once, and the city of Anhe had not burned but vanished, shut inside a dimension that locked behind it like a held breath.  Its backwash had thinned the births in every fleet until they stopped.  Pim, nineteen now and the last human ever born, swept the corridors beside her, and after him there would be no one.  Unless Anhe came back. 

The seam still hung in the old engine vault, a gray pleat in the air, forty feet above the deck.  Sometimes, on the night watch, shapes leaned against it from the other side like faces against frosted glass. 

Chief Oduya wanted to rewire the cannon and blast it open. 

"Force locked that door," Sela told him, standing between him and the firing console as she had stood for years.  "Every scaffold we raised to it, every drone, every pound of pressure only pulled it tighter."  "I will not let you strike it, and I will not strike you to stop you."  "You will simply have to walk through me." 

He never had. 

Her own way was slower.  Along the memorial corridor the survivors had pinned ten thousand photographs of the vanished, faded now to the color of weak tea.  From the rubble at the torn edge of Anhe, salvagers had brought her a teardrop of fused temple glass they called Kwan Yin's Tear, after the goddess who hears the cries of the world.  Held to her eye, it showed what bare sight missed: the faint shimmer of long-dried tears, salt constellations where mourners had wept onto the paper.  Each stain trailed toward one edge, as if the grief itself were pointing, and where two trails met she would stitch the photographs together with thread unpicked from old uniforms. 

Over eleven years the stitching had grown into a vast pale shroud, and its shimmering trails had drawn, link by link, the shape of the seam itself.  All the trails ran toward a single point at its center, the one place a weightless hand might push.  And there the map failed, because the center was a hole the size of one photograph. 

Sela knew which one. 

It lay at the bottom of her locker, unwept upon: a gunnery ensign of twenty-two, grinning beside the fold-cannon, her hand resting on the firing key.  For three decades she had called that girl a stranger and built her gentleness as a wall against her. 

That night she spread the shroud beneath the seam, where the leaning shapes had gathered, and sat down at its empty center with the photograph.  She made herself look.  She let herself remember the pride of it, the clean thrill when the key turned, the cheer she had given before anyone understood what was gone. 

"You are not a stranger," she told the ensign.  "You are my shadow, and I have made you stand outside in the cold while I played the saint."  "My peace is not my innocence; it is what your hands taught mine."  "Come in." 

The tears came then, thirty-one years late, and fell on the grinning face.  Through Kwan Yin's Tear she watched them dry into a new shimmer, a small bright ring that pointed nowhere but here.  She threaded her needle and stitched the ensign into the hole at the center of the shroud. 

What rose in her was not gladness exactly.  It was the thing beneath gladness, the plain happiness of a house with every room unlocked.  She unlaced the heavy boots, and her bare feet left the deck. 

She rose slowly through the dark above the shimmering shroud, weighing less than a moth, less than a word.  In the hatchway Pim stood with his broom, the last child of the world, watching her go up. 

At the seam's center, where the map had promised, she laid one open palm against the gray.  She did not strike.  She pushed the way one pushes a nursery door, so as not to wake whoever sleeps beyond it. 

The pleat sighed and parted, and cold lamplight from a city thirty-one years gone spilled across her face.  On the far side, pale hands were already reaching back.  Sela held the door, weightless and unafraid, for as long as it would take them all to come home.
