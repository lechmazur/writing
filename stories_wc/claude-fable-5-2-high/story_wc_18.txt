In Pellmere, the orchard district that the fog had swallowed three hundred years ago, Maren Voss made mirrors for people who had forgotten what distance looked like.  She was a firmly grounded woman, boots planted, hands steady, trusting only what she could polish.  Her brother Tobiah was the opposite: the district's last astronomer, who had never once seen a star.  Every night he dreamed the same dream, a ragged window of clear sky opening above the dead pear trees, and every morning he described it to her while she pretended not to listen. 

Then, nine nights ago, the dream ran away.  Tobiah had cried out in his sleep, his grandfather's sextant had fallen from the sill, and something bright had gone out through the shutters like a startled deer.  He had not woken since.  He only breathed, in for four counts and out for six, growing thinner than the fog. 

Maren did not believe in runaway dreams, but she believed in what she had seen from the workshop door: a patch of impossible clarity, pricked with lights, drifting between the orchard rows where no clarity had been for centuries.  She had chased it twice with her best framed mirrors, and twice it had slid off the silver like rain off a slope of glass.  A mirror, she understood at last, was all boundary: an edge, a frame, a back that said no farther.  A dream would never enter a thing so certain of where it stopped. 

So she picked up the astronomer's broken sextant and studied it the way she studied any ruined glass.  The brass arc was bent but true enough; what had shattered was the horizon glass, the small pane that is half mirror and half window.  Through it a navigator sees the solid earth and a reflected star at once, then swings the arm until the star comes down to rest on the world's edge.  It was the only glass she knew that was built to erase the line between sky and ground. 

She would have to recreate it, and her silvering salts were useless, for nothing dried in Pellmere.  But each evening, for a few minutes upon the shimmer of twilight frost, the fog froze where it touched, and every dead branch became a blade of crystal.  Frost was a silver that asked for no backing. 

She cut a clear pane to fit the brass, sat an hour at Tobiah's bedside learning his rhythm until her own lungs kept it without counting, and walked out into the rows.  Around her the frozen fog banks rose sheer and glittering, like the glass mountains in the stories their mother told, the ones no rider could climb.  She held the pane to her lips and breathed as her brother breathed, in for four, out for six.  On each long exhale her warmth clouded one half of the glass, and the cold seized it, feathering it white, then bright, then silver.  The other half stayed clear. 

Somewhere up the terraces, something bright stopped running.  A dream knows its dreamer by the tide of his breath, and this one came drifting down the rows toward the sound of home, a torn window full of stars, wary as any wild thing. 

Maren did not lunge.  She set the new glass in the sextant, planted her feet in the frozen mud, and understood what all her stubborn steadiness was for: a star cannot be measured without a horizon, and she was the horizon.  Through the clear half she watched the black, rooted line of the orchard floor.  In the frost half hung the dream's brightest star, trembling.  Still breathing her brother's breath, she eased the bent arm along its arc, and the star sank, degree by degree, as gently as a hand lowering a sleeping child. 

It touched the ground.  For one breath there was no seam in the glass, no window and no mirror, no above and no below, no telling whose lungs were whose.  The dream poured into the pane like light into water and lay there quietly, a thumbnail of night sky rimmed in frost. 

She carried it home at arm's length, never breaking the rhythm, because frost is a brief silver and the house was warm.  At the bedside she held the sextant above Tobiah's mouth, and his own warm breath melted the frost and drew the stars back in. 

He opened his eyes and said, hoarsely, that he had dreamed she was standing in the orchard, holding up the sky.  Maren, who trusted only what she could polish, did not argue.  In the morning she pried the frame off her best mirror and hung the bare glass facing the fog, in case anything else wanted to come in.
