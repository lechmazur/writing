Fog came in with the tide at Marr Salvage, and the garden shaped it.  Her husband Teo had built the garden from the junkyard's leavings: organ pipes, boiler flues, a gutted tuba, all angled so the sea mist poured through and stood up in slow white figures that held a minute before sagging.  Odile Marr kept it running, and the town called her the junkyard prophet, though she had never once seen the future.  She only forgot it early.  A thing would drop out of her mind a few days before it mattered: the ferry's name the week it sank, a neighbor's daughter the month before the girl came home.  So each night, with a piece of plain chalk, she wrote what she still knew on the door of the dead school bus where she slept, and each morning she read the list for holes.  People paid her in eggs and diesel to hear which words had gone strange. 

Inside the bus, on a shelf above the cot, sat thirty-one videotapes.  Every October for thirty-one years, Teo had stood her at one spot in the garden where the pipes hummed loudest and filmed a single minute: the hum, the gulls, the two of them saying the date.  The pipes rusted and the note sank a little each year, and so did their voices.  "I want to capture the sound of growing older," he used to say, "since nobody can hear it happening." 

He had been dead three Octobers.  She had not played one tape since, nor recorded another.  She lived in their hush instead, the way a house lives around a stopped clock, telling herself that an echo unspent could never run out. 

Then one gray morning she read the bus door and stopped at four words in her own hand: where Teo stood me.  She knew the garden, knew the tapes, knew his name like her own teeth, but the spot itself was gone, clean as a pulled nail.  Her heart went out like the water over the flats.  She had only ever forgotten what was on its way.  The spot was coming, then, and October with it, and she would have to locate the place before she could stand there. 

Chalk was no help out among the sculptures; fog slicked every surface, and an arrow drawn at noon was milk by dusk.  But the tide left driftwood along the garden's seaward edge, gray limbs with a thick end and a thin one.  Each ebb she carried an armful in, walked until the hum swelled, and laid a piece down with its thin end pointing where the sound seemed strongest.  Next ebb she began from that marker and set another. 

For nine days the driftwood led her in a long, patient curve through the white figures and delivered her back to the bus door.  She stood there with wet sleeves and understood.  The stacked car bodies around the bus threw the hum back doubled, and she had been following the echo because the echo was louder, and because it led home to the shelf.  It was what she had done for three years. 

She started over, and this time she turned her back on whatever sound came twice.  The true note was thinner and lower than she expected, an old man's hum, and it drew her seaward, marker by marker, past the tuba and the leaning flues to an overturned skiff she had taken for scrap.  The last driftwood pointed straight at it.  She knelt, knees cracking, and heaved the hull over.  On the dry slab beneath, kept from the fog, was chalk: an X, and in Teo's square letters, STAND HERE, ODIE, YOU ALWAYS LOSE IT IN OCTOBER.  The chalk in her pocket was the last stick from his box. 

She fetched the camcorder and the one blank tape, stood on the X, and pressed the red button.  The pipes gave their sunken note.  The fog rose in a figure beside her, held, and let go.  She said the date, and her voice came out rough and small and three years further along, and she let the machine have all of it: the breath, the knees, the gulls, the gap where his voice belonged.  This was the sound he had wanted, she thought, and it could not be kept by keeping still. 

That night the tide came back across the flats and took the driftwood home.  Odile slid the first tape into the player, and the hush in the bus broke on a young man's voice saying a date.  Before sleeping she chalked the door as always, his name first, knowing that some morning it would be the word she could not read, and that she would know then who was coming.
