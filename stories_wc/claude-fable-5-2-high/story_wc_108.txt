The library had been rusting since before I was born, whoever I was.  Its roof had blown off decades ago, so the iron stacks stood open to the stars, and the film crew had strung their cables through the shelves like vines.  I crouched on the third gallery in Calla Dray's costume, forty feet above the airbag, with a child's plaster cast in my lap. 

I am afraid of heights.  I am also afraid of fire, deep water, locked rooms, and being looked at, which is a strange list for a stunt double.  Hask always said the fear was why I was good.  "Adapt firmly, Wren," he would say, checking my harness with the hands that raised me.  "Change the plan, never the spine, and never let them see your face shake."  So for eleven years I fell masked, wigged, and silent, and Calla Dray was never allowed to see my face. 

Yesterday I went looking for spare carabiners in his rigging trunk and found the cast instead.  It was sized for a six-year-old's forearm, and it fit the old break in mine like a memory.  Tucked inside was a newspaper clipping: DRAY TWIN LOST IN SOUNDSTAGE FIRE, SISTER SURVIVES.  Hask had been the safety coordinator on that set.  He had not found an orphan; he had made one, carrying me out of the smoke and keeping me, a spare body for a rising star, taught to fear everything except him. 

I was not Wren Hask.  I did not yet know my name, but I knew whose face I had been hiding. 

Telling anyone was useless, because Hask had spent years explaining how fragile I was, and a frightened double claiming to be the star's dead sister would be driven gently to a clinic.  I needed something only Calla would recognize, delivered where he could not stand between us. 

The cast was covered in a child's writing, smudged by twenty years and, in places, by a deliberate adult thumb.  But a pencil dents plaster even where the graphite smears, so I rubbed rust from the railing across the cast and the letters rose out of it, orange and stubborn.  They were nonsense: LDPOL, FFSIU, BFT.  Then my hand remembered before my head did: one letter forward, written backward, the code two small girls used to pass notes through a bedroom wall. 

KNOCK THREE.  SAY SEA.  I WILL COME. 

Around my neck, where it had hung all my life, was half a seashell, broken clean down its spiral.  Hask called it junk I was clutching when he found me, yet even halved it still whispered when I cupped it to my ear.  Calla, the makeup girls said, kept a broken shell in her trailer and would not shoot without it. 

Below, Hask stood at the foot of the only staircase, arms folded, certain that fear would keep me quiet and obedient until I jumped.  He had forgotten what he taught me.  Change the plan, never the spine.  Tonight I would prove that the shaking he made me hide was the strongest thing I owned. 

He guarded the stairs, but the sound department had taped a microphone under my collar to record the wind of the fall, and that wire ran to the headphones of everyone at the monitors, including Calla. 

"Rolling," the director called. 

My knees shook so hard the gallery rang.  I took off the wig, then the mask, and let the cameras have my face, wet and terrified and exactly like hers.  Hask started up the stairs.  I rapped the railing three times, pressed the shell half against the microphone so its small ocean whispered into every headset, and said, "Sea."  My voice cracked on the single syllable, and I let it.  "I am afraid of heights," I said into the wire, "and fire, and being seen, Calla, and I was your sister before I was your double." 

Nobody called cut. 

Hask stopped halfway up, because the secret he had built my fear to guard was already in everyone's ears, and my shaking only made it sound true.  A hidden fear is a leash, but a fear spoken aloud is proof.  He had betrayed me into a smaller person, and the betrayal, once I knew it, cracked me open into a larger one. 

Then lighter footsteps rang on the iron, and Calla pushed past him without a glance, her half of the shell already in her fist.  "I knocked for years, Tessa," she whispered, "and nobody ever knocked back." 

We fitted the halves together under the open stars, and the whisper inside deepened, as if the sea had finally been given room.  I was still forty feet up and still afraid, and I had never been so sure of anything.
