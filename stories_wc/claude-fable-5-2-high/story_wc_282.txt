Ione made maps for people who would wake blind.  The lamps in the coastal cave network had died sixty years into the Sleep, so her charts used no ink: only the kelp-and-iron smell of the Second Gallery, the floor changing from ribbed to slick, a drip that rang in a low D where the passage forked.  She spoke each landmark into a recorder that the next waker would inherit, and that the sleepers would one day hear. 

Forty thousand of them lay in the sea cliffs, berthed in rows above the tide line, waiting out a century the surface could not support.  Each one held an object.  This was policy, not sentiment: in the trials, people who went under empty-handed came back as strangers to themselves, while those who clutched a blanket, a spoon, a dog's collar crossed over whole.  The engineers called them transitional objects, a term borrowed from the nursery, and issued no berth without one. 

Ione's was an elm leaf fossil, given by her mother, who had gone under in the first wave.  Then Ione drew the waker's lot, and the object meant to carry her into sleep rode in her chest pocket through eleven wakeful years instead.  She touched it at every fork. 

There was a second layer to her map, and she recorded it in the same flat voice as the first.  Dreams give off heat.  This was measurable: through her thermal lens, dread pooled blue-green along the lowest berths, longing rose amber toward the vaults, and once a whole gallery blushed coral when a storm passed overhead and forty sleepers dreamed of rain.  She heat-mapped these emotions the way she mapped drafts and drips, because a waking crowd would move toward what it felt before it moved toward what it knew. 

The trouble was the fork at the low D.  Left climbed to the surface; right dropped into a flooded sump, and beyond the sump her lens showed a chamber glowing a color that was not in her key.  No one was berthed there.  In the last trial waking, three of five sleepers had turned right, toward that warmth, and had to be hauled from the water. 

A map that marked it only as danger would be half a map.  Ione wanted more than that; when forty thousand people woke and asked what had happened to them in the dark, she wanted to be the one voice able to answer, to speak for the unknown instead of fencing it off. 

So at slack high tide she lay back in the sump and let herself float.  The ceiling slid past a hand's width from her nose, the fossil rode on her sternum, and the water carried her through without a single stroke. 

The chamber beyond was a dome, blood-warm.  She understood the mechanics at once: every fissure in the cliff vented upward into this one room, so the dream heat of all the galleries collected here like breath under a blanket.  The color was what forty thousand feelings made when they were summed. 

Still floating, she turned the lens on her own hand, closed around the fossil, and found the same color there.  It was not grief and it was not comfort.  It was the heat of a hand shut on a thing that stands in for someone absent, the feeling of being between, and the wakened turned toward it because it was the only feeling all of them shared.  She named it holding, and entered it in the key. 

Then she looked at what she held.  An elm leaf had lain down in mud, slept forty million years, and kept every vein; the midrib ran straight as an arrow, and a fingertip could read it in total dark.  It had never been a way into sleep for her.  For eleven years it had been a way of staying her mother's daughter, of touching a pocket instead of trusting her own voice.  The object had done its work; the proof was that she could imagine setting it down. 

She floated back out on the turning tide and wedged the fossil into a niche at the fork, at hand height, stem down, midrib pointing left and up.  Then she switched on the recorder. 

"At the low D, you will feel warmth on your right," she said, and her voice filled the passage.  "It is everyone's holding, gathered in one place, and it is real, but it is not the way out.  Find the stone leaf on your left, follow its spine, and when you reach daylight, you may open your hands." 

She climbed toward the surface watch with her pocket empty, touching nothing at the next fork but the wall.
