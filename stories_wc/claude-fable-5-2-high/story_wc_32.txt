At twilight the Ember Nexus glowed like a wound that had forgotten how to hurt.  Seven veins of buried fire met there, in a stone basin at the heart of the valley, and around its rim stood the orchard, every blossom sealed in ice since before Ilse Varrow's grandmother was born. 

The Varrows were bishops of the Nexus.  Each in turn had stood at the basin with the frost-silver crozier, keeping the embers exactly as they were, neither burning nor dead, and each had been buried under the unmoving trees.  Ilse was to be invested at full dark. 

She had one hour, and the crozier lay on her workbench in three pieces.  It had snapped in her father's grip the night he died, and nobody had said aloud what that might mean.  They had only looked at Ilse, because she was the family's alchemist. 

The guild ledger had another word beside her name: hopeless.  She had earned it across ninety trials, each one an attempt to vivify a single frozen branch, each one ending in a blackened twig and a neat line in her notebook.  Somewhere around the fortieth she had stopped hoping and found her hands went steadier without it.  People took her stillness for coldness.  In truth she felt everything, the ache of each locked petal, her mother's fear, the thin pulse of the embers through her boot soles, and she simply wrote it down instead of weeping. 

So she worked the only way she knew, through successive approximation.  A solder of tin and frost-silver, four parts to one: the joint held for a breath and cracked.  Three to one held for two breaths.  She adjusted, noted, adjusted again, each failure a little smaller than the last, creeping toward a mend the way a tide creeps toward a wall. 

The alloy wanted copper.  All she had was the penny her father had pressed into her palm at the end, unable to speak, his eyes fixed on the basin.  She carried the crucible to the rim for heat, set the crozier's pieces beside her, and reached for the coin with fingers gone numb. 

The penny slipped. 

It rang once on the stone, rolled past the crucible, and dropped into the Nexus.  The embers took it with a soft green flare, and for one heartbeat the basin burned gold, real gold, the color of a hearth.  Then it sank back to its old dull red, and Ilse, who felt everything, felt exactly where the warmth had gone. 

It had gone into the crozier.  The broken pieces at her knee were furred with new frost. 

She did not trust a single observation, so she tested it.  She moved the pieces one pace back, and the embers brightened by a shade.  Two paces, and a bead of water trembled on the nearest blossom.  Four, and she could hear the ice ticking.  Approximation by approximation the answer closed around her, and it was the reverse of everything she had been taught. 

The fire had never been dying.  The crozier drank it, and ten generations of bishops had called the drinking a vigil.  Her father had understood when the staff broke in his hand; the penny had not been a keepsake but an instruction.  Ninety trials had failed because she kept trying to add life to the orchard, when life was only waiting for something to be taken away. 

Below, lanterns were climbing the path, her mother in front with the mitre.  Ilse saw the rest of her life in that procession: a mended staff, a cold basin, a grave under glass flowers. 

Whole, the staff could have drunk the Nexus dry, but broken, each piece was small enough to drown.  She dropped in the ferrule first; the embers dimmed, struggled, and swallowed it, and burned brighter for the meal.  The shaft went next.  Last she held the crook, lovely and old, searing her palm with cold, and she grieved for it quietly, the way she grieved for everything. 

Then she let it go. 

The Nexus roared gold, and warmth rolled outward through the orchard like a held breath released.  The ice let go of the blossoms, and the blossoms, a century dead, browned and fell in a slow wet rain. 

Her mother reached the rim and cried out at the ruin.  Ilse said nothing, only took her mother's lantern and raised it to the nearest bough, where the bark had split and a bud no larger than a grain of barley showed green.  With her unburned hand she opened her notebook and, under ninety neat failures, began the ninety-first line.
