In the mirror world, glass never let anything through; it only handed your own face back.  So when the night settled over the alchemical orchard and did not lift, the greenhouse became a box of dark reflecting dark, and the trees began to die. 

Ilse Varn kept the memories there, badly.  She stored them in stoppered vials, in knots tied along her sleeves, in notes pinned to quince trunks, because her own head would not hold them in order.  She could recite a stranger's wedding day and forget whether she had eaten. 

One note, pinned to the oldest tree in her own cramped hand, said only: THE MICA WINDOW IS THE ONE THAT OPENS. 

She found it on the hundredth dark morning, or the thousandth, and climbed the ladder to look.  High in the eastern gable, among a thousand silvered panes, sat a single sheet of mica, amber and layered like the pages of a pressed book.  It did not show her face.  Caught between its leaves were threads, fine as breath, the frayed selvage of something that had once been pulled through. 

When she touched one, it hummed three notes and a memory fell into place: dawns here had never risen.  They had been woven.  Each morning the three settlements around the orchard had sung their separate verses, and the keeper had worked the sound into cloth, wrapped it around the seed of the sun-quince, and held it to the mica until the light went out into the world.  Then the settlements quarreled over ferry tolls and furnace sand, stopped singing together, and the songs slid out of everyone, Ilse included. 

She had three notes and a realm going cold.  So she filled her coat with vials and walked. 

In Cinderwick, the glassblowers barred the door until she uncorked a memory of their founder's first furnace, the roar of it, the orange on the walls.  An old gaffer wept, and in the weeping found himself humming a working tune his grandmother had used to time the bellows.  Ilse knotted it into her sleeve before it could escape her. 

At the salt ferry, she traded the ferrywomen a memory of the river when it still held stars, and they gave back a rowing chant with half its words missing.  Among the root-diggers of Lowmarrow, a child knew a lullaby nobody had taught her, and its tune carried the missing half. 

That was when Ilse understood that no village owned a whole song.  The verses were cut to fit one another, like warp and weft, and they would not hold light alone.  She told each settlement that the others had the rest, and that she, being who she was, would certainly lose it on the road back.  It was the truth, and it worked better than pleading. 

They came grumbling, with lanterns, glassblowers and ferrywomen and diggers who had not shared a roof in years, and stood stiffly among the failing trees.  Ilse dug the sun-quince seed from the ash bed where it lay, gray and barely warm, the size of a newborn's fist. 

"Sing what you have," she said, "and listen for where it catches." 

The bellows tune started rough.  The rowing chant slid under it, the lullaby crossed over both, and in the air above Ilse's hands the sound thickened into threads, amber and rose.  She wove fast, before she could forget how, pulling each voice tight against its neighbor until a small bright cloth lay across her arms. 

Then she did what the oldest note could not tell her and her hands somehow knew: she swaddled the seed, snug as an infant, binding the dawn around it so the warmth had nowhere to go but in.  The seed drank, and woke, and burned gold through the weave. 

She carried it up the ladder and pressed the bundle to the mica window.  The light, held so closely, went through the only pane that would let it, layer by layer, and poured out over the orchard roof, the river, the furnace town, the root fields.  Behind her, every mirrored pane caught it and passed it along, and the night drew back as far as the hills, where it would wait for tomorrow. 

By the time she climbed down, the songs had already left her; she reached for the first note and found nothing.  It did not frighten her as it once would have.  The gaffer was teaching the ferrywomen his verse, the child was correcting them all, and someone was arguing, warmly, about who would walk up first tomorrow. 

Ilse untied the knots from her sleeves, one by one, and let the others keep what she could not.
