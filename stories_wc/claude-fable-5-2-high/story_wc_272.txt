Ione Marsh had painted four hundred faces and could not finish her mother's.  The canvas leaned in the lamp room at the top of the lighthouse, a brow, one eye, a jaw, each lovely and none of them touching.  Since the funeral, the whole world had arrived that way.  The old lens turned overhead, a hundred prisms throwing one beam, and every nine seconds the light swept through and showed her a different tower: her mother's cup steaming, then cold; a chair pulled out, then never moved.  She could not make the pieces agree. 

The surveyor had come that morning to measure the place for sale.  "Who would want a dead light on a crumbling rock?" he had said, snapping his tape shut.  "My mother, for forty years," Ione had answered, "and the cormorants, and possibly me."  He had stared at her, as people always did.  She had never been able to let a rhetorical question lie; a question left unanswered felt to her like a sitter left without a mouth.  He would return at dawn with the buyers. 

So it was dusk, the hour when the walls close in, when she finally went down one flight to the library nobody remembered.  Her mother had built it over decades from wreck salvage and mail-order catalogs, curved shelves bolted to the curved stone, and the damp had swollen the wood until the room seemed to lean inward and breathe on her neck.  She meant only to save a few books, but tugging at a stuck atlas she brought down a small wooden box that struck the floor and finished breaking.  It was her mother's tarot box, its hinge torn years ago, its lid now split along the grain. 

The cards did not scatter.  They slumped out in a single snarl, each one pierced and strung on silk embroidery thread, red and grey and a blue like deep water, hopelessly knotted.  Inside the split lid, in her mother's upright hand, was one line: Who ever learned a face by staring into the lamp? 

Anyone else would have smiled sadly and closed the lid.  Ione sat down on the cold floor and took the question seriously.  Who had?  She had, or had tried to, painting her mother upstairs for a year in the full glare of the beam, all radiance, the keeper as the town described her.  And what had she learned?  Nothing that would hold together. 

She began on the knots.  It was slow work, done by feel between sweeps of light, loosening one loop to free another, and it was the first thing in months her hands had wanted to do.  As each thread came clear, it drew its cards into a row, and she saw that the threads were an order, not a tangle.  Laid face down, row under row, the backs showed what her mother had inked across them, a fragment to each card: a woman's face, mostly black.  It was a self-portrait, and the light in it touched only a cheekbone, the wet edge of an eye, the corner of a mouth.  Everything else was the dark her mother had lived in between flashes, forty years of nights, and it was the dark that held the pieces in one face. 

The blue thread was the last, and one card was missing from its row, a hole where the left eye should be.  The thread itself had never been cut.  It ran out through the cracked corner of the box and over the lip of the stairwell, down toward the oil cellar where her mother had worked on windless nights, and had fallen, and where Ione had not set foot since. 

Above her the beam went round, offering its bright, broken versions of the room, and she understood that climbing back to it would only give her more pieces.  The dark was necessary.  What was the worst the dark could show her?  She answered that too, aloud: the stair, the stain, and a woman who had been tired and stubborn and afraid, and not only a light.  It was the part of the face she had been leaving out. 

She fetched the canvas from the lamp room and, before she could argue, scraped the brow, the eye, and the jaw down to the weave.  She would redesign the portrait from the ground up, black first, and let her mother arrive out of it the way a coast arrives before dawn.  Then, with the canvas under one arm and the blue thread running through her fingers, she went down, past the last reach of the beam, to find the missing eye.  Overhead the light kept turning, and for the first time it ached like something whole.
