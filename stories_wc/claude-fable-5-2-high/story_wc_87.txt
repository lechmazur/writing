The letter from the maritime board was three sentences long, and Odile Marrow read it at the coastal kayak launch because she could not bear to read it indoors.  License not reinstated.  She had expected mercy the way she once expected stars, as something owed to anyone who looked up long enough.  Now the expectation collapsed quietly, like a tent with its pole pulled, and she laughed, because that was her habit.  The fishermen called her merrily melancholic, the broken navigator who whistled jigs in a minor key while renting out life vests. 

In her jacket pocket sat the astronomical compass from the Corvina, brass and heavy, the instrument that had told her the truth on the night she kept silent.  She had sighted a star, known the captain's heading was wrong, and said nothing, because he was certain and she was only right.  The shoal had taken the hull, the inquiry had taken her license, and tonight the letter took the last of her bargaining.  So she would paddle past the breakwater and drop the compass into deep water, a small burial with a cheerful tune. 

She dragged her kayak down the concrete ramp, past the rinse hose and the tide board, and pushed off into a dusk the color of dishwater.  Regret, she had learned, behaves like a wave against a seawall.  Throw it hard and it comes back harder, and out beyond the breakwater the backlash of regrets found her all at once.  The word she had swallowed on the bridge.  The cook's broken wrist.  The apology she had rehearsed for the inquiry and delivered as a list of excuses.  She held the compass over the water, and her hand, sensible creature, refused to open. 

Then the fog arrived.  It came off the sea in one soft wall, erasing the breakwater, the ramp, and every star the compass might have used.  Odile set the useless brass in her lap and whistled, because a navigator without a sky still has her wits, or half of them. 

Her father had taught her this coast before any academy did, and his method was navigating by utility poles.  The shore road carried a line of them, each with a sodium lamp, and the ninth pole from the cannery, the one with the fat transformer, stood directly above the launch.  Through the fog the lamps showed as a row of orange smudges, flickering, going out, returning.  She counted them the way some people count beads. 

She was at six when she heard the voice, thin and young, somewhere seaward.  "Hello, is anyone out there?" it called.  Two rental kayaks, she guessed, the teenagers who had signed her clipboard at four and promised to be back by dark.  A second voice, older and very sure, answered the first: the ramp was left, obviously, follow him. 

Left was the open channel and the ebb tide.  Odile knew it the way she had known the shoal, and for one long breath the old silence rose in her throat, polite and deadly.  Who was she, unlicensed, to contradict a confident man?  The lamps flickered, and so did her faith, and she decided that flickering was enough to steer by. 

"Not left!" she shouted, and her voice cracked like an oar.  "Stop paddling, both of you, and gravitate toward my whistle."  She whistled the minor-key jig, loud and ridiculous, until two yellow bows nosed out of the gray and bumped her hull.  The older boy began to argue, and she pointed her paddle at the shore. 

"Count those lamps with me," she said.  "Every one is a utility pole, and the ninth has a transformer that hums like a hive, and under it is your ramp."  They counted aloud, three cold voices, losing a lamp to the fog and finding it again, seven, eight, nine.  The hum reached them before the concrete did. 

When the boys had stumbled up the ramp, laughing with relief, Odile sat a while in the shallows with the astronomical compass in her palm.  The board would never give her back a bridge.  The regrets would keep rebounding off whatever wall she built.  But tonight her heart had said speak, and her mouth had obeyed, and that small alignment felt truer than any heading she had ever plotted. 

She did not bury the compass.  She hung it on the nail beside the tide board, where it could wait for clear skies, and wrote beneath it in chalk: ninth pole, trust the hum.  Above the launch the lamp flickered, steadied, flickered, and Odile walked home whistling, sad as ever and oddly glad.
