When the arch over Hollin's Reach fell asleep, it fell everywhere at once.  On Verrin, the orchard planet, floating rock arches drifted above the apple rows like slow blue bridges, their lift-ore glowing azure for as long as it stayed awake.  This one had stayed awake a hundred years, then dimmed to gray and dropped across the oldest orchard and half of Tobben Marl's forge. 

Tobben was a meek blacksmith, the kind who apologized to horses.  At the meeting he sat at the back while the assessor declared the orchard lost and offered a fair price to crush the rubble for road gravel.  Tobben said nothing.  He went home, swept grit from his anvil, and took a square of magician's silk from the nail where it had hung since spring. 

A traveling conjurer had paid with it for a mended axle, having no coin.  "It carries waking," she had told him, knotting the azure cloth around his wrist to show him how.  "Tie one end to something awake and the other to something sleeping, and they trade."  "Year for year, though; nothing wakes on this world unless something of equal age agrees to rest." 

He had nodded politely and understood perhaps half.  Half would have to do, because he knew himself: if he waited for the whole of it, he would find reasons to stay home.  So he meant to work in the narrow hour between understanding and confusion, when a thing is clear enough to attempt and not yet clear enough to fear. 

The trouble was the price.  The arch was a century old, and only one thing in Hollin's Reach had been awake that long: the forge fire his great-grandmother had lit and no Marl had ever let go out.  He banked a shovelful of its coals in an iron firepot, wrapped the handle in rags, and walked to the old Carrow diggings. 

The mining company had bored those shafts under the orchards hunting lift-ore, then abandoned them when the ore went dull the moment it was hauled from its home.  No one could shift the fall from above, but the galleries ran beneath all of it like roots, and the arch's keystone had punched through into the deepest one.  A waking begun there would travel every tunnel and touch every broken stone from below.  The shaft timbers groaned, and Tobben, who feared most things, climbed down anyway, one rung at a time. 

He found the keystone wedged in the dark, gray as a dead tooth, with apple roots dangling around it, still alive.  He set down the firepot, tied one end of the silk to the stone, and then could not tie the other. 

He sat on a fallen prop to deliberate, weighing the matter as he would weigh iron in his palm.  A smith without his fire was only a quiet man with a hammer, and four generations had kept this one burning.  He could offer himself instead, but he had only forty years to give, and a sleeping man coaxes nothing.  Then he looked at the roots, still gripping after everything, and at the coals he had revived on a thousand cold mornings by breathing on them.  Resilience, he decided, was never the refusal to go out; it was the being lit again. 

"Rest now," he told the fire, and knotted the silk to the firepot. 

The coals went black without a hiss.  Blue ran up the silk like water up a wick, and the keystone drew a breath of light and began, slowly, to weigh nothing.  Azure poured down the galleries faster than he could follow, and above him the whole fall shuddered and let go of the ground. 

When he climbed out at dawn, the rubble hung over Hollin's Reach in a new arch, crooked and gap-toothed and blazing blue against the paler sky, as if daring it to object.  Beneath it the old trees stood bent and splintered in the first light they had seen in months, and one cracked bough already carried a bud. 

The assessor stared upward with his contract in his hand, caught, like everyone, somewhere between understanding and confusion.  "There is no gravel for sale," Tobben said, not loudly, and it was the longest speech the village had heard from him. 

Then he went home to a forge gone cold for the first time in a century.  The old fire would sleep a hundred years, and that was the barter.  He laid shavings of windfall applewood on the hearth, struck flint to steel, and bent to breathe on a spark that was one morning old.
