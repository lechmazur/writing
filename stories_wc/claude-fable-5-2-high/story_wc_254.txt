The ban lifted at noon, but Maren Vost waited for dusk, because noon light was flat and she would not cross a sacred line in ugly weather.  She was a cloud-shadow poet, which meant she composed only from the dark shapes that clouds dragged over open ground, and for forty years the best ground in the province had been forbidden. 

The cobalt atrium stood on the shore of Lake Hessen, its blue glass roof holding the last daylight like water in cupped hands.  Along its open side ran the quartz colonnade, twelve pale columns that hummed whenever the frozen lake shifted, and between them lay a silver line no one had stepped over since the founders.  The founders had set the hum into twelve fixed verses, the Canon, and the ban had protected both boundaries at once: no foot past the columns, no word past the Canon.  To paraphrase a verse had cost her teacher his license and, she believed, his voice.  She carried his lantern now. 

Inside, Warden Oskel was sweeping frost from the tiles as he had every evening of the ban, and he did not stop when she entered. 

"The council expected you at the ceremony," he said, very quietly, the way everyone spoke in that room. 

"The ceremony had trumpets," Maren said.  She set the lantern on the floor, and two winter moths found it at once and began their soft, patient orbit.  "I came to see whether the crossing can be made beautiful, because if it cannot, the ban was right." 

Oskel leaned on his broom.  "I have recited the Canon here eleven thousand times, and I have never once wanted to change a syllable." 

"Then recite the first verse for me," she said, "and let me listen to the column while you do." 

He recited: The lake is a closed door, and the faithful stand before it. 

Maren laid her palm against the first column and felt the hum, low and even as a sleeper's breath.  Beyond the silver line the ice lay rose-white under the last of the sun, and across it, slow as a hand smoothing a sheet, came the shadow of a cloud.  When the shadow touched the shore, the ice cooled and tightened, and the column's hum dropped half a tone under her hand. 

It was a small thing, a tremor, but it went through her wrist and into her chest and settled there as certainty.  The columns were not singing the Canon.  They were answering the shadows, and the founders, standing where she stood, had only put that answer into the best words they had. 

"Your Canon is a paraphrase," she said.  "The first one, and a fine one, but the original is out there on the ice, and it has no edges." 

Oskel said nothing, but he put his own hand on the quartz, and she saw him feel the next shadow arrive. 

So she began, not by breaking the verse but by loosening it, the way a cloud's shadow loosens the line between field and road.  "The lake is a door that the dark keeps opening," she said, "and whoever watches is already through." 

She walked the colonnade with the lantern, moths trailing, and gave each column its verse again, letting the end of one run into the start of the next until twelve fixed things became a single moving one.  Where a phrase came out clumsy she stopped, waited for another shadow, and tried it again, because a boundary dissolved carelessly is only a mess. 

By the twelfth column the atrium was dark except for the lantern, and a moon had come up over the ice, where the shadows went on crossing, paler but no less sure.  Maren stood at the silver line.  She had imagined this as a leap, and found instead that the words had already carried the inside out and the outside in, so that there was nothing left to violate. 

She stepped between the columns and strode onto the lake, her lantern swinging, her own shadow joining the cloud's.  Behind her the quartz hummed, neither louder nor softer, which she took as the only blessing worth having. 

Then she heard the broom laid down, and slow boots on the ice.  Oskel stopped beside her, breathing hard, an old man standing where he had spent forty years forbidding anyone to stand. 

"The lake is a door," he whispered, testing each word like ice, "and it was never closed, only kept." 

It was not her line, and it was not the Canon's, and Maren thought it the most beautiful thing said all day.  A moth settled on the lantern glass, and the shadow moved on.
