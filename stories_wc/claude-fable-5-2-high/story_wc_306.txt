The last spark of Odalys Brandt's campfire died on the shingle, and that was my cue, because my contract began exactly there.  She had burned eleven years of drafts in it, every version of the report except the one she meant to sign at dawn.  That one named her brother Emil, keeper before her, as the drunk who let the lamp go dark the night the Calloway struck the reef. 

I am a conscience for hire.  People send for me when they are too sure of something to sleep, and too frightened of being unsure to question it alone.  My whole trade is to welcome nuance into certainty, the way you would let a stray in from the rain: carefully, with the door held. 

"I should warn you," I said as we climbed the lighthouse stairs, "I am sincerely insincere."  "I will argue against whatever you hold hardest, and I will mean every word until you pay me." 

"Good," she said.  "I could not stand it from someone who loved me." 

That is the mercy clients rarely name: a rented conscience can be sent away, so it is safe to let one in. 

The gallery door opened onto a dawn the color of dishwater, and the breeze came through it salted and steady.  I took the gray cloak from my bag and set it on her shoulders. 

"What is this?" 

"The cloak of doubt," I said.  "You think doubt is standing naked in the wind, but it is a garment, and it is warmer than the armor you came up in." 

She snorted, but she pulled it closed. 

Along the gallery rail hung five wind chimes, crusted green, ringing against one another.  Below them sat a sea chest holding thirty more. 

"Emil's," Odalys said.  "Every dusk he chose which ones to hang, and I have not touched them since that night." 

"Then he chose these five," I said, "and a choice is a sentence, if you know the alphabet." 

She did know it, though she had spent eleven years refusing to read.  I pointed, and she translated, grudging as a witness.  The brass tubes meant a northerly gale.  The gull bones meant he was keeping watch alone.  The green bottle necks meant what she had always said they meant, and she said it loudly: he had been drinking. 

"I believe you," I said, because the certainty is often partly right, and a conscience that only contradicts is just another liar.  "What about the fourth?" 

The fourth was a row of iron keys, and her voice thinned.  "Keys meant he had written to the Lighthouse Board, something needing repair." 

"And the fifth?" 

The fifth was not in his alphabet at all.  It was a single curved fragment of silvered glass, turning on a wire, throwing the weak dawn around the gallery in a wobbling coin. 

"I never knew what that one was," she said. 

"Unhook it," I said. 

Her hands stayed inside the cloak.  I did not reach for it myself; a hired conscience may point at the door, but the client must open it.  The breeze rang the keys twice before she stepped forward and worked the wire loose from the rail. 

It was a hollow mirror shard, cupped like a palm, a piece of the lamp's own reflector.  On its tarnished back Emil had scratched a date, three weeks before the wreck, and two words: cracked, reported. 

Odalys turned it over and looked into the hollow, and it showed her what such mirrors show: her own face, small and upside down. 

"He hung it up where anyone could read it," she said. 

"He hung the bottles too," I said.  "A drunk man tended a broken light in a gale, alone, after asking for help that never came."  "I cannot tell you which of those things sank the Calloway, and neither can your report." 

She stood a long while in the wind, and I let her, with the cloak doing the work I could not.  Then she went in to the desk beneath the lamp, struck one line from the last page, and wrote five chimes' worth of truth in its place. 

The sun cleared the reef as she counted out my fee. 

"Do you think he was innocent?" she asked. 

"I think what I am paid to think," I said, "and tonight you paid me to think you could survive not knowing." 

She almost smiled, and reached for the clasp at her throat. 

"Keep it," I said, and that was the one thing I told her for free. 

When I looked up from the shingle, she was at the rail in gray, hooking the shard back where anyone could read it.
