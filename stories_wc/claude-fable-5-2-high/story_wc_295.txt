At midnight the dome of Kesri Observatory opened like an eyelid, and Ilse Varga climbed toward it carrying her dead sister's telegram.  A daylily lay pressed inside the fold, its orange gone to rust, staining the words Dora had wired from the temple at Sahr: CANNOT CARRY THIS ALONE STOP COME WITNESS.  Fever had taken Dora before Ilse's ship reached port.  What remained were crates of rubbings, wall after wall of carved lilies in rows, and a guilt that no one could read either. 

Ilse was a lucid dream chronicler by trade.  For a sleep clinic in Vienna she entered her dreams awake, walked them deliberately, and wrote down what she found, and patients who heard her chronicles read aloud tended to mend.  The doctors said she healed with words, the way some nurses heal with hands.  She had never managed it on herself. 

So each night since the funeral she had dreamed herself into Dora's temple, rebuilt from the rubbings, and stood before the lilies trying to finish her sister's work.  Some were carved shut, some half open, some flaring wide, and they followed no grammar Dora had known.  Then, three weeks ago, the dream itself had answered: the stone lilies began to glow and fade in slow, patient rhythm, like lamps breathing.  She woke knowing only that she needed someone who studied lights that breathe. 

Dr. Anselm Pryce was that someone.  Kesri existed to map the whole southern sky onto glass plates, and Pryce kept its catalogue of variable stars, the ones whose brightness swells and sinks on schedules of their own.  He also kept every night watch himself, because since his wife's death the other half of the logbook had stayed empty and he could not bear to see a stranger's hand in it.  He had not slept properly in two years. 

Tonight he was at the eyepiece when she came in, a thin man in a greatcoat, breath smoking in the cold.  "Your lilies," he said, not unkindly.  "Show me the long wall again." 

She unrolled the rubbing across the plate table and set the telegram beside it, daylily uppermost, because she had begun to wonder why Dora had sent a flower that opens and closes within a single day.  "Not letters," Ilse said.  "Read each lily as a night, and each opening as a brightness." 

Pryce bent over the paper.  His finger moved along the row, shut, wider, wide, then narrowing by small degrees, and stopped.  "Quick to open, slow to close, and the pattern returns every ten carvings," he murmured.  "That is the light curve of Beta Doradus." 

Row by row, through the variable stars in his own catalogue, the wall gave itself up: each line of lilies was one star's pulse, watched for generations by priests without a single lens.  And under every night's lily sat two small marks, cut by two different chisels.  "One watches, one records," Pryce said, and his voice caught, because his own logbook was ruled the same way. 

That was when understanding dawned, hours before the sun did.  The one glyph Dora had translated, the word she rendered as burden, stood above the paired marks on every wall.  It did not mean a weight to be suffered; it meant the vigil, the load of the night, divided between two so that neither watcher broke.  Dora's telegram had never been an accusation.  She had been quoting the temple, offering Ilse the second chisel. 

Ilse wept then, quietly, and it felt less like breaking than like setting something down.  Pryce did not look away.  Instead he swung the telescope to Beta Doradus and said, "A reading wants two witnesses; will you take the book?" 

So she sat at the empty half of the page, and he called the star's brightness down to her, and she wrote it in the column that had waited two years for a hand.  Between readings she did the only other thing she knew how to do.  She opened her chronicle and read him the temple as she had dreamed it, the cool stone, the lilies breathing, two figures passing a lamp back and forth until morning. 

Her words did what they always did for other people.  Pryce's shoulders came down from around his ears, and near four o'clock he fell asleep in the observing chair while she kept the count for both of them.  This time, she noticed, they had worked on her too. 

She folded the daylily back into the telegram and, beneath the open dome, wrote her answer to Dora on a clean chronicle page: WITNESSED STOP CARRIED TOGETHER.  Overhead the star went on brightening, exactly as the lilies promised.
