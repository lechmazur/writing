Odell Finch woke before the gulls, in the feral loft beneath the dunes, and listened for the wind to turn.  The house below him had drowned in sand thirty years ago, parlor, kitchen, his grandmother's bed, until only this attic still breathed.  Marram grass grew through the floorboards, a vixen raised kits in the old linen chest, and the chimney stood just clear of the dune like a pipe left in a sleeping mouth.  He had left this house at sixteen, laughing at the old woman's shore-talk and her songs, sure the world spoke something better.  The world had made him a vagrant penny poet instead, a man who sold four rhymed lines for a copper on any quay that would have him.  When he finally drifted home, there was no grandmother to forgive him, only sand, and a roof hatch the dune had not yet found. 

So he stayed, and he gleaned.  People throw away their best sounds, he had learned, and his grandmother's preserving jars, still racked along the rafters, could keep them.  Capturing sounds in glass jars was simple work: hold the open mouth toward a sound, lid it in a beat of true quiet, and the sound stayed until someone let it out, once.  The trick was to sweeten the glass first with a thumb of dune honey, because a sound shut in a sour jar came out as a quarrel. 

The town above had quarrels enough.  The old ones on the net benches still spoke shore-talk, all tide-words and sung instructions, and the children home from the mainland school answered in quick flat slang, and each side heard only noise.  Odell, who had been fluent in both kinds of deafness, wanted one thing now: to become the translator between generations, the bridge he had once burned from the middle.  He carried jarred lullabies to schoolyard walls and jarred skipping rhymes to the benches, and mostly people paid him his penny to go away. 

Then the vixen, digging, turned up a forgotten music box from the bottom of the linen chest.  He knew its walnut lid at once; his grandmother had wound it every night of his childhood, and he had not thought of it in forty years.  Damp had swollen the works, and the cylinder turned only in the dry cold just before sunrise, and then barely, a few faint bars.  Those bars were enough to stop his heart, because he had heard them yesterday on the quay.  Wren Tolley, nine years old, skipped rope to that very tune, quickened, with mainland words, while her grandmother, Hesper, sat ten feet away mending net and scowling at the racket. 

So this morning he sat cross-legged in the grass of the loft with a honeyed jar in one hand and the box in the other, waiting.  At dawn's earliest whistle, the thin note the chimney gave when the land breeze turned seaward, the sand stopped hissing on the roof and the whole buried house held its breath.  He let the key go.  The box gave up its tune slowly, each note separate as a dropped bead, and behind it, he would have sworn, came the creak of a rocking chair.  He lidded the jar on the last note, and the spring, spent, did not catch again.  Light came down the hatch, gilding every blade of grass and every empty jar, and for a moment the ruin was a cathedral with a congregation of one. 

On the quay he found Wren already skipping and Hesper already scowling.  He caught one round of the girl's rhyme in a second jar, lidding it in the hush between two waves, then set both jars on the bench between them and asked a penny for a poem he had not written.  Hesper opened the first jar to be rid of him, and the slow beads of the music box fell out into the morning.  Her needle stopped; the rope stopped. 

"That's my song," Wren said, and Hesper answered in shore-talk that it had been hers first, and her mother's before.  Odell translated, then opened the second jar so the old woman could hear her lullaby running barefoot inside the racket.  He stood between them carrying tide-word into slang and slang into tide-word, until Hesper began to sing the old verses and Wren came in underneath, and he was no longer needed. 

That was the redemption, such as it was: not forgiveness from the dead, but a gleaning, a few bright grains picked from a field he had once trampled.  Hesper pressed a penny into his palm, and he walked back toward the dunes positively wistful, missing his grandmother with his whole chest and glad, for once, to have something to tell her.
