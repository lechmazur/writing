Nine nights after the fire meant to kill her, Sael Orrin sat in the quiet canyon under stars and listened to her own breathing.  It came in short pulls now, each one followed by a pause she could not shorten.  For a glass artist, that was a kind of death the Guild torchman had managed after all. 

Her work had been known across the river towns as luminous: vessels blown thin around a pale canyon mineral that drank daylight and gave it back all night.  Such glass needed one long, unbroken breath, and a furnace fed with better charcoal than the Guild sold.  So she had bought from Tobe Farrow, who burned oak in hidden pits and carried it down by mule, untaxed.  The Guild had answered with oil and a torch while the two of them were stacking sacks in her studio.  Tobe had dragged her out through the smoke, and before his own burns took him he had pressed something into her fist. 

She unrolled it now by starlight: a strip torn from a charcoal smuggler's ledger, brown at the edges, brittle as a moth wing.  It was the only will he had left, a scorched testament in his cramped hand.  Each line named a pit and a load, except that every third line was blank.  Tobe had wasted nothing, not ink, not oak, not words, so the blanks troubled her.  If a careful man had kept his pauses on purpose, perhaps there was a purpose she could find in her own.  That was why she had come, and why she waited, wrapped in a singed blanket, for dawn. 

He had told her once how to find a pit nobody was meant to find.  Buried oak smolders for weeks, he said, and the ground above it will not hold frost. 

The stars thinned, the cold settled, and the canyon floor slowly whitened.  When gray light came, she read the patterns in the morning frost the way she used to read heat in a gather of glass.  Fifty paces upstream a dark oval lay bare in the white, and beyond it another, and between them ran a faint seam where mule hooves had packed the soil and the frost feathered differently. 

She began to retrace his route, strip in hand, stopping whenever her lungs demanded it.  First pit, first line; second pit, second line.  Then the seam bent toward the canyon wall and paused at a flat stone where the frost lay whole and nothing smoldered.  The blank line. 

On the stone sat an empty sack, folded square, weighted with a pebble, and under the pebble a child's charcoal drawing of a mule.  Sael sat down beside it and let the pause in her chest run its length.  Above the stone, a goat track climbed to a herder's hut with a cold chimney. 

She followed the strip to the next blank and found another stone, another folded sack, another hut.  Every third load, Tobe had stopped where no coin was owed and left a sack of warmth for people who could not pay, and he had written nothing because a gift recorded is a debt.  The pauses were not gaps in his work.  They were where the work turned outward. 

The sacks had been empty for nine days, and the frost on those chimneys said the huts were already cold.  Sael could not carry oak, and she could no longer blow a vessel in one breath, but she had always been steadily adaptive, a maker who changed the design when the glass refused her.  That same morning she opened the nearest pit, filled both sacks in small loads, and rested on each stone exactly as long as her lungs asked. 

Through the following weeks she learned his pits, built a small furnace beside them, and taught herself to blow in short breaths.  The glass came out as small globes, each no bigger than a plum, each shaped in the space between one pause and the next.  She set one on every flat stone beside the filled sack, so that a herder coming down after dark could find the gift by its glow.  She kept the scorched strip folded in her apron and started a ledger of her own, leaving every third line blank. 

On the first clear night of spring she sat again on the canyon floor and looked up the dark length of it.  Small lights hung along the wall at even distances, pale as the stars above them, each one marking a place where a man had stopped.  She breathed in, waited through the pause, and found that it no longer felt empty.  It felt like the canyon holding still to listen.
