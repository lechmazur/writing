Odile Vane repaired broken clocks because a stopped clock was the only kind of time that held still for planning.  She kept lists for everything, and a list had promised she would visit her grandmother, the Admiral, on Thursday.  The house burned on Wednesday.  The Admiral got out, lived three more weeks, and spent them laughing at Odile's revised schedules. 

"A plan is a rumor you tell the weather," she said.  "I sailed forty years, and the last ten without my compass rose." 

Everyone in the harbor knew that story: the engraved brass card of the Admiral's compass, lost somewhere, and the old woman steering home anyway.  Odile had never understood how anyone could live like that.  After the funeral, she wanted to.  Her lists had not saved one Wednesday, so she no longer hoped to defeat uncertainty; she hoped, awkwardly, to befriend it. 

Only the greenhouse survived the fire, so she moved her workbench there, among cracked panes and dead vines that nobody had tended in years.  On Monday, at 4:17 in the afternoon, every clock on the bench hiccupped and stopped.  Coincidence, she wrote in her ledger.  On Tuesday, at 4:17, they stopped again, and the fallen leaves on the floor lifted and turned in a slow circle.  Coincidence, she wrote, with a question mark.  On Wednesday she set down her tools at 4:16 and waited.  The floor shivered, the escapements stuttered, and the leaves rose.  She crossed out the word and wrote pattern. 

A pattern was something she could follow, so she walked through the swirling leaves, watching where they went.  They spiraled toward the old boiler at the back and vanished into its ash pit like water down a drain.  Odile knelt and dug with both hands.  Beneath the ashes lay an iron hatch, its ring worn smooth by some patient hand, breathing cold air that smelled of wet stone. 

She made a plan, naturally: go down Thursday at three, with lantern and chalk, and be back up by four.  The stair wound down through rock toward a roar that grew until it filled her chest.  At the bottom, a river fell out of the darkness into a black pool, a waterfall no sun had ever touched.  On a ledge beside it, in a niche cut for the purpose, lay a brass disc engraved with thirty-two points.  The Admiral's compass rose had never been lost.  It had been left here deliberately, without a needle, and one point, north-northeast, was circled with a deep scratch.  On the back, her grandmother's blunt hand had cut six words: know one bearing, guess the rest. 

She was still turning those words over when the roar changed pitch, and she glanced at her watch.  It said 3:40, and it had been saying 3:40 for some time, because the damp had stopped it.  The falls doubled as some hidden basin upstream filled and tipped, exactly as it did every afternoon.  The pool heaved, the cavern shook, and black water rose over the mouth of the stair and stayed there.  Her plan was underwater, and her lantern held perhaps an hour of oil.  Three tunnels opened off the ledge, and no list in her pocket said which one. 

So Odile, who had never improvised anything, improvised.  She took a steel minute hand from her apron, stroked it forty times against her magnetized screwdriver, and laid it on a dead leaf that the draft had carried down.  She floated the leaf in a spray-filled hollow of the ledge and held the compass rose beside it.  The clock hand wavered, drifted, and settled.  It showed her a line, not a direction, since a makeshift needle does not say which end is north.  Turned one way, the scratched point of the rose fell on the left tunnel; turned the other, on the right.  Two tunnels instead of three; the rest, as the brass promised, was a guess. 

She watched her lantern flame, which leaned faintly left, the way the leaves had leaned toward the ash pit.  One leaning flame was only a coincidence, not yet a pattern, and for once she did not wait for the third occurrence.  She pocketed the rose and went left, uncertainly certain, sure of one bearing and nothing else, which she suspected was exactly how the Admiral had come home for ten years. 

The tunnel climbed, narrowed, frightened her twice, and let her out among gorse on the harbor cliff with a thumbnail of oil left in the lantern.  That evening she opened her ledger in the greenhouse and wrote tomorrow's list.  She left the last line blank, set the compass rose on the page, and let the clocks stop when they liked.
