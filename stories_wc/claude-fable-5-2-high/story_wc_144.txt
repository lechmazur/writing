Mist lay over the Calder Quay rail depot like a held breath, beading on dead rails and the windows of the old signal cabin.  Inside, Ilse Marrow oversaw her repair shop the way she had for eleven sightless years, by listening.  She knew which apprentice was filing too hard, which kettle wanted descaling, which clock on the shelf had begun to grieve.  Beyond the last siding, an anchor chain started to climb, link after slow link.  The Meridian Wren was leaving on her maiden voyage, and the whole harbor had gone quiet to hear it. 

The door banged, and Tobin, the pilot's runner, came in breathing like a bellows.  "It is the guide bird," he said, and set something small and shrieking on her bench.  Ilse closed her hands around an artificial hummingbird drone, brass ribs and film wings, no heavier than a plum.  It was built to read the harbor lamps through fog and hum a course the helmsman could follow out past the shoals.  Now it only howled feedback, a raw, climbing whine. 

"It struck a crane cable," Tobin said.  "The engineers say the brain is scrambled, and the anchor is already coming up." 

"Then we have exactly as long as the chain," Ilse said, and her voice slowed the room the way a magnet steadies a needle.  Her thumb found the trouble at once: a hairline crack across the prism eye, and a torn wing behind it.  "Poor thing," she murmured, as she did to everything that came to her broken.  "Senna, patch the wing with gut film, two coats, thin."  She listened to her apprentice's brush until the strokes sounded even, then nodded. 

The eye was another matter, because a new prism would take a week to grind.  But Ilse did not believe in scrambled brains.  When her own sight went, the world had screamed at her too, every echo and footfall a complaint, until she learned that noise is only direction nobody has read yet. 

"Carry the lamp and follow me," she told Tobin, and walked out into the depot without touching a wall.  The old engine yard was full of water, and she had mapped every drop of it.  There was the glass gauge column of the water tower, the brimming rain trough between the rails, and the standpipe that had dripped one bright thread for forty years.  She used them to test lenses, since each one bent light by an amount she knew by heart.  Usually Senna read the angles aloud; today the bird would have to sing them. 

"Shine the lamp through the gauge glass, into its eye," she said, holding the drone up in the wet air.  The whine dropped a third.  Through the trough's shallow sheet it fell lower still, and through the falling drip it fluttered, two notes beating against each other.  Ilse stood very still in the mist, and listened the way other people read. 

"It is not scrambled," she said.  "The crack splits every beam in two, so it sees two lamps where there is one and tells us so, as loudly as it can.  The howl is honest; it is the distance between them." 

Out on the water, the chain's rhythm quickened, and a mate began calling fathoms.  Tobin's lamp shook, but Ilse's hands did not.  She took a stick of clear resin from her apron, chosen because it bends light almost exactly as glass does, and laid one bead into the crack.  "Again, through the drip," she said.  The two notes leaned toward each other.  She drew the resin thinner with a warmed needle, a hair at a time, asking for the lamp at the trough, the glass, the drip, steering the patch by pitch alone.  At the seventh pass the beating stopped, and the howl folded into one low hum as the bird turned in her palm to face the lamp. 

She tested the patched wing against her lip, found it dry, and opened her hands.  "Go on," she told it, "you were never lost, only unheard."  The hummingbird lifted off her palm and went over the sidings toward the quay, its hum thinning into the mist. 

Tobin ran after it, then stopped and turned back.  "What do we owe you?" he called. 

"Tell the helmsman to trust the note," Ilse said. 

She stayed in the yard with the drip ticking beside her, her face turned to the harbor.  The chain gave its last clank as the anchor broke the surface, streaming, and the Meridian Wren's horn sounded once.  Under it, small and steady, ran one low hum, drawing the new ship out through the white toward open water.  Ilse smiled, wiped the resin from her needle, and went back in to see about the grieving clock.
