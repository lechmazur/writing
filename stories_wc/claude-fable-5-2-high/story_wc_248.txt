I burned the Calder Street Lending Library when I was nineteen, and I have never once pretended otherwise.  The council had declared my mother dead after three years missing on Mount Serrat, and a clerk stamped WITHDRAWN across her librarian's card as if she were a water-damaged atlas.  If Odile was withdrawn, I decided, so was everything she had ever lent.  Nobody was hurt, but forty thousand books went up, and I walked out of town before the roof fell.  I took one thing from the flames: the faded tapestry fragment that had hung above her lending desk, a souvenir of her first expedition.  Then I went off the grid, into an abandoned fire lookout on Serrat's northern flank, with no phone, no name, and no mail.  An arsonist keeping watch for smoke; the mountain and I both appreciated the joke. 

Silence did what noise never could.  My mother used to call me analytically intuitive, meaning the answer arrives in my chest first and the proof limps in afterward.  In my second winter of staring at that fragment by lamplight, my chest informed me that its knots were not decoration.  It took until spring to prove it: the raised threads matched Serrat's ridgelines, and one red knot sat where no map showed anything at all. 

I climbed to the red knot and found a lava tube breathing warm air like a sleeping animal.  Inside lay what she had spent her life hunting: the archive of the Ashkeepers, a people the mountain had buried and the world had misplaced.  Thousands of niches were cut into the basalt, each holding a clay tablet or a rolled weaving, each with tally marks scratched beneath.  I understood the tallies before I could justify it: borrowed, returned, borrowed, returned.  A lost civilization had kept a lending library, and I, of all people, was its first patron in nine centuries. 

Not the first. 

On the wall beside the entrance, someone had drawn a small girl in charcoal, holding a red crayon like a sword.  My knees gave out, because I knew that girl, and I knew the hand that had drawn her.  Forty paces deeper, the girl appeared again, taller, modeled in grey clay pressed against the rock, reading.  Then a teenager in ochre, scowling magnificently; then a grown woman woven from scavenged thread, her face left blank.  My mother, sealed in by a rockfall that later tremors had cracked open, had spent her last months trying to portray a daughter she would not see grow up.  Every figure's hand pointed onward, and I followed them the way lava follows a slope, without any choice in it. 

The trail ended in the deepest chamber, at a niche holding a small canvas I had believed was ash.  It was her self-portrait, the one I vandalized at six, the night before she first left for Serrat.  I had slashed red crayon across her painted face until the wax tore the canvas weave.  She had not scolded me.  She had laughed, a laugh with a furnace in it, and said, "Now you owe me a face, and I owe you a homecoming."  "When I am back, you will paint yourself in beside me, and we will hang it over the desk, ruined together."  She came back that time, but there was always another expedition, and the promise sank under years until neither of us said it aloud.  I had forgotten it completely.  She had carried it up a volcano. 

I lifted the canvas toward my lamp, and the hunch landed before the reason: the crayon scars were too dark.  She had written along every slash in tiny ink letters, her sentences following my old rage like vines climbing a fence.  "Vesna, I cannot pay my half, so I am lending it to you."  "Paint yourself in, carry us home, and open these shelves to everyone; no due date, renew me as often as you need." 

I made a sound the mountain must have recognized, something long held under pressure finally finding a vent.  She never knew I would burn her library, yet she had already handed me the means to rebuild it.  I ground her leftover charcoal with ochre and spit, and beside her torn face I painted my own, scowling magnificently.  Then I wrapped the portrait in the tapestry fragment, scratched one tally mark beneath the empty niche, and started down toward Calder Street to confess, and to build.  Borrowed, the mark said.  I intend to spend my life on the word that comes next.
