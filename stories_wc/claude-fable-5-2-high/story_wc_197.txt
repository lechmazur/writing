The plan came apart at four o'clock, when the courier's name vanished from the ferry manifest.  Nell Varga noticed because noticing was her whole job, though the Office of Record believed her job was mopping.  For eleven years she had pushed a bucket past clerks who revised the city's histories every spring, trimming a strike here, a flood there, a few hundred names.  No one held a gun to them.  The old editions simply went to pulp because nobody asked for them, and that shrug, Nell had decided, was the real tyrant.  Somebody had to hold the line against it, even if the line was one woman with a mop. 

The plan had been simple and split in two for safety.  Tobias the ferryman knew which drowned phone booth held the last unrevised directory of the Low Quarter and would carry it to a mainland printer; Nell held the key that opened its coin vault.  The key had come from her aunt, a switchboard operator for the Meridian Telephone Company, which died the same year the Quarter flooded.  The official history now said nobody had lived there.  The directory said four hundred households had, with numbers you could have called. 

Without Tobias, she had brass in her pocket and a lagoon full of identical booths.  Nell did not panic; panic wasted things, and she wasted nothing.  She finished her shift, because a janitor who leaves early is remembered, and carried her bucket down to the quay as the rain stopped.  She turned the key over under the first lamp: MERIDIAN TELEPHONE, and beneath it, stamped small, LINE 14. 

Dusk came on the color of weak tea.  That was when she saw what eleven years of mopping had taught her to see: water never lies about a floor.  The quay stones had settled over Meridian's old cable trenches, and the rain had pooled in them in long thin puddles fanning out from the bricked-up exchange door.  By day they were only wet; now, catching the last light and the lamps, they shone like wires on a switchboard, a map nobody had thought to revise.  She counted from the door.  The fourteenth bright line ran to the quay's edge and pointed, straight as a ruled margin, across the water.  Nell laid her mop along it, sighted down the handle, and fixed the bearing on a chimney on the far shore.  Then she waited for the tide, because the booths reappeared only at the ebb. 

By the time the stars were out she was poling the Office's gutter punt, signed out weeks ago under the heading of drain inspection, onto the Auric Lagoon.  The drowned foundry's brass silt had gilded the water for forty years, and under starlight it lay like a sheet of dim gold leaf, pricked with reflected constellations.  One by one the booth roofs rose out of it as the tide drew down, dozens of them, crusted and alike.  She kept the chimney dead ahead and the exchange door dead behind, and the line delivered her to a booth leaning beside a sunken lamppost. 

The lock was green with corrosion.  She worked floor soap into the keyhole with a hairpin, counted slowly to one hundred, and turned the key.  The coin vault opened with a cough of gold water.  Inside, sealed in a wax-dipped cash tin, lay the directory, dry as the day her aunt locked it away.  Nell read the first page by starlight: Abel, Adair, Akintola, each with an address and five digits. 

Nobody would march for these names, and nobody would bother to burn them; the city would simply never ask.  So, with no courier left, the book would have to travel in code. 

At seven the next morning Nell reappeared in the Office of Record with her bucket, exactly as invisible as before.  She shelved the directory in the open stacks under a label no clerk would ever lift: Drain Inspection, Line 14.  Indifference, turned around, made an excellent lock.  She penciled Tobias's name back onto the ferry manifest, too.  Then, night after night, she penciled Line 14 and five digits inside the back cover of each revised history, a different household's number in every copy.  It looked like a binder's mark.  It was a ring on a dead line, waiting for someone curious enough to pick up. 

In March a schoolgirl came to the desk with a history book open to its back cover and asked the clerk what Line 14 meant.  The clerk shrugged.  The janitor, wringing her mop nearby, said she believed it was in the stacks, third aisle, bottom shelf.  An hour later Nell heard a young voice reading names aloud, and four hundred households began, one number at a time, to reappear.  She went on mopping, holding the line.
