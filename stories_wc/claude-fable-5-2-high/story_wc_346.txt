Ilse Varn climbed the hidden lighthouse at dusk, as she had every evening for thirty years, to adjust the doomsday clock.  No ship had ever seen this tower, because it cast no light; it stood in a fold of sea cliffs and kept only the hour.  Tonight there was one second left on the dial, and nothing left in the world to weigh against it.  Out past the horizon, the sky had begun to smooth itself flat, the way a pond does after the last stone.  The old keepers had a name for that smoothing: time's final ripple, which would roll backward through every year that ever was and quiet it. 

In her coat pocket she carried a broken puppet head.  It had been a wooden fox once, before her daughter Wren dropped it down the cellar stairs and split it from ear to jaw.  After that the hinge clacked on every third note whenever Wren made the fox sing the bedtime lullaby, and she refused to let anyone mend it.  "He taught me the song," she would say, "so he gets to sing it wrong."  Wren had been gone eleven winters, taken by a fever that no adjustment of any clock could have slowed. 

Ilse reached the lamp room, where no lamp had ever burned.  In its place hung the clock, wheels turning inside wheels, and before the clock stood the great lens, ring within ring of ground glass narrowing to one flat disc at the center.  The keepers called that disc a pane of hush.  Sound died against it; a shout thrown at the glass came back as nothing at all.  Yet the first keeper's logbook promised that at the very end the pane would vouchsafe a single passage to a single voice, carrying it out on the ripple to every life the ripple touched.  The price was written plainly: the voice must travel through nested patterns, a song folded inside itself the way the lens and the clockwork were folded, or the hush would swallow it. 

Ilse had prepared for eleven winters.  She wanted one thing from the end of the world: to whisper Wren's lullaby across a thousand lifetimes, so that no child anywhere in time would go into the dark unsung. 

She opened the clock case and set the escapement as she had rehearsed, splitting the last second into halves, the halves into quarters, each beat holding a smaller copy of the whole.  The second opened like a stairwell.  Then she leaned to the pane and whispered the lullaby as a round, verse inside verse, every note exact, the way she had practiced until her throat forgot how to err. 

The glass took none of it.  Her perfect whisper slid off the pane like rain off wax and fell dead at her feet.  She tried again, slower, and again the hush closed over it without a seam.  Outside, the flatness had reached the cliffs; the gulls hung motionless, and the waves below had stopped half curled. 

Ilse sagged against the clock case, and the puppet head knocked in her pocket with its small, wrong, familiar clack.  She drew it out and looked at the crack, and then at the lens, and understood what thirty years of careful adjustment had hidden from her.  A pattern with no break has no place to begin another pattern inside it.  Every ring of the lens met the next at a seam; every wheel of the clock meshed at a gap.  The lullaby had only ever nested at the clack, the stumble where Wren would giggle and start the fox over while the first verse was still going.  It was beautifully flawed, and the flaw was the door. 

Ilse held the fox to the pane of hush, worked the split jaw with her thumb, and whispered the song the way her daughter had sung it, badly, with the clack on every third note and a new verse tumbling out of each one. 

The glass drank it.  She felt the song go out across the hush of time's final ripple, down through the halved and quartered second, ring by ring, lifetime by lifetime, a thousand small rooms each receiving the same cracked tune.  Somewhere far back along the ripple, a small girl would lift a wooden fox to her ear and hear it teaching her a song. 

Ilse laughed once, and it hurt like pressing a bruise, and it was the largest thing she had ever felt.  The hush came through the windows and settled on her shoulders like a blanket being drawn up.  She set the fox on the sill, facing the sea, and moved the hand to midnight.
