Maren Sole mended nets on the wharf at Saltgate and spoke to them in verse, because knots held better when they were told why.  Behind the town rose the Red Warren, a labyrinth carved into the canyon walls four centuries ago by the first settlers, who had cut its corridors as a place to walk their dead toward rest.  Nobody walked it now.  Thirty years back a storm tide had surged up the canyon during a funeral and taken nineteen mourners, Maren's mother among them, and the elders had ruled that the Warren was ruin and would stay ruin.  That ruling became precedent, and precedent became habit: no lamps past the canyon mouth, no names of the lost spoken aloud, no following the sleepers. 

For there were sleepers.  Every night, after the tide pools emptied and the canyon floor lay bare and shining, the bereaved of Saltgate rose from their beds with closed eyes and drifted into the red corridors.  Custom said to turn them gently at the mouth and never ask where their feet meant to go. 

Maren asked. 

She had cause: while dredging a snagged net, she had pulled from the harbor mud a copper storybook illustration plate, engraved in reverse for some printer long dead.  She remembered the book it belonged to, burned with all the others after the flood: The Warren Walk, which her mother had read to her on this very wharf.  The plate showed the labyrinth from above, and at its heart a round chamber open to the sky, crowded with orchids.  But salt had eaten the engraved corridors to green lace; only the heart and a few scattered turnings survived.  The plate alone could not lead her in.  The sleepers, she suspected, could. 

To carve a path from ruin back to reverence, she broke the first precedent quietly, with a needle.  At dusk she went door to door among the bereaved and asked whether they wished to know where their sleep was taking them.  Eleven said yes, and into the hem of each nightshirt she sewed the end of a spool of net twine, every spool dyed a different color. 

That night the pools drained, the sleepers rose, and eleven bright lines unreeled behind them into the dark.  Maren broke the second precedent and carried a lamp past the canyon mouth. 

Inside, the walls glowed like banked coals, and the threads told a strange story.  Each sleeper wandered differently, doubling back, drifting into dead ends where grief kept its private errands.  Yet in certain corridors the colors overlapped, three threads, then six, then all eleven braided along one wall, as if every separate sorrow remembered the same road.  She held the plate to the lamp, reading it mirrored as a printer would, and wherever a turning had survived the salt, it matched the place the threads agreed.  Where the sleepwalking paths overlapped lay the old funeral way. 

She followed the braid, murmuring as she went: "Red stone, hold what the water took; thread, remember what we forgot." 

The last corridor opened into the round chamber, and the sleepers stood there together, eleven faces lifted to a circle of stars.  Around them, rooted in every crack the flood had scoured, grew orchids, pale and violet, thousands of them, flourishing for thirty years with no one awake to see.  On the wall, nineteen names had been scratched by hands that knew the water was rising.  Beneath them, in her mother's slanting letters, ran the last line of the storybook: Walk on; the flowers keep the way. 

Maren wept, and then, being a weaver, she worked.  She broke the third precedent and read the nineteen names aloud, and the sleepers, not waking, breathed easier.  Then she snipped each thread from its hem and walked back out along the shared road, gathering the eleven colors where they ran together and sewing them into a single rope, which she pegged to the wall at the height of a resting hand.  By dawn a handrail of many colors ran from the canyon mouth to the orchids. 

The elders were waiting at the mouth, furious, until she set the copper plate in the eldest's hands and told him his daughter's name was scratched in the heart, with orchids growing around it. 

"You called it ruin so we would not have to look," she said.  "But every night our feet have voted otherwise." 

He stood a long while, thumb tracing the engraved orchids.  Then, awake, in daylight, he took hold of the rope. 

That evening, after the tide pools emptied, Saltgate walked the Warren with lamps lit and eyes open, speaking nineteen names among the flowers.  Afterward, for the first night in thirty years, everyone slept where they lay down, and the orchids kept the way.
