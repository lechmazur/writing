The mist came into the whispering grotto the color of bruised lilac, and Tansy Vell knew the Unlearning had begun.  Every autumn the village filed down the sea stairs to whisper away one thing each had been taught that was no longer true.  The tiled dome, the old whispering gallery, was supposed to carry those words around its curve and out the vent to sea.  This year it gave them back. 

Tansy, who made her living chalking staircases for snails and doors on boulders that children solemnly knocked upon, had come to draw a welcome on the wet floor.  Instead she stood with her chalk bucket while a hundred discarded lessons circled her head like moths.  Never ask for help.  Boys do not cry.  The sea owes us.  The whispers overlapped, frayed, and returned, and the villagers whispered harder to drown them, which only thickened the mess. 

Tansy noticed things about people the way other folk noticed weather.  She saw Odile the baker standing three careful paces from her son Brann, saw old Hesper gripping his cap, saw how nobody's eyes met in the purple damp. 

Something crunched under her sandal.  It was a flake of glazed tile, thumbnail thin, fallen from a pale scar high in the gallery's curve.  When she lifted it to her ear, the muddle narrowed to a single thread: Odile's voice, saying never ask for help, as clear as a spoon on a cup.  She tilted the flake and caught Brann instead: Mother needs no one. 

Tansy's stomach turned over, slowly, like a fish deciding.  The break had not ruined the grotto so much as changed its mind, and she badly wanted to know what it now meant to say. 

Hesper, who led the rite, raised his hands.  "We will seal the vent and shout the old words down," he announced, and his own echo came back mocking him in pieces. 

"Or," Tansy interjected, "we could ask the mess what shape it is." 

People stared at her, which she was used to.  She knelt, crushed her chalks against the stone with the heel of her hand, and mixed the powder with dry sand from the high ledge: lilac, bone, rust, and a blue she had been saving for a whale.  On a flat slab beneath the scar she poured a mandala, ring inside ring, and set the tile flake at its center like a tiny listening ear. 

The flake hummed.  Grain by grain, the sand began to shiver out of her tidy rings and into the whispers' own design.  Rust ridges rose where voices collided; blue pooled where they ran side by side. 

Tansy read it the way she read a room.  The whispers were not scattered at all; they had paired off, each discarded lesson spiraling around the one that answered it.  Odile's and Brann's made a knot of rust with a thin blue seam through the middle.  Hesper's "the sea owes us" circled, alone and enormous, around nearly everyone.  The village had been throwing away halves of conversations it had never dared to finish aloud. 

She did not say so.  Saying so would only add another moth.  Instead she interjected in the mandala's own language, trailing a pinch of blue across the rust knot so the seam widened into a path.  Then she beckoned Odile and Brann over and pointed, first at the knot, then at each of them. 

Odile looked for a long time.  Her lips, which had been moving all morning, stopped. 

"I burned my arm in March," she told her son, in an ordinary voice, "and I hid it from you." 

In the sand, the knot loosened and lay down. 

Others drifted close, found their own ridges, and found whom those ridges leaned toward.  Tansy drew small bridges where she guessed courage was short, and stepped back where it was not.  Nobody called for quiet.  The hush simply arrived, one stilled mouth at a time, the way mist arrives: you cannot say when, only that the far wall is gone. 

It was not a comfortable hush.  It was lilac all the way through, full of people looking at one another and not yet knowing what they would be without their old lessons.  Hesper stood last before the great lonely ring, cap crushed, saying nothing, which for Hesper was a beginning. 

Tansy left the flake where it lay.  The tide would take the mandala by evening, as tides and mandalas agree, and the scar in the dome would go on returning what people tried to throw away.  She found she did not want it mended. 

On her way up the sea stairs she chalked a small door on the wet rock, knocked twice, and waited, uneasy and curious, to hear who would answer.
