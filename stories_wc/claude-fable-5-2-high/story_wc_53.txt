The carousel halted at dusk, and the lagoon above the clouds stopped breathing.  All day its brass horses had hauled the tide in circles, churning the high water so it would not spill off the rim into the weather below.  Now the surface lay in flat panes, cracked along old currents like a dropped mirror that had not yet decided to fall.  Ione waded out with her net and her jars, because the hour of stillness was the only hour worth scavenging. 

She did not hunt for coins or lost shoes.  She hunted for voices.  The lagoon kept what riders said as they leaned from the horses, and when the turning stopped, their words rose in pieces, bubbles of talk that broke against her knees. 

"...the year the rail gave way..." one murmured. 

She caught it in a jar and capped it. 

She needed them because she could no longer put her own life in order.  The water here turned everything, including time, and after enough seasons her memories had come loose like horses off their poles: she remembered her gray hair before her first morning on the rim, and could not swear whether she had arrived last week or forty years ago.  What she carried against this was a small ridged wheel, the winding wheel from a disposable camera, worn smooth on one side by her thumb.  Each time she set a fragment in its proper place, she turned it one click, the way it once advanced a frame.  Twenty-six clicks so far.  One remained. 

The ghost came across the panes without breaking them, as she did every still hour.  She was nineteen, sunburned, and she held the whole camera, yellow cardboard still dry, because she was the Ione who had climbed up here to shoot one roll of the famous moving lagoon, sell the prints, and buy passage to every sky harbor on the map. 

"You said one week," the ghost said. 

"I know what I said." 

"Then what did you do with us?" 

That was the negotiation, every night, and every night Ione had lost it, because she could not answer in order.  Tonight she had the jars.  She uncapped them one at a time and let the strangers speak. 

"...the rail gave way, and the scavenger woman went in after my boy..." 

"...she patched the lead horse with wire and a spoon, or the tide would have quit..." 

"...camera went into the spillway with her; she came up holding only the kid and some little wheel..." 

"...asked her why she stayed, and she said somebody has to hear when the turning sounds wrong..." 

The ghost listened, her face as flat and cracked as the water.  Ione set the fragments in line, first to last, and for the first time the years stood still long enough to be read.  She had not been lazy or afraid.  Her ambition had not been broken; it had been worn down, the way the tide wore the rim, one useful day at a time, until the shape that remained was this one. 

"That is what I did with us," she said. 

"It is not what I wanted," said the ghost. 

"No." 

Ione had come out intending to argue, to prove the trade had been fair.  Instead she looked at the dry yellow camera and let herself regret it, fully, the way she had never dared: twenty-seven pictures never taken, harbors never seen, a girl who had waited on still water for an apology while her older self pretended to be busy with nets.  The regret went through her like cold through a cracked pane, and did not shatter her. 

"I am sorry," she said.  "I spent you.  I would spend you again for that boy, and I am still sorry." 

The ghost considered this for a long time, or perhaps no time; here it was hard to tell. 

"Then those are my terms," she said finally.  "Stop telling me it cost nothing.  And take the last frame." 

She raised the camera and aimed it at the old woman knee-deep among the jars.  Ione turned the wheel its twenty-seventh click, and in the ghost's hands the shutter fell.  There was no flash, only the sense of something finally placed where it belonged. 

When she looked up, the panes were empty, and she knew, in order, how old she was.  Behind her the carousel groaned, the horses leaned into their circle, and the lagoon began again to move.  Ione pocketed the wheel, gathered her jars, and waded home to listen for the turning.
