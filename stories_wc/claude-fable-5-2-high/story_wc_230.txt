The storm came up the ravine like a verdict, and Ines Varga checked the root baskets anyway.  The orchard hung there on cables bolted into both cliffs, forty oaks cradled in mesh above the flood line, their roots bagged in soil where the truffles ripened.  She trusted cables because cables could be measured.  People, since the empathy virus, could not.  The fever had made everyone feel everyone for one unbearable season, and when it burned out it took the brake between thought and tongue with it.  Survivors were involuntarily truthful now, Ines included. 

"Your knot is wrong," she told Tobin, her apprentice, "and I expected it to be."  He flinched, retied it, and said, because he had to, "I dream about leaving you all." 

That was the crew's whole trouble.  Six foragers on swaying catwalks needed to report a frayed line without fear, and instead every report arrived wrapped in some small cruelty nobody had chosen.  Two had already stopped speaking near the cables at all, which Ines calculated would kill someone by winter.  She wanted safety the way she wanted truffles: not forced, but cultivated, by getting the conditions right. 

The smuggler rode down the supply line at dusk, soaked, because nobody staffed checkpoints in such weather and an unasked question could not be answered.  Ines asked. 

"It is stolen from the sealed Surgeons' College in the capital," the woman said, unwrapping an antique glass eye, gray-irised, cold as a pebble.  "The book that recommends it was discredited a century ago, and I am overcharging you."  She laid down torn pages from Hallam's Sympathetic Optics, a textbook of rumored cures: occlude the dominant eye with blown glass, count nine breaths, and the truthful nerve falls quiet. 

"There is no truthful nerve," Ines said.  "I will pay you twelve black truffles, because my crew is desperate and I have run out of better experiments." 

She ran it properly.  For ten days each forager held the relic against a closed eyelid, counted nine breaths, and then spoke while Ines logged the results in her harvest ledger.  The cure failed every trial.  Nobody managed one lie, not even a kind one about the soup. 

But the ledger showed something Hallam never wrote.  Unoccluded, people blurted the first truth, the sharp one on top.  After nine breaths behind the glass, the first truth still came, but the truth beneath it had time to surface and follow. 

Tobin, eye covered, said, "I dream about leaving you all," and then, astonished, "because I think you would not notice." 

Ines took the eye, pressed it to her own lid, and breathed.  "I expected your knot to be wrong," she said, "because I taught you badly, and I am afraid of what that costs." 

She could not have deceived them even to keep the remedy working, so that evening she told the crew everything.  "The glass does nothing.  The waiting does.  The book was wrong about the nerve and accidentally right about the nine breaths."  They kept using the eye regardless, because a habit needs a handle. 

The real test came with the equinox gale.  At midnight the wind sheared an anchor on the ninth cradle, and an oak with thirty years of truffle in its roots began to swing toward the cliff.  The crew ran out onto the catwalks, and the old truths ran out with them. 

"You are too slow," Marit shouted at Tobin.  Tobin froze on the wet planks, and Ines saw the winter death she had calculated arriving early. 

Then Marit, clipped to the rail, clapped a palm over one eye.  Nine breaths were too many for a gale, so she took three.  "You are too slow because your line is fouled, and I should have checked it, and I cannot hold this without you." 

Tobin moved.  He freed the line, called out a frayed splice nobody had dared mention, and six people hauled the oak back into its cradle by lightning. 

In the scramble the glass eye fell from Ines's pocket and struck a bolt.  She found it at dawn, lodged in the mesh, a crack running clean through the gray iris.  A rational person might have thrown it into the ravine.  Ines considered that, counted her breaths, and found the second truth underneath. 

She wired it to the first post of the catwalk, where everyone passed before stepping onto the cables.  "It is stolen, it is broken, and it cures nothing," she told her crew, hand over her eye.  "It reminds us to wait for the rest of what we mean, and I am glad you are all still here." 

Below them the storm water dropped, and the oaks swung gently, holding.
