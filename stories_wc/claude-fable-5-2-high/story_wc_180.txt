Three weeks after her daughter broke the apprenticeship contract, Odile was still arguing with a pear tree about it.  The tree, rooted in a stone gutter sixty feet above the nave of Saint Averil's, declined to take a side.  Nobody had prayed in the cathedral for a century, but the orchard her great-grandmother planted across its rafters had never noticed.  Apples leaned over the choir loft, plums dripped through the broken rose window, and moss carpeted every beam so thickly that Odile walked the heights barefoot. 

The valley called her the quartz-eye mender, because a snapped branch had taken her left eye at nineteen and her mother had ground a replacement from clear quartz.  Through it, strain in living wood showed as bright threads, so she always knew where a limb would split before it did.  It had never shown her anything about Wren. 

Wren had signed for seven years, the way every daughter of the rafters signed, in iron-gall ink before the guild notary.  In the third year she had said that a promise which could not bend was only a cage with nice handwriting, and climbed down the bell rope for good. 

"Seven years is nothing," Odile told the pear, jabbing a finger at its trunk.  "I gave my mother forty, and I only complained daily." 

That night a gale came through the rose window and tore the pear's best limb half off, leaving it hanging by a hinge of bark.  Odile was up the beam before dawn, swearing cheerfully, and found her cord tin empty.  Fetching tarred cord had been Wren's chore, and Odile had been too proud to notice it undone.  The limb would be dead by noon. 

She glared around the rafters for anything that would tie, and her quartz eye snagged on a satchel hanging from a gargoyle's ear, forgotten in Wren's hurry.  Odile upended it on the moss: a stub of beeswax candle, a honey cake gone hard as slate, a notebook, and a green hair ribbon. 

She knew the ribbon.  She had tied it into Wren's braid every morning until the girl turned twelve and announced she could manage her own head. 

"It will have to do," she muttered, "and you are not to read anything into it," which was addressed to the pear. 

She pressed the limb home, wrapped her daughter's hair ribbon around the break, and sealed it with candle wax softened in her fist.  It was a disgraceful mend, and she meant to replace it with proper cord within the week.  Then the plums came ripe all at once, and the week became a summer.  She did persist with the limb, in her fashion, climbing out each evening to scold it, check the wax, and report that Wren had not written. 

In October the leaves let go, drifting down through the dusty light to the pews, and Odile finally saw the mend bare.  The ribbon was gone, rotted to a few green threads that fell away when she breathed on them.  Beneath it the limb had knit smooth and fat, and through the quartz it showed no bright thread at all. 

She turned, frowning, to her mother's old grafts, and for the first time looked at what she had seen for forty years.  Every one wore a pinched waist where tarred cord, which never rots, had bitten into the growing wood, and every waist glowed with strain.  She had always taken that glow for the mark of a serious mend. 

Odile sat down on the beam and opened the notebook she had been refusing to open.  Halfway through, in Wren's cramped hand: Ask Mother about ties that rot off, though she will say no. 

"I would have," Odile admitted to the pear.  "Loudly." 

She wrote to the city that night, and it took her six drafts to stop sounding like a notary.  The contract is broken and may stay broken, the last one said.  Come for one spring, and when the leaves fall you may let the promise fall with them, or make it again if you still mean it.  Call it a deciduous vow.  I have forty years of knife work to give you, and you were right about the ties, which I discovered by being out of cord. 

Wren climbed the bell rope in March with a new satchel and a wary face. 

"One spring," she said. 

"One spring," Odile agreed, and handed her the grafting knife her own mother had handed her. 

Wren, in turn, produced a fistful of cheap ribbons in every color, and they spent the morning binding scions to the old pear, one teaching the cut, the other the knot.  By autumn the ribbons would be gone, and they would both have to decide again.  Odile found she did not mind.
