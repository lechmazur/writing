Eleven days after the old queen died, and thirty before anyone could be crowned, Mara Venn lay down to die on schedule in the whispering gallery of St. Oda's.  The Regency Council wanted a disaster drill before the coronation crowds came, and she was Casualty Fourteen, a professional.  Nobody on the Council knew she had already survived this building once. 

Twenty years earlier the Harrow dam broke, the nave filled like a font, and ninety people climbed to this stone ring under the dome to wait.  Mara was nine.  She remembered the light best: the oculus dropped one bright coin onto the gallery, and its soft edge crept around the ring all day.  The center glared and the shade froze, so they lived in the half-shadow between, shuffling after it with their blankets, a nomadic penumbra with ninety citizens. 

She remembered the wall, too, which carried every whisper to every ear.  No adult could murmur a fear or a number without the children hearing it travel the curve.  So the adults stopped whispering and started walking, pacing slow letters across the black and white tiles, spelling what small ears should not catch.  Mara learned to read feet before she learned long division. 

The verger kept a chessboard in a niche, and when sixteen volunteers went down to wade for help, he gave each one a pawn so the board would know whom to wait for.  None came back.  The board was still there, two small courts facing each other across nothing. 

The official history named the bishop who prayed and the governor who arrived by helicopter; the sixteen were a numeral.  Mara wanted a different office than any the Council could grant: to become the historian of small gestures.  The man who knelt on the cold tiles to tie her shoe before he took his pawn.  The woman who left her gloves folded on the rail, as if for an hour. 

That was why she had become a crisis actor: drills were where she studied hands.  She was benevolently ruthless at it.  She never screamed helpfully or went limp on cue; she gave rookies the quiet, polite dying that real catastrophe produces, and let them fail her here so they would not fail someone later. 

Today's rookie was a medic named Tobin, who bound a black ribbon around her wrist with shaking care.  "Black means deceased," he whispered, and the wall delivered it to everyone. 

Above them, in the lantern of the dome, the Council's camera looked straight down, filming the drill for the state archive.  Mara had read the archive statutes the way other people read scripts.  A sovereign may review and strike any record; during an interregnum there is no sovereign, so every record is sealed as it arrives, whole.  For thirty more days the throne was an empty chair, and an empty chair cannot edit. 

The oculus light had reached the niche.  Its soft edge lay across the chessboard, brightening the squares where no one stood. 

Mara sat up. 

"Ma'am, you're dead," Tobin said. 

"I was counted once already," she said, and began to unbind the ribbon, one loop, then the knot he had tied so carefully.  It would wreck his evaluation, and she was sorry, and she did it anyway. 

The marshal hissed at her from across the dome, and his hiss arrived at her shoulder like a moth.  She did not answer in any way the wall could carry.  She stepped into the half-shadow and walked. 

Down a file of tiles, across, down again: H.  Her feet remembered the alphabet her mother's feet had taught them, each letter wide enough for a lens sixty feet overhead.  HE TIED MY SHOE.  Then the gloves on the rail, then the verger pressing a pawn into each open palm, one sentence per gesture, following the light as it migrated so that every word was written in the penumbra and left behind in the dark. 

The marshal started toward her and stopped, because Tobin had begun reading her feet in a whisper, and the wall was carrying each word around the ring to every clerk and stretcher-bearer.  This time the children were grown, and it was right that they hear. 

When she had no gestures left, Mara laid the unbound ribbon along one empty rank of the board, marking where a front line had stood.  Overhead the camera kept its red eye open, and somewhere a seal closed on the record with no hand to break it.  The half-shadow was already leaving the niche, unhurried, on its way around the ring again.  For one more minute sixteen squares shone, holding no one, keeping everyone's place.
