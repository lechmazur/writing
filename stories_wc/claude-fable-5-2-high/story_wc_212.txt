The gosling broke its shell at midnight, inside Tamsin's coat, against the heat of her ribs.  She had carried that egg for twenty-six days, ever since she found it abandoned on the frozen fountain, and for twenty-six days she had not touched the oak acorn cap in her pocket.  Now she could. 

The atrium of the old conservatory stood icebound around her, its glass dome furred with frost, its dead palms sheathed like candles.  Starlight came down through the ice in slow blue shafts and lay across the seed cabinets she had dragged here, drawer by drawer, when the roof of the farmhouse failed.  Four hundred varieties slept in those drawers: her grandmother's black rye, the speckled beans of three drowned villages, a squash nobody else alive had tasted.  They were dying.  Each winter her germination tests came back thinner, ten sprouts in a hundred, then four, then one pale thread that gave up by morning.  Seeds are sealed futures, and even sealed futures leak. 

Her grandmother Oda had left her two things besides the collection: the cap, and the vortex that slept coiled inside it. 

"Every keeper may reopen it once," Oda had said, pressing the little scaled cup into her palm.  "It turns backward.  Inside its ring, scattered things gather, worn things mend, and whatever is sealed is wound toward the day of its making." 

"And whatever is not sealed?" Tamsin had asked. 

"Pays," said Oda, whose hair had gone white in a single night at thirty.  "Order has to be bought from somewhere.  You will learn the texture of borrowed time, and you will learn who lends it." 

That phrase had lived in Tamsin like a splinter for forty years.  She had sorted seed by lamplight wondering whether borrowed time was slick or coarse, whether it burned, whether a person could feel the seam where it was stitched on.  Wanting to know had become almost larger than wanting to save the rye. 

Then, on the night she finally meant to turn the cap, she had found the egg.  An egg is sealed too.  The vortex would have wound it back past heartbeat, past yolk, into nothing, and the only heat steady enough to keep it alive was her own body, which had to stand at the center of the ring.  So she waited, furiously tranquil, breathing slow while every drawer behind her lost another day.  Her stillness was not peace; it was a storm held in both fists. 

Now the gosling was out, wet and outraged, an open thing in an open world.  She dried it, nested it in her wool hat beside the brazier at the door, well outside the circle of unworn tiles that still marked the reach of Oda's turning.  Every cabinet stood inside it. 

Then she walked to the fountain, set the acorn cap on the ice with its hollow facing the stars, and gave it one turn against the sun. 

The vortex reopened without a sound.  Air began to walk in a slow wheel around her, and everything the wheel touched remembered itself.  Dust lifted from the drawer fronts; a cracked pane knit; the shell fragments inside her coat clicked together into a whole and empty egg.  Inside the drawers she heard a faint dry tightening, like four hundred small fists closing.  This was how it saved them, by decreasing entropy, gathering each seed's scattered strength back into the tight order of harvest day. 

And the disorder had to go somewhere.  It came into her.  She felt it arrive in her knees, her knuckles, the roots of her hair, years settling on her like snow on a branch. 

So this was who lent it.  She had imagined borrowing time for herself; instead the seeds were borrowing hers, and she found, with a wild and widening calm, that she did not want it back. 

And the texture?  She knew it at once, because she had been holding it for forty years.  Borrowed time felt like the acorn cap: rough and overlapping on the outside, where the days rubbed past, and inside smooth as a thumbprint, a hollow made to fit something that was always meant to leave.  You did not spend it; you held it for whatever came next. 

When the rye in the top drawer rattled like new hail, she turned the cap sunwise and the wheel of air lay down.  Her braid hung white over her shoulder, and her shaking hands were perfectly at ease. 

By the door the gosling was shrieking for her, the only mother it had seen.  Tamsin tucked it under her chin, pocketed the cap for the keeper after her, and stood beneath the mended dome, old and unhurried, while starlight fell on four hundred living futures.
