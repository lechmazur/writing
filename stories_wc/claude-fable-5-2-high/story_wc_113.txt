The river had come into the Aurelia Opera House three weeks ago, in the middle of a rehearsal, and it had not left.  Ines Varga waded down the pit stairs with her viola case held over her head, and the lanterns the salvage crew had hung from the rail flickered across black water.  She remembered the last second before the flood: the conductor's baton lifted, eighty lungs filled, a chord gathered and never struck.  The building had been holding that breath ever since. 

For twenty years she had played the middle line, the notes between melody and bass that nobody hummed on the way home, and she had played them the way a clerk stamps forms.  Lately that had changed.  Lately she slept badly, scribbled staves on receipts, and came back every night to a ruined pit because of a piece of paper. 

The flood had loosened a panel under the lip of the stage, in the dark gap between the pit ceiling and the boards the singers walked on, and an antique opera program had floated out.  It was dated 1903, printed on heavy cream stock, and in its list of orchestra players one name under Violas had been scratched out with a knife.  She had set it on her music stand to dry.  It never dried. 

Each night new stains had bloomed on it, brown and exact, and they never touched the print.  They grew only in the empty places: the margins, the gutter between pages, the white band between the cast of Act One and the cast of Act Two where a single word, Intermission, sat alone.  The first stain had been a doorway.  The second was the scroll of a viola, and the third was five level lines with dark ovals hung on them like drops on a wire.  Someone was writing to her in water, and Ines, who had not wanted anything in years, wanted badly to read it. 

Tonight was her last chance, because the pumps were coming at dawn and a dry house would have no ink.  She climbed onto her chair, which stood on a crate above the waterline, and held the program up to the nearest lantern.  Through the lit page she saw the pit wall, where the flood, sinking in stages, had left five tide marks.  The stains in the margins settled onto those lines and became notes.  It was alto clef, her clef, the one nobody else in the building could read without counting. 

She took out the viola and played the phrase very softly, seven notes that climbed and stopped on a question.  The lantern flames leaned, and in the gutter of the program a fresh stain spread, four more notes.  She played those, then added two of her own, the way one finishes a friend's sentence.  The paper answered.  So they went on, bow and water, for an hour, neither of them needing a word, until the melody lay whole across every blank space on the page. 

It was an interlude, music written for the interval, for the minutes when the audience drinks and the pit sits forgotten.  Some violist had written it in 1903, had lost a place and a name for the presumption, and had waited ever since in the gap under the stage. 

One stain remained that she could not play.  It sat beside the word Intermission, and it was the shape of a raised baton.  Ines looked at the empty podium and understood the rest of the code.  The whole company had treated the hush as sacred, tiptoeing through the wreck as if the unstruck chord were a grave.  The ghost was asking her to disrupt it, and to do it from the middle. 

She found the inner note of that abandoned chord, the F sharp she had been about to play three weeks ago, and she struck it hard.  Then she let it fall straight into the interlude, loud, alone, the middle voice carrying everything.  The water shivered in rings, the lanterns swung, and somewhere under the stage a second viola seemed to move a third below her, though it may have been the hollow boards. 

When the last note died, the silence that followed was a different one, a rest instead of a held breath.  On the program, over the knife scar in the list of violas, a final stain had dried at last into the small bent shape of a figure taking a bow. 

The pumps came at dawn.  Four months later the Aurelia reopened, and at the first intermission, while the audience drifted toward the bar, Ines stayed in her chair, opened the old program on her stand, and played into the gap.  A few people stopped on the stairs to listen.
