The first petal let go of its bloom as Oriel reached the edge of the Old Terrace, and she understood that she had exactly one petalfall to cross the garden.  The nebula garden hung in the dust of a dead star, terrace under terrace of gas-blooms rooted in nothing, dark as closed eyes.  At the Far Gate her daughter was budding, and no child of their line could open its eyes without light to open them toward. 

Every mother before Oriel had solved this the same way.  She set a terrace on fire.  The blooms burned beautifully and completely, the child woke in their glare, and the garden was one terrace smaller forever.  The first mother had even built a calculating machine to count how many burnings remained, then, unable to bear its answer, taken it apart.  One gear survived, passed down on a cord of braided root.  Oriel wore it now against the translucent hollow of her throat. 

She was the sort of creature who kept things.  She had names for fallen petals, she slept beside the ash of her grandmother's terrace, and she could not look at the bed where her own mother's blooms still hung without all six of her hands going still.  That bed was the one she was supposed to burn. 

The gear carried the pattern too.  Each mother, in the grief after her burning, had filed every tooth in two, a mourning mark, so the count doubled with each generation.  By now the teeth were finer than frost, a count that approximated infinity, and the rim looked almost smooth.  A wheel with infinite teeth is only a circle, her mother used to say, and this one was nearly there.  Nearly was the word Oriel loved, because nearly never arrived. 

She had brought no ember to the terrace edge.  Seasons ago, stroking the rim for comfort, she had heard the gear sing one thin, high note, and had watched the nearest bloom blush with cold light where the sound touched it.  Light without fire: she had wanted nothing else since.  She wanted to share light with her daughter the way one shares a song, with nothing used up. 

So she drew one claw along the rim, and the gear pinged.  The note went out into the dark and came back shaped, the way it does for any blind thing that listens: terrace wall here, root-bridge there, open drop below.  And along the path of the echo, bloom after bloom woke blue. 

Oriel began her transit.  She crossed her mother's bed without burning it, pinging, stepping where the echoes said the ground was, six hands trailing through cold radiance.  It was the calmest she had ever felt. 

Then she noticed the sound beneath her own sound.  It was a long, soft exhalation, like a sleeper turning over, and it came from behind her.  Every bloom she had lit was letting its petals go.  They were falling anyway, it was the season for it, and she could not tell which petals were the season's and which were hers.  They drifted down glowing, unburnt, whole, and made that quiet sigh as they went. 

A sentimentalist should have stopped.  Oriel named a few of them under her breath and kept walking, because her daughter was nearly awake, and because a fallen petal can root, and ash cannot.  She told herself that was a real difference.  She mostly believed it. 

At the Far Gate the bud hung sealed, the size of her smallest fist.  Oriel pinged once more, and the last terrace bloomed blue around them, and her daughter's eyes opened toward a light that consumed nothing she could see. 

The child reached for the gear at once, as every child of the line had.  Oriel put it in her hands and showed her the stroke.  The small claw found the rim; the note rang out, and the bloom above them brightened and let one petal go. 

Oriel saw then what she had made: not the end of the pattern, only a finer tooth on it.  Each mother would take a little less than the mother before, halving the harm and halving it again, and the garden would last nearly forever.  Nearly, she remembered, never arrives. 

Her daughter laughed at the blue light, delighted with her inheritance.  Oriel folded all six hands, entirely at peace, and watched the final petal settle between them like a breath let out.
