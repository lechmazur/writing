Ume found the biscuit tin at the temple rummage sale, in a box of chipped cups, on the one day each month she walked out of the bamboo to sell her thread.  Inside lay jigsaw pieces, every one an edge, and her hands knew them before her eyes did.  Her father had cut the puzzle from plywood sixty years ago: the family orchard, painted plum by plum, fenced on all four sides by bamboo.  When her sister Lin sailed for the far side of the ocean, she took the middle and left Ume the border.  "You keep the fence standing," Lin had said, "and I will bring the orchard home."  Then came the quarrel over selling the land, conducted by letter, and then no letters at all.  The edges vanished the year creditors emptied the farmhouse, and Ume moved into the packing shed with her wheel.  Forty years went by like that, through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry, each season pulling one more thread from the picture of two girls under a plum tree.  The bamboo closed over the cart road, and the valley forgot there had ever been an orchard inside it.  Ume did not forget; she spun, wistful and exact, for other people's looms and wove nothing of her own. 

She paid for the tin with the day's thread money and carried it home beside a letter from a niece she had never met.  The niece wrote that Lin no longer read, no longer answered to her name, and that words slid off her like rain off a leaf.  Only her hands were still awake, sorting buttons, fitting lids to jars, searching for something that matched.  An ocean was distance enough; a mind going dark behind it was distance no apology could cross.  But hands could read an edge. 

That night Ume laid the border out on the shed floor, and it closed without a gap, a wooden frame of painted bamboo around nothing.  She studied the inner knobs and hollows where the orchard should lock in, and saw pegs.  A frame with pegs was a loom. 

She glued the joints with rice paste, but good thread was another matter, for her market yarn was coarse hemp, fit for sacks.  So she opened the cedar chest and took out her mother's wedding sash, plum silk, the last fine thing the creditors had missed.  She picked it apart row by row, and did not let herself count what was left.  The shed filled with a raveling silence, the kind that comes loose as you pull, and for the first time in decades she heard herself humming.  At the wheel she twisted the old silk new, wound it around the puzzle's knobs, and let her fingers recommence what they had refused for forty years. 

She wove no picture, because Lin's eyes might not keep one.  She wove ridges instead: rows of trees a thumb could walk, and one raised knot where the oldest plum stood.  The knot wanted a heart, so at dawn she went out among the gray trunks.  The oldest plum still wept where two girls had once carved their names, and in the scar sat a bead of hardened sap, amber and thumbnail-small.  It had borne forty winters of frost and sat there yet, stoically sparkling.  Lin used to chew such beads like candy and laugh at the bitterness.  Ume pried it free, pierced it with a hot needle, and bound it into the knot. 

She packed the frame in straw and addressed it to the niece.  She began a note, wrote her own name, then burned the page.  A name would only be one more word to slide off; let the gift arrive from the orchard itself.  The postage took her winter rice money, and the shed looked very bare without the tin. 

Spring came before the answer did.  The niece wrote that her mother had held the frame a whole afternoon, thumb traveling the ridges until it found the bead.  Then Lin had gone to her sewing box, taken out a piece of painted plywood, and pressed it into the niece's palm, laughing, with one word: "Bitter."  The piece lay in the envelope: a single plum branch from the middle of the orchard. 

Ume had no border left to fit it into, and found she did not mind.  She carried it out to the oldest tree and set it in the scar where the bead had been.  Then she went back to her wheel, for a conversation had begun again, one piece at a time, and she meant to have thread ready.
