The fog had set at dusk, hardening from breath to pale stone across the cloud sea, and the thunder beneath it was already dulled to a pulse.  Ilse Varrow, summer-quill archivist of the observatory, stood at the forecourt door and missed the evening before it had finished happening.  That was her habit, and her flaw.  She grieved in advance, so her pages read like elegies for people still eating breakfast.  The quill in her sleeve was cut from a petrel feather shed in high summer, and it held ink only until the first hard frost split the shaft.  Once the fog was solid, frost was days away, and the squall just passed would be the year's last rain.  That mattered, because the forecourt spoke only when wet.  Its crystal paving drank the voice of everyone who crossed it, and after rain it whispered them back, the dead and the living together, the whole memory of the house in one soft tide.  Her task was to write down what it said before it dried. 

She stepped out.  Lightning moved under the fog shelf without sound, and its glow came up over the eastern rim, low and white, laying the shadows of the seven monuments long across the wet crystal.  The whispering rose around her ankles.  A cook counting loaves.  Two astronomers quarreling over a comet forty years gone.  Old Maren, who had trained her, humming while she swept.  Ilse wrote quickly, and as always she wrote we and they, never I.  She had been found on the gate stone of this forecourt as an infant, with no name and no account of herself, and she had spent thirty years copying other lives while her own page stayed bare.  A person with no beginning, she believed, could only collect endings. 

Then, near the tallest monument, the one called the Mourner, a voice she had never transcribed came up thin through the water.  "I will set her where the Mourner's shadow ends," a woman said, out of breath, "so they find her at first light." 

Ilse's quill stopped.  The whisper did not repeat, and nothing in it carried a date.  But the archivists had always kept their calendar by the monuments, because the storm glow climbed a little each night as the fog shelf thickened, and the shadows shortened by a measured span.  She walked the forecourt and examined them one after another, measuring each shadow against the seams in the paving, the way Maren had taught her.  The Mourner's shadow ended on the gate stone tonight, exactly, and by tomorrow it would fall short.  It lay there on one night of the year only, the first night after the fog became solid. 

So she let herself conjecture, carefully, as she would for any gap in the record.  No road climbed to the observatory, so the woman must have crossed the hardened fog on the first night it could bear her weight, carrying a child toward the only lit windows above the storm.  Thirty years ago tonight, then, Ilse had begun here.  Who the woman was, and what she fled, the shadows could not say, and Ilse found, with some surprise, that she would not press them. 

The forecourt was still speaking.  Beside the gate it gave up an infant's cry, and then Maren, young, saying, "Hush now, you are ours, we will work out the rest as we go."  After that came a girl's voice reciting star names wrong, a young woman laughing at the cook, an archivist reading entries aloud to check them.  All of them were hers.  She had been in the common memory the whole time, one thread among the rest, shaped by every errand and argument she had crossed these stones to reach.  Nobody had handed her a self at that gate; she had been cutting one ever since, out of thirty years of days. 

The crystal was drying, and the whispers thinned toward the edges of the court.  She felt the old ache start, the missing of this hour while she still stood in it, and for once she did not turn it into an elegy.  She opened the record to her bare page and wrote, with the summer quill: I came across the fog, I was kept, I am the archivist here.  Then she crouched and said her own name to the last damp square of crystal, so that next year's rain would have it.  The stone took the sound and went quiet.  Below the fog the lightning kept on, bright and soundless, and the Mourner's shadow rested on the gate until the hush was complete.
