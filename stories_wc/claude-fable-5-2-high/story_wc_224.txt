Oriel was mercury glass, blown double-walled in a village that no longer had a name, with silver sealed between her skins so that futures could swim there.  For ninety years she had kept the counter of Corrigan's, the bookstore that had eaten a mountain.  Its shelves ran through the granite like roots, nine hundred galleries deep, and customers hired guides and carried rope.  They came to her with coins, and she looked into her own silver and told them which book they would need before they died.  It was a small talent, and she let them believe it was her only one. 

The larger talent she had buried in the lowest gallery, forty winters ago, on the day remembering began to hurt.  That was the day word arrived that the valley of her makers had been flooded for a reservoir.  Since then, each memory of the kiln village raised a hairline craze in her inner wall, and a bead of her silver bled away through it.  So she stopped remembering, and she buried the stone.  The booksellers' catalog called it the lost language stone: river slate carved in the makers' tongue, which nobody else alive could read.  Once she had read it by touch, as easily as humming. 

Every night at the closing bell, Oriel substituted reality.  Whatever stood reflected in her at dusk became the true bookstore until dawn, and she angled herself with care: the same galleries, the same ladders, but a blank wall where the stair to the lowest stacks should be.  Every morning the stair came back, and every evening she erased it again.  Storms broke over the summit and reached her only as a hum in the shelves, thunder muffled by a mile of paper.  She thought of herself the same way. 

On the last evening of autumn, a goose feather drifted down the central shaft, shed by some bird that had blundered in through the summit door.  It landed on her counter, and she picked it up to throw it away.  Her fingers stopped.  The barbs lay locked together in fine slanted ranks, and when she drew her thumb against the grain they parted with a soft, exact resistance.  She knew that resistance.  Her mind refused to say from where, but her hands were not her mind, and they did not crack. 

The makers had polished new glass with goose feathers.  A vane like this one was the first thing she had ever felt, stroked down her cooling shoulder by someone humming.  And the makers' script had never been meant for eyes; its strokes were cut in ranks like barbs, to be read by the catch of them.  The memory came through her fingers instead of her silver, and for once it did not hurt. 

The closing bell rang.  Oriel stood square to the stair and let it show in her exactly as it was, and for the first night in forty years the mountain stayed true. 

She went down.  The descent took until midnight, past atlases, past heresies, past galleries where the lamps had long since guttered and she walked by her own faint shine.  Above her the storm kept pace, a pressure more than a sound.  In the lowest gallery she knelt and dug through forty years of dust and fallen pages until her hands found slate.  She laid the feather on it and drew the vane slowly across the carving, and where the barbs caught, her fingers read. 

It was a short text, a blessing for a new kiln.  Under all her forgetting she had wanted only to understand the shape of home, and she had expected a map.  Instead, the word the makers used for home was the word they used for sowing.  Home was not the place you came from; it was the place you seeded, and its shape was whatever grew.  Oriel knelt in the dark while that settled into her, and something in her inner wall, long crazed, went still.  She had been guarding a grave when she had been given a granary. 

She carried the stone up through the galleries, and at each landing she stopped, took down a blank ledger, and pressed a line of the makers' script into its first page with the quill end of the feather.  One seed to a landing, shelved among the grammars where a curious hand might find it. 

By dawn she reached the counter, silver-streaked and unbroken.  A guide's boy waited there with a coin.  Oriel set the coin aside and put the feather in his hand.  "Run your thumb against it," she said, "and I will teach you to read."  Far overhead the storm broke at last, and this time the thunder came all the way down.
