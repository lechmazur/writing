Night had held the greenhouse for eleven years, and Ser Maren Voss had kept it company for nine. 

The alchemists who built the place had distilled daylight out of the valley to feed their orchard, then fled when the dark refused to lift. 

What they left was factually mystical: pear trees that fruited quicksilver, apples with copper skins, each miracle obeying rules that could be weighed, timed, and written in a ledger. 

Maren kept that ledger because it asked nothing of her. 

She had once shouted an oath in a bright hall to a king who spent her comrades like coin, and afterward she had ridden until there were no more halls. 

Here she spoke to no one, pruned by lantern, and slept in her armor as though it were a door she could keep shut. 

Then the shifts began. 

Every few hours the greenhouse shuddered and rearranged itself, panes sliding like cards, rows of trees trading places, the cold stove appearing in a different corner. 

The ledger recorded that too: reality shifting on a rhythm, quickening week by week, like a breath drawn before a word. 

During one such shudder a quicksilver pear tree turned its back to her, and in a graft scar she found a folded paper, damp and soft as cloth. 

It was a marionette theater program, abandoned where some alchemist had tucked it. 

The cover read The Orchard of What May Be, a play for wires in seven movements, performed for the Guild on the last evening of the sun. 

Inside were no actors' names, only diagrams: seven patterns of pulls and releases, numbered, with a note in brown ink. 

"To capture the shape of possibility, the strings must move while the world moves; we lost our nerve, so whoever finds this, forgive us the dark." 

Maren looked up. 

She had always taken the glass wires that ran from every branch to the iron ribs of the roof for training lines, the kind any orchardist strings. 

Now she followed them to a gallery above the door, where they gathered into seven wooden crossbars furred with dust. 

The whole orchard was a stage, and the trees were its puppets. 

She climbed. 

Her gauntlets were too thick to feel tension in a wire, and for a long moment she stood with her fists closed, because bare hands were the first thing that bright hall had taught her to hide. 

The glass overhead began to tremble; the next shift was coming. 

She made herself unsnap the buckles at her wrists, one, two, and let the gauntlets fall into the dark. 

Cold bit her fingers. 

It felt like being introduced. 

The first pattern was awkward, the second less so, and by the third her hands remembered something they had never learned, as if the diagrams had been waiting in the grain of the crossbars and only needed warm skin to wake. 

Below her the trees bowed, crossed, and lifted their limbs through forgotten patterns while the panes slid and the rows began to trade places. 

This time the shift did not finish. 

It hung, caught mid-turn in the web of wires, and the greenhouse held every arrangement at once: a stove in all four corners, paths braided, each tree standing beside its own other selves. 

Maren's breath shook, and she was laughing without sound, frost on her teeth, a joy so sharp it made her shiver. 

At the center of the tangle, where every wire crossed, something was ripening out of the air itself. 

It was a fruit only in the sense that it hung, a clear, restless thing that was pear, then key, then small sun, never deciding. 

The seventh diagram ended with a single instruction: speak for it, or it falls. 

She understood then why the alchemists had run. 

They had wanted to own the thing, and it could only be served. 

Maren had sworn loudly once and been wrong, so she did not shout. 

She leaned her forehead against the cold crossbar and murmured, so low the glass barely carried it, "I am sworn to you, not to what is, but to what might be." 

The fruit steadied without settling, a shape that held all its shapes, and descended along the wires into her bare, waiting hands. 

It weighed nothing and warmed her to the elbows, and later she would enter both facts in the ledger. 

Through the panes above, for the first time in eleven years, the dark thinned to the color of a bruise healing. 

Maren stood shaking in the blissful cold, holding a morning that had not yet chosen itself, and did not reach for her armor.
