Three days after the gravedigger opened the small coffin and found a doll of bog oak where her daughter should have been, Maren Holt still went on setting bones.  Bones were honest; they broke for reasons and mended by rules.  The village whispered changeling, and Maren, who believed in nothing she could not splint, said only that someone had made a cruel mistake seven years ago, and that she would rather understand it than punish it. 

That was her grandmother's teaching.  The old woman had kept a ledger in which every bone she ever set crooked was inked as a single dot, and on winter nights she joined the dots into figures and named them like stars.  "A mistake you can steer by is a constellation," she used to say.  Maren kept the ledger still, though her own splints were hinged with brass from broken clocks, an invention the old woman would have scolded and then borrowed. 

It was Pip who split her certainty.  Pip was the foundling she had taken in the week of the funeral, seven now, shrewdly innocent in the way of children who ask the one question a room is avoiding.  At supper he set his first lost tooth beside her plate.  It was cold as a hailstone, and when she held it to the candle she saw rings inside it, fine as the grain of a tree. 

"If the baby in the box was wood," Pip said, "and I am a little bit wood, then I think I know where the real one went." 

Maren did not believe him.  She followed him anyway, because for seven years she had yearned toward a grave, and a yearning that has lost its grave will take any road offered. 

He led her into the hedge maze of the ruined Harrow estate, turning without hesitation, as if walking a house he had dreamed.  At a dead end of black yew he pressed the tooth into a knot in the trunk.  The hedge parted in a narrow cleft.  Perhaps it had always been hollow there, Maren told herself, and stepped through. 

Beyond lay a walled garden where her grandmother's kind of roses, the heavy damask sort nobody grew anymore, bloomed beside flowers she had no names for.  Tall shapes moved among the foxgloves, and she declined to count them.  Under a pear tree sat a girl of seven with Maren's own stubborn jaw and a left forearm that bent where no forearm should. 

"I fell last autumn," the girl said.  "They never break here, so they did not know what to do with me." 

Something in Maren broke then, cleanly, along an old fault.  She had been certain her child was dead, and after the coffin she had been certain her child was stolen by someone she could name and pardon.  Now there was only a cleft where certainty had been, and through it, light. 

She knelt and did what she knew.  "A bone healed wrong must be broken again before it can be set true," she told the girl, as her grandmother had told her.  "It will hurt once, and then it will be yours." 

Pip held the girl's good hand.  Maren gave her a leather strap to bite, found the false join with her thumbs, and broke it in one quick motion; then came traction, the slow turn, the small click of ends meeting, comfrey, willow, and the brass hinge that would let the wrist remember how to move.  When the girl spat out the strap, a loose milk tooth came with it. 

The tall shapes had drawn close.  One of them, with a face like weather, looked at Pip the way Maren had looked at a headstone.  So they had yearned too.  She still did not believe in them, exactly, but forgiveness had never required belief, only a willingness to stop setting the same break crooked. 

The old stories said the trade must be undone: one child back, one child home, the hedge sealed.  Maren, who hinged her splints, thought some breaks should be held open.  She walked back to the yew and wedged the girl's tooth into the knot beside Pip's, two small pegs, and the cleft stayed wide enough for a child to pass either way.  The weather-faced one touched the brass hinge, curious, and nodded. 

That night, with two children asleep in one bed, she opened the ledger and inked three new dots: an oak doll, a crooked wrist, a certainty she had never thought to question.  She joined them with a steady line.  The figure looked like a gate, and she named it that, and left room on the page for more.
