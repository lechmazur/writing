The sycamores at the grotto's mouth shed their bark in quiet curls, growing paler and smoother the older they got, and Ilse Wren had always felt understood by them.  She had been fifty-three when the magnetic grotto took hold of her; tonight, forty-two years of research later, she looked about eleven.  She crossed the stepping stones to the floating rock, a meditation platform of lodestone that hung a hand's width above the pond, repelled by the ore beneath the water.  Overhead the roof had fallen in ages ago, and starlight came down through sycamore leaves and lay on the black water like filings. 

Tobin was already seated on the platform, thirty years old, patient, his breathing slowed the way she had taught him.  He believed she was the dead doctor's heir, a strange solemn child raised on notebooks, and she had never said so and never said otherwise.  Twice before she had told an apprentice the plain fact, and twice the apprentice had laughed, then stared, then packed.  A truth handed over was a stone placed in the palm; it only became knowledge when it was thrown.  So she chose to withhold it, and to weave around the gap only things that were true.  And time was short: each spring she lost a little more of the calculus, and one day she would lose her letters. 

"Will you tell me tonight what the doctor meant," Tobin asked, "by her last page?"  The page said, in a hand he had never noticed was also hers: Teach vanishing to appear. 

"The doctor is not finished with that page," Ilse said carefully.  "Tonight you will read it yourself, in the water." 

She put a river pebble in his hand and watched him sink through the trance, layer by layer, until the slab's faint humming was the only thing holding either of them up. 

"Throw," she said. 

The pebble went in with a small swallowed sound, and the rings walked outward under the stars. 

"Follow the first ring to the bank and tell me where it goes." 

His voice came slow, from far down.  "It ends, it vanishes, no, the reeds are nodding, the sycamore roots are darker, the ring is in them now." 

"Good," she said, and her child's throat tightened around the word.  "Now stay, because in this grotto the water has a second pattern." 

It was the thing she had found at fifty-three, sitting where he sat: under the pull of the ore, the rings struck the bank and came home.  They closed inward, tightening, each circle smaller and brighter than the last, until the pond held one trembling point where the pebble had gone in.  She had leaned too close that night, and the pattern had taken her for one of its own. 

"The rings are coming back," Tobin whispered.  "There is a face in each one, an old woman writing, then a woman less old, then a girl with ink on her thumb." 

Ilse looked at the ink on her thumb and did not hide it. 

He surfaced from the trance the way a swimmer does, all at once, and turned, and saw her as if the starlight had been adjusted.  "There was never an heir," he said.  "There was only the doctor, coming back across the water." 

"Yes," Ilse said, and the withheld thing, spoken at last, weighed almost nothing.  "I could not tell you, because you would have believed me or doubted me, and either way it would have been mine and not yours." 

She told him the rest then, without weaving: that she was closing toward her own small point, that within a few years she would not be able to read her own notebooks. 

"When a ring reaches the bank, the bank keeps it," she said.  "I am asking you to be the bank, so that my vanishing has somewhere to appear." 

Tobin was quiet for a long while, and the sycamores let down one curl of bark that turned on the water and did not sink.  He did not laugh, and he did not pack. 

"I will need you to explain the calculus again," he said, "while you still can, and after that I will explain it to you." 

It was the first thing anyone had offered her in forty-two years that pointed forward.  Then he found a second pebble on the slab and set it in her small palm.  Ilse threw it, and under the starlight they watched the rings go out together toward the waiting bank, while the sycamores kept their hush.
