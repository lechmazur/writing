Maren Voss measured slow things for the county: silt, seawall tilt, the millimeters by which Pell Bay forgot its own shape.  Her talent was noticing what everyone else had gotten used to.  She walked her rounds in April, in the hour after sunrise when the streetlights still burned along the quiet shore, lit for nobody, daydreaming.  The water lay flat as poured glass, and the first light came over it like a fire that had agreed to whisper. 

Lately her notebook held a second column, unpaid.  She wanted to understand why some houses felt alive and others, equally painted, equally occupied, did not. 

Her own was the second kind.  She knew exactly how it had happened, because she had logged it without meaning to.  After her mother died, the kettle stopped changing burners.  The reading glasses stopped turning up in the bread box.  One object at a time, over six springs, everything came to rest, and no single day was the day the house went out. 

Stillest of all was the workroom, where her mother had blended perfumes for forty years.  Maren dusted around each bottle without lifting it, believing that to keep a room unmoved was to sanctify it.  On the bench, on a square of muslin, sat a shard of orris root, pale and hard as a knuckle of chalk.  Her mother had split it from an iris rhizome the last spring of her life.  "Orris smells of nothing for years," she had said, "and then of violets, and you never catch the day it turns."  Maren had never touched it.  To her it was a shard of hush, the silence of the house made small enough to hold, and she did not hold it. 

So she studied other houses the way she studied the seawall, by what had shifted.  The Okafor place, three doors down, practically hummed, and its evidence was all displacement: a colander in the tulip bed, one boot on the porch rail, a violin bow lying across the mailbox.  The dead houses had nothing out of place. 

She tested the theory at home.  She put the kettle on the wrong burner, left a spoon on the stairs, hung her coat on the workroom door.  The house looked like a stage set of a house, and stayed cold. 

The next morning she was crouched at the Okafors' fence, sketching the colander, when a screen door creaked.  Wren Okafor, seven, stood in pajamas and one boot. 

"Are you writing down our mess?" 

"I am trying to learn why it works," Maren said. 

Wren considered the colander with the patience of someone explaining tides to a tide gauge.  "It's not mess.  That's for the snails, and the bow is so Dad remembers his lesson, and the boot is drying.  Everything's just in the middle of going somewhere." 

She retrieved her boot and went in, having no idea what she had said. 

Maren stayed crouched while the streetlights dreamed on above her.  Not displacement, then.  Errands.  A living house was a house full of interrupted intentions, each stray object a sentence someone still meant to finish.  Her spoon on the stairs had been going nowhere. 

She walked home fast, for once measuring nothing.  In all her mother's house, one object had been in the middle of going somewhere for six years, and she had mistaken its waiting for a grave. 

The workroom door stuck, then gave.  She lifted the orris shard from its muslin, and it was lighter than silence should be.  Beneath it, in her mother's slanting pencil, the cloth read: for the front hall, when ready. 

Maren raised the shard to her face.  Violets, powdery and cool, six springs deep; it had turned on some day she never caught.  So slow change could also run toward sweetness, and she, of all people, had failed to notice. 

She carried it down the corridor like a coal in cupped hands.  She took the nutmeg grater from the kitchen and shaved pale curls into the dish by the front door where keys used to land.  The scent opened through the hall the way dawn had opened over the bay, a blaze with its voice turned down.  This, she understood, was how to sanctify a house: not by keeping it, but by continuing it. 

She left the grater on the hall windowsill with what remained of the shard, and the workroom door standing open.  That piece she meant for Wren, once she found a jar. 

Outside, one by one, the streetlights woke from their daydream and went dark.  In the kitchen she lit the wrong burner under the kettle, this time because she wanted tea.  The house, she noted in no column at all, was in the middle of going somewhere.
