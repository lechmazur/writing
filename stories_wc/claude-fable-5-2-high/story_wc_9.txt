Three weeks after the birth of her granddaughter, the parcel found Odile Marrow in her wagon of cards.  Inside lay a folded birthday tablecloth and a single saucer from a dolls' tea set, cracked from rim to center.  There was no letter, because her daughter Wren had never needed words to accuse. 

Odile was an onyx-ink cartomancer, which meant she ground her own black from burnt stone and painted every card she read.  She had painted futures for strangers in forty towns while her own child turned eight, then nine, then nineteen without her.  She knew the saucer.  It had cracked in Wren's small fist the morning Odile walked out, during a tea party set for two. 

That night she asked her deck the only question she had left: where does lost time accumulate?  If it pooled somewhere, she reasoned, it could be carried home like water.  The cards answered with a mountain, a moon, and a clearing drawn above a line of cloud. 

So she climbed.  For two days the fog pressed against her mouth like wet wool, and then, near midnight, she broke through into the glade. 

It was so lovely it hurt to breathe.  Silver grass bent under a moon close enough to bruise, and the clouds lay below on every side, a slow white sea with no shore.  The path she had come by was already gone beneath it.  She was cloudbound now, held up here with nothing but what she had brought and what she had done. 

Odile knelt and began to unfasten the twine around the tablecloth, her fingers stupid with cold.  The linen opened across the grass like a second moon. 

Her family had kept one custom through four generations: at every birthday, each person at the table laid a hand on the cloth and had it traced.  Here was her mother's hand, broad as a spade.  Here was Wren's at one, at four, at seven, a little larger each year, nested inside Odile's own outline like a bird inside a cage it trusted. 

After seven, Wren's hands went on alone.  Beside each one, though, someone had drawn a careful dotted shape the size of a grown woman's palm, and left it empty.  Twelve empty shapes.  At the newest corner was a hand no bigger than a walnut, traced on the day of the birth itself, and beside it waited a thirteenth. 

Odile's throat closed. 

She dealt her cards along the hem, and they made no sense at all.  The Well lay beside the Empty Cup; the Door lay face to face with the Wall.  A lesser reader would have shuffled until the answer flattered her.  Odile had built her whole craft on being willingly perplexed, on sitting inside a contradiction until it chose to speak, and so she sat. 

She set the cracked saucer in the middle of the cloth, because it seemed to want to be there.  Dew gathered in it, thick and bright as mercury.  Then it found the crack and slipped through, and ran along the linen without soaking in.  It passed every traced hand and settled only in the dotted shapes, filling all thirteen until they shone. 

The Well beside the Empty Cup. 

Lost time did not accumulate on mountains.  It accumulated in the places kept open for it, year after year, by someone who went on setting a table for two. 

Odile pressed her palm into the first shining shape, and Wren's eighth birthday went through her like swallowed ice: a cake with one slice uncut, a child watching the road.  She could not lift the time out.  It was not hers to carry home; it was Wren's, every waiting hour of it, and the only thing Odile could do was sign for it. 

She uncorked her onyx ink.  One hand at a time, twelve times, she laid her palm in the cold light and traced it, and each year told her exactly what it had cost.  She wept until the moon blurred into the grass, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and she could barely swallow. 

As the twelfth line closed, the clouds below her tore open along a single seam, and the path showed dark and wet between them. 

She left the thirteenth shape untouched.  That one sat beside a hand the size of a walnut, and it was not yet lost.  It would have to be traced at a table, by someone else's pen, if Wren would allow it. 

Odile folded the cloth around the saucer, left her cards where they lay for the wind, and started down.
