Maren had come to the cliff to bury her aunt and had stayed six years because nobody else would count the trees.  There were four hundred and eleven hazels in the orchard, and every one had been a traveler who touched bark after the night frost formed.  Her grandfather had walled the terraces to keep people off the trees, and the walls, switchbacking down the cliff toward the harbor road, had become a labyrinth that kept people in.  So he had built birds.  Thirty brass rooks, wound by the sea wind, still patrolled the walls in clattering loops, shrieking like hinges at anything warm.  They were meant to follow a lead bird to the gate, but the lead bird hung headless in the workshop, unfinished since its blueprint was lost and, in time, forgotten.  Maren kept her bag packed by the door. 

That afternoon she was on the gatehouse roof, rolling a small brass nut between her fingers, when the rooks began shrieking over the lower terraces.  She had found the nut in her aunt's button jar, hazel-sized, left-hand threaded, fitting nothing she owned, and she worried it the way other people prayed.  Through the spyglass: a tinker and a boy, deep in the fourth switchback, turning the wrong way with great confidence.  Maren glanced at the sun, the kelp line, the color of her own breath.  "Frost in fifty-two minutes," she said to nobody, the way another woman might say it looked like rain.  She was never wrong about such things, and took no pleasure in it.  Reaching them on foot would take ninety minutes there and back, and shouting was useless, because the wind off the cliff tore words to lint. 

She ran to the workshop for a lantern, cursing her grandfather, her aunt, and hazels generally.  The lantern hung on the same nail as her aunt's oilskin, which Maren had not moved in six years, and when she yanked, nail and coat came down together.  On the plaster behind it was a ghost.  A sheet of tracing linen had been pinned there for decades, and the sun had bleached the wall through it, everywhere except beneath the ink.  The linen was long gone, but the drawing remained, pressed into the wall by light alone: a rook's neck, a spindle, a lantern in the breast with a pierced shutter.  Beside the spindle, in her grandfather's cramped hand, darker than the plaster: one small nut, left-hand thread.  Maren opened her fist and looked at what she had been praying with. 

"Forty-eight minutes," she said, and dragged the headless bird onto the bench.  She had never been taught to assemble anything, but she had oiled thirty rooks every week for six years, and her hands knew them the way they knew stairs in the dark.  She seated the head, set the gimbal gap by eye to the width of a fingernail paring, and did not check it, because it was right.  The nut spun on backward, as if it had been waiting, and locked.  She lit the breast lantern, carried the thing to the roof, and threw it at the sunset. 

It flew like a kettle falling upstairs.  But the wind caught its tin feathers, and one by one the thirty rooks broke their loops and fell in behind it, a ragged, squealing arrow over the terraces.  Over the fourth switchback it circled the two warm shapes, then steadied, its locked head pointing home.  Through the pierced shutter its lantern stamped a bright wedge on the path ahead of the travelers, then another farther on, marks pressed down and lifted again with nothing touching anything.  It was how the sun had drawn the blueprint.  It was, Maren thought sourly, how her aunt had raised her: never one embrace, and yet here she stood, shaped. 

The tinker followed the wedges.  The boy, stumbling on the last stair, reached for a branch already silvering at its tips, and thirty rooks screamed at his warm hand until he snatched it back.  Maren hauled them through the gate by their collars with four minutes to spare, which she mentioned. 

Then the frost came down the cliff all at once, and four hundred and eleven crooked hazels turned to white fire under the first stars, and nobody was added to them.  The rooks settled along the wall in a dented, gleaming row, the lead bird's lantern guttering, its head still aimed at the gate.  Inside, the boy asked what the trees were, and Maren told him, exactly, and watched him count his fingers.  She did not unpack her bag.  She did move it away from the door, to make room for strangers' boots, and her empty hand kept turning a nut that was finally somewhere else.
