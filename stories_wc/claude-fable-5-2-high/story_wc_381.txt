Maren kept the lantern-house where the brook bent, a haven that opened only after dark. 
She was a summoner, and solitary by choice, since the night her teacher Osk called something too large and it left with his voice. 
All she had of him was one wing of the paper crane he used to fold as a perch for whatever answered. 
The valley folk came to her nightly anyway, not for summoning but for the water. 
They knelt on the mossy bank and whispered their secrets into the quiet brook, and the brook carried them off toward the sea. 
Maren poured tea, kept the lanterns low, and never listened. 
Then, three nights ago, the brook began to whisper back. 
It spoke in code: a trickle, a pause, two quick trickles, a longer pause, the same pattern again and again over the stones. 
The widow Tamsin, who had once worked the signal drums, caught pieces of it. 
"Alone, fire, end," she translated, pale in the lantern light. 
"It is a prophecy, Maren, and I think it is about you." 
By the second night the whisperers were whispering to each other instead of the water, and their glances at the summoner had edges. 
Maren understood how such things went. 
A frightened valley would drive her out or burn the lantern-house to be safe, and the prophecy would make itself true. 
She had to hush it before dawn sent them home carrying it like a coal. 
She could have summoned something to swallow the sound, but that was Osk's mistake, force answering fear. 
Instead, on the third night, she sat on the bank among the murmuring kneelers and listened for the first time. 
Not to the trickles, which scared ears could bend into any word they dreaded, but to the pauses. 
Silence could not be misheard. 
She unfolded the crane's origami wing across her knee, its creases soft as old skin, and began mapping the silence. 
She timed each gap against her pulse and inked it along a fold, short gaps as dots, long ones as dashes. 
Hours passed that way, her attention burning steady and low, like a banked hearth. 
Around her the brook went on being what it was, terrestrially cosmic, a ribbon of mud and minnows hauling the whole sky on its back. 
When the wing was full, she read the map and laughed once, softly. 
The pauses did not belong to the brook. 
Each one was exactly as long as the breath a kneeler holds before confessing. 
And now that she was listening, she heard what they had confessed here for years: that they feared the woman at the bend, alone with her craft, and whatever she might someday call. 
The brook had worn that single secret into its bed until it flowed back as prophecy. 
Read whole, from the silences, it said: she who calls alone will call a fire, and the whispering will end. 
She could not erase it, any more than she could erase a crease from paper. 
But a crease could be smoothed, and a prophecy could be kept in its gentlest meaning before fear chose a crueler one. 
Maren refolded the wing along the lines of her map and set it on the water. 
Then she called, alone, as foretold, not commanding but inviting, the way Osk had taught her before he forgot his own lesson. 
What she summoned was the silence she had mapped. 
It settled onto the paper wing like a bird onto a perch, and the wing began to flow downstream, and wherever it passed the stuttering code ran together into one even murmur. 
Behind it the reflected stars caught and spread, bank to bank, a cold white fire that burned nothing. 
The kneelers stopped mid-whisper, their faces lit from below. 
It was not the hush of a hand over a mouth. 
It was a smoothed hush, the quiet of a sheet pressed flat, with every old fold still faintly visible and no longer sharp. 
"Is that the fire?" Tamsin asked, aloud, and startled at her own full voice. 
"That is the fire," Maren said, "and this is the end of whispering, if you will tell me to my face what you have been telling the water." 
Tamsin looked at the shining brook, then at the summoner. 
"We were afraid of you," she said, "because you were always alone." 
"So was I," said Maren. 
She fetched the kettle, and one by one the kneelers rose from the bank and sat down beside her to talk. 
Far downstream the paper wing flowed on toward the sea, carrying a prophecy that had already, quietly, come true.
