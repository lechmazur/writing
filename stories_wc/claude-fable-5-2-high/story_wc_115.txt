Her father's confession took nine words, and then the hospice room went quiet as a stopped loom.  "The valve was never stuck, Wren; I was asleep."  He closed his eyes afterward, and Wren Halloran did not answer, because she had no idea what such a sentence weighed.  So she carried the hush out to her truck at two in the morning and drove toward Calder Mill. 

Eleven people on the night shift had died there in 1979, scalded when the main steam line burst.  Her father, nineteen then and minding the relief valve, had sworn for forty years that the wheel seized in his hands. 

Wren knew the mill better than she knew most relatives.  She had been breaking into dead factories since she was sixteen, without a helmet, without a partner, without telling anyone where to look for her body.  But she had never found the relief valve, because the mill's drawings had burned with its office. 

What she had instead was the radio.  Every night on AM 1340, the psychics of The Open Veil took calls, and every night somebody from town phoned in frantic about the ghost shift.  Wren was skeptical the way her grandmother had been Catholic: daily, strictly, with rituals.  She did not believe in the dead clocking in, but she believed in witnesses, so for years she had logged the calls in a notebook like a doubter's breviary. 

Tonight, parked on the shoulder with her eyelids heavy and her pulse quick, she finally read the log as a map.  The woman on Tannery Street always heard knocking under her cellar at ten past three.  The man on Cutler Road heard a whistle rising through his floor drain, and a girl by the feed store swore her bedroom smelled of wet wool.  The psychics told them all to burn sage.  Wren drew a line through the three addresses and found it ran straight along the old coal spur, where trains had once fed the boilers through a tunnel under the ridge.  Nobody was hearing ghosts; they were hearing a pipe, and the pipe would lead to the wheel. 

The tunnel mouth was chained, so she went in over the chain.  Inside, the air was cold and close, and her flashlight made a small yellow room that moved with her along the rotted ties.  She had not slept in two days, and the dark kept offering her a pillow.  Each drip from the ceiling snapped her awake again. 

Half a mile in, the steam main dropped out of the roof like a root, and on it sat the valve.  Somebody had been here before her.  Glass beads were wired around the rim of the iron wheel, blue and green and milk white, the kind the mill girls once sewed onto trim.  Wren counted eleven.  The widows had known exactly where to mourn, which meant they had always known whose post this was. 

She set down the flashlight and did what she had come to do, which was to appraise the thing like any salvage.  Cast iron, fourteen inches across, stem threads greased by someone long dead and still bright under the dust.  She gripped the beaded rim and leaned.  It turned.  It turned easily, a quarter turn, with a gritty sigh, as it would have turned for a boy of nineteen if he had been awake. 

Wren sat down hard on the rail.  So that was the weight: about nine pounds of force, and forty years of saying stuck.  Her own head was nodding, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, how gently sleep takes a person at a post like this. 

At ten past three, the ghost shift arrived.  The pipe began to knock, slow and then faster, like looms starting up, and a thin whistle climbed through the barely open valve.  Night air cooling the iron, Wren told herself, pressure shifting in a dead line; she recited it like a creed.  The knocking did not care what she believed. 

She stood, took the wheel in both hands, and spun it all the way open, the job her father had slept through.  The whistle swelled, broke into a long breath that smelled faintly of wet wool, and stopped.  The knocking stopped with it.  Wren did not pray; she touched each bead once, eleven times, which was only counting. 

Back in the truck, the radio was still on, and a woman was telling the psychic, bewildered, "It just quit, under my cellar, it just quit."  Wren wrote the call in her notebook and offered no explanation beside it.  Then she drove back toward the hospice to end the hush with what she had measured: eleven beads, one quarter turn, and a daughter who would stay awake with him.
