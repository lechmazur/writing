The outage came at midnight without warning, and by dawn the city's lights were still flickering, stuttering awake and failing again like a sleeper fighting a dream.  Inside the Marlow Street Library, the shadows held a deep sapphire hush, the color of ink before it dries.  Above the periodicals room, in the crawl space where she had lived for three winters, Wren lay listening.  She had been the sound archivist here until the budget cut her department and renovation crews gutted the listening room.  The workers, hurried and careless, had shoved decades of oral history reels into the pipe chases and plastered over the gaps.  Wren could not leave those voices in the walls, so she had stayed, unseen, and gone in after them. 

Her work was kindly methodical.  Each night she chose one chase, eased off its access panel, and felt along the cold copper until her fingers met a canister, a spool, a curl of tape.  She brushed away the plaster dust, wrapped each find in a clean pillowcase, penciled a tag, and cached it between the joists, apologizing softly to any reel she could not yet identify.  She told herself the voices needed a keeper, not a face. 

Now a key scraped below, far too early, and two sets of footsteps crossed the reading room.  Through the vent grille Wren saw Tomas, the young branch librarian, guiding an old woman by flashlight. 

"I am so sorry, Mrs. Abara," he said.  "The surge took the server, and the digital copy of Joseph's interview went with it." 

"His memorial is at nine," she said, very quietly.  "I promised our grandchildren they would hear him tell the ferry story himself." 

"The original reel was discarded years ago," Tomas said, and the overhead lamps flared, buzzed, and died again. 

Wren's hands were already moving through the cache in the dark.  She did not need the tags; her skin kept its own catalog.  In the fourth pillowcase lay a ferromagnetic tape reel fragment, half a cracked plastic flange still gripping perhaps ninety feet of rust-brown ribbon, uncovered last spring behind the boiler riser.  She drew the tape across her thumb and the afternoon returned whole.  She felt the chalky drag of the oxide, smelled the faint vinegar breath of old acetate, and with them came rain on the listening room window, peppermint tea cooling at her elbow, and a retired ferry pilot laughing so hard the deck trembled under her resting hand.  Her body had remembered Joseph Abara before her mind could say his name. 

She could lower the fragment through the grille on a string and stay a rumor.  But a broken reel was only a puzzle to anyone who had never threaded one, and brittle tape would not forgive clumsy hands.  The voices did not need a keeper in the ceiling; they needed someone standing where the grieving could find her. 

Wren tucked the fragment inside her sweater, took the battery field recorder she had rescued from the same wall, and unlatched the hatch above the atlas shelves.  She climbed down the rolling ladder into the blue dimness, dust in her hair, her heart loud as a dropped book. 

Tomas swung the flashlight toward her and froze. 

"Please do not be frightened," Wren said, and held out the cracked half-reel in both hands.  "I recorded your husband, Mrs. Abara, and I believe this is the ferry story." 

The old woman stepped closer and studied her in the wavering light.  "You made him peppermint tea," she said.  "He talked about you all the way home." 

Wren set the recorder on a reading table and worked the way she always worked, slowly, explaining each step so that no one would feel shut out.  She wound the ribbon by hand onto an unbroken spool, cleaned the heads with her cuff, and pressed play. 

A hiss, like rain, and then a man's warm voice filled the shadowed library: "Well, the fog that morning was thick as wool." 

Mrs. Abara closed her eyes and laid her palm flat on the table, as if she could feel him through the wood.  Tomas held his phone near the speaker to copy every word, then looked at Wren as though she might vanish. 

"Are there more?" he asked. 

"Two hundred and eleven so far," she said, "and nine pipe chases I have not opened." 

"Then you will need a desk," Tomas said, "and a door with your name on it." 

Outside, the streetlights shivered once more and held.  Wren stood in the steady glow, dusty and plain and entirely seen, while Joseph Abara laughed across the years and the table trembled under her hand.
