The surgeon said nerves regrow one millimeter a day, wrist to fingertip, and that my hands would wake by midwinter.  Until then I carried trunks through Fort Azure by the crooks of my elbows, a bellhop who could not feel a bell.  The fortress was glazed cobalt brick, built to guard the pass, kept now as a hotel, and buried to its second gallery under snow.  Above it hung the cornice, a white wave of ice that had once killed eleven guests in their beds, and so the fortress had one law: nothing louder than a whisper.  Even the staff choir obeyed.  Each dusk we lined the great hall in our brass-buttoned coats and mouthed vespers without breath, forty throats shaping hymns that never arrived.  Guests called us the Silent Choir and found us charming.  My days were seraphically mundane: towels folded like wings, lamps trimmed, boots lined up outside doors, all of it done with the hush of angels who have been told to keep it down. 

I should have been grateful.  The winter before, the porters had found me at the gate by pure chance, hands frozen black, and the fortress had kept me ever since.  But a man kept safe eventually wants to understand what the safety cost, and who paid. 

My coat had belonged to the bellhop before me, and in its pocket lay a rusted bolt as long as my thumb.  Every morning I pressed its threads against my palm to measure how far feeling had crept.  By December the tingling had reached my knuckles, and I had no idea what the bolt was for. 

Numb hands teach the eyes to work harder.  That is how I noticed, on the dusty spiral staircase of the north tower, the slightest discoloration: a coin of deeper blue on every step, where the dust lay thinner.  Somebody climbed there on tiptoe, nightly, and had for years.  I followed them up, turning the bolt in my palm. 

The top room held a shuttered window, a grand piano under a sheet, and Madame Havel, our housekeeper, standing beside a bench that leaned on three good legs.  She saw the bolt and did not seem surprised. 

"The piano bench bolt," she whispered.  "Your predecessor pulled it on the directors' orders, so that I could not sit." 

She told me the rest in a voice like paper.  Before the law, she had played every dusk with the shutters open, so that travelers lost on the pass could walk toward the music.  Twelve winters ago, in the first month of silence, a guide and eight clients walked circles in a whiteout four hundred yards from the gate.  They blew whistles until dawn.  Everyone inside heard them, and everyone obeyed the law. 

"The cornice never fell," she said.  "Perhaps our silence held it up, and perhaps it would have held anyway.  That is what safety costs: you never learn what it bought, only what it took.  It took nine people, and it was an unpardonable silence, mine most of all." 

I thought of the gate I had struck by chance, and of how near I had come to circling it. 

Wind struck the shutters.  Downstairs in the hall sat six trunks I had stacked that morning, sent ahead by sled for a party due before dark.  It was past dark, and snowing sideways. 

I knelt and fitted the bolt to the bench leg.  My fingers were clumsy as mittens, but somewhere in the turning I felt the threads bite, faintly, in the pad of one fingertip, like a spark through wool. 

"They will dismiss you," Madame Havel whispered.  "You could simply leave for the valley, where a man may shout." 

"No," I said, at full voice, and we both flinched.  "Leaving is only another silence.  I will remain." 

She pulled the sheet from the piano and opened the shutters, and snow blew in across the keys.  Her own hands shook too badly, so she set my waking finger on a single key and stepped back.  I sat on the mended bench and struck the note, again and again, steady as a bell.  Each time, the key pushed back a little more clearly against my skin. 

Below us the choir had gathered for vespers, and I swear that one of them, hearing, drew breath.  The cornice groaned once and held, or was still holding.  I could not know what the note would cost.  But far out in the white, thin as a thread of frost, a whistle answered, and I kept playing toward it.
