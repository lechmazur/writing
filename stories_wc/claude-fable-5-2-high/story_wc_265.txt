Odile Venn woke in a rainforest made of machinery, which was only slightly less likely than waking in the ditch where she had fallen asleep.  She had not woken where she lay down in eleven years.  The academies called it an affliction and, when she kept vanishing from their dormitories, called her a stray.  She kept the name, since a philosopher who belonged to no school could at least belong to a word. 

Above her, brass trunks rose into a canopy of copper leaves, and warm oil dripped from them like rain that had learned a trade.  Something small was pressed into her open palm: a gear no bigger than a thumbnail, twelve teeth, still warm.  The forest shed gears the way other forests shed seeds, so one landing in a sleeper's hand was no miracle, merely probable.  Odile believed most miracles were like that, events with decent odds and poor publicity. 

Then she noticed the hum. 

It had woken in her two winters ago, a sixth sense lodged behind the breastbone, a low vibration that rose whenever a thought of hers touched something true.  At first it had been wild, startling her on roads and in orchards, humming at ideas she had no names for.  But she was a philosopher, and so she had put it to work.  She tested every belief against it, kept what hummed, discarded what did not, and built a system so sound that nothing in her head surprised her anymore.  The frontier of her mind had been surveyed, fenced, and taxed.  The hum had dwindled to a clerk's polite cough, and she missed the wilderness the way one misses a language spoken in childhood. 

Now, with the gear in her fist, it was humming like weather. 

She stood and listened, because listening was the only map the place offered.  Wind moved through the forest, and each kind of tree answered it differently.  The brass kapoks tolled, slow and deep, their buttress roots turning hidden flywheels.  The copper figs chimed in thirds.  Tin ferns hissed like rain on a skillet, and the steel bamboo fluted a clean high fifth.  Together they made a chord so perfectly resolved that it bored her, and she recognized the boredom.  It was the sound of her own system. 

Yet the hum in her chest kept leaning eastward, toward a gap in the music, a place where the wind went in and nothing came out.  She followed the silence the way a tongue follows a missing tooth.  Past the tolling and the chiming she walked, turning her head at each gust, steering by which voices grew and which fell behind. 

By noon she found the mute tree.  It was a crooked thing of blackened iron, shaped like nothing else in the forest, and its leaves hung still while every neighbor spun.  In a hollow at chest height sat its works, and across the one empty spindle someone had wound a neat coil of wire, round and round, binding the mechanism shut. 

Odile understood the repair at once, because she had made its twin.  Some long-gone engineer had disliked this tree's note, which spoiled the chord, and had silenced it tidily rather than listen.  She had done the same to the one question her system could not hold: what in her chose where she woke?  She had filed it under affliction and wound it shut. 

So she began to unravel the wire.  It took an hour, loop after stubborn loop, her fingers slick with oil, the hum climbing with every turn she undid.  When the spindle stood bare, she set the small gear on it.  Twelve teeth met their neighbors as though they had only stepped out for air. 

The next gust caught the iron leaves, and the tree sang.  Its note belonged to no scale; it leaned against the chord like a stranger in a doorway, and the whole forest had to shift to make room.  The kapoks wavered, the figs slid out of their thirds, and the music stopped being finished. 

In her chest the hum did what it had not done in two years: it rose at a question instead of an answer.  The part of her that traveled in the dark was not a fault in her mind but its unfenced edge, and it had been carrying her toward this hollow, night after night, with the patience of a river.  That a sleeper should drift to the one silent tree, and wake holding its missing piece, was unlikely, but not very. 

At dusk she lay down among the iron roots.  The strange note rang over her, the hum answered, and she closed her eyes, curious, for once, about where she was going.
