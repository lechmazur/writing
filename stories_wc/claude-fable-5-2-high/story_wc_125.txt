Three years after the energy beings came down like slow weather and drank the lamps dark, Ilse still walked the roads trading poems for bread.  She charged strangely: one true thing per stanza, and she gave change if the truth was too large.  People called that mischief; Ilse called it bookkeeping for the conscience. 

Tonight the road had left her at an abandoned potters' dig, a grotto of terra-cotta scooped so deep that its roof had fallen in and let the stars look down.  The walls held the day's warmth like a palm.  In her lap lay the only thing she never traded: a frayed coil of rope, grey as old breath. 

The being she called Tallow drifted in after moonset, a patient candle-colored hum that had followed her for forty miles.  Its kind lived every moment at once, the way a lake holds all its water, and it could not understand why she kept touching that rope. 

"Because I want to know what time feels like," she said.  "Not what it means, only its grain, its texture against the hand." 

Tallow's voice arrived inside her ribs, gentle as a kiln cooling.  "Time is smooth to us, but I can fold you back to the night that rope was used, and you may choose again." 

Ilse's hands went still.  She had known the offer was coming, as one knows rain by the ache in an old break. 

"Then you should see what you are offering to unmake," she said, and began to unlay the rope. 

The outer layer came away first: sheet-thread, stiff with the soap of the Harrowgate laundry, where she had served her sentence for poems the old governors disliked.  "Last winter inside," she said, laying the strands on the clay floor in a slow curve.  Beneath lay mop cotton, softer, pilfered a wisp at a time through two summers; she set it in a second curve inside the first.  She was patterning now, the way she patterned a stanza, ring within ring, like the years of a tree. 

At the core, no thicker than a vein, ran a braid of dark hair. 

"Dessa's," Ilse said, and her voice did not shake, which cost her something.  "She cut it the first week and said every rope needs something that wants to hold." 

Tallow dimmed, attentive. 

"The night your kind arrived, every lamp on the wall went blind, and we had our dark," Ilse went on.  "But the rope had frayed on the sill, and it would bear one body, once."  "Leave her or be left: either way, one of us would live as the other's price."  "So we drew straws from the mattress, like children." 

She laughed then, low, and the grotto gave the sound back warm. 

"I cheated, Tallow; I nicked the long straw with my thumbnail so I could feel it in the dark and leave it for her."  "She cheated better: both straws in her fist were long, both nicked, and she had already swallowed the short one."  "I went down the wall arguing, and the fever went through Harrowgate before the gates were opened in spring." 

The stars turned a finger's width over the rim. 

"Then choose again," Tallow said, and its light leaned toward her like a held breath. 

Ilse looked at the rings on the floor, linen, cotton, hair, and felt the old wanting rise in her throat, plain as hunger.  Then she smiled the smile she used when a customer tried to pay with a lie. 

"I will, on one condition: when you fold me back, Dessa keeps her cheat as well." 

Tallow was quiet for a long while, by its measure or hers.  "Then the straws fall the same," it said at last. 

"Every time," said Ilse, "because that choice was never mine alone, and I will not win it by robbing her of the best trick she ever played." 

She ran her thumb along the braid of hair, over each small crossing, and there it was at last, the thing she had walked three years to find.  Time was not smooth; it was laid, strand over strand, and its texture was the fray, the place where a thing has borne weight once and cannot be asked again. 

"Put your light here," she said, and Tallow lowered itself to the ragged end.  It flickered once, like something learning the word after. 

Before dawn Ilse pressed the linen and the cotton into the soft wall, ring within ring, and Tallow, asking nothing, warmed the clay until the pattern set.  She knotted the braid of hair around her wrist, left the grotto its poem, and took the road with her accounts, for once, in balance.
