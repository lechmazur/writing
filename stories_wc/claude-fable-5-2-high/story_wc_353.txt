Every evening, Maren waited at the edge of the siskin alder grove until the hedgehog uncurled.  It slept in a cup of roots, and it felt through the ground what she could not hear through the air.  While any tremor lingered, a far cart or a falling branch, it stayed a fist of spines.  When it loosened and showed its small wet nose, the earth was still, and her work could begin. 

Maren had been deaf since a fever in her fourth winter.  She watched the siskins open their beaks among the alder cones and received nothing.  Yet she was symphonically minded all the same.  She saw the grove in parts: trunks swaying slow as cellos, branches answering a beat behind, leaves shivering above everything like a hundred small bows.  She was a tree-song sculptor because she wanted that score to reach her, and anyone like her, through palms and foot soles and the bones of the chest. 

Her first attempt had failed.  She had bolted a wire between two young alders and tightened it until it stung her fingers.  The first strong wind split one sapling to the pith.  She bound the wound with clay and did not string anything for a year. 

Then, following the hedgehog one dusk toward the wet heart of the grove, she found the other harp.  Someone before her had shaped the twin limbs of an old alder into a tall open frame, and ivy had been hiding it for longer than she had been alive.  The strings were gone to rust.  What remained was the damage, and Maren, who trusted damage to tell the truth, came back each evening to read it. 

She peeled the ivy, laid paper on the wood, and rubbed charcoal over every stress crack until the frame's long suffering lay mapped in her lap.  The cracks were not random.  Deep ones ran with the grain where winter gales had hauled the trunk one way and the wire had refused to follow.  Fine checks crossed them, the hairline marks of dry summers.  One slow spiral climbed the left limb, the record of a tree turning year after year toward light.  Bass, strings, a single wandering voice: she read three parts in the rubbings, and understood that the alder had been playing all along.  The harp had simply never listened. 

Every crack fanned out from the iron bolts on the left limb.  On the right limb the wood was whole, and there, grown half into the bark, sat a porcelain insulator taken from the dead telegraph line beyond the grove.  Its glazed groove was worn bright on both sides.  The wire there had not been held; it had been allowed to slide, a finger's width each way, whenever the tree needed to move. 

Maren sat a long time with her thumb in that groove.  The old sculptor had learned the answer too late to save the left limb, and it had waited here like a sentence without a reader.  A string that grips a living thing can only hurt it.  A string that travels with it can sing.  She thought of her split sapling under its clay, and her face grew hot. 

Over the next days she walked the fallen telegraph poles and twisted free four insulators, white and cold as eggs.  She lashed them to two neighboring alders with soft rope, cutting nothing, and ran two wires through the grooves, snug but free to slide.  She weighted each wire's end with a river stone, so the tension would rise and fall with the trees instead of fighting them. 

Then she knelt at the cup of roots and waited.  The light went amber, then gray.  The hedgehog uncurled, tasted the air, and looked at nothing in particular. 

Maren set one bare palm on the nearer trunk, reached up, and strummed the lower wire once.  It came down the alder into her wrist, her elbow, the floor of her ribs, a low round trembling that did not hurt anything.  The hedgehog lifted its nose and did not curl.  Overhead the siskins shifted along their twigs and stayed. 

Then a breeze leaned on the grove, the trunks swayed, the stones rose, and the note bent upward under her hand without her touching it.  The trees were answering.  She felt the second part enter, then the third, the whole slow score she had only ever watched.  She understood what she would make from now on: not frames that held the trees, but strings that moved when they moved. 

Maren waited for the grove to finish its phrase, then raised her fingers to strum again, more softly than before.  At her knee, the hedgehog went on with its evening.
