Vessa dreamed the future every night and understood it only afterward.  That was her whole gift: foresight that arrived as hindsight, a map she could read only behind her.  So she had made a game of it, and she played the game with a surveyor's exactness.  Each dawn in the bunker she wrote the night's dream on a card cut from ration boxes.  Each dusk she matched the day's events to a card, and the rules were strict: one match per card, no forcing, and a wrong guess cost her a pebble from the jar.  Laughing at a bad guess was allowed.  Six years of matched cards hung on twine across the concrete, a dictionary of her own sleep: water always meant time, mouths meant doors, red meant something ending on purpose. 

The bunker sat under so much vine that the forest seemed to be holding it in a fist.  Sixty years back this had been a depot in cleared land; now oaks split the loading yard and wolves used the culvert as a road.  Vessa had not found the place so much as been delivered to it.  A deer trail had taken her feet one autumn and refused every turning she tried, and she had stayed ever since to learn why some paths choose their walkers. 

The only answer the bunker offered was the directive.  It was bolted in a frame beside the vault door, a typed order from an authority that no longer existed, and it was rotting.  Mildew had eaten it down to one legible line: KEEPER SHALL HOLD THE VAULT CLOSED UNTIL.  Until what had gone to silverfish.  Earlier keepers had recopied the line on the wall beneath in pencil, then charcoal, each copy shorter and more certain, until the last one said only HOLD.  Below the frame hung a small envelope marked CONDITION OF RELEASE, closed with a red wafer seal no bigger than a thumbnail, flour and gum pressed flat by a hand long dead.  Across the flap a keeper had penciled NOT TO BE BROKEN, and for six years Vessa had obeyed a sentence whose reasons had fallen off. 

Then the barometer in the watch room began to drop.  By noon the light through the vines had gone the green of old bottle glass, and the whole forest was hissing with a storm still an hour off.  Vessa took down the three cards that had never matched anything. 

A red circle dissolves on a tongue. 

A mouth in a hill yawns, and the wind eats from it. 

A path walks a woman backward to the spot where she already stands. 

She had always treated unmatched cards as losses.  Now, with thunder walking the ridge, she saw they were the only future she actually owned, and that six years of hindsight had quietly taught her the language they were written in.  She read them forward for the first time, the way a person reads, and paid a pebble in advance in case she was wrong. 

Red was an ending on purpose, and a wafer does not need breaking.  Grinning at the loophole, she breathed on the seal, touched it with her tongue like a child testing snow, and felt it go to paste.  The flap opened unbroken. 

The slip inside was dry and whole: UNTIL TREES STAND OVER THE DOOR, THEN GIVE THE VAULT TO THE NEXT GREAT WIND. 

Trees had stood over the door for forty years while keepers guarded the word HOLD.  The order had not asked for obedience forever; it had asked to be finished, and decay had cut out the part that said so.  Vessa did not defy the directive, and she did not obey its ruin.  She went through it, the way you transcend a wall by finding it was a door. 

The vault wheel shrieked rust and turned.  Inside stood drawers of winged seed, elm and ash and lime, the trees the returning forest had never managed to bring back alone.  She dragged the drawers up the ramp with her shoulders burning, threw the blast door wide, and the storm came in like a hungry guest.  Seed lifted in spirals past her face and went out over the canopy, forty years late and exactly on time. 

Through the torn vines she could see the deer trail, and she finally read it too.  A path is a directive written in dirt, decaying unless someone walks it, and this one had been losing words for decades.  It had not wanted a strong walker; it had reached for the one reader who understands things only once they lie behind her, which is the only way any path is understood.  Or so she guessed; tomorrow, as always, would say whether she got her pebble back.
