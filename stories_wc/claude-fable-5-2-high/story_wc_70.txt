Three nights after the city-wide blackout, the dusk came down the color of nectar, and Marit Oyelaran opened her toll window at the foot of the lightning collection tower.  The causeway beneath it was still littered with dead pollinator drones, small brass bodies that had rained from the hive in the tower's crown when the grid collapsed.  The papers called it the drone beehive failure and counted the orchards that would go unpollinated.  Marit swept the bodies into rows each morning, because she could not call them trash.  She could not call anything what it was not.  Twenty years ago a stray arc from the tower had passed through her booth and through her, and since then any question put to her pulled the true answer out like a thread.  Commuters had learned not to ask how they looked.  Marit had learned something better: a truth blurted is a bill, but a truth found is a gift.  So beside her coin tray sat a red lacquer box, and in it lay matchboxes, each holding one match and one riddle in her small square hand.  Every traveler who paid the toll got one instead of a receipt, so that crossing the same causeway twice a day never became only crossing.  Tonight she had written just one, and she had written it for the engineer. 

Tobin Vasque arrived on foot with a reboot key on a lanyard and a clipboard under his arm.  He had the gray look of a man who had spent three days being blamed.  "Staff don't pay," he said.  "Staff don't," Marit agreed, "but you still get the box."  She slid the matchbox across the sill quickly, hoping to send him up the stairs before he could ask her anything.  He pocketed it without looking.  "You sweep up there, don't you?" he said.  "Did you see anything in the hive this morning?" 

The answer left her before she could fold it into anything kinder.  "Bees," she said.  "Living ones, wild ones, building in the dead comb." 

Tobin sighed and wrote a single word on his form, and she read it upside down: infestation.  "The reboot charge will clear them," he said, not unkindly.  "It sterilizes the comb before the drones wake." 

This was exactly what she had feared: the plain fact, handed over flat, had become an item to be cleared.  "That word is why I gave you the box," Marit said.  "The stairwell lamps are still out, so you will need the match anyway.  Read what is inside before you turn that key." 

"Is it going to waste my time?" 

"Yes," she said, because it would, "about four minutes of it." 

He almost smiled, since nobody in the city doubted Marit Oyelaran's arithmetic, and he went in. 

Three hundred steps wound up through the dark between the cold capacitor drums.  At the hive door Tobin stopped, because the silence he had come to repair was not silent.  He slid the matchbox open, struck the match, and read the paper curled in the tray.  "I fell so something else could rise; I hum without a wire; wake me and you bury what I hold." 

He pushed the door with his shoulder, and the tower revealed what no form had a line for.  Late light poured through the louvers like syrup, and in it the brass comb moved, furred and golden, ten thousand small lives walking over the machines that had failed to imitate them.  Wax, real wax, already bridged the charging cells.  The air smelled of clover that had not grown in the city since his grandmother's time.  The match burned down to his fingers before he remembered he was holding it. 

When he came down, the key was still on its lanyard and the form was folded in half.  "You told me the truth at the window," he said, "and I heard nothing.  Why riddles, Marit?" 

She could not have dodged the question, and for once she did not want to.  "Because people cross here every day until they stop seeing the tower, the water, each other," she said.  "I cannot lie to them, but I can make them climb a little for what is true, and then they keep it." 

Tobin looked up at the crown, where the last amber light was thick with wings.  "The Board meets tomorrow," he said.  "They will ask me whether the hive can be restored." 

"It has been," Marit said, which was only the truth.  She opened the lacquer box and set an empty matchbox and her pencil on the sill.  "Make them climb for it." 

Tobin began to write, and above them the tower hummed like a kept promise while the dusk went to honey and then to dark.
