The turbines kept humming after everything else went quiet.  Maren Voss stood in her shed above the cove, sanding a gull's wing from a spar of driftwood, and let the low note carry her thoughts the way a tide carries a cork.  Then the harbor radio crackled: a solar storm had blinded the satellites, and every boat past the reef had lost its GPS.  A real storm was coming in behind the invisible one.  Six fishing boats and the wind farm's service launch were out there, and the only way home was through the Needle, a stone archway the sea had been throwing itself at for ten thousand years.  Miss the channel by a boat's width and the reef took your keel. 

Maren set down her rasp.  She was sixty-one and still considered herself a beginner at most things, which was how she liked it; this year alone she had taught herself knots, lichen names, and enough turbine mechanics to argue with the technicians.  Tonight she needed to learn something older. 

From a tin on the shelf she took her great-grandmother's magic lantern slide, a square of painted glass no bigger than her palm.  Old Ingrid had kept the harbor light and had shown the slide at winter socials, and she always said it must be read three times. 

Maren held it to the work lamp.  The first reading was plain: the Needle at dusk, pretty as a postcard.  She tilted the glass, and the second reading surfaced, two pinpricks scratched through the paint, one low inside the arch and one high on the headland cairn.  Leading lights.  Keep one stacked above the other and you were in the channel; let them drift apart and you were on the rocks.  The third reading ran around the border in letters fine as hair: the names of keepers' families and fishing families, bound by oath to hold both lights for whoever came after.  A picture, a map, a promise, each layer resting on the one beneath. 

The cairn had been bulldozed four years ago for the wind farm's substation, and half the village had not forgiven it.  Maren looked at the last gap in the border, a space Ingrid had left unlettered, and understood that the oath had been built to grow. 

She phoned the substation.  "Dele, it's Maren, and I need your floodlight on the roof mast, pointed seaward, steady, until I say." 

"My crew's out there too," Dele said.  "Tell me where to aim." 

"Where the cairn stood." 

Then she loaded her barrow with driftwood, rope, and a storm lantern, and ran for the Needle. 

The archway roared like a throat.  Spray came through it sideways, and the ledge where the old lamp bracket had rusted away was slick with weed.  Her first tripod, lashed in a hurry, went over in one gust.  Maren laughed out loud, because the wind had just taught her something, and she was never too proud for a lesson.  Every sculpture she had ever made began the same way: three plain sticks holding up whatever intricate thing she wanted to say.  Complexity needed a simple scaffold, and tonight the intricate thing was seven crews, two grudging communities, and a hundred years of promises.  She wedged the legs wider into three cracks in the rock, let the wind press the frame down instead of over, and hung the lantern from its apex.  It held. 

Above the headland, Dele's floodlight opened like a white eye.  On the radio clipped to her collar she said, "All boats, this is the Needle: put the small light under the big light and come home."  One sentence, simple enough to steer by. 

They came in a line, running lights wobbling and then steadying as each skipper found the stack.  The service launch came last, and its technicians raised their arms to her as they slid beneath the arch.  Maren stayed until the count reached seven, soaked through, listening to the turbines hum behind the gale like a held chord.  In that drone she could almost hear Ingrid's projector hissing, the same light passing through the same glass toward people not yet born. 

By morning she had decided to reimagine the slide rather than copy it.  She would carve it large in driftwood for the harbor wall: the arch, the two lights, and the border of names, with the wind crews lettered into Ingrid's gap.  And she would leave a new space empty at the end, because she did not yet know who was coming, and she looked forward to finding out.
