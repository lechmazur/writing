Odile Marrow oiled the hand crank before attempting anything mystical, because rust has never respected prophecy.  She was the last celestial signal interpreter in Antenna Reach, a city of radio towers lashed together with cable and catwalk and hung across the Vesper Gorge like laundry.  Forty years ago the stars had stopped talking, and the residents, being sensible, had moved somewhere with soil.  Odile stayed, partly from stubbornness and partly because her mother had taught her the interpreter's one rule: find the beginning in every ending, then write it down legibly. 

Tonight was lunar perigee, and the moon rode so close that the whole city leaned toward it.  Cables hummed.  Guy wires went taut as fiddle strings, and the towers, pulled a finger's width nearer the sky, caught things they missed all year.  If the silence had anything left in it, tonight was when it would show. 

She climbed to the receiver room on Tower Nine with a thermos, a logbook, and the fragment.  The fragment was a wedge of pink terrazzo edged in brass, broken from a Hollywood star on a sidewalk the sea had long since taken.  Her grandmother had carried it inland in a coat pocket, and the family had used it ever since as a paperweight for the logbooks.  Three brass letters survived on it, the tail end of the name of a silent film actress nobody could look up anymore. 

Odile set it on the open log and put on the headphones.  Static, as always, forty years of it, a gray wool sound.  She began to tune, easing the dial across the bands the way her mother had, hunting for a voice.  At the seventh band she found what she found every perigee: a gap.  It lasted less than a second, a thin clean absence in the noise, sharp at both edges, a shard of hush.  Every interpreter before her had logged it the same way: dead air, signal ended, nothing to report. 

Odile uncapped her pen to write the same, and stopped, because the fragment under her wrist was trembling.  She was a practical woman, so she checked the obvious first: the tower's sway, the generator, her own pulse.  Not those.  The brass rim buzzed only when the gap came around, as though the stone knew its cue. 

Very well, she thought, and started discarding assumptions one at a time, like emptying pockets before the wash.  Assumption one: a signal is a sound.  Assumption two: silence means the sender has gone.  Assumption three: a paperweight is only a paperweight.  The woman whose star this was had held a million people rapt without one audible word; hush had been her entire instrument. 

Odile unscrewed the detector housing, lifted out the dead quartz, and wedged the terrazzo in its place, brass edge against the contact, with a folded logbook page to stop it rattling.  Then she did what no interpreter had done, and tuned away from the voices they had all been hunting, toward the gap itself.  She narrowed the band until the static fell off on either side like banks along a river. 

The shard widened.  It was not empty.  It was the quiet of a theater after the lights go down, of blank leader ticking through a projector gate, of a breath drawn before the first line.  It felt serenely electric, calm as deep water and live as a wire, and it ran down the headphones into her teeth.  Inside it, slow as the moon's pull, came a pattern: long hush, short hush, long hush, the stars' old call sign rendered in pauses instead of tones. 

They had not stopped talking forty years ago.  They had changed reels, and nobody in Antenna Reach had thought to listen to the splice. 

Odile laughed once, drank some tea, and got to work, because perigee would last only until dawn and there was a great deal of quiet to transcribe.  She logged the frequency, the cable tension, the seating of the fragment, and the angle of the moon.  She copied the pauses in neat columns, legibly, as instructed. 

By sunrise the moon had slackened its grip, the towers had settled back, and the hush had thinned to a sliver again, but she had six pages.  The first line, once she worked it out over cold toast, was not complicated.  It said: end of first program, stand by for the second.  So her mother's rule held: the ending had been a beginning all along, merely badly tuned. 

Odile pinned a copy to the door of the empty post office, lit the arrival lamp at the gorge road, and went to oil the crank on Tower Ten, because a city resuming business ought to look open.
