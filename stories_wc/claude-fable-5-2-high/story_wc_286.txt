Tavi came to the oasis with a kick wheel strapped to her back and one question she refused to dress up.  Why do some places resist change?  The wasteland had scorched her village to glass in an afternoon, yet here, forty miles into the burn, date palms still stood over rows of empty hives.  The beekeepers were gone, and nobody could say when.  She did not coddle clay, and she did not coddle herself, so she built a kiln from the rubble of the honey house and got to work. 

In the keeper's hut she found a pane of faded lantern glass, amber gone nearly to straw, its frame long rusted away.  When she propped it on the sill, the sun threw a pale honey-colored patch onto the floor, and inside that patch the floor was different.  Swept.  A sandal rested there that did not exist when she reached for it. 

Tavi sat with that for a full breath, then another.  Fear was only weather; it could pass through without being offered tea. 

So she studied the thing by following sun patterns, carrying the glass from sill to stump to hive lid as the day turned, chalking where each patch of amber fell and what it showed.  Morning light showed an oasis in full hum, women in veils lifting dripping frames.  Noon showed ash, the palms black stumps, nothing spared.  Afternoon showed a third place, half burned, where one old keeper limped between dying hives with a cracked bowl. 

The oasis was not one place that had survived.  It was a knot of fractured timelines, each a shard of what the fire might have done, wedged together like a pot badly mended.  That explained the palms.  It did not explain why the shards held. 

For weeks she threw pots and watched.  Her chalk marks crept across the yard, and slowly a pattern stood up out of them.  The seams between timelines were not random.  They bent.  Every fracture curved toward the same few spots: the trough where she had watched keepers set out sugar water in drought, the bench where a stranger was given shade, the small grave dug for a dead queen. 

The place was phototropic to kindness.  It leaned toward each small mercy the way a seedling leans toward a window, and where it leaned, it rooted. 

Tavi distrusted pretty answers, so she tested it.  She had noticed that the seams showed clearest at dusk, in the minutes when chimney smoke flattens under cooling air and lies along the palm tops like a lid.  In those minutes the amber patch stopped being a picture and became a door left ajar. 

So she threw a bowl, wide and plain, and fired it hard.  It was not her best work, and she said so aloud, because flattery ruins a potter faster than cracks do.  It would hold water.  That was the whole of its job. 

The next evening she fed the kiln, filled the bowl at the spring, and set the lantern glass on the trough's edge so the low sun passed through it.  The smoke rose straight, wavered, then spread flat. 

In the amber, the old keeper appeared, kneeling by a trough gone dry, her cracked bowl in pieces beside her.  Around her, the last hives had gone quiet. 

Tavi did not hesitate, and she did not hurry.  She slid the full bowl into the light.  Her hands went cold to the wrist, as if dipped in another season, and then the weight left them. 

The old woman stared at the bowl, then straight at Tavi, with the look of someone who had done this same thing for others and never expected it back.  She drank a mouthful and poured the rest into the trough for her bees.  Of course she did. 

The ground began to thrum.  Tavi felt it in her shins first, a low, steady note, like a wheel finding center.  The seam by the trough bent inward and closed, the way a crack closes when clay is pressed with a wet thumb.  Then the thrum rose out of the ground and into the hives, and it had wings in it.  Bees, a thin gold thread of them, crossed the last light and settled on the wet rim of the trough. 

Tavi sat down in the dust and let herself be corrected.  She had asked the wrong question.  This place did not resist change; it had been chosen, again and again, by tired people with a little water to spare, until choosing became its grain.  Nothing held unless someone kept holding it. 

In the morning she moved the lantern glass to the sill, swept the hut, and wedged clay for a second bowl.
