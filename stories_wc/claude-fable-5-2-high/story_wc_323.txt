Six months after the alien vessel sank into the trench beside Lowlight, Ilse Varga still began every class the same way.  "There is no horizon down here," the navigation instructor told her pilots, "so we steer by edges."  Edge geometry was the benthic city's oldest craft: sonar found the lip of a ridge, the corner of a canyon, the rim of a vent, and the angles between those edges told you where you were in the dark.  For nine generations that had been enough. 

Then the vessel came down like a slow leaf, its three occupants already dead, and enough was over.  Inside the hull, the salvage divers found a curved wall pricked with thousands of bright points, one of them ringed.  A sky, the archivists said; a home, marked the way a child marks a home.  The council wanted a crew sent to the surface to find that ringed star in the real sky, so that Lowlight would at least know which way to face when it mourned, and perhaps, someday, which way to call.  They asked Ilse to train the crew.  She told them the truth: she had never seen a star, and before she could teach anyone, she would have to learn to navigate by starlight herself. 

Lowlight had split over the visitors.  Some lit lamps outside the cold hall where the bodies lay and called them angels; others wanted the trench sealed and the hull buried in silt.  Ilse was balancedly curious, which pleased no one.  She visited the cold hall each week and looked at the long folded limbs without worship and without flinching, giving wonder and dread the same weight, the way she weighed two sonar returns before trusting either.  They had been navigators too, she thought; they had simply run out of edges. 

The problem of the sky kept her awake until she remembered the link.  It hung above her bunk, a single boat anchor chain link, rust-pitted and heavy as a fist, cut from the last surface ship before her ancestors rode the ballast down.  Her grandmother had left it to her with one instruction that had never made sense: hold it out, and the world will fit inside. 

Ilse carried it to the archive, stood at the center of the copied star wall's curve, and held the link at arm's length.  The iron oval framed a patch of points, and its inner edges, she saw, cut a fixed angle out of anything she looked at.  A star was only an edge too far away to touch.  Edge geometry did not have to end at the seafloor; it could expand. 

For weeks she measured the wall in links: four links from the ringed star to a bright triangle, one and a half from the triangle to a long crooked line.  She taught her pilots the same measures, each with a ring of scrap steel filed to match, and they grumbled that she was teaching them to read a ceiling. 

The ascent took two days of slow decompression in a tender never built to leave the dark.  They broke the surface at night, as she had planned, and Ilse climbed through the hatch first. 

Nothing in her training had prepared her for distance.  All her life the horizon had been folded up tight around her, a few meters of lamplight and then rock; now it lay unfolded in every direction, a black line with no wall behind it, and above it the alien wall made real and without end.  One of the pilots began to cry quietly, and nobody told him to stop. 

Ilse's hands shook, so she did what her grandmother had said and held the link out.  The sky fit inside, one oval at a time.  She found the long crooked line, stepped one and a half links to the bright triangle, then four more, edge to edge, across the cold.  There, low over the water, steady and small and yellowish, was the star they had ringed. 

It was so ordinary that her throat closed.  Three navigators had crossed all of that to sink beside a city that had forgotten the sky, and the only thing she could give them was a bearing.  She gave it, reading the angle aloud while a pilot wrote it down: a heading home, taken by starlight, for those who could not use it. 

Then she turned to her crew, who were staring at their steel rings as if the rings had grown.  "Lesson one of unfolded horizons," Ilse said, her voice not quite steady.  "There is a horizon up here, and past it, more edges than we will ever finish measuring."  She raised the old link again, and they began.
