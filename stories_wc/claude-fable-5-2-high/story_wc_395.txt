Lattice was dying politely, and Imani Reyes, its corporate memory keeper, had been hired to stay until the lights went out.  Her office was the social network archive itself, a cold vault of racks where twenty years of other people's lives waited to be sealed.  Each night the verification routine played one second of every audio post before locking it, so she worked across the echo of forgotten songs, a chorus of beginnings with no one left to finish them. 

The envelope arrived in March, addressed only to Whoever Keeps What You Kept.  Inside was an embroidered patch from the Kestrel 9 mission, the one that had crashed in the Mojave on reentry, its silver bird fraying at one wing.  The letter was from Ruth Halloran.  Her husband had died aboard Kestrel 9, and her son Joss had worn the patch on his jacket every day afterward, until he vanished at nineteen, twelve years ago, with the jacket still hanging in his closet.  Three days later his Lattice account was deleted, and the police took that as a goodbye.  The case went cold.  "He would not have left this," Ruth wrote, "and he would not have erased his father's photographs, so tell me where they went." 

Imani pinned the patch above her monitor, because it was the question she had carried for years.  She had deleted her own dead father's profile once, a single click, and had wondered ever since where a click could send a man.  Official policy said nowhere.  But Imani was brightly attentive, the kind of person who noticed which stair creaked and which coworker had stopped humming, and absences had always felt to her like objects with edges. 

She searched for Joss Halloran and found the edges: tagged photographs with a gray square where his face belonged, comment threads answering someone who was not there, a playlist that skipped.  Then she began to see the number.  Every broken pointer where his posts had been ended in 2291.  She told herself it was coincidence, until the nightly log blamed every skipped song on error 2291, and the power audit listed rack 2291 drawing current in an aisle the floor plan said was empty.  The number was not haunting her.  It was an address, stamped by the system on everything that pointed at something removed. 

She walked the aisle at midnight while one-second songs flickered overhead, and found the rack behind an unlabeled cage, warm and blinking.  Deleted files, it turned out, went nowhere at all.  Years ago some engineer had decided that erasing cost more than forgetting, so every deletion was simply unlisted and shelved here, in partition 2291, and then the engineer had left and the company forgot it had forgotten. 

Joss was there, whole.  So were his father's photographs, a song he had recorded on a cheap guitar, and the deletion request itself.  It had come from a phone that was not his, in a desert town beside the Kestrel crash site, three days after anyone last saw him.  Joss had not said goodbye; someone had said it for him. 

The next morning, counsel sent the wind-down certificate for her signature: all user-deleted data confirmed unrecoverable, all unlisted hardware cleared for shredding on Friday.  If she reported the rack, liability would shred it by Wednesday.  Imani had never falsified a record in her life; keeping the truth was the entire job.  She looked at the silver bird with its fraying wing, and she chose to lie.  She signed the certificate, wrote that aisle nine held only dead cooling units scheduled for a later haul, and bought herself a week.  Then she called the county cold case unit and told a tired detective exactly what to subpoena, down to the partition number, before anyone could unplug it. 

The preservation order arrived Thursday.  Counsel was furious, then careful, then quiet.  Imani mailed the patch back to Ruth with a short note: nothing he made was ever gone, Mrs. Halloran; it was only waiting, and now the detectives are listening. 

It was not an answer.  Joss was still missing, and a rack of preserved posts was only the outline of a young man, drawn more sharply.  On her last night, Imani overrode the one-second limit and let his guitar song play through the empty vault from first note to last.  Above her monitor, four pinholes marked where the patch had been, and she left them there for whoever came to turn off the lights.
