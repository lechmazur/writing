On Knot Hill, plumb lines hung crooked and rain fell slightly uphill.  Oda had lived there eleven years as the valley's celestial signal interpreter, listening to starlight that arrived folded by the hill's wrong gravity.  Her work was to unbend it: to lay each pulse flat, compare it with its neighbors, and tell the farmers what kind of year was coming. 

This spring she had told them dry.  They sowed dust millet in every terrace, and then it rained for nineteen days.  Now the wrong seeds were up, yellow and drowning, and nobody in the valley had said one unkind word to her, which was worse. 

So she climbed to the summit hut, where the Knot was tightest and her ears ached, to do the thing interpreters were told never to do.  The first ledger lay there under a stone that weighed nothing on Tuesdays.  Every forecast ever given to the valley was written in it, and the old story held that time folded so close at the summit that a line amended in the ledger was a season amended in the world.  Strike out dry, write wet, and the valley would wake having planted marsh rice all along. 

Only one pen could mark those pages.  She found it in the ledger's spine: a fountain pen with a nib hammered from meteorite iron, three tines instead of two, dark as a burnt seed.  The tines were curled back on themselves like a dead spider's legs, and the barrel was dry to the threads. 

Oda sat down, took the pliers from her signal kit, and began to unbend the nib the way she unbent starlight, a little at a time, checking each tine against the others.  It took an hour. 

While her hands worked, her mind did what it always did.  Her teacher had called interpretation threshing: no pulse means anything alone, so you beat the whole sheaf of them against one another until the grain drops out.  This flicker matters only because that one followed it; that one only because a third did not.  Meaning lived in the contingencies, never in the points. 

So she threshed her mistake.  Because the millet drowned, the Pell children had come up the hill with a basket of eggs to ask if the stars were angry, and she had laughed for the first time in a month.  Because the terraces flooded, old Tamsin had opened her hoarded rice beds to the Harrows, with whom she had not spoken since the boundary quarrel.  Because Oda had been wrong, the valley had started climbing the hill to check her figures, and two of the young ones could now read a pulse chart nearly as well as she could.  Each of those hung from the wrong seeds like fruit from one stem.  Write wet, and she could not say which would survive the correction.  She would also be left holding a clean spring she had learned nothing from, a harvest with no chaff and therefore no grain. 

The last tine came straight.  She opened the ledger to her own entry, set the dry nib beside the word, and only then noticed the margins. 

They were scored with inkless marks, hundreds of them, three fine grooves apiece, beside frost that came early and floods called late.  Her teacher's hand was there, pressed hard next to a hailstorm he had never once mentioned.  Every interpreter before her had climbed up here ashamed, straightened this same nib, and found it dry.  Every one of them had left a scratch instead of a correction, and then, she supposed, bent the tines back to buy the next fool an hour. 

Oda laughed, and the laugh fell slightly uphill.  Had the pen ever held ink?  Could a wet nib truly have rewritten a spring?  She did not know, and she knew the exact size and shape of her not knowing.  Confidently uncertain, her teacher had called it, the only honest posture for anyone who reads bent light.  This spring she had forgotten the second word; next time the valley would get her doubt along with her forecast, and could plant a little of both. 

She pressed the nib into the margin beside dry and drew her three grooves among the others.  It felt like signing a guest book in a house where everyone had broken the same plate.  Then she curled the tines back with the pliers, gently, slid the pen into the spine, and set the stone back on top. 

Below, the flooded terraces shone like starlight laid flat, and small figures were already wading out with Tamsin's rice seedlings.  Oda unbent her own back, sore from the hour and the month, and went down to help them replant.
