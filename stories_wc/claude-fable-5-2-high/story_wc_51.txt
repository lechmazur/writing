After the first snowmelt, when the pass opened like a throat clearing, the burdened began climbing to the bronze grotto.  Bell-founders had poured there once, and their spilled metal still sheathed the limestone walls, green-gold beneath a roof cracked wide to starlight.  Ilse, the fractal weaver, waited inside at a loom framed from the ribs of her father's first whale.  He had gone under the ice chasing his last, and nobody had ever been able to tell her what that weighed.  So she collected the weights of other burdens, hoping to find by comparison the measure of her own. 

A widow's ring, a ruined seed sack, a letter never sent: each hung from her whalebone balance against river pebbles until the beam lay level.  Then she wove a row of exactly that weight, and inside the row a smaller row, and inside that a smaller, because every burden holds little copies of itself.  Each row overlapped the last like slate on a roof, and where they overlapped, sound died.  Travelers called it the shingled hush, and they walked back down the pass lighter for leaving their silence layered in her cloth. 

Ilse did not walk down.  The grotto had no door, the pass no gate, yet she was boundary-lessly trapped, because a pattern that repeats inward forever has no edge, and a weaver cannot leave a cloth without one.  Nine springs she had sat there, dreaming of baleen and green water, while meltwater ticked through the roof and left its lime on everything that stayed still.  It had long ago calcified the burner of her own lamp into a white knuckle, so she wove by starlight and by touch. 

This spring the first climber was a girl named Perrin, mud to the knees, holding out a kerosene lamp burner in both hands.  No font, no chimney: only the brass collar, the little wheel, the flat slot where a wick had been. 

"The thaw took Gran's house off the hill," Perrin said.  "This is what I found, and I need to know what it weighs." 

Ilse hung it from the balance, and it rose against a single pebble, light as a wren.  The girl's face said that could not be the answer, and Ilse, who had worn that face for nine years, did not offer it.  Instead she chipped the dead burner off her old lamp, screwed Perrin's onto the font where old oil still slept, fed it a wick of loom thread, and struck a light.  The flame smoked and guttered in the draft from the grotto mouth. 

"You have it broadside," Perrin said.  "Gran said a flat wick only burns clean edge to the wind, like a boat." 

Ilse turned the lamp a quarter round.  The flame steadied into a blade, and its light raked low across the cloth, throwing a shadow under every row.  For the first time she saw how the rows lay: every lap pitched inward, toward the stool where she sat.  A roof shingled that way does not shed the rain; it pours the whole storm into the house.  Nine years of other people's silence had been running downhill into her, and she had called the pooling of it a search. 

"Help me turn it," she said. 

Together they walked the whalebone frame around on its worn feet until the laps faced the grotto mouth and the topmost row sat beneath the drip.  Meltwater struck, slid from row to row as it would down any honest roof, and ran off toward the pass.  They watched while the stars wheeled a hand's width across the broken ceiling.  Where the water left the cloth, the lowest threads began to calcify, stiffening pale as whalebone into something the weave had never had: a hem.  The pattern could still repeat inward forever, but now it stopped somewhere, and so could she. 

A burden, she understood, is weighed by orienting properly: not how heavy it is, but which way it runs.  Her father weighed the same as he always had; he had simply been pitched toward her, with nowhere to drain. 

"So what does it weigh?" Perrin asked, nodding at the lamp. 

"One pebble," Ilse said, "and it leans downhill, toward wherever you build next, so we will carry it that way." 

She lifted the lamp, kept its flame edge to the wind, and stepped past the place where a wall had never been.  Behind them the bronze walls held the starlight, and the cloth went on hardening, row over row, into a small stone roof for all that quiet.  Below, the pass ran loud with snowmelt.
