Three weeks after her resuscitation, Ilse Marrow went back alone to the funhouse where her heart had stopped.  The carnival had been abandoned since that night, and the building had ripened in its solitude like fruit left in a drawer.  Mildew bloomed in candy stripes across the painted clown mouth of the entrance, sweet-smelling, almost pretty, and she breathed it in more deeply than she meant to. 

For twenty years she had been the whispering advisor to Maestro Corvane, the impresario, standing at his shoulder and murmuring which debts to pay and which rivals to ruin.  She knew the trade's one law: a whisper rules only while a single ear holds it. 

Now something stood at her own shoulder. 

It had arrived with the photograph.  On closing night Corvane had posed the whole company in the hall of mirrors, and when the flash powder went off, her heart went with it.  The plate caught her mid-fall with a pale shape bending over her, and since then she had been photographically cursed: every lens, every window, every spoon showed that same glow at her back.  It was the color of pearl and of pus, and she could not stop looking for it.  It whispered, too, in her own professional cadence, advising her against stairs, against sleep, against the next beat of her mended heart. 

Ilse recognized a colleague, and colleagues could be played.  Every advisor she had ever known shared one vanity: none could leave an error uncorrected.  So she bought a secondhand dream dictionary, since the thing was boldest while she slept, and began writing nonsense in its margins before bed.  Beside MIRROR she wrote, To dream of your reflection means you are absent and also watching.  Beside FALLING: You land before you leave. 

Each morning the notes had been amended in handwriting like hers but slanted the wrong way, fussy and contemptuous.  It let most paradoxes stand, improving them, enjoying itself.  Then, beside CONFESSION, she wrote, To tell everything is to lose nothing and to keep nothing, and in the morning the entry had been scratched out so savagely the page was torn through. 

There it was, the flinch.  Fear was only a whisperer, and it was terrified of the one thing that had always terrified her: being overheard. 

Which was why she had come here, where the luminance was strongest.  The hall of mirrors had never let go of the flash.  Weeks later the glass still held it, a lingering light like milk on the turn, and in every warped pane the same scene hung faintly: a woman falling, a brightness leaning down to greet her.  It was lovely.  That was the nauseating part.  Her skin crawled, and she walked toward it anyway, the way a tongue goes to a sore. 

"You should not be here," the thing whispered, close as breath.  "Your heart is a borrowed thing." 

"Then advise me," Ilse said, in the ruined rasp the breathing tube had left her. 

She touched the clasp at her throat.  It had fastened Corvane's opera cloak, the one he had flung over her on the floor while a carnival strongman pounded her chest back to life; she had worn the cloak home from the hospital and never returned it.  The clasp was two silver heads that hooked together, one mouth pressed forever to the other's ear.  It had flared in the flash, the last thing she saw, and its imprint was still bruised into her breastbone. 

She unhooked it.  The heads parted, the cloak slid from her shoulders, and with her bruise bared to forty glowing mirrors she began to unbosom herself, not in a whisper but as loudly as her throat allowed. 

"I was dead for four minutes, and it was beautiful." 

"Stop," it hissed. 

"I saw that light and I wanted it.  When they hauled me back I was furious, and I have been ashamed ever since, and I am afraid because some part of me is still leaning toward it.  That is everything you know about me.  Now the whole house knows." 

Her voice went caroming down the crooked corridors, multiplied, public, impossible to take back.  At her shoulder the whisper thinned to a wet little clicking and then to nothing, an advisor with no secret left to sell. 

The glow did not leave.  It hung in the mirrors, soft as rot, exquisite, and she let herself look at it for a long, honest minute.  It was only light now, and light could wait. 

She left the whispering head on the floor among the dead moths, pocketed the listening one, and walked out through the clown's mouth into the morning.
