The fog came in the way old knowledge leaves, slowly and without a sound.  Oda Brenn watched it roll across the harbor from the end of the Golden Dock, where a century of spilled flax oil had stained the planks the color of honey beneath the new snow.  She was a collector of seeds, and seeds had made her a philosopher.  A seed, she liked to say, is a question the dead put to the living, and the only honest answer is to wait. 

In her coat pocket, in a twist of waxed paper, lay eleven seeds of sea flax, the last she had found anywhere on the coast.  For four winters she had tried to wake others like them, and for four springs the soil had stayed dark.  The plant had once clothed this harbor in sails and nets.  The women who grew and spun it were called the secret weavers, because they worked at night, wrote nothing down, and taught only daughters.  When cheap hemp arrived by steamer, the daughters found other work, and the method of waking the seed went quiet with them. 

Oda did not believe it had died.  People who refuse to write still need somewhere to keep what they know. 

That belief had brought her to Tam Hollis, the oldest fisherman on the dock, who sat on an upturned crate mending nothing, his breath joining the fog.  Beside him lay a coil of pale rope, older than his boat, knotted along its whole length in ways no sailor would bother with.  At its end, bound in a cage of cord, hung a rough gray tube of fulgurite glass, sand fused hollow by a lightning strike. 

"My grandmother's," Tam said.  "She called it her mooring line, but she never moored anything with it." 

"And the glass?" Oda asked. 

"A weight, I always thought, or a charm against storms." 

It was her seventh morning at his crate, each one spent pouring tea and asking for nothing, because a thing held three generations cannot be pried loose, only coaxed.  Now Tam lifted the coil and set it in her lap. 

"Read it, if it can be read," he said.  "Somebody should know what she was saying before I go." 

Oda took off her gloves.  The fog had reached the dock now, and the harbor shrank to a small gold room with walls of white.  Somewhere a bell buoy spoke once and was swallowed.  She let the rope pass through her fingers the way she let a year pass, without hurrying it toward meaning. 

The knots came in families.  "Nine of these in a row," she said, holding up a small hard knot. 

Tam squinted.  "Ice hitch; you only tie that on a frozen line." 

Nine nights of frost, then.  After them came three loose knots, gritty to the touch, and when she tasted her fingertip it was salt, old brine dried deep in the lay.  Three tides of soaking.  Last, just above the glass, sat a single knot scorched black on purpose, and below it the cord cage, which was not a cage at all but a cradle woven to hold the tube upright. 

Oda sat very still, hearing the water breathe under the planks.  "It is not a weight," she said.  "It is the bed." 

Frost to crack the seed's coat, brine to tell it the sea was near, and then the lightning's own hollow, glass that drinks a little water and holds no rot.  The weavers had not hidden their method; they had tied it where only patient hands would find it. 

Tam looked at the rope as if it had spoken in his grandmother's voice.  "All those years I thought she was just fidgeting," he said. 

Oda unfolded the waxed paper and chose one seed, dark and flat as a fleck of night.  She packed her empty teacup with snow and pressed the seed into it, where the first of its nine frosts could find it.  You cannot order a seed to wake, she thought; you can only coax it, saying in its own slow language that the world is ready.  If this one failed, there were ten more, and after them other coasts, and she had as many winters as it took.  Her patience, like the sea's, had no far shore. 

"Will you show me the ice hitch?" she asked.  "A method only lasts while someone's hands still know it." 

Tam cut a length of twine, and in the gold and white hush, with the fog pressing close as a held breath, the old man and the seed collector began, knot by knot, to weave.
