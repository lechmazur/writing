Odile Varr sold counterfeit emotions out of an amber grotto, and she was fond of her customers the way a lighthouse is fond of ships.  The grotto sat above the tide line, its walls fossil resin the color of strong tea, and at dusk the low sun came through the sea mouth and lit the whole place like the inside of a lamp.  She kept dusk hours for that reason, and for another.  Amber holds a charge.  Each evening she rubbed the wall beside her bench with a lambswool cloth until the fine hairs on her forearms stood up, and after that she could feel a person coming before she could see them.  Bodies disturb a field according to what they carry: fear prickled, longing dragged, shame lay flat as wet silk.  It was a way of knowing people without the inconvenience of feeling along with them, and for twenty years she had considered that the ideal arrangement.  Lately she was less sure.  Insight without empathy had begun to seem like a perfume with no base note, bright for a minute and then simply gone. 

The man who came in that evening was named Tobiah Marsh, and he prickled nowhere.  "My brother's funeral is tomorrow," he said.  "I need grief, and I am told you sell a convincing one." 

"I sell a counterfeit one," Odile said, because she was professionally thorough and began every sale with the disclaimer.  She opened her ledger and asked the questions she asked everyone: the brother's age, the last words exchanged, what the house had smelled like when they were boys.  Tobiah answered in half truths, which she noted without offense, since half truths were her raw material.  He said they had not been close.  He said he felt nothing but relief that the long illness was over.  He said the house had smelled of pipe smoke and boiled wool, and when he said it the hairs on her left arm lay down all at once, as if a hand had smoothed them. 

So the grief was there.  It was only buried under the relief, heavy and uncharged, like a coin at the bottom of a coat.  Odile did not tell him this.  People who are told what they feel tend to argue, and she had never once won. 

Instead she took two blotter petals from the tin, the stiff white paper every fragrance chemist uses for testing, which she cut by hand into the shape of magnolia petals because customers held a petal more gently than a strip.  She built the accord in front of him: a sharp saline top note that would sting the eyes within a minute, and under it, in quantities he would not think to question, pipe tobacco and lanolin.  She dipped both petals, gave him one, and kept its twin, as she always kept a control. 

"The top is the counterfeit," she told him.  "It will make you weep on schedule, and it fades in an hour.  Wait here for the drydown, so I can see how it settles on you." 

That was also a half truth.  What she did not say was that the top note existed only to open a door, and that the base was his own boyhood, which no chemist can counterfeit, only return. 

He sat on the driftwood bench and held the petal under his nose while the sun went down the wall.  The salt took him first, and he wept politely, like a man signing a form.  Odile watched, timed it, and wrote the figure down.  Then the light went from honey to rust, the top note thinned, and the transformation she had engineered began to complete itself. 

The field in the grotto changed.  Every hair on her arms leaned toward him, and she understood that the coin had come up out of the coat.  His shoulders dropped, and the weeping stopped being polite.  The real grief did not push the counterfeit aside; it rose and subsumed it, the way a tide subsumes the little channel dug to invite it in. 

Ordinarily this was where she filed the control and reached for the receipt book.  Tonight she lifted the twin petal and breathed it in.  Pipe smoke, boiled wool, and for the first time she let what her skin already knew travel inward: a cold house, an older boy holding the door, a debt of tenderness never paid.  It hurt in a small, exact way, and she found she could stand it. 

She did not cross the grotto, since the kindest thing she owned was distance, but she let the lamp go unlit so he could finish in the dark.  When he rose to pay, she charged him half, which was the honest fraction.
