The bells of Skarn Hollow had hung fettered for two winters, their clappers bound in sealskin since the reeve struck music from the Ledger of Essentials.  Oil was essential, the ledger said, and salt, and rope.  Wonder was not. 

Ilsa, the last prism-song bell ringer, climbed down into the ice cavern anyway, as she did each dusk, to sit beneath the silent bronze and breathe.  She was synesthetically blessed, her grandmother had called it: every sound arrived in her eyes as color, and every color hummed.  Dripping meltwater was pale green.  Her own pulse was a slow rust, rising and settling, rising and settling. 

In the cavern's center stood the block of white marble the village had traded forty sleds of cod to buy, meant to become a memorial for the nineteen the lean winter took.  Halvard the sculptor circled it every evening with his chisel and never once struck. 

"I cannot find them in it," he told Ilsa.  "The stone is only loud." 

She understood, because to her the untouched marble blared a flat, unbroken white. 

From her coat she drew her grandmother's amber music sheet, a pane of resin thin as a fish scale, holding no written notes at all, only bubbles and the trapped wings of midges.  It could be read one way only: through the colors of the aurora, when the northern lights soaked down through the cavern roof and the amber sorted them into a score.  Scratched along its edge was the single instruction her grandmother left: begin on a white note not your own.  For two winters Ilsa had not known what that meant.  Watching Halvard lower his chisel yet again, she knew. 

She went up to the reeve's house that night and set the amber on the open ledger. 

"Your own law allows a trial of use," Ilsa said.  "A thing is essential if necessary work cannot begin without it."  "Come down when the lights are strong and watch; then certify wonder as essential, or I will bind the bells myself forever." 

Reeve Marit, whose husband was one of the nineteen, looked at the amber a long while, then reached for her coat. 

Three nights later the aurora came, and the cavern filled like a slow-breathing lung, green swelling to violet, violet thinning back to green.  Ilsa moved from rope to rope to unfetter each clapper, and the bells hung free and waiting, swaying a little in the cold.  She held the amber up to the roof.  Light poured through it, and the bubbles and wings threw a ladder of colored shadows across the marble, each color a bell she knew by name.  Between the colors lay wide bands of dark, and those, she finally saw, were the true music. 

"Strike anywhere," she told Halvard, "and I will catch it." 

His hands shook, steadied, and at the moment the sculptor's chisel first struck marble, a crack of pure white leapt across Ilsa's sight.  A white note, not her own.  She pulled the first rope and split it. 

The low bell answered in deep red, the next in gold, the next in the green of the sky itself, each tone peeling a color from that one hard stroke the way a prism unbraids daylight.  Then, as the amber commanded, she let the ropes go still. 

The tones faded up into the ice, and what remained was not the old dead quiet of the fettered years.  It was a sculpted hush, a silence with edges and hollows, cut to shape by the sounds around it exactly as a figure is cut by taking stone away. 

Into that hush Halvard struck again, and Ilsa rang again, and rested again.  Stroke, color, stillness; stroke, color, stillness; the cavern rocked between them like a cradle.  Chips fell.  The white blare of the marble broke into planes, and in the pauses Halvard could finally hear where the stone wanted to be absent: the curve of a bowed shoulder, a net over an arm, a face turned toward shore. 

Marit stood with the ledger shut against her chest.  She had no gift for seeing sound, yet in one long rest she heard her husband's absence given a shape she could stand beside, and she wept without hurry. 

When the aurora thinned and the amber went blank, Ilsa stilled the last bell with her palm. 

"The work began," she said quietly.  "It could not begin without this." 

Marit opened the ledger on the marble's rough shoulder, and beneath oil and salt and rope she wrote wonder, and pressed the village seal beside it.  The bells swayed on, unbound, slower and slower, toward rest.
