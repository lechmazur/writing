The Meridian Works floated on the Vitriol Sea, a workshop of gears and cogs paddling in slow circles so that no plank sat long enough in the acid to dissolve.  Tonight the great escapement wheel had cracked, the paddles were slowing, and the hull had begun to hiss.  Tova Brine, the last alchemist aboard, stood over the broken wheel and trusted none of her own measurements.  Her master, Oriel Vane, had gone over the rail a month ago, mid-lesson, and the sea had not returned so much as a button.  He left her half an education and his key ring, which held no keys, only a charm: a brass disc the size of a thumbnail, pierced with seven tiny holes.  "When the wheel breaks, ask the Founder," he had said, "and let the charm find its place."  The Founder was a bronze statue four centuries old, scarred by generations of acid spray before she was lowered into the ballast well, the cistern of sweet rainwater that kept the Works upright.  Tova half wanted to cast a new wheel instead, smooth and seamless, as if nothing had ever broken, but she doubted her hands, and the hull would not last the cooling.  She lifted the floor hatch, clenched the charm in her fist, filled her lungs, and dropped into the dark water.  The Founder loomed below, green and vast, her skin a galaxy of pockmarks, thousands of them, from brow to boot.  Tova pressed the disc to the bronze cheek, then the throat, feeling with her fingertips for seven pits that answered seven holes, and found nothing before her chest began to burn.  She surfaced gasping, and in the space between breaths she did the one thing Vane had never needed to correct: she narrated.  "First patch," she told the laboring gears.  "The day I arrived, nine years old, and Vane let me wind the smallest clock."  It was her only sure craft: she quilted memories together, speaking each one aloud and stitching it to the next, because Vane had taught her that mending gold would not hold a thing whose story it had not been told.  She dived again.  Shoulder, sleeve, the hem of the bronze coat; the charm slid over craters that almost matched, six of seven, a cruelty.  Above, something groaned, and a thread of acid wept through a seam and smoked upon the water.  She rose, breathed, and stitched on.  "Second patch: the night the storm peeled the roof and we bailed until dawn, singing."  "Third: your hands, Oriel, steady over mine."  "Fourth: the rail, and the hiss, and no one left to tell me whether any of this ever mattered."  That was the true fear under all her doubting: not that she would sink, but that the sea would close over the Works and leave it meaning nothing at all.  She took a third breath and swam deeper than before, to the Founder's open left hand.  In the center of the palm her fingertips read seven pocks, and the charm settled onto them like a word onto its meaning, every hole over every pit.  She pressed; the palm swung down like a hatch, and from the hollow wrist she drew a sealed glass flask and a small, heavy cog.  She broke the surface and held them to the lamp.  The flask held lacquer thick with gold.  The cog was older than the statue, and it had been shattered and rejoined a dozen times, every fracture traced in a shining seam, no break hidden, each one honored.  The Works had never been whole; it had only ever been mended, by keepers as frightened as she was.  Vane had a word for it from some drowned country, kintsugi: mend with gold, and let the scar be the truest part.  Tova climbed out dripping and knelt at the escapement, her doubt still with her, but her intention finally larger than it.  She did not disguise the crack.  She poured the gold along it, narrating every patch again, arrival and storm and steady hands and the rail, quilting them into one telling while the lacquer listened and took hold.  The wheel shuddered, caught, and ticked.  The paddles bit the sea, the Works turned, and the hissing under the floor thinned to a whisper.  The acid would win someday; nothing here pretended otherwise.  But on the wheel a golden scar caught the lamplight on every turn, saying that someone had been here, and had cared, and had mended.  She returned the cog and what gold remained to the Founder's palm on one last held breath, then hung the key ring by the hatch, its charm waiting for the next uncertain hands.  Then, finally, she let herself breathe.
