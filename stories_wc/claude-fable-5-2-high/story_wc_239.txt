Ilse Varran had watched the last star twice.  The first time, it guttered and went out, and history ended as her order had always promised it would.  Then the universe hiccuped, one caught breath between a beat and the next, and she saw the same sky with a star that had never been lit at all.  A witness to the end of history is permitted one testimony, and she now carried two. 

So she came, inside that hiccup, to the only place that could still receive her: the temple library of Oum, which had slipped out of time so long ago that dust there had stopped falling and simply hung.  The silence in it was not empty.  It rang, the way a bell rings after the sound is gone, a hush with a great room's shape. 

The stacks coiled outward from a stone reading well at the center, ring upon ring, to a single door in the outermost wall.  Carved on the well's rim was the monks' rule for records that contradicted each other: spiral arbitration.  Carry both claims from the center to the door.  At every turning, each claim must surrender one word it cannot define.  What reaches the door is shelved as true. 

Beside the rule, chained to nothing, lay the arbiter's instrument, and Ilse almost laughed, which in that place would have been like breaking a window.  It was a softcover dictionary, its spine creased white, its corners soft as cloth from centuries of thumbs.  No jeweled codex; the monks had wanted a judge that could bend without tearing. 

She wrote her two testimonies on two slips, one for each hand, and began to walk.  The left slip read: I saw the last star end.  The right slip read: I saw the star never begin. 

She had taken the witness's oath to unravel cosmic illusions, ever since her mother pointed at a star ten thousand years dead and called it dependable.  She had expected the end to hand her one hard fact, not two that canceled. 

At the first turning she opened the dictionary to "last."  The entry was wanderingly exact, as all its entries proved to be: it strolled through cobblers' forms and things that endure before arriving, without fail, at a meaning that could not be argued with.  Last: after which, having counted everything, there is no other.  She had not counted everything; she struck the word and the hush seemed to lean closer. 

The rings widened as she went, yet every step outward carried her further in, past the oath and the order, toward the child who had felt cheated by a pretty light. 

At the second turning, "end" said see "begin," and "begin" said see "end."  The monks had refused to pretend the two were different things, so she struck both, one from each hand.  "Never" needed time, and time had not been admitted to this building in ages; struck. 

"Star" took her longest.  Star: a light seen from far off, often already gone; see seeing.  Her mother had not lied, then.  She had pointed at the only part of a star anyone ever owns, and Ilse, wanting the rock behind the shining, had spent a lifetime calling the shining false.  She struck "star" from both slips gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeper. 

Each slip now held two words: I saw. 

The door stood one turning away, and through its crack the stalled universe waited like a held breath that could not decide whether it was going in or coming out. 

She looked up "I."  The page was there, and the headword, and beneath it only the pale grain of cheap paper, worn through by every arbiter who had reached this turning before her.  This was the innermost room, then, found at the outermost wall: the illusion that a witness stands apart from what is witnessed.  Her hand shook, because the word was the last thing she had that was hers.  She struck it anyway. 

"Saw," said the left slip, and "saw," said the right, and there was nothing left to arbitrate.  She laid one slip upon the other to unify them, and shelved the single record in the niche beside the door.  The dictionary she left on the sill, open to the worn place, for whoever came next. 

Then she opened the door and walked out, and the universe, no longer asked to choose between ending and never beginning, finished its hiccup.  Somewhere a light was seen.  Behind her the hush went on resounding, a bell with no more need of sound.
