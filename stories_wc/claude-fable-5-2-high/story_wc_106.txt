The sunset over Emerald Station would last eleven minutes, and Vesper Lark had planned to steal something in nine.  Gold poured through the glass roof and slid down the green tiles until the whole concourse looked like the inside of a bottle held up to a candle. 

The newspapers called her the golden-hour portrait thief, because she took only the paintings of Odile Marrow, who had painted strangers at dusk in the last minute before their lives turned.  A bride about to bolt, a clerk about to confess, a soldier about to go home.  Vesper had eleven of them in a rented attic, and she studied their faces the way a locksmith studies tumblers.  She wanted to press her ear against destiny's door and hear the bolt slide before it opened, because once, at nine years old, on this very platform, she had not heard it at all.  Her mother had kissed her forehead, boarded the evening train, and never come back. 

Old Anselm Quill, who kept the flower stall beneath the verdigris clock, told her where the portraits were.  He never spoke of it; he simply arranged his front bucket, and she read it walking past without slowing.  Irises meant a message, their number the platform, the ribbon's color the hour.  Tonight there were seven irises, a yellow ribbon, a ring of marigolds, and one white moonflower shut tight as a fist. 

Platform seven, sunset, a painting of grief, she translated, and kept walking.  But Vesper was quietly savvy, the kind of clever that never announces itself, and the moonflower itched at her.  In six years Quill had never wasted a stem.  She noted, too, that the guard on seven had been sent to tea, and that someone had swept the bench. 

No courier waited there, no crate, no train.  Only a small canvas leaned against the bench, wrapped in florist's paper, and beside it a pickle jar, sealed with wax, that seemed to hold nothing but a faint silver restlessness. 

She unwrapped the canvas in the last gold of the day and forgot how to breathe.  It was a Marrow, unmistakably, the light laid on like warm honey.  It was also a girl of nine on platform seven, hand half raised toward a departing train. 

"No," Vesper said aloud, to no one, and then, more softly, "No, that's me." 

The rumored twelfth Marrow, the one she had hunted for six years, was her own worst minute, and around the child the canvas was strangely bare, a gray emptiness where a station should be. 

The sun went out behind the signal towers.  By every rule she owned she should have tucked the canvas under her coat and run.  Instead she sat on the swept bench and made herself reinterpret the bucket, stem by stem.  Marigolds, in the older grammar Quill had taught her that first winter, did not only mean grief; ringed around another flower they meant stay with it.  And a moonflower meant nothing at all until dark, when it opened.  Not steal this at sunset, then, but stay past sunset, and open what is closed. 

So she waited, which she had never once done on a job, while Emerald Station cooled from gold to green to a deep undersea dark.  When the moon cleared the glass roof and the tiles began to glow like wet leaves, she broke the wax and unscrewed the lid. 

Moonbeams came out slowly, like moths deciding, and settled on the canvas.  The gray emptiness filled.  In a pigment that only this light could wake, the station appeared around the child: the clock, the tiles, and behind her a flower stall, where a younger Quill stood with a brush in his hand, watching her as though she were the only train that mattered. 

Vesper laughed, a small cracked sound of pure disbelief, because it could not be true and it was shining in her lap. 

"One beam for every night I saw you safe on that bench," Quill said from the dark, setting down his watering can.  "Odile Marrow was only a name I signed, and the gold was only ever half of any illuminated moment."  "You wanted the moment before the door opens; I wanted you to see who stood beside you after it shut." 

She thought of eleven stolen faces in her attic, each one waiting for its moon, and understood that she had been listening at the wrong side of the door.  Destiny had not slipped past her at nine years old; it had stayed on the platform and sold flowers. 

"Sit still," Quill said, lifting a brush, and for once the thief let the light take her.
