Odile Marrow brokered lanterns the way other people brokered peace, quietly and at a loss.  She matched lights to needs: oil flames for vigils, storm lamps for ferrymen, and tonight, six ultraviolet lanterns for the Greenhouse of the Departed, where extinct plants were coaxed back from museum seeds.  She came with a scarf wound over her nose.  Odile was aromatically cautious, and had been since childhood.  Certain smells ambushed her, a stranger's collar, a wet garden wall, and she would be weeping before she knew she was sad.  She had never learned why, so she breathed shallowly through the world and sold other people their light. 

The keeper, a stooped botanist named Hask, met her among the glass aisles.  Moonlight fell through a thousand panes and broke on the floor in pale, sharp-edged shadows, as if the building were made of ice remembering water. 

"You are in time," Hask said.  "The veilroot has been turning for nine days, bud to bloom, and it finishes tonight."  "Nobody alive has seen it open." 

"Why ultraviolet?" Odile asked through her scarf. 

"The old herbals say its petals carry markings only moths could read," he said.  "I want to read them before it fades again." 

She hung the lanterns herself, because a broker who would not climb a ladder was only a shopkeeper.  When she lit the first, the greenhouse changed.  Ferns that had been gray wore veins of cold fire, and a lichen thought lost for two centuries glowed like spilled salt.  On its iron stand, the veilroot bud showed faint violet script along its seams, still folded, still unreadable. 

Something glowed at her own hip as well. 

Odile looked down, and her hand went to her coat pocket, to the crystal perfume vial she had carried for thirty years and never once unstopped.  It had been her mother's, the only thing left after the fever, and Odile kept it sealed because she was afraid of what a single breath of it might do.  She had been four; she could not recall her mother's face, and it shamed her. 

She drew the vial out, and under the lantern the blank crystal was no longer blank.  A label had been painted on it in some ink that daylight had always hidden, and now the letters burned a steady blue. 

Veilroot, last pressing, for Odile when she is grown. 

Hask read it over her shoulder and went very still.  "The last wild veilroot grew along the garden walls of the river quarter," he said softly.  "Perfumers stripped them bare the year before it vanished."  "They signed the true pressings in moth ink, so forgers could be caught under the right lamp."  "Then your mother made this with her own hands." 

Wet garden walls, Odile thought, and the collars of old people who still wore the last of it.  All her life she had been crying at fragments of one flower, at a mother her nose remembered and her mind did not. 

Behind them came a sound like a page turning.  The veilroot was opening, its nine days of change arriving at their end, each petal unrolling until the violet script lay flat and whole.  Hask fumbled for his notebook, but Odile was not looking at the markings.  She was unwinding her scarf. 

The scent reached her as the last petal settled, green and rainy and faintly sweet, like bread cooling in a room with the window open.  And there it was, not a face yet, but a wrist, a sleeve pushed up, a voice humming over a copper still while a small girl sat beneath the table counting dropped petals.  The tears came as they always had, but for the first time they had a name to go to. 

Odile sat down on the cold tiles between the lantern shadows and grieved, properly, the way she had been too young to do, for a woman who had bottled the end of a species so her daughter might someday smell home.  Hask did not write anything.  He set his notebook down and held the nearest lantern steady, so that her vial kept shining. 

When she could speak, she pulled the stopper at last, and the flower on its stand and the flower in the crystal answered each other across thirty years. 

"Smells do not make us cry," she said.  "They only carry what we could not." 

By dawn the veilroot had begun to close, and the panes threw their thin bright shadows over an unscarfed woman breathing deeply, one lantern still burning beside her like a kept promise.
