From the top terrace, Osk watched the final bus pull away from the vacant station below, its taillights thinning into the valley fog.  Nobody had boarded.  That troubled him, because Dario, the hired cellarman, had been paid off at noon and was meant to be on it.  Osk had been the vineyard's lone sentinel for nineteen autumns, guarding the late-harvest rows against frost, boar, and thieves once everyone else went home.  He knew the hill's night habits, and a light in the tasting pavilion was not one of them. 

The pavilion stood among the last unpicked vines, glazed in amber glass so that sunlight could never spoil an open bottle; in October, with the leaves turned, it seemed poured from the same honey as the hill.  Inside, Dario crouched at the frost brazier, feeding it a folded page.  Osk did not shout.  He reached past the young man with his pruning knife and flicked the paper onto the flagstones, where it curled and went dark.  Only one edge survived, brown as a dried leaf, carrying four words in careful ink: the way you count. 

"It was for Ines," Dario said, as though confessing a theft. 

"I watched the bus too," said a voice from the doorway.  Ines, the grower's daughter, stood there with a lantern and her ledger still under her arm, and her face shut like a cellar door. 

Osk had heard their quarrel a week ago, carried uphill on a still evening.  She had asked for dates and a plan; he had gone silent; she had called his silence an answer.  A woman who tallied every crate and a man who tasted a barrel for an hour before saying one word: anyone would call them mismatched.  Osk had once been called the same, and had let a bus settle the question for him.  He had spent nineteen autumns learning what he should have asked her instead. 

"Sit a moment, both of you," he said, gently, the way he coaxed a nervous dog off the rows.  "The fire is already lit, and the next bus is not until morning." 

They sat, a bench apart.  Osk drew a stub of old vine from his coat and turned its swollen joint toward the coals. 

"You both know what this is," he said.  "Every vine on this hill is two plants that were never meant to meet: a delicate French scion on a wild American root."  "The root never learns to fruit, and the scion never learns to dig."  "Yet alone, one gives sour grapes and the other dies of the louse, so I call their mismatch destined, not accidental." 

Ines looked at the scar instead of at Dario. 

"A graft only takes if the cut faces are pressed together," Osk went on, "so that each can feel the other's sap."  "So I would ask a small thing: tell the quarrel again, but each of you from the other's side of the cut." 

The brazier ticked.  Dario spoke first, slowly, to the floor. 

"You asked for a date because the season was ending and I was a line in the ledger about to be closed; you were not pinning me down, you were asking whether to keep the page open." 

Ines's grip on the ledger loosened.  "And you went quiet," she said, "the way you go quiet over a barrel, because you will not say a thing until it is true."  "I heard no, when you were still tasting." 

Osk held out the burned edge, and she took it as carefully as a seedling. 

"The way you count," she read.  "What was the rest?" 

"That I love the way you count," Dario said, "because you count me in, every evening, before you close the book."  "I burned it because I could not put a date beside it." 

"Then say the rest slowly," Ines said, moving down the bench until her shoulder met his, "and I will wait as long as a barrel takes." 

Osk left them the fire and the old vine stub and went back out to his rows.  Behind him the pavilion glowed like a lantern full of wine, two shadows leaning into one line on the amber glass.  Below, the station lay dark and empty, and for once the sight did not ache.  He knelt at the nearest vine, found the graft scar with his thumb, and rediscovered a name he had not spoken in nineteen autumns.  He said it quietly, the way one tests a barrel, and resolved to write to her before the morning bus.
