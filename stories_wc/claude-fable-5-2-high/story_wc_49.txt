The harvest moon hung low and honey-colored over the western ridge when Pim Alderwick wheeled her barrow into the old arbor.  In the east, the sky had only begun to pale.  Once each year the two lights shared the vines for a few breaths, and her grandmother had called that moment the aligning.  Everything green seemed to be listening for it. 

Pim was the village tinkerer, a mender of kettles and a maker of things nobody had asked for: a weathervane that sneezed before rain, a ladle that whistled when the soup was ready.  She was better known for her tongue than her tools.  While she wound brass wire on her spindle, she spun golden stories for the children, tales of grapes as big as lanterns and wine that cured sorrow, back when the arbor was sung awake each harvest.  The stories were golden because the truth was not. 

The truth was that her grandmother, the last Vinesinger, had offered to teach her the waking songs, and Pim had been too busy with a clockwork cricket to sit still.  Then the old woman died, and the songs went into the ground with her.  For nine autumns the village had picked in silence, hurrying, and each year the clusters came smaller and more sour.  People blamed the soil.  Pim blamed herself, quietly, between jokes. 

The remedy had come to her in spring, by accident.  Pruning the oldest vine, she had noticed that its dried tendrils did not coil evenly; they wound tight, then loose, then tight again, like the pinned cylinder of a music box.  A tendril grows toward what it feels, and these had grown, season after season, around her grandmother's singing.  So she had built the Coilreader: a brass comb, a crank, and a cradle sized for a single vine tendril.  All summer it had given her scraps, three notes here, five there, forgotten songs returning one curl at a time. 

Now she set the machine on its tripod beneath the great trunk and laid in the longest tendril she had saved, cut from the year her grandmother last sang.  Dew ticked from leaf to leaf.  Behind her, pickers were arriving with their baskets, and she heard them stop at the arbor's edge, heard their muttering about Pim and her toys fall away into a gathering hush. 

The first sunlight touched the top leaves just as the moon rested its chin on the ridge.  Gold from one side, gold from the other.  Pim turned the crank. 

The comb plucked the coils, and a melody stepped out, thin and bright as wire, the waking song from its very first note.  She had planned a speech, something funny about vegetables with opinions.  Then, eight bars in, the brittle tendril snapped. 

The comb rang once on nothing, and the silence afterward was enormous.  The moon was already sinking. 

Pim looked at the broken curl in her palm, and for once no story came to gild the moment.  There was no spare tendril long enough, no gear to tighten.  There was only what the machine had already taught her through a summer of listening, and the one instrument she had never trusted. 

She opened her mouth and sang. 

Her voice was cracked and plain, nothing golden in it, but it carried the eight bars and then, trembling, the ninth.  At the tenth she faltered, because the scraps had never fit together past that point. 

From the arbor's edge, an old voice took the tenth bar for her.  It was Hobb the cooper, who as a boy had carried baskets behind her grandmother.  Then the widow Sarn found the refrain, and someone's aunt remembered the low part, and the song came up out of the villagers the way water comes up when you finally dig in the right place.  It had never been wholly forgotten, only scattered, a line in each of them, waiting for somebody to start. 

They sang as the moon slipped under and the sun came over, and in that doubled light Pim took her grandmother's hooked knife and made the first cut the old way, one cluster lifted in both hands.  Nobody hurried. 

Whether the grapes would be sweeter, she could not say yet; a remedy takes more than one morning.  But the pickers moved down the rows humming, teaching the children as they went, and that was the practice itself, restored.  Pim wound the broken tendril around her finger like a ring.  Tonight she would spin the children a story, and this one would be golden and, for a change, true.
