Above the cloud sea, where the spire of Hollin broke through into thin blue air, the library kept its cold like a promise.  Frost furred the shelves, and the silence had the grain of quartz, clear and hard and faintly ringing.  Odalys Brenn, Chief of Records, moved through it in felt slippers so as not to chip it. 

Her charge was forty thousand magic lantern slides, each one a vow.  For three centuries pilgrims had climbed the spire to speak a promise into the great brass lantern, and the lantern had fixed each promise in painted glass.  Slid back before the cold flame, a slide threw its speaker onto the ice wall, a figure of light whose colors were the words themselves.  Luminous vows, the founders called them, and built the library high so nothing would melt. 

But no pilgrim had come in eleven winters, and the flame, which fed on fresh promises, was starving.  Every figure it cast came thinner than the last time, illusions fading toward memory, and Odalys was the only memory left on the mountain. 

She could not transcribe forty thousand vows in full before the light went.  So she had built a method, because a method was how she grieved.  She would measure each vow by sampling frequencies: a quartz prism split the beam, a brass plate with seven slits let through seven chosen colors, and a caliper gauged how tall each band stood on the wall.  Seven numbers per promise, entered in the ledger, enough for the old color key to give back the gist.  It was not the whole of anyone, but it was something pulled back from the edge of oblivion, and she meant to make it mean. 

She was gentle about it.  She warmed every slide against her wrist before the lantern could shock it, squared each ledger line with a bone rule, and murmured thank you to strangers three hundred years dead. 

On the ninetieth night, checking her columns, she saw what the numbers had been saying.  Six bands sank from page to page as the flame weakened.  The fourth, a pale green that matched no word in the key, never moved.  She had been logging it as lantern noise.  It stood at the same height in a shepherd's vow to forgive his brother and a queen's vow to end a war.  Noise did not hold that steady; something in every vow was refusing to fade. 

To test it she needed a slide made differently, and she knew of only one.  Tobiah, the chief before her, had left an unlabeled slide in the lantern's drawer the winter he died, and in all the years since she had not had the heart to light it. 

She warmed it and slid it home.  The old man stood on the ice wall, faint as breath on a window, his mouth moving.  She set the plate and measured.  Six bands, low but legible; the fourth slit was dark. 

Odalys sat down on the cold floor with the caliper in her lap.  Every pilgrim had spoken with a records chief standing by; Tobiah, on a night she slept through, had spoken to an empty room.  The green was not the promise.  The green was the hearing of it, and it was the one part the flame had never been asked to feed. 

She worked his six numbers through the key, slowly, checking twice.  I will keep the light until someone gentler comes. 

"I heard you," she said aloud, and her voice rang in the hush like a struck glass.  She measured again.  The fourth band stood at full height, and for one breath Tobiah brightened, every wrinkle sharp, before he thinned and was gone.  He went the way a guest goes, not the way a candle does.  She understood then what the cold had really been for: not to keep the illusions, but to give someone time to receive them. 

In the morning she ruled an eighth column in the ledger and headed it Heard.  She kept sampling, seven slits, seven numbers, but now she read each vow aloud once before she drew the slide, and marked the column only when she had.  It was slower, and she let it be slower. 

At dusk she took a blank slide from the founders' stock, warmed it, and spoke into the lantern.  "I will hear each of you before you go."  There was no one to witness it but forty thousand fading strangers.  She measured anyway, because she was who she was.  The green band held, and the starved flame, fed at last, stood a finger taller in the cold.
