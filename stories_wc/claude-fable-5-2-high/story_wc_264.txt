Hush, little ember, don't you... 

That was all Oda had of the song, because her mother had sung it through smoke, and coughing had broken every line.  Eleven years after the last forest burned, she still hummed the pieces while she walked the freight car, rifle slung, eyes open. 

The Ledger Office hired only guards who did not dream.  Oda never had; sleep for her was a door closing and a door opening, with nothing in the room between.  That emptiness was her qualification, because the freight was the Divergent Archive, and a dreamer would have ruined it. 

The archivists had not saved the forests that were.  They had saved every forest that might still be, each one stored unchosen, a wave function folded in cold glass.  To look too hungrily at any of them, even asleep, was to choose it and kill the rest. 

Bolted to the vault door was a chess clock, both faces frozen at midnight.  The archivists had stopped it the night the last trees went, one face for What Was, one for What Might, neither side allowed to move.  Press either button, and the game resumed; the archive would collapse into a single forest and forget the others. 

That was what waited at Marrow Terminus.  Auditor Pell had boarded at the last water stop with a ledger and a clean thumb. 

"One plantation pine, guaranteed," he said.  "Countable, taxable, real, and better than a thousand maybes." 

Oda said nothing.  She had stopped thinking of the archive as cargo months ago, because it did not stay in its car.  It was occasionally everywhere.  Some nights she found moss on her boot soles, or smelled rain in the caboose, or heard one owl calling from three stations at once.  By morning it was ash and rail again. 

And in the unlit corners of the car, where the vault lamps did not reach, there were sparks. 

The first time, she had reached for the fire bucket, because everyone alive knew what sparks did.  But these were cool and green, and they flickered like things unsure they were permitted.  They were the faintest forests, the ones the arithmetic gave almost no chance: the bog cedar, the cloud oak, the orchard nobody planted.  Pell's pine would erase them first. 

She could not order them brighter; a wave function does not obey, it only leans.  So she had tried the one thing that had ever leaned her toward sleep. 

The dark is only a... 

She sang the broken lines into the corners, and the sparks swayed, because a song is also a wave, and waves add.  Where her voice met their trembling, they stood a little taller.  It was not science as the archivists wrote it; it was charm, worked through wave functions the way her mother had worked it through smoke.  Night after night she tended them, a gardener of what nobody had budgeted for. 

She did not know where the brightness went until the train braked hard, two hours short of Marrow. 

A dune lay across the track, and it was glowing. 

Oda climbed down into green light.  The whole slope was in bloom: lichen like spilled milk, ferns uncurling from ash, cedar seedlings thin as wicks, every leaf lit faintly from inside.  Her sparks, occasionally everywhere, had finally been somewhere long enough to root. 

Behind her, Pell saw it too, and understood faster than she liked.  "It's leaking," he said, and ran for the vault door and the clock. 

Oda had a rifle and eleven years of orders.  She used neither.  She stood in the doorway of the car and sang, louder than she ever had, every fracture left in. 

Hush, little ember, don't you... 

The archive answered.  It came through the walls as weather: rain smell, owl call, a floor suddenly soft with needles.  Pell's thumb stopped an inch above the button, because a fern had opened in his ledger, across the column where the pine was priced. 

He looked at it for a long time. 

"My daughter has never seen one," he said, to no one, and his hand came down empty. 

Oda unbolted the clock herself and carried it up the dune.  She did not press What Was, and she did not press What Might.  She set it among the seedlings and kept singing, and somewhere in the third broken line both faces began to tick at once, which no chess clock is built to do.  One minute past midnight, twice, and nobody losing. 

Near dawn she lay down in the glowing ferns and slept.  She did not dream.  A door closed, a door opened, and the forest was still there, and an owl finished the line for her.
