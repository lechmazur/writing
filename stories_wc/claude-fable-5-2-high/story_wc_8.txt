The final petal dropped from the old rosebush on Platform One the morning Odile Marsh arrived at Vell Halt, as if it had been waiting for a witness.  She watched it settle on the cracked paving, then took the letter from her coat.  Stationmaster Tobias Wren had sent it to her forty years ago, sealed, with instructions on the envelope: open when my rose is finished. 

For forty years Odile had been the Republic's official historian, and privately she called herself its fictional one.  Her celebrated chronicle said the troop train from Vell carried three hundred local sons to the front, where they died holding a ridge.  Wren had dictated that version to her when she was twenty-three, and she had written it down because he wept while he lied.  She had judged then that a man does not weep over a lie that profits him. 

That was how Odile had always worked: she treated her feelings as witnesses, heard each one out, and believed none without corroboration.  Now the feeling on the stand was shame, and its testimony was specific.  Last month the Ministry had printed her ridge on recruiting posters, asking a new generation to die like the sons of Vell.  A lie told to comfort mothers had become a pit, and boys were being marched toward its edge.  She had come to recapture the truth she herself had helped bury, if any of it could still be found. 

She broke the seal.  The letter was a single page, and most of it was taken up by one enormous illuminated capital, a gilded T painted with a monk's patience.  Beneath it ran one line of plain script: "The truth is where I kept it, Miss Marsh, and you know how to read." 

Odile sat on a bench the ivy had not yet swallowed and studied the letter T.  Its crossbar was the station canopy, drawn bracket for bracket.  Its upright was the signal post at the platform's end, and at its foot Wren had painted a red rose in full bloom.  She worked the way she worked in archives, by drawing parallels, setting each painted thing beside its real counterpart and looking for the one that had no match.  Canopy to canopy, though the real one now sagged under green.  Post to post, rails to the two gold lines ruled across the bottom of the page.  Rose to rose, except that the painted roots ran down past the gold rails into the margin and closed around a small black rectangle.  Nothing above ground answered to it. 

Under the rose, she thought, and almost laughed at the old man's Latin joke.  It also explained the wait: he would not have his secret dug up while the bush, or the mothers, still lived. 

She found a rusted fire shovel in the ticket office and dug among the old roots until the blade rang on tin.  Inside the box, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the signal register for the night of the departure.  The entries were in a child's round hand.  Wren's signalman had gone to the war already, and his replacement, Peter Lunn, was fourteen years old and had been awake for thirty hours.  He cleared the troop train onto the Kessel bridge four minutes after clearing a coal train onto it from the other side.  No ridge, no last stand; three hundred sons had died six miles from home, and their own town's exhausted child had sent them. 

Odile closed the register and let the witnesses speak.  Pity spoke first, for Wren, who had invented a battle so that Vell would not tear a boy apart.  Fear spoke next, of what the Ministry did to historians who embarrassed it.  She weighed them honestly and found that neither contradicted the facts: the parish rolls had Lunn thirty years dead, the mothers were gone, and the only people the lie still touched were the ones it was recruiting.  A noble lie, she concluded, is noble only while it shelters someone.  This one had outlived everyone it sheltered, and her shame was simply an accurate measurement of that. 

She opened her notebook on her knee and ruled the page into two columns, parallel as the rails rusting below her.  On the left she would set the chronicle as she had published it, line by line.  On the right she would set the register, and beneath both Wren's reasons, so that no reader could mistake his mercy for the Ministry's use of it.  She pressed the fallen petal inside the register to mark the place, propped the illuminated letter where she could see it, and began the left column with her own name.
