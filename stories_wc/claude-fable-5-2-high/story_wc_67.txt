The girl gave her grandmother's rocking chair one push, as the rite required, and stepped back.  Maren had until it stopped.  That was the law of the Vanishing Point Gallery: a scatterer drew only while the dead one's chair still moved, because a likeness begun in stillness was a likeness of no one. 

She tipped a spoonful of Odile's ashes into the mortar with a stick of burnt willow and began to powder them together.  Around them the hall narrowed as it had been built to do, floor and rafters and walls all slanting toward one small window at the far end.  Portraits hung in order of age, unfixed, shedding, the oldest so pale they were nearly bare paper by the time they reached the sill.  From that sill, each spring, Maren brushed what remained into the wind. 

Wren set a mask on the drawing table.  It was made of shattered porcelain, dozens of white shards glued to linen, a blue painted river running broken across the brow. 

"She wore it her whole last year," Wren said.  "She said the washbasin broke and it seemed a shame to waste it.  She said the mask kept the hearth dust from her chest.  She said she was tired.  All of it was true, and none of it was enough, and I believed we were safe until the morning the chair was empty." 

The chair creaked behind them, its arc already shorter. 

"Draw her without it," Wren said.  "I want the face she hid from me." 

Maren turned the mask over.  She had built a life out of this: not likenesses, which any street sketcher could sell, but one feeling caught before it thinned, held on paper the way frost holds a breath on glass.  To do it she wove truth carefully, choosing which threads to show, and she had never once drawn a lie. 

Here she had almost nothing to weave.  A hollow of linen, shaped by a face she had never seen.  A granddaughter's fragments.  A spoonful of gray. 

The hollow was too narrow at the cheeks.  Beneath each eyehole the cloth had stiffened in a pale crust, and when Maren touched a fingertip there and then to her tongue, she tasted salt.  So Odile had wasted, and wept, and kept both behind a broken basin.  Maren could guess at that face.  But a guess drawn in a dead woman's ashes would be a lie, and a cruel one, dressed as the truth Wren had asked for. 

The chair's creak had dropped to a tick. 

"Where did you sit," Maren asked, "in the evenings?" 

"On the hearthstone, at her feet.  She rocked and hummed, and I shelled beans and told her everything."  Wren's mouth twisted.  "I was happy.  I was a fool in a painted room." 

Maren lifted the mask to her own face and looked through it.  The world shrank to two ragged openings, and in them stood a girl with her fists clenched, furious, alive.  There was one face from that house Maren could swear to. 

She dipped her thumb in the powder and worked fast.  First the dark: a border of jagged shards seen from inside, their edges catching firelight.  Then, within the two openings, a girl on a hearthstone with a bowl in her lap, head tipped back, mid-sentence, entirely unafraid.  Odile's ashes became the firelit shadow under her chin and the soft dark of the room that held her. 

Behind them the chair gave one last small nod and was still.  Maren lifted her hand from the paper. 

Wren stared at the drawing for a long time.  "That is me," she said at last. 

"That is what she saw," Maren said.  "I cannot give you her face; I was not told enough, and neither were you.  But the inside of that mask is stiff with salt, so she was afraid, and she looked at this every night anyway and kept it whole.  The safety was a mirage, child.  The woman who painted it on the air for you was not." 

Wren picked up the mask.  She held it to her face, and through the eyeholes she looked down the long hall, past a hundred fading strangers, to where every line met in one bright square of sky. 

"It makes everything so small," she whispered. 

"Small enough to carry," Maren said.  "It will hang here unfixed, and shed, and in spring I will give what is left to the wind.  Come back then, and wear that, and watch how she goes." 

Wren lowered the mask and laid her palm on the arm of the motionless chair, as if it were a shoulder.  On the table a few grains had already let go of the paper, and neither woman brushed them away.
