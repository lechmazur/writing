Odile Varga wrote fortunes for a living, and nobody ever understood them on the first reading.  That was the point.  A fortune that explained itself was only advice; a fortune that puzzled a stranger for a week was a cue, a lifted baton, and somewhere a life came in on the beat.  She would never hear the music, which was exactly what she wanted: to become the conductor of unheard symphonies, ten thousand small decisions swelling in kitchens and taxis she would never enter. 

Tonight the score was one slip short.  The bakery truck would cross the causeway at eight for the new year's batch, three hundred sixty-four fortunes sat boxed by the door, and Odile had been awake for thirty hours in the top room of the glass spire, hunting the last.  The spire had been a weather station once, a needle of panes on the shore of Lake Halda, and in January the lake froze so hard and flat that the whole building seemed to stand on a held breath.  She knew the feeling behind her ribs was fatigue gathering, a wave still far out.  She had perhaps an hour before exhaustion hit and turned every sentence to porridge. 

By her own rule, the last fortune had to be one she needed herself, and Odile's trouble did not fit on a slip.  She was homesick, but not for any place.  She was homesick for a year: the summer she was nine, when her grandfather ran the Halda ferry and let her stand at the rail and name what she heard.  Chronologically homesick, he would have called it, with his fondness for long words; you could buy a ticket to a town, but nobody sold passage to a year. 

All she had left of that summer was in her cardigan pocket: a segment of nautilus shell, one pearly chamber snapped from the spiral he kept on the wheelhouse dash.  He had broken it off for her himself.  "The animal never lives in the old rooms," he said, "but it never throws them away either." 

She set the segment on the sill, took up a grease pencil, and did what she did whenever the words would not come: she mapped.  On the cold east pane she drew the ferry dock as a small square, and beside it the first memory, labeled in her cramped hand: engine hum, low as a cello.  Then the next, curving outward: gulls arguing in thirds.  Then the rope creaking, the thermos lid, the passengers who never spoke and whom her grandfather called his orchestra, because each carried a tune nobody else would hear.  That was where the wanting had started; she marked it with a star. 

Memory by memory the map coiled across the glass, each chamber a little larger than the last, and she saw without planning it that she had drawn the shell.  The outermost chamber, the one where the living animal would be, was empty.  She stood with the pencil raised, swaying a little, the wave of tiredness nearer now. 

Beyond her drawing, the dark was thinning.  The ice had no wind on it, no snow, nothing but polish, and as the first gray came up the sky laid itself down on the lake so completely that the horizon became a seam between two identical mornings.  A mirrored horizon: one dawn above, one below, and her grease-pencil spiral hanging exactly on the fold.  Odile tilted her face up, and the room went quiet in the way a hall does when the baton rises. 

She had always pictured that summer behind her, sinking, a shore she was sailing away from.  But the lake did not put the sky behind anything; it held the whole of it and handed it back, and the shell did the same, every old room still attached, still shaping the curve of the new one.  The year she ached for was not lost; it was the reason she listened for music in strangers at all.  Every fortune she had ever written was that ferry, still crossing. 

She picked up the nautilus segment and held it against the glass, in the empty chamber, where it fit the line she had drawn as if she had traced it.  Then, with the sun's rim breaking in two places at once, she sat down and typed the last slip before her hands forgot how. 

The room you miss is the floor of this one; stand on it and dawn. 

Some stranger would crack it open in March and frown for a week.  Odile sealed the box, pocketed the shell, and let the tiredness finally break over her, face still lifted toward both suns, listening to nothing, conducting all of it.
