Sefa walked the line at dusk, when the dunes cooled and the buried parapet began to move.  It was an old rampart, older than any name for it, and it crept beneath the sand like a slow spine turning in sleep.  No one could find it by looking straight down.  The wall slid away from any stare, the way a dream slides away from telling.  So the line walkers wore half masks, lacquer over the right side of the face, blinding the eye that wanted to aim.  With the other eye turned toward the horizon, Sefa caught the parapet through peripheral awareness alone: a faint ripple at the edge of sight, a hum in her heels. 

The pavilion rode the parapet's crest, its poles sunk into the moving stone, its silk walls murmuring.  Every teacher who had ever spoken there had left a little voice in the weave, and the cloth kept them all, overlapping, soft as wind in grain.  That murmur was the echo of wisdom, and Sefa's whole work was to preserve it. 

She did this by conjuring clearings.  Wherever the wall carried the pavilion, drift piled against the silk and muffled it, so each evening she walked ahead along the line, knelt, and consecrated a patch of ground.  The rite needed no oil and no song.  It needed one true thing, said plainly, with nothing added to make it kinder.  When the truth was clean, the sand drew back in a wide ring, and the pavilion drifted into the hollow and murmured freely.  Years of this had made her strangely honest.  She told traders their dates were sour and told herself, aloud, when she was lonely. 

Then the prophet arrived. 

He came out of the salt country with three hundred followers, drums, and a voice like a struck bell.  He preached inside the pavilion from dawn until the lamps guttered, and his people answered him in one great breath.  After nine days Sefa pressed her ear to the silk and heard only him, repeated, and beneath him the old voices thin as thread.  Worse, the crowd stared at the ground where the wall was said to lie, and the parapet, flinching from so many eyes, had begun to sink. 

On the tenth evening the prophet followed her out along the line. 

"My people need room," he said.  "Consecrate a clearing wide enough for all of us, and I will bless your pavilion with my teaching forever." 

"I cannot tell whether your teaching is wise," Sefa said.  "It may be.  I only know it is loud, and that the wall is leaving." 

He did not bristle, which surprised her.  He looked down at the sand, hard, searching, and she felt the hum in her heels fade. 

"Not like that," she said.  "Look at the far dune and let the ground come to the corner of your eye."  She untied her spare mask and held it out. 

He hesitated, then covered half his face, and after a long quiet he breathed, "It ripples." 

"Now say one true thing." 

He knelt and said, in the bell voice, "I carry the final word for this age." 

The sand did not stir. 

Sefa knelt beside him.  "I am afraid," she said, "that when you are done, yours will be the only voice left in the cloth, and I will have kept nothing." 

The dune sighed open around them, a ring twenty paces wide, floor smooth as poured milk.  In that conjured stillness the pavilion's murmur reached them from behind, faint and many.  The prophet listened for a long time.  A potter's saying about patience surfaced, then a widow's, then a child's question no one had answered. 

"I began speaking before I finished listening," he said at last, quietly, to no crowd. 

The ring widened until it could have held three hundred.  Under their knees the parapet rose, steadied, and resumed its slow travel. 

After that the prophet taught at noon only, and softly, and his followers learned to sit with their eyes on the horizon.  His voice went into the silk as one thread among the others, neither first nor last. 

Sefa kept walking the line each dusk, half her face in lacquer, the wall humming faithfully at the edge of what she could see.  When she knelt to consecrate the next clearing, she said the truest thing she had: "I am not lonely tonight."  The sand opened, and the pavilion came murmuring in.
