The arboretum clung to the cliff like a stubborn prayer, its terraced gardens cascading toward the Atlantic where the old lighthouse stood dark. [23] After the last keeper retired, the coast guard had automated the beacon, and the keeper's cottage became a storage shed for the cliffside botanical preserve. [48] Yusra had volunteered here for eleven months now, coaxing rare alpine species from the rocky soil with the quiet talent that had once made her grandmother's rooftop garden in Sana'a legendary. [79]

She pressed her palms into the earth near the succulent beds, feeling for moisture, reading the soil's needs through her fingertips. [100] This was how she worked - through the body, through somatic markers that told her when a plant was thirsty before its leaves ever showed strain. [126] The slight coolness, the give and resistance, the scent that changed from mineral to organic - her nervous system translated these signals into knowledge her mind couldn't articulate. [152]

In her pocket, a crumpled photograph curled against her thigh. [162] She never looked at it anymore, but she couldn't discard it. [173] The image showed her sister's wedding party frozen mid-laugh, the sky above them impossibly blue, the moment before the drone's missile turned celebration into smoke and scattered flesh. [201] Seven years ago. [204] Forty-three dead. [207] She had been reaching for a glass of juice when the blast threw her against a garden wall, and that reaching - that ordinary, human reaching - had saved her life. [238]

Today she was transplanting seedlings of Silene acaulis, moss campion, into the south-facing crevices where the cliff caught morning sun. [259] These plants grew in the harshest conditions, thriving where wind and salt and cold punished everything else. [276] She had read that they could live for centuries, anchoring themselves in rock, blooming pink against gray stone. [295] She liked that. [298] She liked the idea of endurance without spectacle. [306]

A helicopter passed overhead. [310] The sound started in her chest before it reached her ears - a clenching, a held breath, the sudden rigidity that locked her muscles and pinned her to the ground. [339] Freeze. [340] The somatic marker was unmistakable: her heart rate didn't spike but plummeted, her vision narrowed, her body became a held note. [362] She knew the clinical name for it - tonic immobility, the prey response that played dead when flight and fight were impossible. [384]

She stayed frozen for forty seconds, counting, feeling the cold soil under her palms, letting the tactile reality anchor her. [404] The helicopter was a Coast Guard patrol, not a Predator drone. [415] The sky above the arboretum was sky, not surveillance. [424] Her body would learn this distinction eventually, or it wouldn't, but either way she would keep planting. [442]

[469] She would not pretend that her hands didn't shake sometimes or that certain sounds didn't collapse time. [486] But she would also not pretend that survival was meaningless or that a seedling couldn't take root in stone. [506]

[531] The lighthouse would sweep its automated arc across waters that had swallowed ships for centuries, indifferent to the fact that no human hand lit the flame anymore. [557]

[577] The somatic memory was always there - the weight of the paper, the crease from when she had crumpled it in the hospital, then smoothed it again because she couldn't destroy the last image of her sister's face.
