Prince Valdris stood at the edge of the crystalline platform, his bedraggled robes dragging in the condensation that pooled along the floor. [22] Above and around him, the suspended palace hung from enormous stalactites that descended from the cavern ceiling like the roots of dead trees, swaying gently in thermal updrafts. [50] Below, the world had become a continent of gears and pistons, a mechanical civilization that had long since forgotten color, warmth, and song. [73]

In his palm, he held an egg. [80] Not a stone, not a relic for museum shelves, but the intact, opalescent egg of the Suncrest Falcon, extinct for four hundred years, preserved in the deep cold of the palace's lowest vault. [113] He had found it three weeks prior, sealed in a chamber he had hypothesized was a cryogenic nursery left by the palace's original architects. [137] That discovery had sent him spiraling through hypotheses about everything: the biochemistry of dormant embryos, the atmospheric conditions of the pre-mechanical era, the precise frequency of light that might stimulate cellular division in a fossilized yolk. [173]

His motivation was singular and absolute: to reawaken beauty in a mechanical world. [186] The automata below manufactured and traded and computed with flawless efficiency, and their perfection was a kind of death. [205] Valdris had watched the last garden wither and the last songbird die when he was a boy, and the memory had calcified inside him like amber. [231]

Now he began to assemble the apparatus. [238] Copper coils salvaged from a broken music box, lenses polished from stalactite quartz, a heating element repurposed from a defunct engine, and the egg placed at the center, resting on a bed of dried moss he had found in the nursery chamber alongside it. [282] His hands shook as he connected the final wire, not from cold but from the quaking reverie of a man standing at the threshold of something impossible. [309]

He powered the device. [313] The copper coils hummed. [317] The lenses focused a warm amber light onto the egg's surface. [328] Minutes passed, then an hour. [333] He hypothesized about the thermal expansion rate of the shell, about the respiration rate of a four-hundred-year-old embryo, about whether the faint discoloration on the surface was a sign of life or decay. [366]

Then, through the discovery, it happened. [372] A fracture line, thin as a spider's silk, split across the shell's widest curve. [386] Valdris held his breath. [390] The crack widened with a sound like ice breaking on a winter pond, and the shell fell open in two halves, revealing not a living chick but something that made his heart stutter. [423]

Light emerged. [425] Not electric, not mechanical, but organic light, warm and golden, pooling upward from the remains of the yolk like a living flame. [447] It coalesced, shaping itself into the form of a bird, wings outstretched, head tilted as if listening to a sound only it could hear. [471] The Suncrest Falcon, rendered in pure luminance, hovered above the broken shell and the trembling hands of the bedraggled prince. [491]

It was a flawed genesis. [496] The bird was not alive in any biological sense. [505] It had no heartbeat, no cells, no capacity to reproduce or grow. [517] It was a resonance, an echo of life encoded in the egg's crystalline structure and released by heat and light. [537] Valdris had not resurrected a species; he had summoned its ghost. [548]

Yet when the light-bird opened its beak, a sound emerged, a fragment of birdsong preserved in the mineral lattice of the shell, and the palace walls caught it and carried it through the stalactites like a bell. [585] The song was bitterly sweet, beautiful and irretrievable at once, a melody that could never be repeated because the creature that first sang it was gone forever. [612]

Valdris sank to his knees. [617] The light-bird circled once, twice, then dissolved into a cascade of golden sparks that drifted downward through the gaps in the platform, falling toward the mechanical world below like seeds scattered on iron soil. [651] Whether anything would grow from them, he could not say. [661] He could only hypothesize. [665]

But for one moment, beauty had lived again, and the stalactites above him rang with the memory of its song. [685]
