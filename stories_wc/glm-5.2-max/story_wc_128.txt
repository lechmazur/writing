Elias Marsh had built his reputation as the most shrewd seaside cargo broker in Marenhollow, where every shipment told a story and every story concealed a deal. [27] He could estimate a hull's tonnage by sound alone, and he never let sentiment interfere with a contract. [45]

But after the symphony ends on evenings like this, when the community orchestra's final note still hums beneath the pier, Elias climbs to the breezy library above the harbor, where open arches frame the stars and the salt wind riffles through pages left on reading tables. [91] He comes here to be something other than the broker. [101]

It was on one such night, the orchestra's echo barely faded, that he found the envelope wedged inside a copy of Marian Navigation, tucked between the spine and the back cover as if someone had hidden it. [138] The address was wrong. [142] It read "Elias March, Marenhollow Library" instead of "Elias Marchetti," and the street number belonged to the music hall, not the library. [164]

Someone had meant this for someone else. [171] Elias should have turned it in to the front desk. [181] That would have been the efficient thing. [188]

Instead, being whimsically attentive in the way that made him impossible to fool in any negotiation, he noticed the return address: Lia Wendt, c/o Marenhollow Stagehands Union. [215] A name he didn't know. [220] A role he understood. [224] Stagehands carried the instruments, rigged the lights, swept the stage after the applause. [237] Silent contributors. [239] He knew the type. [243]

He opened the envelope, not to snoop but because the wrong address meant it might never reach its intended recipient if he left it. [267] Inside was a letter and a locket photo. [275]

The letter was brief. [279] Lia had found the locket in the prop room after the spring concert and believed it belonged to Marchetti's wife. [299] She thought he would want it back. [306]

Elias had never met Marchetti. [348] But he knew who the woman in the photo was. [358] Maria Foss, who had played second cello quietly and brilliantly for six seasons before she passed. [374] He had attended every concert where she played and noticed her because no one else did. [390]

He returned the letter and locket to the envelope. [399] Then he did the thing that Elias Marsh the broker would never do. [412] He decided to initiate. [416]

He found Lia at the music hall, hauling cable into the storage room, her workshirt dark with sweat. [434] She looked up, wary. [438]

He explained that he had received her envelope by mistake and that Marchetti had left town two years ago. [457] The address would never reach him. [463]

Her face tightened. [466] "Then I don't know what to do with it," she said. [477]

"Neither do I," Elias admitted. [482] "But I know what to do with the photo." [491]

He told her about Maria Foss, about the second cello, the unnoticed player whose music held the orchestra together from the back row. [514] About how, after every concert, the silence sounded like her note fading last. [527]

"She was the only one who learned our names," Lia said. [538] "She was kind to the stagehands." [544]

Elias held up the envelope. [549] "Then she belongs to both of us." [556]

He proposed an idea, cautious but unable to hide the excitement breaking through his voice: a small display here in the breezy library, under the stars, where Marenhollow could honor its forgotten people.
