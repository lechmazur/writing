Edda worked the late shift as the pier's lamplighter, walking the salt-warped planks each night with her brass pole and wick. [21] She was a woman of few words and fewer falsehoods, which people mistook for openness. [36] They were wrong. [39] Edda hid behind truth the way cuttlefish hide behind ink, obscuring herself with plain facts, answering every tender question with tide tables or blood pressure readings rather than feeling. [68] Honesty was her fortress, and she admitted its dimensions to no one. [80]

Tonight, the annual sky lantern release turned the harbor into a cathedral of ascending light, hundreds of luminous vows rising toward a meteor-hush sky, each lantern carrying some citizen's private promise into the high dark. [115] Edda lit the pier lamps as the lanterns rose behind her, and the twin lights, one grounded and one ascending, made her feel as though the world were breathing in two directions at once. [149]

She touched the hotel skeleton key tag in her coat pocket, brass worn nearly smooth, stamped "Mariner's Rest, Room 7." [169] Her mother's tag, from the night she disappeared twenty-three years ago, leaving nothing but that small brass rectangle and a reputation for impossible perceptions. [193]

Edda's mother had been a musician who described notes as colors, who claimed that middle C was the amber of old honey, that minor chords carried the green of deep water. [224] People called her gifted or broken, depending on their generosity. [234] Edda, thirteen when her mother vanished and already building her fortress of fact, chose not to decide, so she buried the question and spent two decades not asking it. [263]

But each night she wandered purposefully along the pier's full length, past the final lamp post to the rotting end, studying the water with the patience of someone waiting for a door to open. [297] The old mariners spoke of the Meridian, a vessel sunk in the shallows fifty years back, carrying a cargo of rare books, a forgotten library swallowed by the sea. [326] Edda's mother had been a passenger on that final voyage, though the records listed her only as lost. [344] Edda sought the library for one reason: to find her mother's journals, if they survived, and discover whether sound truly had color, or whether her mother's mind had drowned before her body did. [377]

Tonight, the lanterns' glow revealed what years of searching had not. [388] The tide had dropped to its lowest level in a generation, and there, half-risen from sand and kelp just beyond the pier's end, lay the Meridian's fractured hull, its cargo hold gaping open. [421]

Edda descended the ladder, boots finding wet sand, and waded into the wreckage. [434] The broken deck gave way to a lower hold where, wrapped in oilskin on preserved shelving, hundreds of waterlogged but legible volumes waited in the dark. [460] The forgotten library, real after all her years of searching. [470]

She worked through the collection by the trembling light of lanterns reflected on the flooded floor. [486] Near the back, she found a leather case bearing the same hotel name as her key tag. [503] The tag had been a marker, a way to signify which cargo belonged to whom among the passengers. [521] Her hands fitted the brass key into the case's rusted lock, and it turned. [535]

Inside lay her mother's journal, pages filled with musical notation annotated in luminous ink, formulas describing how each frequency of sound manifested as a specific hue, the pigment still faintly glowing in the flooded dark. [570] These were her mother's luminous vows: promises written in bioluminescent pigment to prove that her perception was real, that sound carried light within it, that she was not broken but merely seeing what others could not. [606]

Edda hummed a note from the open page, and the ink brightened in response. [620] She hummed higher, and the glow shifted from deep blue to amber. [632] The color of middle C, exactly as her mother had described it. [644] Sound had color. [647] Her mother had been telling the truth, and Edda had spent twenty-three years refusing to look. [663]

She wept, and her honesty became something new. [671] "I missed her every single night," she whispered to the flooded library, and the words did not hide behind facts or tide tables but stood bare and unprotected in the meteor hush of the ascending lanterns above. [708]

The luminous vows glowed around her, sound and light finally married in the belly of a sunken ship, while the sky above filled with the color of every silence she had ever refused to break. [743]
