The amphitheater exhaled. [32] Maren had learned to time her speeches to the building's breath. [43] She waited for the exhale, then spoke. [50]

"We don't have to choose between the valley farms and the ridge towns." [63]

[89] Maren stood at the amphitheater's center stage, where the worn stone had been polished smooth by generations of disputants. [108]

She was a floodgate machinist, not a politician. [116] Her ingenious work -- twenty-three years designing and maintaining the systems that controlled water flow through the volcanic aqueduct network -- had earned her something rare in this room: trust from both sides. [147] She understood water and pressure. [152] She understood that the valley farmers needed the reservoir levels lowered before spring planting, while the ridge towns needed them kept high through summer to prevent rationing. [179]

Both sides had valid claims. [184] Both had families, crops, and futures at stake. [192]

"This chain was used by the original engineers who built this place," she said. [244] "They measured every aqueduct, every gradient, every gate with it. [254] And they left records." [258]

"Three weeks ago, I found a once-sealed envelope in the floodgate archive. [294] The seal was broken, but the flap still held traces of adhesive. "Inside was a letter from Chief Engineer Vasquez. She was slyly heartfelt in moments like these -- presenting emotion as engineering, wrapping care in brass and calculation so that her compassion passed through the room encrypted, legible only to those who knew how to read between the schematics. [476] The assembly didn't know that she had tested the system twelve times, that she had nearly broken her back calibrating the eastern vent's pressure valves, that she had done it because her mother's family farmed the valley and her father's people lived on the ridge.
