Vell designed mazes the way others wrote confessions: with the desperate hope that someone would get lost inside them and never find the exit. [24] She worked in the living bridge garden, where the paths between hedgerows were spanned by bridges grown from living roots, woven and grafted over decades by hands she had never met but whose fingerprints she could sometimes feel pressed into the grain of the bark. [69] Her hands knew only the present, but her mind did not. [80]

She was retrocognitively trapped. [84] The past arrived without warning, layered over the present like a transparency laid on a photograph. [100] She would be pruning the eastern wall of yew and suddenly she was also standing in this same spot forty years ago, watching a woman she did not recognize kneel in the soil with a halo fragment cupped in her palms, weeping. [142] The fragment caught light in a way that seemed to come from inside it, a broken ring of something that had once been whole. [166]

Vell kept photographs in a tin beneath the tool bench. [176] Fading photographs, their edges curling, their faces bleeding into fog. [186] She had collected them from estate sales, from the garden's archived records, from strangers who did not know what they were handing over. [209] Each one showed the living bridge garden at a different decade, a different arrangement of the same organic architecture. [228] She studied them the way a cartographer studies coastlines that no longer exist. [241] The photographs were fading, yes, but she was fading faster. [251] Each retrocognitive episode left her less certain which layer of time was the one she breathed in. [268]

Her motivation was not nostalgia. [273] She wanted to discover the texture of time, the actual grain of it, the way it folded and pressed against itself. [294] She believed the maze could reveal it. [301] Not a metaphor. [304] A mechanism. [306]

She had begun designing the new central corridor using hyperbolic geometry, curves that widened as they approached their own centers, paths that seemed to multiply the deeper you walked. [335] Hyperbolic space was not decoration. [340] It was the architecture of memory itself, the way recollection expanded disproportionately the closer you came to its origin point. [360] She laid out the hedgerows in patterns that should not have been possible in Euclidean space, and the garden responded. [380] The living bridges shifted. [384] Their roots reoriented overnight, following angles that hurt to look at directly. [396] Gardeners on the staff quit within the month, claiming the paths moved when they weren't looking. [412]

It was in the third week of construction that she found the halo fragment herself, half-buried where the central path diverged into impossible branches. [436] She had seen it before, in the retrocognitive vision of the weeping woman. [449] Now she held it, and the past and present synchronized with a violence that made her knees buckle. [467] The fragment pulsed once, and she saw the woman's face clearly for the first time, a face she recognized from the oldest photograph in her collection, the one so faded it was almost blank. [501]

The photographs in her tin began to disintegrate. [509] Not fade. [511] Disintegrate. [512] Through hyperbolic geometry, the same warping she had built into the maze, the images curled inward on themselves, their surfaces multiplying into infinite recession. [536] Each photograph folded along lines that should not have existed in two dimensions, and the faces, the garden, the bridges, the weeping woman, all of it compressed into a point that was simultaneously every moment and none. [573]

Vell understood then that she had not been designing a maze. [584] She had been building a lens. [590] The halo fragment was the last piece of something that measured time not as sequence but as surface, and the maze was the frame that held it. [617] She stood in the quaking reverie of a garden that was every version of itself at once, the living bridges above her growing and dying and growing in the same breath, and she felt the texture of time against her skin. [658] It was not smooth. [662] It was not linear. [666] It was porous and infinite, like a hedge maze with no center and no exit, and she was already inside it, and she had always been inside it, and the fragment in her hand was warm with a light that had not yet been emitted and would never stop. [715]
