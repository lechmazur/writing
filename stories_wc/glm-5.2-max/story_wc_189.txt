The words settled like sediment: "I'm not sure I can do this anymore." [13]

Cora sat with her knees drawn up, her face half in starlight, half in the grotto's deep shadow. [31] She had been Fen's apprentice for four years, and in that time they had rarely spoken about anything that mattered. [51] Now they were trying, and the trying had led them here, to this place where the sea came inland, and to a silence that held more weight than either of them could lift. [84]

Fen was warmly observant by nature, the kind of woman who noticed the way hermit crabs tested a shell before committing. [105] She had spent thirty-five years as the tidal-pool chronicler for the coastal village of Thornbeck, recording the comings and goings of creatures in the pools that formed along the ancient grotto's floor. [137] Thirty-five years of paying attention to things that happened at the edges, in the margins, in the pauses between the tide's arrival and its retreat. [161] These pauses were where she had learned to live: not in the rush of change but in the held breath before it. [183]

She did not answer Cora immediately. [189] Instead she watched the pool. [194]

A small green crab moved along the basin's edge, testing the water with one clawed leg. [210] The tide had retreated an hour ago, and the pool was cut off from the sea now, a tiny world between worlds. [232] Everything here existed in transition—the pool would drain or refill, the creatures would shelter or emerge, the light would shift or fade. [254] Nothing was permanent, and that was not a flaw. [263] That was the entire point. [268]

On the ledge beside her, the jade cicada that she kept as a paperweight and touchstone caught a scatter of stars. [289] She had found it in this grotto decades ago, wedged in a fissure where ancient hands had placed it. [308] Whoever carved it had understood patience. [314] The insect's kind spent years underground in silence before emerging into a few bright days of song, and the carver had captured the moment of emergence—the poised wings, the body taut with what it was about to do. [352]

Fen had studied transition all her life, but she had never been good at living through one herself. [370]

She watched the crab disappear under a red-rock shelf. [380] She watched a limpet tighten its grip. [387] She noted, without writing it down, how the water level dropped another quarter-inch as the night progressed—the pool's own difficult conversation with gravity, and how it handled the loss, and how the small creatures adjusted, not all at once but in a series of small consentings. [432]

And through this tangential watching—through the crab and the limpet and the lowering water and the cicada that had been carved by someone who also knew about waiting—Fen began to see where their conversation was going. [469] She saw that Cora would leave, not because the work was wrong but because she had outgrown it. [487] She saw that the loss would pass through her own life the way the tide passed through the grotto, leaving everything rearranged but undestroyed. [511] She saw that the texture of time, which she had spent her life trying to describe, was exactly this: the uneven grain of a moment that holds both grief and strangeness and the first faint stirring of what comes next. [551]

She turned back to Cora, who was watching her with the wary patience of someone who had expected an argument and was now unsure. [576]

"You already know you're leaving," Fen said. [583] "You knew before you said it." [589]

Cora's mouth opened, then closed. [594] "I didn't know how to say it." [601]

"Nobody does." [603] Fen touched the jade cicada one last time. [611] "That's what the pause is for." [617]

Above them the stars moved in their slow circles, and the pool waited for whatever would arrive next, and neither of them spoke again until morning. [643]
