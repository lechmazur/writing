Aldous Greer had kept the lighthouse at Opal Point for thirty-one years, long enough to have forgotten whether his eccentricities had caused his isolation or the other way around. [29] He spoke to the lamp as though it could answer, named the seabirds by the rhythms of their flight rather than their species, and kept a ledger not of ships but of the silences between them. [65] At nautical twilight, when the sky turned the color of a bruised plum and the mist rolled in from the Opal Dock like something breathing, he lit the lamp and began his watch. [98]

The stranger had arrived three nights ago, stumbling off a fishing trawler that never registered in Aldous's ledger because it left no gap in the pattern of expected traffic. [127] The man wore a coat too heavy for the season and a dried Edelweiss boutonniere pinned to his lapel, its petals flattened and translucent like something pressed between pages of a book no one had opened in decades. [165] He did not speak. [169] He walked the length of the Opal Dock and stopped where the planks warped and separated, leaving gaps through which the dark water showed. [193] Then he looked at Aldous, raised one hand -- not waving, not pointing, but holding the gesture somewhere between -- and let the boutonniere fall from his coat into the gap between the boards. [225]

The next morning, the stranger was gone. [232] No trawler, no footprints in the salt crust, no witness but the lamp and the birds. [248] Only the Edelweiss remained, caught on a rusted nail beneath the dock, visible only when Aldous lay flat and peered down through the gap. [272]

He was not a man given to investigation, but the softly persistent question of that gesture worked into him like water into stone. [295] Why the gap? [298] Why the flower? [301] He began to investigate the way he investigated everything -- by observing gaps. [313] Not what was present, but what was missing. [321]

He checked his ledger. [325] There was no gap in the expected arrivals, which meant the stranger's ship had been invisible not by absence but by fitting too perfectly into the pattern. [352] He checked the dock. [356] The gap where the boutonniere had fallen was wider than the others, wide enough for a hand to pass through. [376] He checked his own memory and found the first gap that frightened him: he could not remember the stranger's face. [396] Not because it was forgettable, but because remembering it felt like trying to recall a dream someone else had dreamed. [416]

That was when he understood the borrowed memories. [424] They had been arriving for weeks -- images of mountain passes, the smell of snow he had never stood in, the sound of a woman humming in a language he did not speak. [456] He had dismissed them as the loneliness playing its usual tricks, the mind generating company from nothing. [473] But the Edelweiss was a mountain flower. [480] The humming was an alpine song. [486] The memories were not his. [491] They were the stranger's, deposited like messages in a bottle, arriving not through speech but through proximity, through some mechanism Aldous could not name but could no longer deny. [520]

The final gesture made sense now -- or nearly. [528] The stranger had not dropped the boutonniere by accident. [537] He had placed it in the gap because the gap was the point. [550] The dock was a membrane between worlds, and the gaps were where things passed through. [565] The stranger had come to leave his memories with the only witness available: a man so isolated he would notice when something didn't belong in his own mind. [593]

Aldous retrieved the boutonniere that evening, at nautical twilight again, lying flat on the opal dock while the mist softened everything. [614] The dried petals crumbled slightly in his fingers. [622] He pinned what remained to his own coat and stood watch, carrying another man's mountains inside him, keeping them lit the way he kept the lamp -- not because he understood them, but because he was the only one left to witness the dark. [665]
