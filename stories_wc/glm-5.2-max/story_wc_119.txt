The lighthouse on Carreg Island had been decommissioned for thirty years, but its light still swept the waters each night, a phantom vigil that no one had ordered and no one could explain. [34] Wen Zhao kept it burning. [39] He told the mainlanders it was for nostalgia, and they accepted this because Wen was their fortune cookie writer, and fortune cookie writers were allowed their eccentricities. [64]

What nobody said, though everyone knew, was that Wen's fortunes came true. [76] Not in the vague way of horoscopes, but precisely, devastatingly. [85] The waitress who found "A door you forgot exists will open" discovered her estranged daughter waiting at the diner entrance the next morning. [105] The fisherman who got "Stillness yields more than motion" pulled his best catch in forty years during a forced harbor day. [121]

These were open secrets. [125] The islanders read their fortunes with steady hands and said nothing, because saying something would make it real in a different, more dangerous way. [149]

Wen himself didn't understand it. [155] He wrote what felt right, what arrived in the space between breaths, and the words simply landed, took root, and grew. [175]

He'd come to Carreg to escape the cookie factory in San Francisco, where his words had traveled to millions of diners with unsettling accuracy. [198] The company had called it coincidence. [204] Wen had called it terrifying. [209] He'd requested a transfer to obscurity and gotten it, becoming the sole supplier for the island's single Chinese restaurant, the Jade Lantern, which served twelve tables on a good night. [237]

The microdot film specs were his father's, bequeathed without explanation along with a note that read: "For when you need to hide the truth in plain sight." [264] Wen had studied them, learned to write small enough that a single period at the end of a fortune could contain an entire paragraph of text. [290] He'd never used the technique. [296] Until tonight. [299]

It was after last orders at the Jade Lantern. [308] The last ferry had departed. [313] The last batch of fortune cookies for the season had been consumed, cracked open by locals who read their slips with the careful attention of people defusing bombs. [339]

And Wen sat at his desk in the lighthouse keeper's quarters, the beam rotating above him, and began to write. [359]

He was orchestrating something. [363] Not a prank, not a scheme, but a redirection. [372] For thirty years he'd been a passive vessel for whatever power shaped his words. [387] Tonight, he would direct its flow. [394]

The fortune read: "The music changes when you stop playing the old song." [406]

In the period at the end, invisible to the naked eye, he'd encoded the microdot specifications for a camera lens his father had designed decades ago — one that could photograph documents in total darkness. [437] The specs had no practical value anymore; the technology was obsolete, a relic of Cold War tradecraft. [455] But they were proof that his father had been a spy, proof that Wen had grown up inside an open secret he'd only fully understood at fifty. [479]

And the fortune, he realized with a familiar dread, would come true. [491]

He placed the slip inside a cookie, sealed it, and set it on the windowsill where the morning supply boat would collect it. [515] The lighthouse beam passed over the cookie like a benediction. [526]

What Wen wanted — what he'd wanted since the day he'd realized his words shaped the world — was to transpose his life into a new key. [550] Not to stop writing, but to write without the weight of prophecy. [562] To be accidental in a different direction. [570]

The fortune would reach Mrs. Calloway, who ordered takeout every Tuesday without fail. [585] She would read it, and something in her life would shift, some old song she'd been playing would finally end, and a new one would begin. [611] And in the period at the end, if she ever thought to examine it with a magnifying glass, she'd find the ghost of a dead spy's work, a secret hiding inside an open one. [645]

This was Wen's orchestration: an open secret wrapped in a true prophecy, a gift that couldn't be thanked for because the giver remained invisible. [675]

The lighthouse turned. [678] The tide pulled. [681] The flow he had set in motion would reach the mainland by dawn. [694]

He sat back and waited for the dread, but what came instead was something closer to peace. [713] For the first time in thirty years, the fortune he'd written felt like his own. [731]

Outside, the beam swept the rocks, and the island held its breath, and the old song, at last, began to fade. [754]
