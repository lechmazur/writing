In the abandoned spaceport repair bay, where starship hulls once echoed with the grind of plasma welders, Maren pressed her palms against cold deck plating and felt the edges of elsewhere. [30]

She was a sensory map maker, one of the last. Her craft was not cartography in any traditional sense. She mapped the contours of perception itself, the textures of reality as it pressed against human consciousness. Each world she touched left a signature: the metallic taste of one universe, the amber warmth of another, the sound of wind through crystal forests that had never existed in any timeline her ancestors would recognize. [97]

The repair bay had been her workshop for eleven years, ever since the last transport left Orin Station and the port authority stopped sending power to Sector Seven. She did not need much. Her work required silence, solitude, and the kind of stillness that only abandonment could provide. [152]

The green glass shard sat on her workbench, nestled in a cradle of copper wire she had bent during her first year here. She had found it embedded in the hull of a derelict tug, fused into the metal as though it had grown there. Its surface was uneven, rippled, and when she held it to the half-light filtering through the bay's cracked skylights, she could see layers within it, dozens, each slightly different in hue, as though the glass had captured slices of light from places that should not have been reachable. [250]

She had spent years learning what it was. [259]

The shard did not open doorways. It did not glow or hum. What it did was more subtle, and more dangerous. When Maren pressed her fingertips to its surface and focused her attention through the layers, she could feel the thinning of the boundary between her world and others. Not one other world, but many, stacked and folded against each other like pages in a book compressed until the ink bled through. [349]

Weaving between worlds was the term her mentor had used, back when Maren was still apprenticed on Pellicia Prime, before her mentor disappeared and left only a notebook filled with sensory notations and a single word circled three times: redirect. [394]

That word had haunted her. Her mentor's notes described the process in fragments. Reality, she had written, is not a fixed vessel but a weave. Threads can be pulled, loosened, and most importantly, redirected through parallel universes. The danger was not in the act itself but in its nature. The weaving was cyclically spontaneous. You could prepare, center yourself, press the shard, and nothing would happen. Then, in an unguarded moment, the boundaries would thin without warning, and you would feel yourself slipping sideways into a place that felt almost like home but was not. [584]

That was where her mentor had gone. [591]

The truth she sought was simple and devastating in its simplicity. Her mentor had not been lost. She had chosen to go. She had found something in the spaces between worlds, some truth about the nature of the weave, and had decided it mattered more than any single life, more than the apprentice she left behind. [659]

Maren wanted to understand why. She wanted to know the weight of that withheld truth, to feel its mass in her own hands and judge for herself whether it justified abandonment. [697]

Tonight, in the murk of half-remembered dreams, the bay around her softened. The plasma welders on the far wall began to look less like machines and more like the ribs of some sleeping creature. The deck plating warmed beneath her hands. She felt the familiar sensation, the one that came without warning, the thinning. [762]

She pressed the shard. [766]

The layers parted, and she fell sideways into elsewhere. [775]
