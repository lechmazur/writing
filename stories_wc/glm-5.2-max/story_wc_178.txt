Iltani counted everything. [3] Grain portions, lamp oil, steps to the observatory tower. [12] In the old king's final months, when the treasury bled dry and court astronomers fled for richer courts, she stayed -- not from loyalty, but because the palace library held seven thousand tablets she had not yet finished reading. [50] Now the king was dead. [55] His nephew marched from the eastern provinces, three weeks distant. [65] The palace staff had scattered. [70] Guards, cooks, scribes -- all gone to declare allegiance somewhere profitable. [80] Iltani remained because leaving would cost a donkey she did not own. [92]

The interregnum suited her. [96] For the first time in nineteen years, no one interrupted her work to request auspicious marriage dates or battle omens. [116] The observatory was hers -- its clay dome cracked but functional, its bronze instruments tarnished but true. [132]

She had found the tile three nights ago, wedged behind a crumbling wall in the lower archives. [149] Square, the size of her palm, made of a dark stone she could not identify. [164] On its face were etched numbers in a grid -- three by three. [176] She recognized the pattern: a magic square, where every row, column, and diagonal summed to fifteen. [192] But the Babylonians had not, as far as she knew, constructed such things. [205] This was older. [208] The cuneiform strokes were archaic, pre-Sargonic. [214]

And the numbers were wrong. [219]

Not mathematically. [221] They summed correctly. [224] But when she transcribed them into her own notation, mapping each value to its corresponding star position in the Mul.Apin catalog, the result was not random. [250] It was a coordinate. [254] A point in the heavens she had never observed, in a gap between constellations she had studied for two decades. [274]

She was an empiricist. [278] She did not believe in what she could not measure. [288] Yet here was measurement itself pointing toward something immeasurable. [297] That was the irony she kept returning to -- the tile operated by rules she trusted, arithmetic she could verify, and it led somewhere her rules could not follow. [325] It was empirically ironic. [329] Data-driven faith. [331]

For three nights she cross-referenced. [336] She drew schematic representations -- grid overlays, star maps, geometric projections -- spreading her reed-drawn diagrams across the observatory floor. [354] With each iteration, the tile's true purpose uncloaked itself, revealing layer after layer of concealed meaning. [370]

The tile was not merely a mathematical curiosity. [378] It was a message, encrypted in the only language she would trust: numbers. [391] And the message was not instructions. [397] It was devotion. [400]

She could see it now, through her schematics. [408] The coordinate pointed to a place between stars, yes, but the magic square's internal logic -- its symmetry, its self-referencing sums -- encoded something else. [431] A pledge. [433] A covenant, offered to whatever existed in that dark gap. [443] Someone, long ago, had built this tile as a promise to the unknown: I will find you. [460] I will speak your language. [465] I will come. [468]

Iltani understood covenants. [471] She had made one herself, years ago, to a woman named Belit who had taught her to read the sky. [491] Belit had died in the fever season, and Iltani had never properly mourned because mourning required time she classified as unproductive. [512] She had been frugal with grief, as with everything. [521]

But now, in the amber dusk that filled the observatory each evening -- that dusky harmony of failing light and rising stars -- she felt the weight of what she had hoarded and what she had withheld. [556]

She aligned the tile with her final schematic. [564] The coordinate matched a gap she could see tonight, if the clouds stayed clear. [578] She would observe it. [582] Not because she expected an answer -- she was still an empiricist -- but because the act of looking was itself a covenant with the unknown. [606] To turn your eye toward what you cannot measure and acknowledge its presence was the only honest prayer. [624]

The last light drained from the dome's crack. [632] Stars emerged. [634] She found the gap. [638] It was empty. [641] Of course it was empty. [646]

But her hand, resting on the tile, felt warm. [655] The schematic beneath her knees seemed to hum with a pattern she had not drawn -- a fourth row, faintly glowing, numbers she could almost read. [680] She did not record this. [685] It would not survive scrutiny. [690] But she did not move her hand, and she did not look away from the empty space between stars, and when dawn came, she was still there, watching, as the unknown uncloaked itself through the language she had finally learned to speak. [732]
