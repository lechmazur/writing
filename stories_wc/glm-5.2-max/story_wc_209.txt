Sera pressed her thumb against the amber wall of the pavilion, feeling the resin warmth that the autumn sun had stored there since dawn. [22] The structure crowning the hill above the village had stood for three centuries, octagonal and golden, its windows of etched glass reputed to hold patterns that no modern artisan could replicate. [48] She had come here to reckon. [54] Reckon with what, exactly, she still could not say, only that the murals she painted had begun to feel like questions rather than answers, and the feeling had grown teeth. [85]

Across the melting of winter's last snow, she had traveled with nothing but her pigments, her brushes, and a collection of old postcards bound with twine that her grandmother had left behind. [118] The postcards depicted night-blooming flowers: queen of the night cereus, moonflower, evening primrose, each specimen painted in meticulous watercolor on the front, each back scrawled with her grandmother's cramped handwriting. [148] It was to understand why flowers bloom at night that Sera had accepted the commission to restore the murals inside the cathedral below the pavilion, though she understood she was really trying to understand her grandmother, who had spent forty years studying nocturnal flora and had died before explaining any of it. [195]

She was a vivid mural artist, her colors saturated and her compositions bold, but inside the cathedral she had felt something shift. [219] The stone interior absorbed her paints differently, swallowing her usual brilliance into something quieter, something shadowed and tender. [240] For weeks she had worked on scaffolding beside the great western window, tracing the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window that depicted the life of a saint she did not recognize. [271] The glass was extraordinary: three panels fused together, each from a different century, each etched with images that only aligned when viewed from a precise angle. [298]

She had found the angle by accident one evening, standing back to assess her work on the opposite wall. [317] The fading light had struck the window at such a slant that the layers merged, and suddenly the saint was also a woman kneeling among flowers that opened beneath a crescent moon. [345] Night-blooming flowers. [348] The same species from her grandmother's postcards. [357]

The discovery left Sera quietly driven. [362] Each evening she abandoned her mural work and studied the window, mapping how the layers interacted as light shifted, documenting which etchings appeared and which disappeared. [390] She began bringing the postcards into the cathedral, propping them on the scaffold platform, matching their watercolor flowers to the ones hidden in the glass. [415] Her grandmother's handwriting on the postcard backs suddenly seemed less like annotation and more like letters addressed to someone specific. [440]

On the final evening of her work, as the last snowmelt dripped from the pavilion eaves outside and trickled down the hillside toward the village, Sera sat beneath the window and untied the postcard collection. [476] She had not yet read the handwriting. [485] She had been waiting, though she could not have said for what. [497]

Now she understood the waiting. [503] A dawning hush had settled into the cathedral, not silence exactly, but the sound of illumination arriving. [521] She read every postcard in order, and the story assembled itself like the layers of glass: her grandmother had known about this window, had visited it as a young woman, had designed her botanical postcards as a record of the flowers depicted here, believing that night-blooming flora encoded a language older than written speech, a language that spoke of what emerges when ordinary attention sleeps. [596]

The murals Sera had been restoring were her grandmother's early work, unsigned but unmistakable in their technique. [617] The woman who had spent forty years studying why flowers bloom at night had first painted flowers on these very cathedral walls, then returned to them as a botanist, searching in living specimens for the pattern she had intuited in paint. [658]

Sera gathered the postcards and climbed to the scaffold one final time. [672] The window glowed. [675] She held each postcard against the glass where its matching flower was etched, and through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window she saw what her grandmother had left for her: instructions encoded in pigment and script, the completion of a cycle that had waited one generation to close. [725]

Outside in the amber pavilion, the autumn air cooled around the place where she had sat that morning touching the warm wall. [747] Inside, with umbral tenderness, she began painting the final mural, knowing now what blooms at night. [803]
