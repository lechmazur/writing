The silo had been a grain repository once, its curved concrete walls holding wheat against the seasons. [17] Now it held books, or what remained of them. [26] Maren called it the Reclaimed Silo Library, though no formal reclamation had occurred. [39] She had arrived one autumn with a lantern and a purpose, and the silo accepted her the way walls accept those who listen. [62]

Maren was an odd historian. [67] Not odd in manner, but odd in the way she understood history as something walls held in their grain. [86] She was sensitively perceptive to the residue of voices, the way lime and plaster absorbed breath and grief and confession. [106] Where others read pages, she read surfaces. [113] The silo's interior, a vast vertical column of shelving, hummed with accumulated utterance. [126]

She had come after reports confirmed what everyone felt: the last human dream had been dreamt. [142] In a hospice in what had been Iceland, an old woman named Yrsa had closed her eyes and dreamed of her mother hanging laundry, and then the faculty was gone. [172] No one dreamed anymore. [176] The axis had displaced. [180]

That was the phrase the scientists used, displaced axis, to describe what had shifted in the human brain. [198] Not the Earth's tilt, though that confusion filled the newspapers for months. [210] Something internal. [212] The axis around which sleeping consciousness once rotated had moved or dissolved or settled into permanent waking. [229] Without dreams, stories began to die. [235] Not all at once, but the way lamplight dies when the oil is gone. [249] People could still invent, but invention without dreaming was carpentry without warmth. [261] The walls, though, still held what had been spoken inside them. [272] And Maren wanted those stories. [277] Her motivation was simple: to collect the stories walls had heard before silence completed its long occupation. [294]

She found the diaries on a Tuesday in the silo's lower level, in a fireplace that had been bricked over and then unbricked by someone before her. [321] They were half-burned, their spines curled, their pages gone amber and soft as old cloth. [336] She knelt and began to probe them, not reading in the conventional sense, but pressing her fingertips to the char and letting the words surface the way groundwater surfaces under pressure. [367] The first diary belonged to a farmer named Aldric, dated some four hundred years earlier, when the silo was new. [387] He wrote about a tapestry. [392] His wife had woven it, a medieval-style work showing dragons coiling around a tower that might have been the silo itself, though the silo was concrete and the tower in the tapestry was stone. [426] The dragons were not fearsome. [431] They were listening, their heads turned toward the tower's walls as if attending to something inside. [447]

Maren turned the brittle pages with the care of someone handling a living wound. [461] The diary described how the tapestry had hung inside the silo for decades, absorbing the grain dust and the conversations of workers who spoke to the walls as laborers do, complaints and prayers and addresses to the dead. [499] Then fire took the lower level. [505] The tapestry burned, mostly. [509] But a fragment survived, a section showing one dragon's head, its eye open, its ear toward the wall. [527]

She found it behind the fireplace's back brick, wrapped in oilcloth that had mostly held. [542] The fragment was no larger than her hand. [550] The wool had kept its color surprisingly well, greens and umbers, a thread of gold in the dragon's eye. [569] She held it and the silo went quiet in the particular way that bronze goes quiet: not silent, but resonant, as if the metal of the air had been struck and was still vibrating. [603]

The diary's last legible entry read: she said the dragons in the tapestry were not decoration. [619] They were the shape of what the walls heard. [628] Every wall has its dragon. [633] Every wall has its listener. [638]

Maren set the fragment on the shelf and pressed her palm flat against the silo's wall. [654] Under her fingers, something in the concrete was still listening. [664]
