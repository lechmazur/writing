The spectral grotto breathed saltwater and starlight, its limestone walls catching glow from a sky that shouldn't exist this far below the surface. [23] Marcus hauled rope on the deck of the last commuter ferry, the Pelagia, a vessel that had been vanishing, passenger by passenger, rivet by rivet, for the better part of an hour. [55] He was ardent about his work, had been since his first day on the docks twelve years ago, and even now, with the boat dissolving around him, he coiled line with careful loops and checked the mooring cleats, because that was what a deckhand did. [100] The hourglass mounted on the wheelhouse wall had run empty three crossings ago. [113] Not emptied; never been full. [118] Marcus had noticed it his first shift: twin bulbs of clear glass with no sand between them, mounted like a compass. [139] He'd assumed it was decorative. [144] Now he understood it was diagnostic. [150] When the Pelagia slipped into the grotto on its 11:47 PM run, that hourglass should have been full, should have been draining. [172] Its emptiness meant the ferry had entered a place where time couldn't measure itself normally. [187] He was, against all evidence, glad to be here. [196] That was the thing about Marcus; loss had taught him that panic was a luxury and joy was a discipline. [216] His mother had raised three kids alone on a cleaner's wage; she'd laughed more than anyone he knew. [234] He'd inherited that, or learned it, or both. [242] Even standing on a dissolving deck in an impossibly beautiful cave lit by impossible stars, even watching the last commuter, a woman in a grey coat, blink out of existence near the starboard rail, even then he felt the strange electric privilege of witnessing something no one else would believe. [292] The radio crackled. [295] He'd been trying to reach shore for twenty minutes, getting only static and fragments that sounded like his own voice played backward. [317] He keyed the handset again. [322] "Mayday, mayday, this is the Pelagia, in the grotto passage, requesting—" [333] The static shifted, and he heard himself finishing the sentence from a moment ahead: "—any vessel, any vessel, we are losing structure." [355] He paused. [357] Keyed it again. [360] "Who is this?" [363] His own voice, delayed and reversed, threaded back through the speaker, syllables rearranging into new words: "This is you. Both of you. The pattern's almost complete." [389] Marcus stared at the radio, then at the hourglass, then at the grotto walls where starlight moved in slow spirals across the wet stone. [413] The spirals weren't random. [417] He'd been watching them without watching them, the way you watch highway lines. [430] They were interconnected, each whorl linked to the next, branching and rejoining, and where they intersected, the air shimmered and the ferry's deck flickered, solid for a half-second, then translucent, then solid again. [463] The clocks on the bulkhead ran backward. [470] He'd noticed that when they entered the grotto, the big brass ship's clock spinning counterclockwise, the digital readout on the radio cycling 11:47, 11:46, 11:45. [495] Time here moved in reverse, which meant the ferry wasn't vanishing. [506] It was arriving. [509] Being assembled. [511] Every passenger who'd ever disappeared on this route was somewhere ahead in the passage, and every crossing that had ever happened was layered into the walls like geological strata, visible in the way light bent. [546] His own voice came through again, clearer now: "The timelines are touching. They've been touching for years. Every ferry that entered the grotto split. One made it home. One stayed here. They want to merge." [581] Marcus felt the deck solidify under his boots. [589] Felt the weight of the rope in his hands become real in a way it hadn't been a moment ago. [609] The woman in the grey coat flickered back into existence at the starboard rail, looking confused but whole. [627] He picked up the radio one more time. [635] "Then let's bring them home." [640] The hourglass, still empty, began to fill, sand rising from the lower bulb into the upper, time running sideways, two versions of every moment pressing together like palms meeting across a mirror. [672] The grotto filled with light, and the Pelagia became the most real thing in the world, and Marcus held his station on the deck and felt, as he always did, the permanent sweetness of being exactly where he was, however briefly, before moving on.
