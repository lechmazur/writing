The orchard clung to the cliff's edge like a wound that refused to close. [14] Its branches bore fruit that shimmered between ripeness and rot, and Ward tended them because someone had to. [32] The curse came down from his mother, who got it from hers, reluctant caretakers stretching back four generations. [50] He hated the trees. [54] He hated their low, whispering moans at dusk and the way their roots cracked the stone paths he maintained. [73] But mostly he hated the labyrinth. [79]

The cliffside labyrinth wound below the orchard in tight, spiraling corridors of basalt and salt-hardened earth. [95] Mechanical birds patrolled its turns, brass-winged things with gear-driven eyes that clicked and whirred as they scanned for movement. [114] They did not attack. [118] They only watched, and whatever they saw, the orchard seemed to know. [130] Travelers who wandered in rarely wandered out. [137] The trees fed on their confusion, on the slow panic that rose like sap. [151]

Ward had stopped trying to dismantle the birds years ago. [161] Instead, he studied them. [165] He mapped their routes on parchment scraps, noting times and positions with a precision that visitors would have called obsessive. [185] He was not obsessive. [189] He was offhandedly exact, the kind of man who counted cracks in a wall without looking up. [206] It was simply how his mind worked, cataloguing details without effort, filing them away for later. [222]

Later came on an evening in late autumn. [230] The first traveler in months had stumbled into the maze, a woman with a torn coat and bleeding hands. [249] Ward saw her from the orchard's edge, a small figure moving wrong among the corridors. [264] The sun was low. [268] He had perhaps two hours before the night frost formed. [278] After that, the woman would die in the maze. [287] He knew this the way he knew everything about this place, without drama, without surprise. [302]

He did not go down into the labyrinth. [310] He never did. [313] The trees would not allow it; their roots would trip him, their branches would whip across his face. [331] But he had learned something in all his years of watching. [342] Pressed into a hollow in the oldest tree, he had found a small nut, bronze and threaded, no larger than his thumbnail. [364] He did not know its origin. [370] What he knew was that when he held it near the ground and walked the orchard's perimeter, the earth below the cliff responded. [393] Footpaths darkened. [395] Corridors shifted. [397] The labyrinth's geometry softened, barely, but enough. [404]

He held the nut out over the cliff edge and began to walk. [417]

He could not touch the labyrinth directly. [424] But he had discovered that by leaving impressions without touching, tracing patterns in the air above the maze, the nut's influence reached downward like a hand pressing into clay. [453] Corridors realigned. [455] Dead ends opened. [458] The woman below stumbled, stopped, and turned left where there had been only a wall. [473] She looked confused. [476] She looked up. [479]

Ward did not wave. [483] He kept walking and tracing, his arm aching to hold the nut steady. [496] Behind him, a mechanical bird landed on a fence post and watched, its gears clicking rapidly. [512] It did not interfere. [516] He wondered if some ancient instruction in its brass heart recognized the nut's authority. [530]

He had pieced together the rest over years. [538] The orchard's curse, the labyrinth, the birds, all of it followed a design. [551] He had found fragments of that design scratched into the root cellar's walls, a forgotten blueprint for something that had never been finished. [574] Whoever built this place had meant to protect travelers, not trap them. [586] The maze was supposed to guide. [592] The birds were supposed to watch for the lost. [601] But the blueprint had been abandoned, and the half-built system curdled into something predatory. [615] The nut was the last piece of the original mechanism. [625]

Ward did not have the parts or the knowledge to assemble the whole thing. [639] He could not fix what was broken. [646] But he could use what remained to steer the wreckage toward its original purpose. [660] He could guide lost travelers to safety, one at a time, with a small nut and a slow walk along the cliff's edge. [683]

The woman found the exit as the first frost crept across the labyrinth walls. [697] She looked back once, then ran. [703] Ward lowered the nut and pressed his hand against his burning shoulder. [715] The mechanical bird on the fence post tilted its head. [725]

"Fifteen minutes early tonight," he told it. [732]

It clicked twice and flew away. [738] The orchard groaned, branches heavy with fruit that would never be picked. [750] He looked at the ugly trees, alive with something he could not name. [763] Broken things had their own graceless beauty, when you knew what they were meant to become. [779]
