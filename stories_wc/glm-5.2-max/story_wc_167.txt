The harbor glazier Maren stood at the edge of Pearl Bay, where the tide had left a shallow pool on the stone quay. [23] Dawn crept along the waterline, turning the mist into something like breath held between sea and sky. [40] He'd been awake for hours. [45] Before everything shifts, he needed this. [51]

[81] He'd built it over twelve years, replacing each cracked pane with glass he'd cut and polished himself, fitting the pieces together so they'd catch morning light and fracture it into spectra that crawled along the harbor floor like living things. [121] Most people walked past it without stopping. [128] Most people saw glass and saw through it, never into it. [139] That was the trouble with the mundane: it was invisible precisely because it was everywhere. [154]

Maren was a reflective man, not just in temperament but in habit. [166] He'd spent thirty years reading light through glass, learning how imperfections told stories: a bubble trapped in a pane spoke of haste in the furnace, a wavy distortion told of uneven cooling. [198] He'd learned to read between lines the way he read between panes: finding meaning in the spaces others overlooked. [217]

This was how he communed. [276] Not with any god or spirit, but with the water itself, with the logic of disturbance and response. [294] The first ring was always sharp, a clean circle of energy. [305] The second lagged slightly, its wave already interacting with the stone quay's edge, bouncing back in subtle interference patterns. He watched the rings cross and re-cross, and in their intersections he read the night's reckoning. [366]

Noctilucent reckonings, he called them: the luminous truths that surfaced only in the dark hours, when the mind grew still enough to receive them.
