Vera was brightly attentive to gaps. [6] As a corporate memory keeper for the Auricle Social Network Archive, she maintained the vast repository of user data that the living left behind—posts, messages, music playlists, all preserved in amber-light servers that hummed in the basement of a building most people walked past without noticing. [54] But she noticed what others missed: the spaces where things had been removed, the silences between data points where something used to exist. [77] Files didn't just disappear when users deleted them. [85] They went somewhere. [88] And Vera wanted to find where. [94]

It started with the numbers. [100] Seven-three-four-nine. [101] They appeared in corrupted metadata, in timestamps that shouldn't exist, in the play counts of songs no one had streamed in decades. [123] The same four digits, recurring like a heartbeat in the archive's dead data, pulsing where the system had tried to forget. [144] She found them embedded in a user's playlist titled "For When You Come Home," a collection of songs long forgotten except for their ghost-frequencies still echoing through the archive's deepest layers. [175]

The playlist belonged to Diana Lao. [181] Diana had been a communications specialist on the Meridian-4 mission, a deep-space reconnaissance vessel lost during reentry sixteen years ago. [200] The crash was ruled mechanical failure, the investigation perfunctory, the mourning brief. [212] Diana, along with two other crew members, was declared dead. [223]

But their bodies were never recovered from the wreckage. [233] The case went cold—a missing persons file that gathered dust while the world moved on to newer tragedies. [250]

Vera pulled Diana's archived social network presence. [258] The last activity was a photo of a mission patch—blue and silver thread forming a phoenix rising through atmosphere, the crew's names stitched along the border. [283] "Eleven days until launch," the caption read. [290] "I'll bring back stars." [295]

The patch should have been in the official crash debris, catalogued and stored in some military facility. [312] But here it was, photographed in Diana's quarters before the mission, and then Vera found it again—duplicated in three other users' archives, people who had no apparent connection to the crew. [345]

She traced the 7-3-4-9 sequence deeper into the system. [356] It wasn't random. [359] It was a file path fragment, broken apart and scattered across deleted entries throughout the archive. [376] Someone had dismantled a crucial file and distributed its pieces across forgotten songs, abandoned playlists, and orphaned messages—hiding the truth in plain sight, trusting that no one would think to look. [408] Across the echo of forgotten songs, Vera reassembled what had been erased: a video file, small and corrupted but watchable. [428]

She lied to access it. [433] Told her supervisor she was performing routine maintenance on Diana's sector, that there were corrupted files needing attention. [451] The lie came easily—too easily—and that disturbed her more than the numbers had. [466]

The video showed Diana, alive, three months after the crash. [477] She was in a facility somewhere, wearing civilian clothes, speaking to someone off-camera. [490] "They want to know if the signal was natural," she said, her voice thin but steady. [506] "I keep telling them I don't know. [514] But they won't let us leave until I figure it out." [526]

The Meridian-4 hadn't crashed. [531] It had been redirected. [535] The crew had discovered something during their mission—something that made them too valuable to release, too dangerous to acknowledge publicly. [556] The "crash" was a story, and the social network archives had been scrubbed to support it. [572]

But deleted files go somewhere. [577] And Vera had followed them back to the truth. [586]

She sat in the glow of her terminal, feeling the weight of every absence the archive contained. [603] Diana's playlists, frozen mid-queue, waiting for a listener who might never return. [615] Messages that would never be answered. [621] The patch that shouldn't have existed in duplicate, a clue left by someone who wanted the truth found eventually. [640]

The numbers were still appearing—7-3-4-9, 7-3-4-9—cropping up in new files now, as if the system itself remembered what had been taken and refused to let go. [666]

Vera was brightly attentive to gaps because she understood them. [677] Absence wasn't emptiness. [680] It was a shape, defined by everything that had been removed. [691] And in that shape, she could see the outline of three people who had been erased from the world but not from the data. [716]

She saved the video to her personal drive. [724] Then she began looking for the next set of numbers, trusting that somewhere in the archive, the missing were still speaking. [745] Vera just had to learn how to listen. [753]
