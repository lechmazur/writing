Lenna knelt at the edge of the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes, where volcanic glass held the afternoon like a dark mirror. [22] She watched the sky, gracefully alert, with the poise that had made her the youngest captain in the county fire brigade, her body loose but ready, her eyes tracing the migration of clouds that moved eastward in slow white herds. [62] Eleven years ago, her father had poured kerosene along these dunes and lit the match that scarred forty acres of coastal wetland. [84] Mal Aronson, they called him afterward, though she had always known him as Dad, as the man who sang while he cooked breakfast, who wept at wildlife documentaries, who set six fires in four years because something in him burned hotter than reason. [127] She had been seventeen when the judge asked her to testify, and she had looked at her father across the courtroom and said nothing, because silence was the only honest thing she had left. [161] She opened the jar of black sand from the volcanic beach three miles south, sand she had gathered at dawn, still warm from the earth's memory. [187] The sand was fine as ash, black as the obsidian shelf beneath the lagoon's surface, and she worked it into the soil around the base of a scorched manzanita stump that had refused to die. [222] For three seasons now she had come here during the cloud migration, when the wetland's humidity rose and the soil softened enough to accept what she brought. [249] She selected a living cutting from the manzanita's one surviving branch, a green slip of life emerging from blackened wood, and began to graft it onto a neighboring rootstock using the technique her mother's father had taught her before the arthritis took his hands. [293] The graft required precision, patience, and the kind of unyielding compassion that does not flinch from damage. [310] She had learned that from fighting fires, that compassion was not soft. [322] It was the thing that made you walk back into a burning building. [335] From her pocket she drew a foreign coin, an old Spanish real her father had brought home from a shipping job years before the first fire. [361] She had drilled a hole through its center and strung it on red thread, and now she hung it from a twig above the graft site, where it would turn in the wind and keep the birds from settling. [400] The coin caught the low light and threw it across the water in small bright circles. [416] Her father had written to her from prison, once. [425] He said he dreamed about the lagoon. [432] He said he was sorry. [437] She had read the letter in the firehouse kitchen at three in the morning, between calls, and she had folded it into her pocket and carried it until the creases gave out. [469] The graft took. [472] She pressed the black sand around the union, sealing the wound with volcanic mineral, and sat back on her heels. [492] The manzanita would live or it would not. [500] The scar tissue where the cuttings joined would harden into a ridge, an uneven seam where two damaged things had been persuaded to grow as one. [526] If it survived, the ridge would become part of the branch's grain, and in years, when the wind moved through the manzanita's leaves, the sound would carry something of both rootstock and cutting, a chord made of survival. [564] She had become a firefighter because someone had to stand where her father had stood and do the opposite. [583] She came to this lagoon because the land needed what she needed, which was proof that scars could sing. [602] The clouds continued their migration, throwing shadows that moved like dark fish across the obsidian surface. [618] The coin turned on its red thread. [625] Lenna stayed until the light changed, breathing the slow kelp-drift peace of the wetland at dusk, her hand resting on the earth where, beneath the black sand and the glass and the memory of fire, something was learning to grow.
