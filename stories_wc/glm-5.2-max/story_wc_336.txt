The waterfall had whispered the same secret for three hundred years, but only Maren knew how to listen. [18] She stood on the verdant parapet beside it, her pruning shears resting against her hip, and watched the mist rise into the dawn light like souls finding their way home. [48] The orchard grew here, on this narrow shelf of green stone between the falling water and the void below, and its trees did not belong to just one world. [77] Their roots drank from soil that existed in two realities at once — the world of daylight and certainty, and the world behind the veil, where colors had not yet been named and time ran like honey. [113] Maren had pruned these trees since she was old enough to hold a blade, learning from her grandmother that every cut was a prayer, every trimmed branch a doorway opened or closed. [145] She was insatiably creative in her craft, always searching for new ways to shape the living wood, always listening for what each tree needed to become. [171]

This morning, upon the hush of budding hope, she had come to tend the oldest tree — the one her grandmother called the Anchor, whose trunk was wide as a doorway and whose branches reached into both worlds at once. [210] But when she pressed her palm against its bark, she felt something wrong. [223] The Anchor was dimming. [227] The colors that ordinarily shimmered along its grain, deep golds and impossible blues that had no names in any language, were fading to gray. [251] Through that contact, Maren sensed the world behind the veil growing thin, its wonder leaking away like light through cupped hands. [272]

She reached into the leather pouch at her belt and withdrew the heirloom seed — small, dark, warm as a living heartbeat. [293] Her grandmother had given it to her on her deathbed, pressing it into her palm with whispered instructions: plant this only when the color begins to fail. [320] Maren had carried it for eleven years, waiting for the moment her grandmother had foreseen but never described. [338]

That moment had arrived. [342] She knelt at the base of the Anchor, where the roots split in two directions — one plunging into the stone of the parapet, the other vanishing into the shimmering air that led to the world behind the veil. [380] She scooped soil from the junction point, soil that was warm and faintly vibrating, and placed the seed within it. [400]

But planting alone would not be enough. [407] The seed needed succor — nourishment of a kind that ordinary water and light could not provide. [423] Maren closed her eyes and began the work she had been born to do: capturing essence. [439] She reached for the waterfall's mist, not its water, but the feeling of it — the exhilaration of falling, the surrender of becoming something smaller and more free. [466] She gathered that essence with her hands, shaped it with her will, and pressed it into the soil around the seed. [487]

Then she reached deeper, into the Anchor itself, drawing out the memory of every season it had witnessed, every shade of light that had touched its leaves. [514] She wove these essences together — the waterfall's joy, the tree's patience, the stone's silence — and fed them to the seed in a slow, radiant stream. [539] This was the heart of pruning, her grandmother had taught her. [550] Not cutting away, but weaving. [555] Weaving between worlds, threading what was dying with what was yet to be born. [569]

The seed split. [572] A pale green shoot rose from the soil, trembling, and Maren felt both worlds shudder in recognition. [589] The shoot grew upward, and as it grew, color returned to the Anchor's bark — gold first, then blue, then shades Maren had never seen before, colors that existed only in the space between wonder and memory. [625] The world behind the veil strengthened, its boundary thickening, its unnamed hues blooming back into being. [641]

Maren opened her eyes. [645] The parapet was bathed in light that seemed to breathe, and the waterfall sang a note she had never heard before — deeper, fuller, alive with gratitude. [671] The young shoot had already grown its first pair of leaves, and they shimmered with all the colors she had woven together. [693]

She sat back on her heels and let out a breath she had not realized she was holding. [711] The color of wonder would endure a little longer. [720] She picked up her shears, brushed the soil from her hands, and turned toward the next tree. [737] There was always more weaving to be done, and she was, after all, insatiably creative — a pruner of doorways, a keeper of what could not yet be named. [765]
