Maren had inspected hundreds of impact craters during her career with the Lunar Geological Survey, cataloging their geometry and marking their history before commercial consortiums could claim them. [28] During the forty years since the first lunar colony had been established, the moon had filled with infrastructure, and the unmarked craters were disappearing one by one under excavation. [57] This particular crater in Mare Marginis would be excavated within the next Earth-cycle. [70] Excavation ships were scheduled to arrive tomorrow. [77]

She had come to photograph the last dawn on its floor - the dawning hush, a geologists' term for the moment just before lunar twilight finished, when the regolith paused between extreme cold and brutal heat. [112] Three billion years of that silence, soon to end. [121]

Maren's purposeful curiosity was not casual wonder. [128] Each crater she recorded taught her about disappearances: dust patterns erased, glassy ejecta consumed by processing plants, mineral strata ground to cement for habitats that would never come down. [157] The crater inspector was a witness to endings. [165]

A single sand grain hung in a glass capsule from her suit's collar. [178] It had come from a nightwatch's hourglass, her uncle Theo's. [188] Theo had spent forty years as the last nightwatch at Hadley Observing Station, opening and closing the observation doors each evening as others' work continued under artificial lights. [216] When the station automated, he'd gifted Maren the hourglass. [225] When he passed, she'd kept one grain - dark from decades tipping inside glass. [238]

Maren stood on the moonlit summit of the crater's eastern rim, having already spent eleven Earth-days waiting to outlast the lunar night. [260] Supplies remained; her patience remained. [265] Around her, the cold of two weeks without sunlight had begun to ease, the regolith's stored heat bleeding slowly outward into the vacuum above. [289]

To communicate with her partner at Lagrange Station, Maren used chemical reactions for timed messages. [304] Ampoules of copper sulfate and barium chloride, arranged in sequence within a timing unit, mixed at calculated intervals to produce colored flames - greens, blues, reds - flares visible through her partner's telescope. [335] Electromagnetic signals degraded through regolith. [340] Chemistry was more reliable than satellites here. [347]

Twelve ampoules had been consumed during the prior eleven-day vigil. [357] The dawning hush approached, and only four remained. [365]

The phenomenon was not silence - sound required atmosphere. [373] It was a cessation of motion: settling dust stopped migrating as thermal gradients across the crater walls stabilized. [391] The regolith itself seemed to pause, holding breath it had never possessed. [403]

Maren aligned her plate camera toward the basin floor. [412]

The sun's first penumbral rays bled over the eastern rim. [422] Slow light pooled, revealing radial striations from the original impact so pristine they might have been cut yesterday. [440] For several minutes, no dust moved, no machinery disturbed, no orbiting craft crossed that arc of sky. [457] In one frame, the basin flourished with shadow and edge, features that would be ground into regolith cement within the cycle. [478]

She photographed. [480]

The exposure took twenty seconds. [485] Moisture gathered at the corners of her eyes, recalling nothing she could name but everything she had cataloged and lost. [505] She had learned years ago that grief and wonder shared the same gesture: holding still and waiting for the world to tell you what was about to be gone. [534]

Other craters she had documented stood already carved apart, their walls scarred by boreholes, their silence broken by operations dawn to dusk. [556]

She triggered the first ampoule. [561] Color bloomed into black sky - confirmation to her partner that the final image of this crater's dawning hush had been secured. [582]

When dawn fully broke, the consortium's ships would appear as bright sparks above the terminator. [597] By evening, boreholes would scar the basin floor. [605] By next cycle, no one would remember radial striations. [614]

Maren packed the camera and turned away from the rim. [624] She carried the image on a glass plate, the sand grain at her throat, and something wet that her faceplate's ventilator could not name or filter. [650]

Crater 317 was no longer anyone's to preserve. [658] But her witness would outlast it, carried in glass and in habit and in the worn grain of a nightwatch's patience. [679]
