The apprentice Maren had been aboard the Verdant for three weeks, the antique airship creaking through cloud banks no modern vessel would dare navigate. [24]
As a map apprentice to the Geographical Society, she had expected solitude, but not this particular kind -- the sort where you felt accompanied by every handwritten margin note in forgotten ship logs. [56]
She was diligently curious by nature, which made her both excellent and terrible at her work: excellent because she traced every anomaly to its source, terrible because she rarely stayed on assigned tasks. [89]
The Verdant had been her mentor's vessel before his disappearance, and she had taken this solo survey mission with one private goal: to chart the next great discovery, whatever form it might take. [122]
On the seventeenth evening, vast flocks of starlings swept alongside the airship in murmurations, their thousands of wings catching the last copper light. [145]
Their patterns were not random. [150]
Maren began to note this around sunset, sketching rapidly as the birds folded into shapes that looked less like instinct and more like instruction. [174]
She followed their movements to the old cargo hold, where she found something she had somehow missed before: a dusty hopscotch board painted onto the floorboards, half-hidden beneath canvas tarps. [204]
The board was no children's game. [210]
Its squares contained symbols she almost recognized, and by noting secret patterns in the arrangement -- prime numbers in the fourth column, navigational bearings in the seventh -- she realized it was a cipher. [242]
The key came from an unlikely source: her mentor's old polyglot's phrasebook, which she had kept for sentimental reasons, its pages dog-eared and coffee-stained from years of travel. [270]
More specifically, the dog-ear on page 146, which she now unfolded to find marginalia in four languages, each contributing a fragment to the board's code. [295]
She worked through the night as the starlings wheeled outside the portholes, their murmurations now clearly tracking the airship's path toward coordinates the hopscotch board had hidden. [322]
By dawn, Maren understood what she had found. [330]
Her mentor had been mapping stranded luminance -- light from dead stars, trapped in the upper atmosphere like fireflies in amber, invisible to ordinary observation but detectable to those who knew where to look. [363]
The starlings knew. [366]
They flew through it, their coordinated movements somehow tracing the contours of this trapped radiance. [381]
The hopscotch board was her mentor's shorthand record of where these fields existed, encoded in a children's game so that only someone with the phrasebook -- and the curiosity to note secret patterns -- could follow. [415]
Maren felt the full shape of her sociable isolation in that moment: alone at ten thousand feet, yet in dialogue with a vanished man through painted squares and folded page corners. [446]
She was not truly alone, she realized. [453]
The starlings, the old ship, the cipher -- all of it was conversation, just conducted over years instead of minutes, through objects instead of voices. [477]
She adjusted the airship's heading to match the coordinates she had decoded, and the stranded luminance began to appear in the morning fog: faint violet threads, impossible to photograph, visible only to someone who knew they were there. [515]
Maren marked her charts with the same symbols her mentor had used on the hopscotch board, adding her own notes to the phrasebook in her own handwriting, a new voice joining an old conversation. [549]
She thought about how strange it was that discovery often meant finding what someone else had already found, then understanding it for the first time in your own way. [578]
The next great discovery was not a place but a process -- a way of listening to light that had been trapped for millennia, and to mentors who had left only paper and patterns behind. [612]
The starlings dispersed as the sun rose fully, their work done. [623]
The Verdant continued forward into quiet skies, Maren at her charts, dog-earing the phrasebook's next blank page in case someone after her might need to find what she had found. [653]
She smiled at the empty cabin, and at the world outside filled with invisible light, and began to chart. [672]
