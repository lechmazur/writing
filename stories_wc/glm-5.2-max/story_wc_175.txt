Sera had always been an esoteric realist—someone who found the practical mechanics beneath mystical patterns, who could read the grammar of nature without pretending it spoke in riddles alone. [30] She stood in the verdant grotto above the clouds, where moss bearded the stone walls and water threaded through cracks like silver veins, and waited. [55] The last monarch butterfly was migrating. [61] That phrase still sounded wrong—singular where it should have been plural, "was" where "are" had always belonged. [79] But the cadence of their passage had been interrupted for decades, each year fewer, until only one remained, beating its orange-black wings southward through air that no longer remembered the shape of its kin. [113] She had come to honor overlooked histories. [120] Not the grand narratives of extinction that everyone knew—they'd written their elegies and moved on—but the smaller, quieter record: the way the monarchs had used this grotto as a waystation, resting on the mossy ledges before continuing their impossible journey. [162] No one had documented this. [167] No one had thought it important enough. [174] Sera knelt and began to build, her method simple—temporary structures as signals, assembled from what the grotto offered. [193] She gathered fallen leaves, twigs, strips of bark, and arranged them in small cairns along the ledge, each one facing south, each one a marker that said: you are not lost, this path was once well-traveled, someone remembers. [231] As her fingers worked, they trembled with a kind of blushing stillness—the warm, quiet urgency of doing something you know is futile but cannot bear to leave undone. [260] The grotto held its breath around her, humid and green and patient. [272] She found the moth wing near the back of the cavern, pressed flat against a wet stone—pale, translucent, veined with something that looked like writing in a language she couldn't read. [304] Moths and butterflies shared an order, Lepidoptera, but moths flew at night and butterflies by day, and their histories rarely intersected above ground. [327] She picked it up gently, feeling its weight—almost nothing, almost everything. [339] The wing was transiently rooted to her fingertip, adhered by the dampness of the grotto, and she thought about how nothing was truly permanent here—the cairns would dissolve in the next rain, the moss would shift, even the stone was slowly being carved by water. [385] She was building signals for the dead and nearly-dead, and the signals themselves were mortal. [400] She tied the moth wing to the topmost cairn with a thread of grass, knotting it carefully—once, twice, three times—so it would catch any breeze. [427] A knot was a promise of intention, she thought. [436] A way of saying: I chose this, I held this, I fastened this to something solid even if the solid thing was already falling apart. [461] The monarch came through an hour before dusk. [469] It entered the grotto as if remembering it, circling twice before landing on the ledge near her cairns. [487] Its wings were faded, the orange more like rust, the black veins thicker than she'd seen in photographs. [505] It rested. [507] Sera did not move. [511] She let the blushing stillness hold her, let the interrupted cadence of a million-year migration find its brief, final rest in this place above the clouds. [537] The moth wing trembled on its knotted perch. [545] The monarch opened and closed its wings once, slowly, as if testing a memory. [559] Then it lifted, and was gone. [565] Sera remained until full dark, building more cairns, tying more knots, making signals that would outlast the moment but not the season. [587] She honored the overlooked history by doing the only thing she could—she saw the pattern clearly, understood its ending, and still chose to build something temporary and true. [616] The grotto would forget her by spring, but the knots held, and somewhere below the clouds, the last monarch flew on. [637]
