Elias Vane cataloged the dead for a living. [8] He was a solemn civil-rights archivist aboard the Ariel, a floating library mounted in the gut of a decommissioned zeppelin that drifted over the American South, collecting oral histories and fragile documents that terrestrial archives refused to shelter. [46] Three weeks ago, he had found the letters, seven of them, wedged behind a filing cabinet in the segregation collection. [66] They were written by a voting-rights organizer named Cora Dell, and they described, in code, the location of an obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes outside Pascagoula, where witnesses to a 1966 lynching had buried their testimony in sealed jars. [105] Now, after the letters were found, the diary surfaced. [114] It was encrypted, its pages dense with symbol-cipher, wedged into the spine of a hymnal donated from a shuttered church. [134] Elias worked through the night, cross-referencing the cipher against Cora Dell's letter-code, and by dawn he had cracked enough to understand what the diary contained: names, dates, and the identities of the men who had killed a teenager named Boyd Marsh and dumped his body in that obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes. [186] The diary did something else to him. [193] As he read, the cipher dissolved into sensory fragments, the smell of pine resin, the sound of a screen door clapping shut, the weight of a humid Mississippi night pressing against his skin. [226] He was retrocognitively trapped. [230] Each decrypted passage pulled him deeper into the memory of events he had never lived, locking him inside the recollections of the people who had written and hidden the diary. [260] He felt Boyd Marsh's terror, Cora Dell's grief, the trembling hands of the witness who had buried the jars. [279] He could not stop reading. [284] He could not stop falling. [289] Each night the past poured through him like flame through paper, and he let it. [304] He brought the diary to Maren Lucca, the zeppelin's captain and a former Freedom Summer volunteer who had her own reasons for suspicion. [327] She refused to discuss it aboard the Ariel, where two federal observers had recently boarded under the pretense of catalog review. [348] Instead, she lowered the zeppelin to a sandbar along the Gulf coast, where tide-washed debris accumulated in long, curving lines. [368] They parleyed on the sand. [373] Maren insisted they communicate without paper, without speech that could be recorded. [385] So Elias arranged beach debris as messages, shells for names, driftwood lengths for dates, fragments of glass arranged in patterns only Cora Dell's cipher could decode. [411] He spelled out what the diary revealed, and Maren answered with her own arrangements, adding details from memory: the sheriff's deputy who had overseen the dumping, the minister who had provided the shovel, the judge who had quashed every attempt at an inquiry. [454] The truth emerged in tension between them, between what Elias had decrypted and what Maren had survived, between the archive and the living. [477] She had known Boyd Marsh. [482] She had been fourteen. [486] She had watched from behind a church window as the men drove him toward the dunes, and she had spent forty years believing no record would ever surface. [514] Elias understood now why the diary was encrypted, why the letters used code, why the testimony had been buried in jars beside an obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes. [542] The truth was dangerous. [546] The truth was still dangerous. [551] The men in the diary had descendants who held office, who sat on school boards, who donated to the very institutions that funded the floating library. [577] Every fact Elias uncovered was a thread that could be pulled until someone's world unraveled, or until his did. [596] He arranged one final message in the debris. [604] It was a question: what do we do now? [613] Maren studied the shells and glass. [619] The zeppelin groaned above them, its massive shadow blotting the sand. [630] She knelt and rearranged his message into a single word that Cora Dell's cipher rendered unmistakable: recover. [647] They would go to the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes, dig up the buried testimony, and carry it back into the sky where no one could bury it again. [676] Elias felt the retrocognitive pull again, sharper now, Cora Dell's hands in wet sand, her voice whispering a prayer. [695] The past was not behind him. [701] It was beneath him, black and glassy and waiting. [710]
