Ms. Yuen sat in the velvet-lined reading room of the Aldridge Library, where autumn light filtered through mullioned windows and turned the dust to gold. [25] For thirty years she had taught choir at the community center across the street, and for thirty years the enrollment had thinned. [47] The folk songs of the Hmong, the Cantonese opera scales, the gospel call-and-response she had once braided together into performances that left audiences weeping -- all of it was vanishing. [76] Not with a dramatic crash, but with the quiet withdrawal of a tide leaving shore. [91]

She was expectantly resigned to this. [97] Not bitter. [99] Not defeated. [101] Resigned in the way one resigns oneself to weather, while still, expectantly, packing an umbrella. [116]

On the table before her lay a zither soundhole rosette -- a delicate carved wooden piece, intricate as lace, from her grandmother's qin. [138] The instrument itself had been lost during the years her family fled from village to city, from city to coast, from coast to country. [162] Only the rosette survived, carried in a coat pocket through every border crossing. [175] It was small enough to close a fist around, and intricate enough to denote an entire tradition: the finger-plucked songs of the Pearl River delta, the music that had accompanied weddings, funerals, and harvests for centuries before the phonograph, before the radio, before the streaming service that now fed algorithmically curated silence into earbuds on every train. [232]

She had spent decades unlearning hatred. [238] Not the dramatic kind -- no shouting, no clenched fists. [247] The quiet kind. [250] The kind that settles in a child who is told, in a thousand small ways, that her grandmother's music is backward, that her accent marks her as provincial, that the old songs are embarrassments best forgotten. [286] She had hated herself for believing it. [293] Then she had hated the teachers who taught her to believe it. [305] Then, slowly, across years of standing before mixed classrooms of Hmong children and Somali refugees and white farm kids and Korean adoptees, she had unlearned even that hatred. [333] She had learned that the teachers had also been taught. [343] That the forgetting was a chain, and she could be the link where it stopped. [358]

Her method was simple. [362] She did not add. [366] She subtracted. [368] By subtracting noise -- the self-conscious giggles, the whispered mockery, the internalized shame -- she cleared space for what was already there. [388] Every child arrived already carrying a voice. [395] Her job was not to install one, but to remove what covered it. [408]

She picked up the rosette and turned it in the autumn light. [420] The carved petals threw tiny shadows across the velvet tablecloth. [430] Once, someone's hands had carved this with a knife, slowly, knowing it would outlast them. [445] She wondered if that carver had felt the same expectant resignation -- the knowledge that the world was changing, paired with the stubborn faith that beauty, once made, leaves a mark that vanishing cannot fully erase. [480]

The library was quiet. [484] Not empty-quiet. [486] Traveled-quiet. [487] The kind of stillness that accumulates after many footsteps have passed through and settled. [501] She had brought the rosette here because tonight, in this room, she would meet three former students -- now in their thirties, scattered across the country -- who had asked her to help them start a new choir. [537] A small one. [540] Twelve singers from six traditions. [545]

She set the rosette down and smoothed the velvet beneath her palm. [557]

They would not save the vanishing arts. [564] She knew this. [567] The songs would continue to thin, the traditions would continue to fray. [579] But they would braid what remained -- Hmong vocal slides over Ethiopian drone tones, Korean pansori breath techniques meeting Appalachian modal scales -- and the braid, even if temporary, would hold. [608]

The door opened. [611] Footsteps. [612] Voices, tentative and then warm. [617]

She stood, and the traveled stillness shifted to make room for what came next. [631]
