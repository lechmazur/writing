Mara had worked at Cloudhaven Hospice for eleven years, tucked into the mountains at 9,200 feet where the air thinned and the world below disappeared into white. [27] She was a music therapist, serene by nature, and she had learned that dying required the same patience as breathing, each moment arriving, each moment releasing. [53]

[72] Mara read the results in the hallway, folded the paper once, and walked toward the room with the unhurried certainty that her patients trusted most. [97] She was, above all else, decisively kind. [104] She did not flinch from what was true, and she did not let truth land without music to soften its fall. [125]

Edith was eighty-one, a former schoolteacher who had come to Cloudhaven with a single suitcase and one request: she wanted to say goodbye properly. [149] Not the slow, unraveling kind where family stood around unsure what to say. [162] A flawless goodbye, she had told Mara on her first day. [173] "I want it to be complete." [179]

[250] The record had cracked during the drive up the mountain, and Edith had wept, not for the object, but for what it carried. [273] Her mother's hands lowering the needle. [279] The scratch and hiss before the melody bloomed. [287] The feeling of being small and held and unafraid. [296]

[475] Hymning the mundane, she called it in her training, though here it felt more like excavation.
