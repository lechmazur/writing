The towers of bone rose around her like the ribcage of something that had swallowed the sky and died standing. [19] Each one was centuries old, built from the stacked vertebrae of creatures that had no name, and the dust of their making lay in layers so deep that her footsteps raised ghosts of dust from eras she would never live to see. [61] She was a harmonic stone skimmer, the last one perhaps, and she had come here through centuries of layered dust not by walking but by listening. [87] Stone to stone, vibration to vibration, she had followed a pulse left wandering, an ancient residual heartbeat in the deep earth that had been loose and drifting since before the towers were built, since before the canyon had a name. [127] It moved through the rock like blood through a dead vein, and she had learned years ago to follow it by pressing her ear to the ground and listening for the faintest trace of its frequency. [163] The pulse was what brought her here, to this canyon of endless echoes, where every sound lived forever, bouncing between the bone towers until it frayed into something unrecognizable, a chorus of every voice that had ever entered the canyon and never left. [206] She carried with her a stone arrowhead, smooth and ancient, its edges worn by more hands than she could count or name. [228] She was historically reverent by nature, not as an affectation but as a necessity of her craft. [245] To skim a stone properly, to read the harmonics that came singing back when it struck the earth, you had to understand what that stone had already endured. [273] Every chip and fracture was a record, and she had learned to honor those records the way a scholar honors a manuscript, with careful hands and a quiet mind. [302] The arrowhead was her anchor in this place, its resonance steady and clean, a fixed and reliable note amid the canyon's relentless noise. [325] Today she would appraise the pulse that had drawn her here across such impossible distance. [340] She would measure its age, its direction, its intent, the way a physician reads a heartbeat for signs of disease or health. [362] But she could not do it through the ground, not here. [373] The endless echoes in the canyon made stone-skimming unreliable, the vibrations bouncing and layering against bone until they collapsed into pure noise. [395] She needed a different method. [400] She sat cross-legged at the canyon's center, placed the arrowhead flat on the ground before her, and waited. [418] Above her, birds appeared, black and small, nesting in the hollows of the bone towers where marrow had long dried to powder. [440] They flew in shifting patterns that meant nothing to ordinary eyes, but she had spent long years learning to read them as others read maps or stars. [467] Through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight, she could appraise the invisible currents of the pulse moving below, because the birds felt it too, the way the air shifted and the thermals bent around something unseen and heavy. [508] Their formations would tell her what the stone could not. [518] The flock rose suddenly, turned, and tightened into a long, narrow spiral above the third tower from the east. [537] She watched them curve and scatter, reform and dip low over a shallow basin, and she read the pulse like a sentence written in wingbeats. [562] It was old, far older than the towers, older than the dust beneath her. [576] It was not searching for anything and had no destination. [586] It was simply lost, a heartbeat without a body, wandering through the stone because it had nowhere else to go and no memory of where it began. [613] She felt the weathered resolve settle into her bones like sediment settling to the bottom of still water. [631] She was not so different from this pulse, she thought. [641] She had given up walking years ago, had trained herself to hear the world through vibration and harmony so that she could learn to navigate without moving, because the world was too vast and too broken to cross on foot. [681] The body stayed still while the mind traveled stone to stone, ridge to ridge, riverbed to riverbed. [698] It was a form of reverence, she thought, to let the world come to you, to listen rather than to stride across it and claim it with footprints and noise. [728] The birds settled back into the towers. [735] The pulse drifted on beneath her, aimless and eternal. [744] She pressed the arrowhead to the ground and felt it hum, a small and perfect note against the wandering pulse, and she began the long, patient work of mapping its path through resonance alone, sitting still as the dust of centuries settled softly on her shoulders.
