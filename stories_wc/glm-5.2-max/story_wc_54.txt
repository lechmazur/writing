After the carnival's last caravan pulled away, Lina Cho walked into the abandoned spaceport passenger terminal with her clipboard and her questions. [22] She was twenty-two, a civil engineer intern assigned to assess the old Meridian Terminal for possible demolition. [39] But curiosity had always pulled her deeper than job descriptions allowed. [50]

The carnival had left marks everywhere. [56] Flashing lights and colored wires draped over cracked departure screens. [66] Ticket counters had been turned into carnival food stalls, their old surfaces stained with cotton candy residue. [83] The whole terminal had been briefly alive again, the carnival's maze of intentions layered over the structure's original purpose. [102] People had come here not to fly anywhere, just to wander, to play, to remember. [117]

Lina walked through the main concourse, tracing the building's bones. [127] She had arrived to identify load-bearing walls, foundational issues, and structural risks. [139] But the terminal kept surprising her. [145] Old departure gates had been rearranged into a carnival funhouse mirror maze. [157] Corridors that once directed thousands of passengers to their gates had been bent into a labyrinth of stalls and attractions. [177] Looking at the chaos left behind, Lina began seeing patterns in the routing—the carnival hadn't just occupied the building, it had redirected its flows. [202] Foot traffic patterns showed on the floor in scuff marks and spilled glitter, revealing where crowds had moved, gathered, dispersed. [222]

She found the sextant piece in what had been the first-class lounge, now a carnival's prize redemption booth. [240] It was just a fragment—an arc of polished brass with graduated markings and a small sighting mirror, part of an old explorer's navigation instrument. [265] Lina held it and felt the weight of journeys measured in starlight, not satellite signals. [280] Someone had left it as a carnival prize, unclaimed. [289]

She turned the fragment in her hand, angling the mirror toward the broken skylights. [303] Light bounced across the concourse, tracing lines that almost matched the old gate numbers still visible beneath the paint. [322] Her functionally creative mind started working. [328] If she could map these light paths to the original airflow blueprints she'd found in the city archives, she might understand the terminal's original ventilation system, how air was meant to move through this space. [363]

The building, she realized, had been designed to breathe. [372] Massive ductwork ran through its walls, and intake vents still lined the eastern face where prevailing winds entered. [390] Decades ago, the ventilation system had failed, and the terminal had suffocated, slowly abandoned as air quality dropped. [408] Lina pulled out her notebook and began sketching. [416] Not demolition plans. [419] Something else. [421] She was tired of the calculus of removal and demolition, the engineering of erasure. [435] She wanted to puzzle out new equations for hope, something her coursework never covered. [449] The terminal didn't need to be torn down. [457] It needed to be reborn. [462]

Working methodically, she used the sextant piece to measure angles of light throughout the day. [477] She mapped how the sun moved across the terminal, and where redirected airflow could be restored using the building's existing ductwork. [498] The carnival's improvised rerouting gave her ideas. [505] They had redirected foot traffic, and she could redirect air. [515] Old vents that had been sealed could be reopened. [524] New channels could be cut through non-load-bearing walls. [532] The building's own structure could be made functional again through redirected flows that followed the carnival's logic of creative repurposing. [552]

By evening, she had filled three pages with calculations. [561] She had found a way to resurrect the terminal's ventilation by reconnecting seven major ducts, creating a passive airflow system that needed no power. [585] Air would enter through the eastern vents, travel through the restored ductwork, and exit through the skylights, creating a continuous slow breath through the building. [610]

Lina stood in the main concourse as dusk fell. [619] The carnival's ghosts lingered in the colored string lights someone had left strung across the ceiling. [635] Through her redirected design, the first breeze in fifty years would soon move through this space again. [652] The sextant piece sat on the counter beside her, a fragment of old exploration that had helped her navigate toward the future. [674] The terminal had been a maze of intentions—passengers seeking departure, carnival seekers chasing delight, and now an engineer pursuing renewal. [695] Each had redirected its flows in their own way. [704] Lina packed her tools, steadied by the quiet certainty that the building would breathe again. [719] She had found her equations, not in textbooks, but in the architecture of what was possible when you looked closely enough at what remained. [743]
