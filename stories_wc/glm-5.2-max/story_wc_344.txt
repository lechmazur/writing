The frozen library above the clouds had waited three hundred years for someone with the right hands, and Sera Tann was a lunar-eclipse locksmith, born under a darkened moon with fingers that understood locks the way musicians understood strings. [39] She had come to learn the rhythm of repair. [48] Not the repair of hinges or latches--she had mastered those in her apprentice years beside her father, working in his driftwood shop on the coast, mending what the sea had ruined. [80] What she sought was older. [85] Her father had died without teaching her the final lesson: how to mend what time itself had broken. [103] The library's doors were frozen shut, but Sera knelt before them under the soft hush of a distant morning, her tools spread across the ice like silver fish. [131] She pressed her ear to the lock and listened. [140] The tumblers were glass, not iron--hollow spheres that chimed when they caught the light. [155] To uncap her smallest pick, a needle-thin instrument stored in a vial of warm oil, she twisted the brass seal and slid the pick into the keyhole. [182] The door breathed open. [186] Inside, the air was still and cold, and Sera's breath made clouds. [198] The shelves stretched upward into darkness, and between them, where there should have been aisles, there were mirrors. [216] Floor to ceiling, each one slightly warped, each reflecting not Sera but versions of her--older, younger, broken, whole. [235] A hall of mirrors, threaded through a library like veins through a body. [248] She ignored them. [251] She had not come for reflections. [257] The prophecy scraps were where her father's journal said they would be: tucked in a corner behind the astronomy texts, folded into books no one had opened in generations. [286] Each scrap was a sentence, sometimes two, written in a hand that trembled. [299] Sera collected them one by one, spreading them across a reading table that groaned under the weight of ice. [318] The words were fragments of instruction disguised as prophecy: "When the great wheel stutters, the small teeth must be reset." [338] "The broken heartbeat can be taught again, if the pattern is held." [350] "Repair is not restoration--it is the willingness to sit with what is damaged until it tells you what it needs." [371] By dawn, she had fourteen scraps arranged in a sequence that made her hands ache with recognition. [388] This was what her father had tried to teach her. [398] Not technique, but patience--the rhythm of repair. [406] She found the clockmaker's gear assortment in a glass case at the room's center. [420] Dozens of gears, each no larger than a thumbnail, arranged in concentric circles. [433] Some were brass, some were copper, some were made of materials she couldn't name. [447] Each was etched with symbols that matched the prophecy scraps. [457] When she lifted the case's lid, the gears caught the first light through the frozen windows, and the reflection in the mirrors behind her was hauntingly bright--a white flare that made her shadow jump across the floor. [495] The gears began to turn on their own, clicking into the rhythm the scraps had described. [511] Each gear matched a fragment of text, and as they meshed, she understood: the mirrors were not reflecting versions of her. [532] They were showing her the moments when things had broken--her father's hands shaking, a clock stopping, a lock rusted shut. [553] Each mirror held a fracture. [558] Sera sat with them. [562] She did not look away. [567] The gears turned, and she listened to their rhythm, and slowly she began to understand that repair was not the absence of damage. [590] It was the act of staying present with the broken thing until its pattern revealed itself. [606] Under the soft hush of a distant morning, the lunar-eclipse locksmith held a single gear between her fingers and began to work. [628] The mirrors dimmed. [631] The library settled. [634] Somewhere far below, past the clouds, the world kept its broken time, and Sera Tann learned, at last, to keep pace with it. [657]
