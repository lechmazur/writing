The world had grown slippery. [6] After friction disappeared, every surface became treacherous, every step an act of faith. [19] Biret understood slippery things. [24] She had always been an eel herder, guiding the silver bodies through their coastal pools, her hands finding purchase where others found only slick desperation. [51] But now the eels were impossible, sliding through crumbling nets, and the people were worse, sliding through her fingers in other ways that mattered far more. [79]

She had made a promise, once. [87] To her mother, who had taught her the old tidal songs and the meaning behind the aurora's green sheets that unfurled above the bluff each night. [114] "Find the pattern," her mother had said, pressing something into Biret's palm — a vial of collapsed star dust, gathered from the crater where something ancient had fallen long ago. [145] "Find it, and you'll understand why the world holds together the way it does." [162] Her mother had died the next morning, slipping on a surface that no longer remembered how to grip. [183]

That was two years ago. [188] Two years of cursed flashes that Biret had borne since birth — sudden searing visions of futures that might or might not come, glimpses of someone falling, someone leaving, someone dying. [223] The precognition was not a gift. [230] Every path foreseen became a path feared, and so she had retreated to this bluff where the only futures she glimpsed were those of eels and tides. [261]

Now Biret stood on the tidal bluff, the aurora spreading its borrowed light across the sky. [279] Borrowed, because the real dawn had grown weak in a world where nothing held friction long enough for warmth to build. [307] The light was pale and temporary, like a hope remembered from someone else's story. [324]

She knelt on the wind-scoured stone and opened the vial. [337] The collapsed star dust was almost gone — she had used it sparingly, mixing it with seawater and pigment from the bluff's iron-rich clay to paint symbols on small rocks. [371] Her mother had called them the language beneath language. [382]

She placed the first rock at the bluff's edge. [394] Then the second, three lengths apart. [402] The third formed an angle with the invisible line. [413] Fourth and fifth, each one continuing a sequence that spiraled inward, the painted symbols catching the aurora's shimmer. [433] She was trying to harness something she could not yet name. [449] The waves below had lost their friction too, sliding over the shore without sound, without the familiar crash. [471] The aurora above was the only steadfast rhythm left in the world. [485]

As she set the sixth rock in place, completing the first arc, the aurora responded — a single beam of green light, more focused than she had ever seen, descended toward the bluff. [523]

She felt the familiar stab. [530] A flash: herself placing the seventh rock, the light splitting into threads, mathematics made visible in the air above the sequence. [554] She saw one possible future in which the world regained its grip. [569] But that was the nature of her affliction. [578] She saw futures. [581] She could never be certain which one would arrive. [592]

She set the seventh rock down. [600] The light touched it and refracted, just as her vision had shown. [614] She moved to where the eighth rock needed to go, calculations running through her mind in the way the sequences demanded — prime intervals and sacred geometry, the patterns her mother had whispered were the architecture beneath all things. [658] She wanted to understand. [662] Not to control — she had learned that precognition could not grant control — but to understand the sacred patterns that held the world together even when the world itself forgot how. [697]

The elusive eel herder worked through the night, placing painted stones in their mathematical spiral, each one a voice in a conversation with whatever had fallen to earth long ago and left its dust behind. [740] The aurora wove between the stones now, forming shapes she had never seen in the sky, connecting the sequence into one continuous, breathing pattern. [774]

She remembered her promise. [778] She understood, finally, what she was seeing — not an answer but a language, connection made visible when something bridged the space between worlds. [804] The collapsed star dust was that bridge, and her mother had known it all along. [824]

Her hands were steady, even on this slippery stone. [836] She had found something worth gripping for. [844]
