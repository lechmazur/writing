The junkyard prophet woke already grieving. [6] She knew, with the particular certainty of her affliction, that by evening she would have forgotten the dream that mattered most. [27] She could feel the forgetting approaching like weather off the water, the shape of what would be erased, but never the content. [49] Precognitively forgetful, the old hermit down the coast had called it, as though naming the condition might pin it down like a specimen under glass. [74]

She rose and stepped out among the fog sculptures. [83] The garden had been here before her, perhaps, or perhaps it had grown from the accumulated debris of her collecting: car fenders twisted into shapes that held mist, concrete forms soft with moss, mirror shards catching light that shouldn't have existed in such density of fog. [129] The sculptures emerged only when you stopped trying to see them directly, appearing in peripheral vision like something almost remembered. [149]

She was known in the nearby town as the junkyard prophet, though she never called herself that. [166] She collected what others abandoned and, from the patterns of discard, tried to read what was coming. [183] Sometimes she was right. [187] More often she couldn't remember what she'd predicted until after it had happened, and then only as a faint recognition, like hearing a song whose words you'd forgotten. [215]

In the shed beside the garden, a shelf of old videotapes stood unwatched. [228] She had gathered them from the dump over years, recordings of other people's ordinary days, birthday parties and Christmas mornings and someone napping on a sunlit couch. [255] She never played them. [259] Their hush was the point. [264] They held recorded time that someone had bothered to preserve and then released, and now they sat with their labels fading, growing older in the dark. [290]

Today she carried a piece of plain chalk and armfuls of driftwood, pale and salt-bleached, gathered from the shore below. [310]

For three weeks she had been arranging the driftwood as directional markers throughout the garden, each piece pointed toward the center, toward the oldest sculpture, a mass of twisted rebar and poured concrete that might once have been a fountain. [350] The arrangement was not random. [355] Each morning she woke with a fragment of direction lodged in her mind, a sense of where the next piece should fall, and she followed it without understanding why. [384]

She was trying to locate something she had heard once and lost. [396]

Three years ago, standing among the fog sculptures at dawn, she had heard it. [410] Not a sound exactly, but the awareness of a sound, a deepening in the air, like the difference between a new string and one that has been tuned a thousand times. [441] The sound of growing older. [446] She had tried to capture it with a tape recorder, but the recording came back empty, just the hiss of blank magnetic tape, and she had wept at the loss of something she couldn't name. [481]

She marked a stone with the chalk, a white X on wet gray, and placed the last piece of driftwood so that it pointed toward the center. [508] Then she sat in the arrangement's heart and waited. [517]

The fog moved through the sculptures in slow pulses. [526]

She thought about the unwatched tapes. [532] All that recorded time, aging in its cases. [540] She thought about her own forgetting, how it came in waves, how she clung to the echoes of things she could no longer name, a voice perhaps, or the angle of light through a particular window. [576] The chalk softened in her damp hand. [583]

She listened. [585]

And there, between the breath of fog and the creak of settling driftwood, she heard it again. [602] Not the sound itself but its wake, the way the air changed in its passing. [617] The sound of growing older, of becoming less and more at once. [629] She closed her eyes and tried to hold it, tried to let it fill the space where the forgotten dream had been. [651]

But already she could feel it going. [658] Already the next forgetting approached, patient as tide, pulling what she loved back out to sea. [674]

She opened her eyes and marked another stone, though she could no longer remember why. [689] The chalk left its plain white trace against the wet. [699] Somewhere in the fog, the sculptures stood like witnesses to what could not be kept, and the unwatched tapes held their silence, and the tide came in. [726]
