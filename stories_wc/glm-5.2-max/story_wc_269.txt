Maren worked at the Koliba Sea Level Monitoring Station, where the ocean's breath was measured in millimeters. [17]
The station sat on a platform above what used to be a coastline, anchored to the seabed by pilings that groaned at high tide. [41]
After the fossil fuel ban, the seas had continued rising for decades, the result of thermal expansion, the ocean's long memory of being warmed. [65]
Now the water had finally steadied, and Maren's days had settled into a rhythm as regular as the tide itself. [85]
She had been a coral bleaching witness. [92]
That was the formal title they gave volunteers who documented the die-offs in the 2040s. [107]
She had watched the Kesh Reef turn ghost-white in three days, its polyps surrendering their symbiotic algae in a quiet exodus. [128]
That image never left her, living architecture becoming bone. [137]
Maren had come to the station to understand something that had nagged at her since the reef: why some paths disappear. [158]
Currents shifted, migration routes vanished, whole ecosystems rerouted themselves without explanation. [169]
The reef had been thriving one month and gone the next. [180]
She wanted to know where the signals went, what made a living system simply stop following the path it had always followed. [202]
Her method was peculiar but effective. [208]
She had developed a practice of tapping rhythms on surfaces: the instrument housings, the desk, the railing. [225]
She matched patterns to data sequences, each tap corresponding to a reading, a timestamp, a coordinate. [241]
Her colleagues thought it was a nervous habit, but it was how she processed information. [256]
The rhythms let her feel anomalies in her hands before her eyes could catch them in the data. [274]
One evening, while tapping through a month's tidal records against the steel table edge, she felt it: a gap. [293]
A sequence that should have continued simply stopped. [301]
She pulled the raw logs and found that data from Sector 7, a stretch of open water to the northeast, went blank every forty-seven days. [326]
Not corrupted, not missing. [330]
Erased, as though the ocean there periodically ceased to exist. [340]
She began to assemble what she could find: old navigation charts, satellite imagery, shipping logs. [355]
In a drawer of donated materials from a decommissioned naval office, she found an atlas with a page torn out. [375]
The binding still held the ragged edge. [382]
Sector 7 had been on that page; she could tell from the coordinates printed on the adjoining sheet. [400]
Someone had removed it deliberately, long before the ban, before the station even existed. [414]
The torn page haunted her. [419]
She tapped its edge in a slow rhythm, four beats, pause, three, and stared at the empty space where the map should have been. [443]
Weeks passed. [445]
She cross-referenced the forty-seven-day cycle with old naval records and found something that made her hands go still. [463]
The gap had been recorded before, decades ago, by a survey vessel that noted "anomalous stillness" in the sector before its instruments reset. [486]
The report had been filed and forgotten, buried under the urgency of a warming planet. [501]
The revelation came slowly, then all at once, the way a reef dies. [514]
Sector 7 was not a dead zone. [521]
It was something periodically eternal, a place where the ocean, at fixed intervals, became unstuck from time. [538]
The data did not disappear because it was erased. [547]
It disappeared because it was never recorded. [554]
The instruments, for those hours, existed in a space where measurement had no meaning. [568]
Maren understood then why paths disappeared there. [575]
Fishermen's routes, currents, migration lines: they did not shift. [584]
They simply ceased, briefly, and when they returned, they came back altered, as though reassembled from memory rather than physics. [604]
The reef she had witnessed had not died from heat alone. [615]
It had been in the sector's pull, caught in a cycle of erasure and return that wore it down like a stone in a tide. [640]
She sat at the station's edge, feet dangling above water that looked ordinary and was not. [656]
The tone of everything around her, the creak of pilings, the hum of instruments, the lap of waves, felt both adrift and anchored. [679]
Drifting, moored. [681]
Like her. [683]
Like the ocean itself, which kept its secrets in cycles no one had thought to measure, because everyone had been too busy measuring what they expected to find. [711]
She tapped the railing one last time, five beats, pause, one, and began her report. [726]
