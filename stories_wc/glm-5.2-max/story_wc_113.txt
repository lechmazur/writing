Sera waded into the flooded opera house at midnight, lantern held high. [12] The water reached the third row of seats. [20] She didn't care about the seats. [26] She cared about the pit. [31] She lowered herself onto the conductor's platform, boots finding the rim of the sunken orchestra pit where floodwater had swallowed the first two rows of music stands. [58] Their brass surfaces caught the lantern light in trembling gold filaments. [69] She'd hung three lanterns along the pit's edge, their flames bending low over the water like readers hunched over a page. [90] This was where she belonged, in the in-between. [98] Not the stage, not the audience, but the trench between them. [109] The pit was a throat, an architectural whisper, and Sera had spent fourteen years inside it with her cello, speaking for characters who couldn't be heard without her. [137] She hadn't played in eleven months. [143] Not since the levee broke and the Callas Opera House became a shallow lake. [157] But lately, inspired by something she couldn't name, she'd started returning at night, wading through black water with her lanterns, searching for the pieces of her old life the flood hadn't swallowed. [189] Tonight she found the program. [194] It floated near the timpani, a square of yellowed paper riding the current like a patient boat. [211] She recognized it, an antique opera program from the house's inaugural 1891 season, the one the archivist had framed behind glass in the lobby. [235] The glass had shattered. [239] The program had escaped. [243] Sera lifted it from the water. [249] The damage was extraordinary. [253] Where floodwater had soaked the paper and receded, it had left shapes. [265] Not stains, images. [268] A woman's face, rendered in the sepia tides of old ink meeting river water. [282] Below it, markings that looked like notation but weren't. [291] Too irregular, too deliberate, arranged in patterns that reminded her of notes clustering at the bottom of a score no one had finished writing. [315] She carried the program to the platform and held it beneath a lantern. [328] The flickering light made the water-formed images shift, and she understood that she was being spoken to. [345] Marguerite Voss had been a pit cellist in 1891, the first woman to hold a chair in the orchestra. [364] She had died during rehearsal when a stage lamp fell into the pit and struck her. [380] The official record said instant death. [386] The musicians' quiet tradition said otherwise, that she had lived long enough to scratch something into the floorboards above her, something no one had ever found. [412] Sera looked at the program. [417] The water-drawn face was Marguerite's. [422] The notation wasn't music. [426] It was a map. [430] Her hands shook, not from cold but recognition. [438] She had spent her life in the space where Marguerite had died, playing the same repertoire, breathing the same rosin dust. [459] And now, amid the hush of unstruck chords, in the silence between what was played and what was meant, Marguerite was answering back. [482] The map led to the stage. [488] Sera climbed the flooded steps to the boards, water barely covering her ankles here. [502] She moved to center stage, following the program's pattern, three steps left of center, then forward until her toe found a groove in the floorboards. [527] She knelt. [529] The groove was a carved line filled with silt. [538] She cleared it with her fingers and found not one line but several, scratched deep into wood in 1891 by a dying woman with whatever sharp object had been in reach. [569] The scratches were not words. [574] They were the same notation from the program, the same irregular patterns, each completing the other. [590] The paper's water-formed images and the stage's knife-cut symbols fit together like halves of a torn letter. [607] She understood what Marguerite had written. [613] Not a message. [616] Not a name. [619] A chord. [621] A chord that had never been played, written by a woman who died before she could sound it, encoded into a program and a stage floor, waiting for water to reveal what violence had hidden. [656] Sera pulled her bow from her coat. [663] The cello was home, but these floorboards were spruce, the same wood as a cello's front panel. [680] She pressed the program flat against the wood, set the bow against the groove, and drew it across. [698] The note that rose was unlike anything she had heard. [708] Deep, fractured, alive. [711] It disrupted the silence completely, filling the flooded house with a sound that was not grief but answer, a wordless conversation between two cellists separated by a century, finally finding each other in the in-between.
