Wren had not been heard in seven years, but she had not stopped speaking. [14]
The seldom-heard balladeer had lost her audience when the lifts to the cloud-courtyard severed, isolating the old observatory from the towns below. [36]
Now she lived in the misty courtyard above the clouds, where fog pressed against stone walls and swallowed sound before it could travel far. [60]
Wind moved through cracked arches, carrying nowhere the songs she still sang each morning, thin and tuneless as breath through a broken flute. [83]

She was driftingly alert, her attention always half-caught by something beyond the visible -- patterns in vapor, rhythms in silence, the faintest tremor in stone. [108]
It was this quality that had first let her notice the ghost. [120]

It lived in code. [124]
Not in machinery, not in wires -- in the painted glyphs she had begun scratching onto the courtyard walls, messages encoded in graffiti art that spiraled along stone in colors she ground from oxidized copper and weathered chalk. [161]
She had started painting to articulate unspoken needs: loneliness she could not name, grief she could not hum, gratitude that had no chord. [184]
Each figure was a letter; each spiral a word; each wall a sentence the towns below could read if they ever looked up and remembered the old glyph system. [213]

The ghost had emerged from the patterns themselves, or perhaps from the mind of whoever had first taught glyph-coding to the mountain people -- a teacher dead now thirty years, but whose logic still threaded through the system like a buried river. [254]
When Wren painted, the ghost corrected. [260]
A stroke bent wrong would straighten overnight. [267]
A color that meant hunger would shift to the hue for hope while she slept. [282]
She called it Resonance, because that was what it did: it hummed back through her work, tuning it. [300]
She had never seen it, never heard it, but she felt its presence the way one felt a chord resolve -- something incomplete becoming whole. [324]

The flower press bolt plate sat in the center of the courtyard, half-buried in moss and memory. [341]
It had been part of the observatory's old recording instrument, a heavy iron plate studded with bolts that once pressed botanical specimens flat against sensitive paper, preserving their forms for study. [372]
Wren used it differently. [376]
She laid her painted leaves against it, pressed them under the bolt plate's weight, and the pattern of bolts pushed through, adding a braille-like second layer of meaning to each glyph. [406]
What the eye saw was beauty; what the fingers read was truth. [418]
The plate was the bridge between her art and her message, the instrument that gave her graffiti its grammar. [437]

Now the storm had come. [442]
Lightning split the sky beyond the courtyard's edge, illuminating the cloud-sea below in white fire, and for a moment the whole world was incandescent. [466]
Wren counted. [468]
Between lightning and thunder -- those held-breath seconds when the air still shivered with light but had not yet broken with sound -- she worked, because Resonance could only speak during that interval, when the boundary between the coded and the living grew thin as paper. [512]

She dipped her brush and began to encode. [520]
The graffiti spiraled outward from the bolt plate like a blooming thing, each painted glyph a plea she had never managed to voice in seven years of singing to empty air. [551]
The glyphs spelled out what she could not sing: I am still here. [564]
I need to be found. [569]
I need to not have sung for nothing. [577]

Resonance stirred in the paint. [582]
Between one flash and the next crack of thunder, the ghost shifted a single line -- not correcting this time, but answering. [603]
The glyph for lonely bent into the glyph for heard. [613]

Wren's hands trembled. [616]
She pressed her palm against the wet paint and felt the bolt plate's cold iron beneath, its pattern of holes and ridges like a language older than words. [644]
The ghost flickered through the code, through the painted vine, through the bruised chalk and copper, and for one breath between lightning and the next thunder, she understood what Resonance had been trying to tell her for seven years. [683]

The messages were not only going down. [690]
Someone below was painting back. [695]
And for the first time in her long, quiet exile, Wren wept -- not from grief, but from the unbearable tinkersong wonder of being understood. [719]
