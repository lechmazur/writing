Vera had quartz eyes. [4] Not metaphorically. [6] Her irises were clouded quartz, pale and fractured, the result of an old accident in the glassworks where she had been apprenticed at fourteen. [30] They saw differently than other eyes. [36] They saw the fault lines in things: cracked plates, torn fabric, broken promises. [49]

She was a mender now. [54] The village called her for what others threw away. [63] She was endearingly feisty about it too, arguing with anyone who called something beyond repair, hands on hips, quartz eyes flashing like they had caught inner light. [90]

Her daughter Senna had been the same way. [98] Headstrong. [99] Certain. [100] Senna had sworn she would learn the mending trade, had made a formal vow at sixteen before the village elders, a contract written and witnessed. [125] But the vow was young and the world was wide. [135] By nineteen, Senna had left for the coast cities, and the contract lay breached. [149]

That had been three years ago. [155]

Now Vera climbed the rot-soft ladder into the cathedral's upper reaches, where the old brothers had once kept doves. [174] They had abandoned the place when the diocese restructured, a breach of their own contract with the town, leaving the cathedral to the orchard that some long-ago gardener had planted in the rafters. [207] Apple and pear trees grew from the accumulated soil of decades, their roots threaded through stone, branches reaching through broken stained glass toward the sky. [232] It was lush up here, improbably so, the canopy filtering light into green-gold dust that drifted through the nave below. [252]

Vera had come because the orchard needed tending and nobody else would climb. [265] She had come because work was better than sitting in the workshop where Senna's stool sat empty. [282]

She found her old mending bag wedged behind a buttress where she had left it months ago, forgotten, half-buried in leaf litter. [304] Inside were her usual tools: twisted wire, gut thread, a small anvil. [316] But at the bottom, tangled in a coil of waxed cord, was Senna's hair ribbon. [331] Blue cotton, faded, the one Senna had worn braided through her hair every day as a child, before she had decided braids were provincial and let her hair hang loose. [361]

Vera held it. [364] The ribbon was thin and soft with washing. [372] She had mended it twice when Senna was small, after the cat got it, after it caught on a fence nail. [393] Small repairs. [395] Small promises kept. [398]

She had been angry about the broken vow. [406] She was still angry, if she was honest. [414] A contract was a contract. [419]

But sitting in the cathedral orchard, light falling through apple leaves, she looked at the ribbon and thought about the trees. [440] The orchard did not keep its leaves year-round. [448] It let them go, and the falling was part of the growing. [460] The leaves came back different each spring, broader, the tree having learned something from another season of standing in the weather. [481]

She thought about Senna's vow. [486] Maybe she had made it too young, the way you would plant a sapling in a pot too small. [505] Maybe the vow was not broken but shed. [513] Deciduous. [514] The way the orchard shed its leaves not to die but to grow. [527]

Vera tucked the ribbon into her pocket and began pruning a dead branch from the nearest pear tree. [545] She was a mender. [549] She would persist. [552] But maybe mending was not always about holding things together. [562] Maybe sometimes it was making space for things to grow back different. [574]

She worked until the light turned amber through the broken glass. [585] Then she climbed down, quartz eyes catching the last of the sun, and walked home to write her daughter a letter that did not mention the vow. [612] Just asked when she might visit, and whether she still liked pears. [624]

She would find out. [628] Either way, the orchard would keep its seasons. [636]
