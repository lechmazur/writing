The smog pressed against the rooftop greenhouse of the smog-choked city like a second skin. [16] Mira had not slept in three nights, not since the envelope appeared beneath her door bearing no name and no address, only a folded sheet of paper covered in symbols she did not recognize and a faded thumbprint in one corner. [57] She was a painter by compulsion and an insomniac by the city's doing. [70] The smog had sealed the sky for months, and the sky held too many unremembered things. [86] But tonight the waxing moon's glow pressed through the haze, and the greenhouse filled with a saffron quiet, warm and dim, like tea steeping in a glass. [113] Mira sat cross-legged on the stone floor among her trays of tomato seedlings and tried to decode the message. [132] The symbols resembled nothing she had studied. [139] Not Sanskrit, not cuneiform, not the tidy strokes of Mandarin. [149] They belonged to one of the lost languages, the thousands that had vanished from human mouths and survived only in fragments on cave walls and broken pottery. [176] She recognized the feeling of the script, though. [184] Something in the shapes reminded her of an afternoon years ago when a stranger in a market had pressed a warm wrapped bun into her hands during a rainstorm, said nothing, and walked away. [218] She had been broke and hungry and twenty-two. [226] She still remembered the weight of that bun, the warmth of the paper against her palms. [242] This was what drove her now, each night, to paint greetings in dead scripts on small cards and leave them in library books and on park benches and tucked into the cracks of bus shelters, motivated by a single stubborn belief: to prove that strangers remember kindness, even when the words are unreadable, even when the gesture lives only in the body's memory. [305] Mira called herself methodically hopeful. [310] She did not believe in miracles but she believed in showing up, in leaving something behind, in the slow accumulation of invisible grace. [333] She unrolled a length of rice paper across the greenhouse stones. [344] Beside her sat the tool she had used for six months, a calligrapher's moon-tipped brush, given to her by an old man at the night market who had said only, "This one remembers light." [378] The bristles were pale, almost silver, and when dipped in ink they released a faint luminescence, as if the moon lived inside the tool and merely waited to be coaxed out. [409] She dipped the tip into her inkstone and touched it to the paper. [422] The waxing moon hung fat and amber through the glass ceiling, its glow fractured by the smog into something washier, the color of old tea stains. [448] And then the daydreams came, the way they always came, sparked by moonlight catching the wet ink. [465] She felt her attention loosen, her eyelids grow warm, and she slipped into waking visions as real as the tomato sprouts pushing up beside her. [490] She saw a woman finding one of her cards on a subway platform, turning it over, confused by the script, then pressing it to her chest and tucking it into her coat. [522] She saw a boy discover one in a library book and trace the characters with his finger, not reading but feeling, his mouth making shapes he could not name. [551] She saw an old man find one beneath his windshield wiper and stand in the rain reading nothing and everything at once. [573] The visions roused something in her chest, a warmth that started beneath the sternum and spread outward like a slow tide. [594] She returned to herself slowly, the greenhouse reassembling around her, glass panels fogged with condensation, the green smell of seedlings, the distant hum of the city below. [621] The brush was still in her hand. [628] She looked down at what she had painted without knowing she had painted it. [642] A series of symbols flowed across the rice paper in luminous strokes, and though she could not read them, she felt their meaning the way one feels warmth through a closed hand. [673] They said, she thought: I was here, and I hoped you would find this. [688] The brush had painted in a language that no living person could read, but that anyone would understand in the place where understanding begins before words. [714] She set the paper aside to dry. [721] Outside, the smog shifted and the moon grew a fraction brighter. [732] Mira, methodically hopeful, began another card. [738] She would leave this one on the stairwell railing of the building across the street, where a stranger would find it on their way to work tomorrow, and would turn it over in their hands, and would not know what it said, and would carry it with them anyway. [787] That was enough. [790] It was always enough. [794]
