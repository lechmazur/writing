After the last resource war, the scrapyard settlement of Rustmarch still held its ground, spanning a cracked wasteland where the wind came in long, patient waves. 
The people called me the bloodhound because I had spent twelve years finding bodies in the wreckage, and I had earned my retirement by refusing to find the last one. 
I lived in a leaning water tower at the settlement's edge, where metal groaned and the wild hush seemed to swallow every word before it could become gossip. 
My only company was a phonograph cylinder no one could play and an autumn leaf preserved in wax, both pulled from the same crate of prewar debris. 
I had kept the leaf because it belonged to my daughter, Maren, before she walked east with a salvage crew and vanished into the distance. 
The settlement elders came to my door with the cylinder because they knew my reputation was not merely a nose for death, but a mind that was intuitively descriptive. 
They said the grooves in its wax surface held secret texts, cut in a code too small for the scavengers' magnifying lenses. 
I agreed to help not for coin, but because I had spent every quiet hour trying to translate distance into closeness, and Maren's absence was the longest distance I knew. 
I worked by lamplight, turning the cylinder beneath a tin reflector, and I made my first progress by reading secret texts in the grooves of a phonograph cylinder as others might read a plowed field. 
The first lines were coordinates, but they turned to riddle when the groove depths changed: "What does a season leave behind when the tree is gone?" 
I had no answer until I lifted the waxed leaf to the light and saw its veins mirror the ridged lines around the cylinder. 
Through the amber wax and the leaf's dark skeleton, the grooves cast colored shadows, and I saw what could only be called tinted revelations. 
The code was tactile, not visual, and the leaf's preserved veins formed a key that told my fingers how to read each groove. 
I traced the riddle's answer into the cylinder: the leaf was not a souvenir, but a map of an underground channel that had survived the war. 
The secret texts said the channel ran beneath the cracks for forty miles, opening near a settlement called Harlow's Breath, where Maren's salvage crew had been seen. 
I did not tell the elders everything, because I had learned that a good bloodhound keeps some trails for himself. 
Instead, I offered them a trade: they would repair my cart and spare me two lanterns, and I would return with a route that made their scrapyard a waystation rather than a grave. 
I set out at dusk, with the cylinder wrapped in canvas, the waxed leaf in my breast pocket, and the wild hush settling over the rust like a held breath. 
The cracked wasteland had no birds, no engines, and no straight horizon, only tilted sheet metal and drifts of powdered paint that whispered against my wheels. 
At the first fissure I stopped, not from fear, but because the cylinder had grown warm in my pack and I needed to understand why. 
I held it against my ear and heard the faint rasp of hidden ridges, each one a word I had not yet earned. 
The riddle had been a promise: distance could be folded, if one had the patience to read the smallest marks. 
By the third night I reached the hidden mouth of the channel, a pipe vent breathing cool, mineral-scented air from beneath the slag. 
I crawled inside with the lantern low, and the tunnel walls were scratched with old survey marks that made my skin tighten with recognition. 
The tunnel opened into a hall of stacked shipping containers, lit by a single blue bulb, where a woman was sorting copper wire with the same steady hands I remembered. 
"Maren," I said, and my voice cracked in the wild hush as if I were again a young man introducing myself to silence. 
She looked up, and I opened my hand to show her the autumn leaf preserved in wax, its colors still defiant after all the years of ash. 
She touched it, then the cylinder, and I realized the tinted revelations had never been meant to find the dead; they were a way to translate distance into closeness. 
The riddle, the leaf, and the cylinder had led me not to a grave, but to a door, and I had finally retired.
