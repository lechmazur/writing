Sera slipped into the catacomb orchard under the city, moving within the glow of half-lit corridors. 
She was a reckless idealist who believed that no soul was too broken to mend. 
The orchard grew in tiers of packed earth and femur, where tree roots snake between bones. 
Lanterns guttered in alcoves, and every corridor smelled of rain that never fell. 
Sera had come to patch holes in the soul, though the keepers called her a thief. 
In her satchel she carried bonewire, silver dust, and a burned tarot card. 
The card had belonged to her sister before the brokers turned bartered destinies into coin. 
They had scorched its edges to mark a life already sold. 
Sera still remembered the heat of that fire and the way it hollowed her out. 
She did not want revenge; she wanted the orchard to stop eating what could not be returned. 
Her hands were fiercely gentle as she selected a rib and cleared the moss from its curve. 
Her method was inlaying meaning everywhere, pressing names into bonewire and setting the wire in a seam. 
Each name was a small anchor, a refusal to let the dead become fertilizer for a debt. 
The orchard stirred, but the words held, and the nearest root drew back from her work. 
She meant to trammel the great root that fed the weighing table at the orchard's heart. 
It was that root that turned one person's tomorrow into another person's cure. 
Sera had seen the ledger: trades of laughter, trades of memory, trades of a daughter's whole future. 
The city above pretended not to know, but the keepers prospered on holes in the soul. 
At the weighing table, the Custodian waited with a mother and a trembling child. 
He offered the girl's future as if he were offering water. 
Sera stepped out of the half-lit corridor and felt the reckless old hunger to bargain. 
Instead of pleading, she laid the burned tarot card on the scale's cold pan. 
The empty space where a fate had been glowed brighter than any coin. 
She said that a bartered destiny is not a patch but a wound traded into a family. 
The Custodian's guards moved, but the bonewire sang as it tightened around the great root. 
Sera had inlaid the weighing table with the child's true name and the notes of her mother's lullaby. 
Meaning rose underfoot, snaring the guards without breaking a single bone. 
She was fiercely gentle even now, steering the orchard's own weight against its keeper. 
The Custodian offered to restore the tarot card if she would leave the child's fate in place. 
Sera's hands shook, because she wanted her sister back more than she wanted victory. 
She chose the harder mercy and pressed the burned card into a hollow of the great root. 
Then she trammeled the root's drinking vein with the last coil of inlaid wire. 
The tree shuddered, and the scale tipped in a way no ledger had ever allowed. 
The child's name returned to her own lips, and the mother wept with a whole soul. 
The corridors dimmed further, filling with a dusky resolve that felt like evening and iron. 
Sera sat among the bones while the orchard settled into a quieter dark. 
She had not saved everyone, and the brokers still lived in the city above. 
Yet she had patched one hole in the soul with truth, not theft. 
The roots loosened, and the burned card remained visible in the bark like a scar that meant something. 
The Custodian fled through a closing door, and the half-lit corridors swallowed his voice. 
Sera stayed because she knew that meanings need a keeper or they fade into habit. 
She would tend the orchard until the trees grew on memory instead of debt. 
When desperate people came, she would offer them labor, patience, and names worth keeping. 
She would remain a reckless idealist, but her recklessness would now have roots. 
Above, the city slept, unaware that its cheapest cures had been trammeled. 
Below, Sera walked the catacomb orchard and read the bones like a slow hymn. 
The tree roots no longer snaked between bones to bind them but to remember them. 
Within the glow of half-lit corridors, she waited for the next wounded soul. 
She would offer no bartered destiny, only her fierce and gentle hands. 
The orchard breathed, and no root dared forget a name again.
