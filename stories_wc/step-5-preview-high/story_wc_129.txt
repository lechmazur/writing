The crystalline orchard perched at the edge of a shimmering crater had outlasted every tongue that named it.  Beyond the murmur of lost languages, its glass trees still chimed in the wind, though no one remembered the words.  Into this silence came the marsh-reed sculptor, carrying bundles of pale reed and a hunger to belong.  They had wandered far from the delta where reeds grew tall and speech had never died.  Here, the crater folk measured time in shifts of light rather than syllables, and their bonds were visible as webs of kinship spun between hearths.  The sculptor watched those threads stretch and tremble, realizing they were an outsider knot, frayed and unanchored.  To graft oneself onto community, they decided, required more than observation.  It required a shared wound, or perhaps a shared search.  The village had been troubled for seasons by an elusive street artist who marked the crystal trunks with glyphs no elder could translate.  Each morning a new tag would appear, luminescent and aching, vanishing by dusk.  The folk called it the Crater's Question, and they chased it in pairs and trios, though always alone.  The sculptor approached the weavers with boldly sympathetic ears, offering reed baskets for their finds and hands for their ladders.  They did not mock the superstitions or boast of distant rivers.  Instead they listened until the grief in each voice shaped itself into a map.  On the seventh dawn, a child found a tag inside a grove of singing prisms, beside a hollow the sculptor recognized as an arbor frame.  The obsidian there was not volcanic but grown, glimmering with a darkness that held light rather than swallowed it.  Someone had begun a structure long ago and abandoned it.  The sculptor understood then that the street artist had not been fleeing them but building something too fragile to finish alone.  They brought reeds to the obsidian, weaving flexible stems through the rigid glimmering arbor so that shadow and stalk became a single trellis.  The village joined the work, each person tying a knot of kinship into the lattice, until the glimmering obsidian arbor stood not as a monument but as a living joint.  They searched no more for the artist, for in the shared search for an elusive tag they had become the tag, the grammar of their togetherness written in reed and black glass.  At the center, where the glyphs had first appeared, the sculptor placed their final reed figure: a hand open in giving.  The village elder, whose face was a topography of lost words, asked what it meant.  The sculptor answered that some meanings were dedicated not to speech but to use.  They would dedicate the arbor to those who came after, when the last murmurs of old languages had thinned to nothing.  It would shelter travelers, hold rain, and cast shadows that spelled welcome in every tongue yet unborn.  The elder wept, and the sound was a luminescent ache, beautiful because it could not be kept.  The ache was not absence but the exact weight of belonging, a sorrow that glowed.  As the crater shimmered and the glass trees rang, the sculptor felt the first thread of kinship settle against their skin, no longer frayed.  They had come to graft themselves onto community, and in return had become both graft and living grower.  The marsh-reed sculptor remained in the orchard, weaving new arbor limbs each season, tending the webs that bound the crater folk to the reeds and the reeds to them.  And when the street artist's final tag faded from the crystal bark, it left behind only the arbor, glimmering, patiently holding the light of a people who no longer needed to ask.
