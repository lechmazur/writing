After time became visible, the baroque asteroid Orphelin wore its centuries like coral on its gilded spires.  Cassel the defunct revolutionary walked the colonnades where the old regime had once executed poets and clockmakers.  He was poetically inclined, though he had buried every sonnet under rifle manuals and manifestos.  Now he catalogued the wreckage of a civilization that no longer agreed upon its own name.  The dispute was archaeological: the Excavators claimed the asteroid was a tomb for dead gods, while the Continuists insisted it was a nursery for unborn ones.  Cassel had led the revolt that toppled the Continuist Curators, and when the victory soured, he had become defunct, a monument to his own violence.  In his coat pocket he carried a crystal vase shard, sharp as a frozen note of music, the only relic he had stolen rather than surrendered.  The shard had been found in the Hollow Choir, a cavern where time collected like dust on a broken instrument.  Because the archaeologists fought over the shard's provenance, Cassel knew the object could either crown a dynasty of interpretation or shatter it.  He was too old for another war, but not too old to devise a way of ending this one.  The storms came every seventh evening, when visible time rained upward and turned the sky into a scrolling archive.  During those storms, every mirror became a door, and behind each door stood a version of the viewer that history had discarded.  So the inhabitants practiced covering mirrors during storms, draping velvet over glass, taping paper over polished bronze, shutting out the selves they could not bear.  Cassel kept the largest mirror in his tower wrapped year-round, for he had seen too many dead revolutionaries staring back at him.  He wanted to reimagine an entire civilization, not merely win an argument about bones and pottery.  He believed the vase shard could be tuned like a bell if placed where all interpretations would pass through it.  For a month he worked in his observatory, composing verses that mapped orbital resonance to grief, each stanza calibrated to the fracture in the crystal.  The astronomers called his work madness, but they read his drafts in secret, for his poetry had the burnished sorrow of tarnished gold.  When the next storm arrived, Cassel climbed the central basilica with the shard wrapped in lead and his poem folded like a flag of surrender.  The archaeologists had gathered there to debate under the covered mirrors, each faction holding lanterns of different colors.  He stood before them, poetically inclined and obsolete, and asked them to uncover one mirror.  They obeyed out of superstition, and in the glass they saw not the storm, but the asteroid as it had been in every age they claimed.  Cassel fixed the shard to the mirror's frame, and through the crack in the crystal, the reflected timelines braided into a single image of continuity.  It showed no tomb and no nursery, only a people who had broken their own image and kept the pieces in order to remember the breaking.  The Excavators wept, and the Continuists lowered their lanterns, and for the first time the dispute did not require a victor.  Cassel had not restored the old regime nor founded a new one; he had given the civilization a mirror it could face uncovered.  In the weeks that followed, children were taught to cover mirrors during storms, but also to leave one small pane clear.  They learned that sorrow could be polished until it shone, and that revolution was not only an ending but a way of seeing fragments whole.  Cassel returned to his tower and unwrapped the great mirror for an hour each evening, letting the dead revolutionary stare until he grew tired.  He wrote one last poem on the back of the crystal vase shard's empty case, a poem about a baroque asteroid that carried time like water in a broken cup.  Then he set sail on the slow cometary orbit, defunct no longer, but transformed into a quiet curatorship of unfinished things.  The civilization he reimagined still argues, yet it argues now beside uncovered mirrors, with the burnished sorrow of those who have seen themselves and chosen to continue.  After time became visible, he finally understood that every shard was a sentence, and every covered mirror was a stanza waiting for the storm to end.  When the next storm came, no one screamed, for they had learned to cover the glass and keep the light.
