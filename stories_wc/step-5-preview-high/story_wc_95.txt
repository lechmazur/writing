Madame Orla Vane, a dueling philosopher with a mechanical arm, believed that patience was simply combat conducted at a lower volume.  She had come to the glass atrium beside a quiet brook to answer a question that would not stay still.  The hour was late afternoon, and she was working while waiting for Ashwin Cleve, a former student who had learned too well how to fence and how to argue.  Orla was diplomatically fierce, which meant she could refuse a war without raising her voice and win the argument before swords were drawn.  Her motive was not revenge; it was to turn questions into destinations, a habit she had learned from maps that behaved like enemies.  In the center of the atrium stood a half-painted sundial, its brass face exposed on one side and veiled by someone's careless cobalt on the other.  The caretaker had called it vandalism, but Orla recognized a hidden overture disguised as a restoration.  Someone had painted over the dial to keep a message from being read too quickly.  She set her gloves beside a Danish tin sewing needle card that had belonged to her first fencing master.  Its printed grid of needle sizes was also a cipher, and she had kept it for years because it was the only key that made sense of the dial's angles.  Sunlight slipped through the glass roof and struck the brook outside into moving coins, while the atrium's shadows turned with the patience of old debtors.  Orla measured the dial by eye, then with a brass protractor, but the numbers refused to resolve into language.  She opened the tin card and slid out a needle whose eye was no larger than a raindrop.  When she fixed the needle upright at the dial's center, its shadow fell along an unfinished seam in the paint and completed the sundial's coded geometry.  The message did not arrive as words; it arrived as a set of instructions to read the angles in order and trust only the ones that pointed toward the water.  She copied the angles onto the card's grid and felt the hidden overture open like a door that had been waiting for the right shoulder.  It was an invitation from the Meridian Council, addressed to a duelist who could be trusted with a truce because she would never mistake courtesy for surrender.  The Council asked her to meet at the old ferry landing before dawn, and the half-painted dial had kept the request from watching eyes.  Orla had suspected that Ashwin would come to stop her, but she had not expected him to arrive with a teapot, two swords, and an apology.  He bowed with the grave courtesy of a man who understood that their friendship had become a standing appointment for violence.  "You still read other people's dials," he said.  "You still leave them half-finished," Orla replied.  He poured tea with one hand and offered the cup with the other, a diplomat's habit he had stolen from her and polished until it shone.  Then he took up a slim dueling blade and waited, because he knew she would not discuss an overture without testing its edges.  Orla's mechanical arm whirred once as she rose, a sound like a clock deciding to be brave.  They fenced beneath the glass roof, not to kill, but to argue in a language that had no room for flinching.  Ashwin pressed high, then low, trying to force her from the dial, and she answered via the coded angles in a half-painted sundial rather than with anger.  Her blade moved to the exact degrees the shadow had taught her, turning each parry into a sentence and each advance into a reply.  When he cut at her left, the mechanical hand caught his blade on a line that spelled a question, and she held it there like a doorstop.  "Ask it properly," she said.  He hesitated, then asked why the Council trusted her more than him.  She released his blade and stepped back to the card, where she laid her palm flat as if calming a horse.  "Because you answer questions with victories," she said, "and I answer them with destinations."  Ashwin's shoulders dropped, and he sheathed his sword with a care that betrayed his relief.  "The ferry is an hour out," he said.  "Then we will go together," Orla answered, "and you may carry the needle card."  The brook murmured against the glass as though applauding a performance it had watched many times and still could not explain.  The duel left them with bruised wrists, cold tea, and a coded map promising peace to anyone fierce enough to be polite.  They walked into the dignified chaos of the hour, two philosophers with two blades, ready to make the next question a destination.
