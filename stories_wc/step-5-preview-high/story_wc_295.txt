Ellery Vance was the only lucid dream chronicler employed at the Meridian Celestial Mapping Observatory, and his post was as solitary as the tide-bound moon.  The brass domes above the frozen plateau hissed with night air while he sat among leather-bound ledgers and glass photographic plates, transcribing the sorrows that strangers carried in their sleep.  He had learned early that dreams deposit their heaviest cargo in the bones of the witness, so he collected those burdens not to own them, but to share them with the turning sky.  His only companion was a daylily-pressed telegram from Mara, pinned beside the transit slit, its petals flattened into brittle orange whispers and its ink faded to the color of dried blood.  The message read, "The temple is not stone but the space between heartbeats," and though Mara had sent it a decade ago, Ellery still needed to hear it whenever the observatory felt like a coffin.  Mara had been the one to teach him that he heals with words, that a precisely written sentence could stanch a nightmare the way tourniquet silk stops blood.  Patients from the valley below would mail him their nightmares, and he would return them rewritten, softened by cadence until the terror became a manageable dusk rather than a devouring dark.  But his private obsession remained the temple inscriptions, glyphs that surfaced again and again in the dreams of dying miners and widowed cartographers, spiraling shapes that no philologist could match to any known tongue.  Ellery believed the glyphs were astronomical, a star-language older than the mountain, and he pursued them through variable stars, those luminous clocks whose pulse-lengths might encode meaning for those patient enough to witness the rhythm.  Night after night he timed the dimming and brightening of Cepheid and Mira-type stars, aligning their flickering signatures against the curves of the glyphs until his eyes ached with silver afterimages.  The work was a shared burden, though he bore it mostly alone, for the observatory board saw no scientific value in decoding dream-glyphs, and his budget had been cut to candles and cold tea.  Then came the night when a miner's dream supplied the missing line, a vision of a staircase that spiraled not upward but inward, each step etched with the same inscriptions that haunted Ellery's ledger.  In the lucid border between waking and sleep, the chronicler stepped into the miner's stairwell and witnessed the final chamber, where a great stone dial showed not constellations but the variable cycles themselves, translated into blessing and warning.  Understanding dawned not in a flash but as a slow tide, when he realized that the temple inscriptions were instructions for distributing weight, for letting each star pulse in turn so that no single light need burn constant.  He thought of Mara, of how she had pressed the daylily into the telegram as a reminder that some flowers open only for a day, yet their color outlasts the season if kept with intention.  The temple was a map of shared burdens, telling all who could read it that grief must be passed along constellations, that a witness is required to carry the message from one pulse to the next.  Ellery lit a fresh candle and began to write, not a report, but a letter to every dreamer who had ever trusted him with their midnight cargo, healing with words the exact shape of their load.  He told them the stars were variable so that they need not be, that the sky itself dimmed and brightened in their honor, and that the burden was no longer theirs to carry in the dark.  When the first gray rim of dawn touched the eastern shutter, he pinned the daylily-pressed telegram above his final entry and closed the ledger with the quiet certainty of a man who had finally set down a weight.  The observatory hummed in midnight solace, and somewhere among the variable stars, a new pulse began, measured, witnessed, and understood.
