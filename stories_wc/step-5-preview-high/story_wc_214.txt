The cosmic janitor swept stardust from the observatory deck, his broom worn smooth through the hush of an overlooked era. 
Above him, the shooting star tracking station hummed with instruments older than memory, their brass fittings dull beneath layers of comet residue. 
He was spiritually curious, a trait uncommon among maintenance crews who scrubbed meteorite stains and muttered about pay cycles. 
Instead, he studied the sky with reverence, searching for patterns in the chaos of burning debris. 
Each night he lifted the yellow lantern glass to his eye, its amber pane filtering the cold light into something warm and forgiving. 
Through this lens he served the night by tracing the path of falling stars, mapping their descent from the dark into the waiting atmosphere. 
The station logs demanded coordinates, velocities, and impact estimates, but he recorded something else, a quiet ache he could not name. 
Other janitors saw only debris to be cleared, so his quiet duty was to empathize with each streak of light, feeling the terror and beauty of its final plunge. 
To find strength in vulnerability, he believed one must first witness collapse and understand its necessary grace. 
His motivation was not duty but a hunger to learn how fragile things could burn so brilliantly and still matter. 
The station sat on a ridge of black stone where primal elegance ruled the horizon, jagged peaks softened by ancient ice and the slow breath of nebulae. 
Storms moved across the valleys like prowling beasts, and the janitor welcomed their roar, for even chaos kept a rhythm he could sweep alongside. 
In that wild place he discovered flourishing stillness, not as silence, but as the charged pause between a meteor's ignition and its dissolution into ash. 
He held that moment with the yellow lantern glass, turning it so the trapped light danced across his calloused palms. 
Other workers mocked his rituals, calling them superstition unbecoming of a technician. 
They did not see the drawings he made in the dust, spirals and arrows that mirrored the trajectories of those who fell with dignity. 
When the great sky burn came, a cascade of fire so bright it bleached the clouds, he climbed the tower alone. 
The instruments shrieked and then failed, leaving only his eye and the yellow lantern glass. 
He pressed the lens to the sky and traced the path of a falling star larger than any recorded, a wounded giant shedding molten tears. 
In its roar he felt an ancient vulnerability, a splendor that did not resist its ending. 
He empathized so completely that his own small fears dissolved, replaced by a vast, trembling acceptance. 
Strength rose in him not as armor but as openness, the willingness to shine while knowing he was breakable. 
The star struck the ridge, and the shockwave shattered every window in the station. 
Glass fell like rain, yet the yellow lantern glass remained whole in his grip, warm as a living coal. 
When the others emerged, trembling and cursing, they found him kneeling in the crater's edge, sweeping fresh stardust into careful patterns. 
He looked up, his face lit by the last embers of the impact, and smiled with primal elegance. 
The stillness that followed was not empty but flourishing, a quiet that pulsed with the certainty that endings seed beginnings. 
Through the hush of an overlooked era, he had found his answer, that vulnerability was not a wound to hide but a fire to tend. 
The station would be rebuilt by engineers who understood metrics but not mystery, and that was acceptable. 
He would remain the janitor, spiritually curious and quiet, tracing new paths through the dark with his yellow lantern glass.
