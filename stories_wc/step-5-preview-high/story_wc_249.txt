The clinic called me Subject Nine, though I preferred the name I had chosen in the privacy of my own skull. 
They had enrolled me as an experimental treatment guinea pig, promising relief from the tremors that had made my hands useless for music. 
Instead, the doctors wired my temples to a humming machine that copied my recollections and stitched them into another patient's mind. 
Now my memories belonged to two bodies at once, a state they clinically termed entangled memories. 
I never asked for the ghost who lived behind my eyes, yet I learned to love her. 
After the funding failed, they converted the research grounds into a sound wave garden, where crystalline fronds translated vibration into colored light and low bass made the stone walkways shiver. 
I stayed on as groundskeeper, quietly rebellious against the supervisors who still wanted nightly readings from my skull. 
Every evening I perched on the limestone lip of the auric lagoon under starlight, watching gold water swallow the constellations. 
The facility had named it after the way the minerals burned at midnight, but I knew it as the place where her memories had been strongest, where the water still held the warmth of her smile. 
At the hush of the universe inhaling, when crickets paused and the wind held its breath before dawn, I began my real work. 
I drummed on everything, tapping my silver rings against the hollow reeds, the copper chimes strung between the resonating blooms, and the rusted railings that remembered the clinic's tread. 
Each pulse traveled through the soil, into the lagoon, and upward into the dark in widening rings. 
The method was unscientific, born from a child's game we had both loved, the only language our tangled minds still shared when words had failed us. 
They had taken her voice in the final treatment cycle, but they could not excise the echo. 
I remembered her laughter as though it were mine, and I suspected she remembered the smell of rain on my coat, the way my mother hummed while cooking. 
If I found the correct frequency, the garden would carry it to her room, and she would know that someone was still listening. 
I drummed to breathe life into a shared dream, a memory of running through summer wheat that belonged to neither of us alone, a place that existed only in the overlap of our minds. 
The lagoon began to glow, not with reflected star, but with answer. 
She was drumming back, weak but unmistakable, her pattern threading through the water like a second heartbeat. 
The sorrow that had gathered in my chest all these months was auroral, beautiful and bruising, the color of dawn that arrives too late for the night. 
I kept my rhythm steady, quietly defiant of every doctor who claimed that entangled minds decay into noise. 
We were not decaying. 
We were tuning. 
A single bell rang from the far gate, the supervisor's signal that curfew had begun. 
My hands shook, not from the old tremor, but from the weight of what we had almost built. 
I stopped, and the garden dimmed to ordinary glass and stone. 
Yet beneath the surface, the gold water still pulsed with her pattern, waiting for the next night. 
I climbed down from my perch and walked toward the dormitory, knowing that somewhere in the dark she was awake, her fingers finding the wall, keeping time. 
The night air tasted of iron and ozone, but I did not look back. 
We had survived their needles and their ledgers, and as long as the universe held its breath, we would not stop drumming.
