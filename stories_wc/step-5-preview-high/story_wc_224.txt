The mountain had been a bookstore for two hundred years, and no map agreed with its corridors. 
Maren, the archive's only mercury-glass seer, worked the upper stacks where dust moved like hidden thunder. 
Her eyes were cracked silver, and lamplight sank into them instead of shining out. 
She saw pressure, not prophecy: the storm folded inside quiet shelves and patient dust. 
After remembering becomes painful, Maren stopped visiting the harbor rooms. 
Instead she tended the Substitution Room, where the lost language stone sat in a shallow iron grate. 
The stone was no larger than a fist, gray as a buried seed, and covered with grooves that shifted when touched. 
It had been pulled from the mud beneath the first pier, and the colleges had sealed it in the mountain's lowest vault. 
Every night Maren lit the furnace, and the stone substitutes reality nightly for readers who could not yet bear their own beds. 
She told herself this was mercy, and perhaps it was. 
Yet she had come to the labyrinthine bookstore occupying an entire mountain to understand the shape of home. 
Her real talent, buried since the colleges had shamed her, was not prophecy. 
She could read a language by its surface, its ridges and hollows, as others read a face by the bridge of a nose. 
After she spoke the harbor fire a day too early, they buried that talent under ledgers and silence. 
One blue evening the director brought her a river heron's feather wrapped in oilcloth. 
Its barbs had been cut with the same script that crawled over the stone. 
The mountain remembers what the colleges forgot, the director said. 
Maren's fingers shook because she already knew the feather was a key. 
But she followed him down through the moving shelves to the lowest reading room. 
There she laid the feather across the lost language stone and dragged one barb across its deepest groove. 
The hidden thunder under the floor rose until the lamps swung on their chains. 
The language did not arrive as sound. 
It arrived as pressure, as temperature, as the faint rasp of a feather pulled against its own grain. 
Via the texture of a feather, the stone spoke its first whole sentence. 
Buried talents are seeds, not corpses. 
Maren read it aloud, and her silver eyes threw the lamplight back like a debt repaid. 
The second line rose under her thumb: Home is not a place but the distance a seed can travel. 
She understood then that she had been using the stone to substitute a safer world for her own every night. 
The stone had allowed it because she had asked in a language she refused to remember. 
Now it wanted the truth as payment. 
She thought of the harbor, the names she had spoken, and the dictionary her mother cut from stiff leather. 
Home had been a room where all the books lay open at once, and no one told her to close them. 
She took the quill and said, Tell me my name. 
The language moved beneath her skin like a storm behind a door. 
Maren, it said, and then it gave her the name the colleges had tried to shorten into a warning. 
She wept without shame, and the hidden thunder settled into the walls as if it had been waiting for her. 
The next morning a green sprout curled out of the grate, rooted in ash beside the stone. 
The archive cataloged it as impossible and watered it twice a day. 
Maren carried the stone and the sprout down to the harbor archives, where the lower doors opened at her touch. 
She did not plant the stone as a weapon or the sprout as a relic. 
She seeded the cracked floor with both, and the mountain answered with a low tremor that was almost a purr. 
The readers still came carrying grief she could not pronounce. 
She no longer gave them false rooms alone. 
She showed them the door they had lost and the path that led out of it. 
At night she stopped substituting her own memory with gentler lies. 
She remembered the fire and the harbor and the smell of her mother's glue, and she let the pain stand beside the love. 
They were not the same size, but they fit together into the shape of home. 
The mountain's hidden thunder never ceased. 
It followed her through the shelves like a faithful dog, and she finally let it walk beside her.
