Mira Vane adjusted the copper collar of her signal coat and stepped onto the swaying gantry of the highest radio tower in Cindral, the city of dead antennas suspended over the gorge. 
Below her, rusted cables hummed in the wind, and far beneath them the gorge breathed fog like a sleeping animal. 
Tonight was lunar perigee, and the moon hung so close that its gravity made her back teeth ache and the old receivers whisper in dead languages. 
As a celestial signal interpreter, she had spent eleven years translating star static into shipping forecasts, collision warnings, and the occasional treaty. 
But her true work began now, when the swollen moon dragged the tide of the heavens low enough to expose the hush. 
From her waxed canvas pouch she withdrew the hollywood star fragment, a palm-sized wedge of black crystal salvaged from a boulevard star dedicated to an actress who had later become a true constellation. 
It was serenely electric, humming against her callused fingers with a calm voltage that raised the fine hairs on her forearm without any sting of pain. 
The fragment held a shard of hush, a compressed silence shed by a dying star and trapped in the vault of a cinema prop house before the archives burned. 
Most interpreters treated it as an ending, the flatline after the last broadcast, but Mira knew that every silence concealed a seed. 
Her motivation was neither fame nor fortune, but the stubborn belief that one must find the beginning in every ending. 
She climbed the maintenance ladder to the array platform, where twelve parabolic dishes leaned toward the perigee moon like deaf giants hoping to overhear a secret. 
Cindral groaned around her, an abandoned radio tower city held over the gorge by rusted cables and older stubbornness. 
The practical work mattered more than the romance of ruin, so she set the fragment into the central receiver, a brass cradle lined with vacuum tubes pulled from a projection booth. 
The hush resisted, spreading a velvet pressure against her eardrums that felt like the inside of a bell. 
She needed to tune the receiver to the silence rather than against it, which meant applying the method she had learned in a monastery of dead frequencies. 
That method was to discard assumptions. 
She closed her eyes and released the belief that all signals must be loud. 
She unlearned the habit of treating the moon as a mirror instead of a source. 
She let go of the fear that stillness meant death rather than gestation. 
As each assumption fell away, the serenely electric charge of the fragment deepened, its calm voltage rising until the air around her tasted like ozone and milk. 
The shard of hush opened like a door, and inside the silence she heard a first syllable, the infant gasp of a star being born behind the perigee moon. 
The signal was raw, unshaped, and terrified, and it needed an interpreter who would not flinch from its nakedness. 
Mira pressed her palm flat against the brass cradle and began to tune, not to raise the volume, but to widen the space for meaning. 
The gorge wind screamed below, but the platform settled into a pocket of stillness so complete that a falling snowflake seemed to announce itself with a shout. 
She translated the newborn pattern into pulses of light and sent it down the cables to the dormant city. 
One by one, the abandoned towers woke, their lamps flickering on like sleepy eyes ready to receive the beginning she had rescued from the ending. 
The hollywood star fragment dimmed, its labor complete, but the shard of hush remained in her pocket, warm and patient. 
Mira smiled, because she had learned that every flatline was merely a held breath, and every horizon waited for someone willing to tune the quiet. 
Above her, the perigee moon slid behind a cloud, and in the hush between heartbeats, the city answered.
