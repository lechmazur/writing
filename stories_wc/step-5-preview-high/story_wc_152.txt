The cobalt observatory stood alone among dying maples, its glass panels turning the autumn sunset into bruised blue. 
Inside, beneath ribs of brass and copper, the galaxy-chart embroiderer bent over her loom, pulling silver thread through indigo silk. 
The keepers called her Marn, and because she hummed nonsense songs and stitched constellations slightly off their true axis, they believed her mind wandered where her fingers did. 
Only she understood that a fool's chart invites correction, while a wise one invites censorship. 
Before the coup, the keepers whispered of unrest in the lower city, yet the silence between their words carried more weight than the words themselves. 
The keepers locked their doors at sunset and spoke only in the echoing silence of the stairwell. 
Marn had come to the observatory to listen for unheard stories, believing every star held memories no tongue had shaped. 
The eternal candle at the room's center never guttered, its flame a fixed point against which she measured the tremble of light. 
Its wax had burned through three reigns, and the old sisters claimed it sang when treason walked the corridor. 
Others said it was only a wick soaked in phosphor, but Marn felt its heat answer her heartbeat. 
One wind-battered evening, she noticed that the flame leaned toward the meridian window whenever the bell in the lower tower rang twice. 
She stitched the observation into her margin as a stray thread, then returned to her song. 
But curiosity is a constellation that refuses to stay hidden, and soon she was comparing the flame's lean against the ledger of official star-positions. 
The ledgers had been altered; a whole arm of the sky had been shifted east to mask a fleet gathering in the dark. 
Someone meant to navigate by that lie, to steer the coup into a victory it had not earned. 
This was no accident of ink but conspiracy woven thread by thread. 
To unravel it without raising alarm, Marn disguised her inquiry as folly. 
She sang louder, pricked her finger more often, and asked the young acolytes whether stars might be sewn back on if they fell. 
They laughed and called her the dim-bulb seamstress, never noticing how she recorded their answers. 
Her wisdom hid behind the mask of foolishness, waiting for the right moment to synchronize. 
On the night the frost first silvered the maples, she climbed to the great eyepiece and aligned it not with the current sky, but with the adjacent possibilities that the flame suggested. 
Each tilt of the candle's light opened a corridor of what-if, a narrow path where truth walked beside the lie. 
Through these adjacent possibilities, she saw the coup not as one future but a braid of many, each strand dependent on where the next guard stepped or which bridge burned. 
Her needle moved faster, synchronizing the stitch with the telescope's hum and the flame's shiver, until silk and sky breathed as one. 
The eternal candle flared once, bright as a falling star, and settled into a calm that felt like approval. 
When the head astronomer entered at dawn, he found her asleep at the loom, mouth open, drool on the eternal candle's stand. 
He sneered at the ruined chart, the silver threads spelling out only a child's tangle of nonsense. 
What he failed to see was that the tangle held the true sky, and that the nonsense rhymed with the footsteps already gathering in the square. 
Marn opened one eye, let a slow, foolish smile cross her lips, and kept counting the threads in her head. 
In the corridors, guards passed her door and heard her snoring, never seeing the ledger of truth she kept beneath her apron. 
The coup would come by autumn's end, and the observatory would burn or bow, but somewhere in the city a girl would pull this chart from the embers. 
She would not read the stars from it, but the stories that had waited centuries to be heard.
