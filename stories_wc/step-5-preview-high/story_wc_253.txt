The farmhouse sat beneath three wind turbines that turned even when the air refused to move. 
Their blades carved shadows across the kitchen floor in slow, patient arcs, counting time that no one remembered how to spend. 
In the farmhouse, a blank-faced performer named Elias sat at the table, his features smooth as untouched dough, his eyes fixed on the dried flower bouquet centered before him. 
The stems were brittle now, holding the shape of daisies that had stopped blooming four years ago, when the happiness mandate finally collapsed under its own brittle smile. 
He had once worn a painted grin for twelve hours a day, enforcing joy in the town square until his own face forgot how to feel anything at all. 
He was, by the account of every surviving neighbor, morally ambiguous: a man who had smiled while dragging the grieving to rehabilitation, yet who had also hidden children in his costume trunk until the inspectors passed. 
Now, in the remade hush he had constructed after the mandate fell, Elias survived on ritual. 
The silence here was not natural. 
He had chosen it, packed it into the walls like insulation, and refused to let the turbines' hum enter the rooms. 
Every morning he stood, approached the counter, and began to whittle a block of pine into something that might resemble a bird, though he no longer remembered why birds mattered. 
"Behold," he announced to the empty room, his voice hollow and theatrical, "the knife approaches the resistant grain." 
Dramatically narrating mundane tasks was the only method that kept his hands steady. 
He described the way the shavings curled from the blade as if they were petals falling from a conquered empire. 
He narrated the filling of the kettle as though summoning rain to a desert that had never known thirst. 
The performance had outlived its audience, but he continued it, because stopping meant facing the arithmetic of what he had done. 
The dried flower bouquet had belonged to his sister, who had died during the third year of the mandate for the crime of weeping at her husband's funeral. 
He had pressed the bouquet himself, carefully arranging each stem in the order she liked, before burying her alone because public mourning required a permit that had been revoked. 
To cultivate lightness in grief was his stated motivation, the phrase he repeated to the empty chairs as though it were a prayer. 
He believed that if he could narrate the world with enough ceremony, the weight of loss might float like dust in a sunbeam. 
But lightness never came. 
Instead, the emotional void expanded, filling the farmhouse with a pressure that no turbine could generate. 
One afternoon, the knife slipped. 
A thin line of blood opened across his thumb, and Elias stared at it with the fascination of a man watching rain hit stone. 
"Observe," he whispered, dramatically, "the crimson pearl rises to claim the digit's landscape." 
He did not bandage the wound. 
Pain, he reasoned, was merely another task to be narrated, another mundane reality to elevate through performance. 
The remade hush absorbed his words, refusing to echo them back, which he took as permission to continue. 
Weeks passed, or perhaps days; the turbines outside maintained their indifferent rhythm, and the pine bird grew a wing, then a beak, then nothing at all. 
Elias began to address the dried flower bouquet directly during his narration, giving the stems titles like The Council of Dried Sorrows and The Court of Quiet Bones. 
He whittled faster, the blade flashing, his blank face illuminated by the gray light of a sky that no longer required anyone to be happy. 
"The blade severs the past," he intoned, though he could not say whether he was freeing himself or committing another quiet violence. 
He was a performer still, though the stage had rotted and the mandate had crumbled into apologies and unmarked graves. 
His moral ambiguity was not a costume he had discarded; it had simply changed fabrics. 
He had enforced joy, then enforced silence. 
He had buried his sister, then preserved her flowers. 
He had caused harm in the name of happiness and attempted absolution in the name of grief, and now he could not distinguish which sin weighed more. 
The farmhouse settled around him, overshadowed by turbines that generated power for towns he would never visit again. 
He placed the finished bird beside the bouquet, a wooden thing with no eyes, and narrated its arrival with the gravity of a coronation. 
"It is done," he said, and the remade hush received the declaration without judgment. 
Outside, the wind turned, and for the first time in years, Elias felt something almost like lightness, though it may have been only the draft beneath the door.
