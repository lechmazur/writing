The campfire's last spark died. 
Its smoke drifted east, leaving the breezy lighthouse at dawn wrapped in gray silence. 
On the gallery above the tide line stood a conscience for hire. 
She had lit that fire herself, knowing its end would mark the hour when certainty came begging. 
Her clients arrived when the night no longer offered them shelter. 
Tonight was no different, though the sea breeze carried footsteps up the iron stair. 
The hired woman wore the cloak of doubt like a second skin. 
Its hem was stitched from other people's maybe and perhaps. 
She was sincerely insincere, her voice soft enough to hold truth but sharp enough to keep it at bay. 
In the corner hung a wind chime installation of bone, shell, and copper. 
For years she had earned her bread by deciphering wind chime selections. 
Each collision of material spelled an answer the universe was willing to give. 
Tonight, however, she needed the hollow mirror shard. 
It had no silvering left, only a concave void that caught sound instead of light. 
She lifted it from its hook and turned toward the stairwell. 
The lanterns swayed as Elara emerged, her coat salt-stiff, her eyes fixed. 
"I need to know if I should forgive him," Elara said. 
The hired conscience nodded, sincerely insincere, and gestured to the chimes. 
"The wind will select, and I will read," she offered. 
Elara struck the nearest tubes; a dissonant clang echoed. 
The hired conscience closed her eyes and deciphered the pattern. 
"That is a yes wrapped in a no," she murmured. 
Elara's jaw tightened. 
"I paid for clarity, not poetry." 
The woman in the cloak of doubt sighed. 
She reached up to unhook a strand of blue glass that had fallen silent. 
"Listen," she said, placing the hollow mirror shard against Elara's ear. 
Inside its curve, the chimes whispered what the dawn refused to say. 
"Your certainty is a locked door," the conscience for hire continued. 
"I am not here to lock it again, but to show you the key is already broken." 
Elara stepped back, her protected vulnerability exposed by the salt air. 
She had come after the campfire's last spark died because daylight demanded decisions. 
Yet here in the breezy lighthouse at dawn, she felt the weight of nuance settle on her shoulders. 
"What if I am wrong?" Elara whispered. 
The hired woman smiled, sincerely insincere, the expression reaching nowhere. 
"Then you will be wrong in a way that belongs to you." 
She unhooked the cloak of doubt and let it fall across the threshold. 
Underneath, she wore only the plain wool of someone who had also been certain once. 
Elara stared at the hollow mirror shard now lying open in the sand and shell. 
It reflected no face, only the possibility that forgiveness might be a question. 
The wind shifted, and the remaining chimes began a new selection. 
"You will welcome nuance into certainty," the conscience said. 
It was not a prophecy, but an instruction. 
Elara picked up her coat and turned toward the stair. 
At the door she paused, unhooking her own scarf from the nail where she had tied it. 
She left it there, a small white flag against the iron. 
The conscience for hire stood alone as the sun breached the horizon. 
She had welcomed nuance into certainty one more time. 
The hollow mirror shard remained on the floor, catching the dawn. 
She did not rehook it. 
Some tools were only meant for the hour between campfire and morning. 
The cloak of doubt pooled by the door, heavy with borrowed hesitation. 
Outside, the wind chimes played a chord she had never deciphered before. 
It sounded like permission. 
The conscience for hire allowed herself one moment of protected vulnerability. 
She pressed her palm to the glass and felt nothing but the breeze passing through. 
In that emptiness, she found the nuance she had promised Elara. 
Certainty, she realized, had always been a costume she wore for others. 
To welcome nuance was to finally unhook herself from the performance of knowing. 
The sun warmed the lighthouse stones as she swept the floor. 
She would meet the next client when the next campfire died. 
Until then, the breezy lighthouse at dawn belonged only to the wind and her.
