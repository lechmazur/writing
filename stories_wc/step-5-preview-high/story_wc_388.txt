After the liquidation, Mara Vane still took jobs that ruined pretty stories. 
The courts called her a conspiracy debunker, a title earned by dismantling rumors for frightened towns. 
Her motive was simple and ugly: to excavate truth from comfortable lies. 
At Grange Orchard, the settlers had paid in clean water to discredit one rumor. 
They said the matron's flawed diamond was cursed, and that its curse kept their wasteland orchard thriving amid radioactive dust storms. 
Mara had read the reports before she came: impossible yields, impossible dust counts, impossible survival. 
She was sharply observant enough to suspect that impossible places usually hid ordinary machinery. 
The orchard met her without ceremony, with pear trees bent under fruit and roots knitting red soil out of ash. 
Outside the fence, brown dust storms scraped ruined refineries and glowed faintly at night. 
Yet the storms broke apart a mile out and fell as harmless grit on the windward leaves. 
The settlers explained this with the diamond, because a cursed gem was easier to honor than to question. 
Mara set up her scale and light meter in Hedda's parlor, where the Blackthorn Stone sat on velvet like an accusation. 
It was ugly for a famous stone, clouded at the center, one facet split into a frozen starburst. 
Hedda watched her with the patience of someone who had buried the truth and preferred the story. 
"It killed three owners before the liquidation," Hedda said. 
"One drowned in a dry well, one walked into the storm, and one stopped speaking." 
Mara wrote the names down and asked for the company offices. 
She found them in a sorting shed, among records the liquidators had judged worthless. 
The bins were full of paper confetti, and she spent two days reassembling shredded letters with starch and a fine brush. 
The letters were hydrogeologic reports from a company man named Ives to the board that had owned the orchard. 
Ives had found a hidden stillpoint beneath the eastern fence, a pocket where the air stalled and radioactive dust fell straight down. 
He wrote that the orchard was not blessed but planted, a living filter anchored over the node. 
The board had wanted the stillpoint measured, valued, and quietly sold, not shared with families who could not read the instruments. 
Then came the paragraph that made Mara sit very still. 
Ives had used the flawed diamond as a calibration stone because its fracture cast a shadow exactly like the wind map. 
It had not killed anyone. 
The deaths had been staged to make the stone unholy and the orchard grateful. 
Mara held the Blackthorn Stone to the window and let the storm light pass through. 
The fracture was not random; it matched the dust seams in Ives's letters. 
The curse was a lock, and the flaw was the keyway. 
She carried the stone into the dark and walked the eastern fence until her instruments began to disagree. 
There was no whisper, only a patch of grass standing upright in the wind. 
When she held the diamond edge-on, its flaw drew a thin black line across the ground, and she began to intuit what the company had buried. 
The stillpoint was not treasure but a seam that trees and soil were holding shut. 
The orchard survived because the roots drank fallout while the air braided around a wound in the ground. 
If people dug there for value, they would tear the stitches out and let the storm pour in. 
The next morning the settlers gathered, wanting either a miracle or a murder. 
Mara set the diamond beside the taped letters and did not dress the truth in ceremony. 
"The stone cursed no one," she said. 
"It was a measuring flaw in a company toy, and the murders were announcements." 
"Your orchard lives because it holds shut a stillpoint that the liquidators forgot to sell." 
Hedda's hands closed over the velvet. 
"So we are not chosen." 
"No," Mara said. 
"You were managed." 
"The lie kept you from asking why the storms bend, and the truth can keep you alive if you guard it." 
She left them the letters, the diamond, and the coordinates, and walked east with only water.
