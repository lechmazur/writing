The canyon echo singer arrived at the serpentine nexus under starlight while the universe hums its secret song. 
She had walked three days of dry washes to learn why some people echo. 
Her name was Orel, and her voice could return any cry with an extra note of grief, but she did not know what that gift cost. 
In her pack lay a whispering seashell half, cool as riverbone, wrapped in oilcloth. 
The shell had belonged to her sister, and it spoke in pieces, so that Orel had learned to trust fragments. 
She was richly perceptive, noticing how the wind threaded the paths and how the traders at the roadhead avoided her questions. 
They were part of the webs of intrigue that ran between the canyon towns, though she had not yet seen their shape. 
At the nexus, five stone channels braided around a dry altar, and the night smelled of cold dust. 
Orel set the shell against her ear and heard her sister say, "They price our voices." 
The whisper rose again as she laid the shell on the altar, and this time the words came with a cadence of reed-blown melancholy. 
Orel understood that the shell would disclose its knowledge only through progressive disclosure, a notch at a time, as if it feared being known all at once. 
The first disclosure led her to a line of cairns beside the northern channel, where a trader had hidden a ledger beneath flat stones. 
The ledger recorded payments for children with resonant voices, and Orel's hands shook as she recognized her own name in the column of sellers. 
Her mother had told her the family debt was paid in grain, but the ink said the debt had been paid in sound. 
When she held the shell to the starlight, the inner spiral showed a map of the channels, and one mark burned like a small blue star. 
She followed the mark to the southern channel, where wind combed the reeds and the echo of her bootsteps came back a half-beat late. 
There she found a listening post built into the cliff, its doorway curtained with hides that muffled the canyon. 
Inside, a clerk sat before resonators of bone and copper, sorting slips of sound into labeled baskets. 
Orel's gorge rose; she saw now that echoes were not gifts but traffic, gathered by those who had arranged the webs of intrigue to keep singers poor and afraid. 
She stepped into the lamplight, and the clerk reached for a bell. 
Orel did not call out; instead she set the whispering seashell half on the desk and let it finish her sister's warning. 
The shell said that the council would meet at moonrise to decide which voices would be sold, and Orel understood that she had perhaps one hour. 
She had no army, but she had a singer's knowledge of stone and a richly perceptive ear for the way fear travels in a crowd. 
In the clerk's baskets she found a sealed proclamation meant to make the trade lawful, and she altered the first line with a knife and a steady breath. 
She carried it to the dry altar and placed it where moonrise would find it, then sang a low note that the canyon returned as if it had been waiting. 
The councilors came in ordered rows, cloaks moving like slow water, and their guards carried lamps that guttered under the rising moon. 
Orel stepped onto the altar stone and let her voice climb until every channel carried it, so that no one present could pretend they had not heard. 
She read the altered proclamation aloud, naming the sellers and the buyers, and the ledger's pages snapped in the wind like moths. 
The guards hesitated; the canyon gave them back her words with an extra sorrow that made their hands uncertain. 
An old merchant tried to seize the shell, but the clerk who had sorted the voices stepped forward and said the ledger was true. 
In that fracture of the webs of intrigue, the council's authority came apart, thread by thread. 
When the lamps were out, Orel returned to the northern channel and held the shell to the dark, and the shell finally gave the last piece without being asked. 
Her sister had died because she had sung the same note as the stone, and the stone had answered too well. 
Some people echo, the shell whispered, not because the canyon chooses them but because they refuse to let a true sound stop at their own bodies. 
Orel walked out beneath the last stars to ensure that every child in the wash towns would know the price of their voices and the route home.
