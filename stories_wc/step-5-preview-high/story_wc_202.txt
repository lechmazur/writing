In the alchemist's market strung high above the city rooftops, where vendors sold bottled fog and memories carved from amber, Mira worked as a between-worlds translator.  She sat at a stall draped in harbor tapestries, waiting for clients who needed words carried across the veil separating the living from the forgotten.  The item that had brought her here was a lighthouse lens fragment, small enough to fit her palm but heavy with a weight that shifted depending on who held it.  It was elusively tangible, sometimes warm as breath and other times cold as the deep sea, slipping through her fingers when she tried to examine it too closely.  Mira had found the fragment years ago, in the span between childhood and now, when her grandmother pressed it into her hand during a final conversation that had never quite ended.  The old woman had spoken of anchors of intuition, saying that certain objects could pin truth to the world when history tried to erase it.  Now, in the eternal instant of the market's twilight hour, when gas lamps flickered but did not fully illuminate, Mira unfolded a canvas she had carried for weeks.  It was a painting of the old lighthouse, unsigned and catalogued as worthless by archivists who had never bothered to look past the varnish.  But Mira had learned to read through hidden details in paintings, finding in the lower corner a cipher woven into the foam, a map of what the city had buried.  She needed to unfasten the binding that kept the true history locked inside the artist's deliberate mistakes, the almost-invisible fractures matching the cracks in her lens fragment.  Around her, the market hummed with commerce between species and times, yet she felt suspended, as if the entire rope-bridged kingdom existed inside a single held breath.  A customer approached, cloaked in the gray of unfinished letters, and asked what a translator might charge to restore a name washed from municipal records.  Mira recognized the tone of desperation; it was the same she had heard in her own voice as a child, standing at the water's edge and sensing that something enormous had been lost before she could name it.  She quoted no price in coin, only that she required his help to unfasten the frame's hidden clasp, a mechanism only another between-worlds soul could touch without burning.  Together they worked, the stranger's fingers passing through solid wood while Mira aligned the lens fragment against the painting's concealed geometry.  Light erupted, not bright but profound, a beam that lit the harbor exactly as it had been on the night the lighthouse truly burned, not with oil but with the collected memory of every sailor who had trusted it.  The anchors of intuition were not metaphors after all; they were lines of force that allowed the overlooked to hold their ground against the relentless current of official forgetting.  Mira saw the names then, hundreds of them, painted in invisible pigment that only the lens could reveal: dockworkers, wives, a child who had warned the harbor master and been ignored.  The eternal instant stretched, allowing her to trace each face, to feel the texture of their lives against her skin, elusively tangible as smoke but undeniable as stone.  The stranger wept as his own name surfaced, and Mira translated it aloud into the languages of three districts that had pretended he never existed.  When the light faded, the painting was changed, no longer a harbor scene but a portrait of collective endurance that no restorer could ever reverse.  Mira fastened the lens fragment around her neck with a cord of braided seaweed, understanding finally that her work was not to move between worlds but to make them recognize their missing pieces.  The market continued around her, suspended above the waking city, where the rooftops began to steam with morning and the living stirred without knowing how close they had come to remembering.  She packed her stall with deliberate slowness, knowing that tomorrow other paintings would surface in estate sales and pawnshops, other fragments would call to her from drawers.  The between-worlds translator did not seek fame or gratitude; she sought only the quiet satisfaction of unfastening one more lock, one more silence, one more history that the powerful had tried to drown.  And in the hush between the market's gas lamps and the city's awakening horns, Mira stood at the edge of the platform and felt the eternal instant settle into her bones, a promise that nothing truly forgotten was ever truly gone.
