Del Marr, an ex-band roadie turned antiques appraiser, had learned to trust his hands more than his instincts, which was why the livestream had humiliated him. 
Three days earlier, he had called a scrap of speaker grille cloth a generic replacement, valued it at four dollars, and watched the chat rewrite his reputation in real time. 
Now the quarry office trailer stood at the end of a gravel road, its tin walls sweating in the autumn cold, its windows facing a limestone bowl that threw every sound back at the listener. 
Del had come to catalog equipment left behind by the quarry's previous owner, but the real assignment was private: to discover purpose after loss. 
Inside, the air smelled of diesel, moldy plywood, and old tubes warming up in memory. 
He set his clipboard on a folding table and began reading labels aloud, insulating emotionally with facts. 
Speaker, eight-ohm, seventy watts; cotton grille, replacement probable; case latch broken; handle scarred by amp chains; dust consistent with twenty years in an unheated structure. 
The words were armor. 
He was confidently uncertain about every number he wrote, though his handwriting never showed it. 
Twenty years ago he had tuned these cabinets before the valley swallowed the music. 
His band, The Long Meridian, had played its final show in the quarry after the singer, Lena Voss, was told she had months to live. 
The crowd had stayed past midnight, calling into the dark until the limestone answered. 
After the applause, Del had packed the amps in silence while the rest of the band argued about whether to release the tapes. 
Lena died before spring, the band folded, and Del found work grading porcelain for auction houses because objects did not ask him how he felt. 
He told himself he had survived by reducing everything to provenance, condition, and market comparables. 
Facts were clean. 
Facts did not scream. 
In the trailer, a stack of milk crates held a blown speaker with its grille half torn away. 
Del measured the tear without meaning to touch it. 
A scrap of speaker grille cloth had caught beneath the frame, stiff with age and smoke, threaded with a wire that had once carried voltage and now carried a memory he could not grade. 
His first instinct was to pry it loose with a flathead, but the cloth had fused to the wire mesh over decades of damp. 
He set down the tool and used both hands to wrest the scrap free, feeling the threads give in a sound like a held breath released. 
Under the speaker, wrapped in oilcloth, he found a leather folio of tour paperwork, one page signed by Lena. 
The page listed the final set and, at the bottom, a line addressed to him: Keep the valley cloth for me; you always heard the ending better than anyone. 
The scrap in his hand matched the fragment he had dismissed on camera, thread for thread, right down to the scorched silver warp. 
Some archivist had fed the host the concert recording, and Del had watched his own voice call it debris while the audience heard Lena introduce the song behind him. 
He had failed publicly not because his gauge was wrong, but because he had measured the wrong thing. 
He had treated the scrap as an object to be priced rather than a piece of the only applause that mattered to him anymore. 
Outside, a truck downshifted, and the quarry wall returned the noise as a long chord. 
The office heater clicked, then settled into its electric hum. 
Del opened his folio to a blank page and began an appraisal in a new hand. 
He recorded the grille's construction, the speaker's serial number, and the date of the quarry show. 
Then he wrote the words he had spent twenty years refusing to say: this scrap is a document of last night, not a defect; it is the physical remainder of a voice that sang into a valley and asked for nothing but to be heard. 
The quarry office trailer was not a place to hide. 
It was a listening post. 
He understood now that after the applause, someone had to stay behind and keep the echo from disappearing. 
He would return to the auction house with a correction, a valuation, and the scrap; then he would find the other pieces of the night before they were thrown away. 
Until then, he sat with the cloth in his palm, confidently uncertain about every price, and completely certain of what he owed the valley.
