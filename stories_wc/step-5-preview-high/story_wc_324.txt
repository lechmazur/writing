The mist-clad atrium waited in twilight, its glass dome weeping condensation onto marble benches that had not known sunlight since the bells began. 
Sela adjusted the decades-old hearing aid pressed to her ear, its copper coil warm against her jaw as she listened for the first faint pulse of the evening. 
As the facility's only contact tracer for hearts, she traced the emotional residue of the recently departed, mapping the final beats that tethered them to the living before the mist swallowed their names. 
It was the hour when the dead call, a brief twilight window when voices could still cross the threshold if someone bothered to listen. 
A reeling hush settled over the atrium, the kind of silence that felt bruised, as though the air itself still rang from some distant, devastating chord. 
Sela was persistently curious, a trait her supervisors called inefficient but which had twice uncovered mislabeled corpses and once revealed a grieving widow's confession hidden in static. 
Tonight, that curiosity snagged on a discordant thrum buried beneath the atrium's usual chorus, a pulse skipping like a stone across deep water. 
She followed it by tracking faint pulses in the decades-old hearing aid, turning her head slowly until the signal pulled her toward the eastern colonnade where the shadows pooled thickest. 
There, half-hidden under a bench, lay a cracked leather wallet and the last photograph of a young man, his smile frozen at the edge of a lake that no longer appeared on any map. 
The pulse sharpened when she touched the glossy surface, not with the warm certainty of a peaceful passing, but with the frantic stutter of someone still fighting to be heard. 
Sela's chest tightened; to protect vulnerable voices had always been her charge, the ones the system dismissed as background noise because their heartbeats never fit the expected pattern. 
She pressed the hearing aid harder against her ear, filtering through layers of bureaucratic static until she isolated the call: a name repeated like a prayer, and beneath it, a warning about the lower west wing where the floodgates had failed. 
The atrium's mist began to chill, and Sela realized the dead caller was not merely remembering the flood but living it, trapped in the moment the water rose. 
She did not know the man in the photograph, yet something in his fractured cadence demanded she assure him that his message had crossed the divide, that someone still breathed who would carry his warning to the living. 
"I hear you," she whispered into the coil, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands. 
"Your sister will know, and the water will stop." 
Whether by mercy or malfunction, the pulse eased its frantic rhythm, then flattened into the quiet acceptance she had been trained to recognize. 
She lifted the hearing aid away, but the reeling hush remained, heavier now, as the atrium's other voices resumed their slow, mourning cadence. 
Only later, when she filed her report, did she learn that the man in the photograph had died three days prior, and that his isolated sister, deaf since childhood, had been sitting alone in the west wing when the pressure valves burst. 
Sela had never intended to be a hero; she had simply been too curious to ignore a pulse that did not belong. 
She returned the photograph to the lost-and-found bin, though she kept the memory of the smiling man beside the vanished lake, a reminder that some beats endure even after the chest falls still. 
The next twilight found her back at her post, the decades-old hearing aid warm against her jaw as the mist curled in, and the dead began to call again. 
Her incidental heroism would never appear in the facility's commendations, yet it was enough that the sister survived, and that somewhere in the static, a voice had finally found its way home.
