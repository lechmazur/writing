The storm-tossed ridge fort groaned beneath the weight of black clouds, and in its highest tower a chronicler named Iven sat mildly obsessed with a single purpose. 
He had come to draw a map of an endless night, one that would soon swallow the last star in a dimming sky. 
The watchers called this hour when only one star remains in a dimming sky, a pale wound in the bruise-colored heavens, and its light failed by the minute. 
The chamber smelled of salt and old rain, and the pebble-shore oracle sat across from him with her hands folded like closed shells. 
She had arrived on the tide of the previous moon, bringing nothing but a flaking gold leaf booklet bound in kelp-cured leather. 
Its pages were thinner than moth wings, and each time a corner turned, a speck of gold drifted down like a dying ember. 
Iven could not stop watching those flecks fall; he was mildly obsessed with the idea that every flake carried a memory of light. 
The oracle, who had no name he could pronounce, worked by summarizing silence, pressing her palm to the stone until the storm outside seemed to hold its breath. 
In the ambered hush that followed, she would retell what the walls had witnessed, not in words but in the gaps between heartbeats. 
Iven wrote quickly, translating her pauses into lines and currents, hoping to capture the darkness before it swallowed the horizon. 
Outside, wind tore at the ridge fort, but inside the tower a marbled hush settled, veined with the gray of storm and the white of snow. 
The oracle lifted her fingers from the granite, and a thin whistle escaped the mortar. 
She said the night was not merely an absence but a country, and to survive it one needed boundaries drawn not by lanterns but by listening. 
Iven nodded, his fingers stained with ink and gold dust, and turned to a fresh page of the flaking gold leaf booklet. 
The paper trembled as he marked the first contour of the endless night, a boundary shaped like a held breath. 
Hours passed in the ambered hush, each one thicker than the last, until the last star in the dimming sky seemed caught behind the fort's eastern watchtower. 
The oracle grew still, and when she finally spoke, she chose to retell the moment the tide first turned against the moon, her voice a blade wrapped in velvet. 
But even her retelling was built by summarizing silence, each word placed like a stone on a cairn so travelers would find the path. 
Iven recorded it all, his obsession growing not with frenzy but with the quiet certainty of a man stocking firewood before winter. 
The flaking gold leaf booklet was half full now, its edges shedding brilliance onto the table like a slow snow of light. 
With the last star reduced to a pinprick, the two of them bent over the final quadrant, the storm-tossed ridge fort swaying beneath their feet. 
Iven pressed his pen too hard, and a long crack split the page, releasing a shower of gold that vanished into the dark. 
The oracle caught a single flake on her fingertip and closed her eyes, her mouth moving in a method he had learned to trust. 
By summarizing silence, she showed him that the endless night had a center, a still point where the map need not be drawn but remembered. 
He looked up as the last star finally slipped below the jagged skyline, leaving only the marbled hush of cloud and stone. 
In the dimming sky's absence, the map on the flaking gold leaf booklet began to glow, its lines pulsing in time with the oracle's steady breathing. 
He realized then that she had not come to give him answers but to retell the dark so he could name his way through it. 
The storm-tossed ridge fort settled into an ambered hush, and the endless night spread around them like a sea with shores of pearl. 
Iven closed the flaking gold leaf booklet, mildly obsessed no longer, because the map was no longer something to draw but a silence he could finally keep.
