The junkyard prophet walked barefoot through the fog sculpture garden at low tide, where corroded angels and rusted televisions stood half-swallowed by moss and salt. 
Behind him, the dead video rental building exhaled the hush of old videotapes left unwatched, a silence that pooled between the statues like condensation. 
He had come to locate the frequency of growing older, a sound he believed hid in the hollow throat of a shell-shaped sculpture near the waterline. 
Because he was precognitively forgetful, he could foresee the path for only a heartbeat before the vision dissolved, leaving him to trust the driftwood he carried. 
He knelt on the wet sand and began arranging driftwood as directional markers, stacking bleached branches into arrows that pointed toward the louder echoes. 
Each marker received a glyph from a piece of plain chalk, crude letters that warned him of turning points and soft mud, though he rarely remembered writing them. 
The garden had been built from a bankrupt scrap yard, and its sculptures were held together by wire and memory rather than mortar. 
Fog clung to the metal like breath on a window, and every footstep seemed to release the ghosts of sounds that had never been properly buried. 
He was clinging to echoes, he knew, because the present kept leaking out of him like air from an old tire. 
Each echo was a raft, and he, a castaway who mistook the tide's repetition for rescue. 
In a side room of the sculpture garden, stacks of unplayed videotapes leaned against one another in towers of magnetic hush, their spools frozen mid-spin. 
He had stopped counting them years ago, when he realized each one held a birthday he had never bothered to celebrate. 
Now he preferred the garden's raw acoustics, where a shout could return as an older voice, cracked and slower. 
He had no use for recorded images; he wanted the texture of time, the creak of joints and the sigh of lungs. 
The tide came in with a sigh that sounded like regret, and he felt the old tidal longing rise in his chest, pulling him toward the water. 
He followed his driftwood arrows past a whale rib cage of copper pipe and a dome of satellite dishes that once caught voices from the sky. 
The chalk marks were fading, but he trusted their slant, for they had been drawn by a version of himself who still remembered the way. 
At last he reached the shell-shaped sculpture, its concrete opening damp and green, and he pressed his ear to the cold interior. 
The sound of growing older was there, low and rhythmic, like a boot dragging over gravel, like a spine settling into its own curve. 
He fumbled for a rusted coffee can he had modified into a recorder, its lip brushed with chalk dust, and held it up to catch the resonance. 
But the fog swallowed the first note, and he knew that some echoes refused to be kept by anything as temporary as a man who forgot the future as soon as he saw it. 
So he arranged one final driftwood marker in the shape of a spiral and wrote a single word on the shell's flank with the plain chalk. 
The word was Listen. 
Then he walked back into the fog, letting the tide erase his footprints while the videotapes behind him kept their unwatched hush, and the driftwood stood ready to guide him again when memory failed. 
He was a junkyard prophet of small salvage, and the sound of growing older was not a thing to own, only a thing to keep chasing, tide after tide. 
In the distance, the fog shifted, and a new echo drifted toward him, young and hungry, asking to be located. 
He smiled, forgetting the answer he had just found, and reached for more driftwood.
