Orie, the blithe cemetery gardener, tucked wildflowers into the cuffs of her cardigan. 
She moved between headstones with a lilt, her tenderness organized into neat rows of seed packets, twine, and chalk. 
In her coat pocket she carried an antique sovereign case, its brass clasp worn smooth by generations of worry. 
She told the living that it held coins, but it truly held a folded slip of parchment and three brass thimbles. 
The thimbles were dented from use, and the parchment smelled of cedar and time. 
Every evening, when the last mourner had gone and the crickets began their score, Orie climbed the hill behind the chapel. 
There stood a cobalt silo under stars, its rounded roof open to the black and studded sky. 
On its wall hung a brass chess clock that she wound each dusk, its twin flags poised like sentinels. 
She only entered after the chess clock ran down, when the flags fell and the silo's hush deepened into invitation. 
The old clock had belonged to the cemetery's founder, who believed that time's expiry cleared the air for honest ghosts. 
That hour belonged to the uncharted echoes, whispers not yet pressed into stone or epitaph. 
They drifted from the soil itself, from names half-worn from marble, from roots that had learned to listen. 
Inside, a tapestry hung on a copper rod, depicting a harbor no cartographer had named. 
Its border had frayed into threads of crimson, ash, and sea green, each strand loose and knotted with old meaning. 
Orie opened the antique sovereign case and laid the thimbles and parchment on the silo floor. 
She had come to untangle what the weaver left tangled, believing that every knot concealed a shape. 
Her motivation was not idle; she wished to discover the shape of intentions, the geometry beneath human choices. 
She began by decoding the frayed threads in the tapestry's border, matching each color to a cipher scratched inside the case. 
The crimson spoke of debts, the ash of silence kept too long, the sea green of voyages never booked. 
As Orie loosened each knot, an uncharted echo rose around her, soft as moth wings. 
A mother's second thoughts about emigration rose with the smell of rain on iron. 
She heard a blacksmith's apology, a widow's lie told kindly, a boy's plan to run before dawn. 
The velveteen gloom of the silo seemed to absorb the voices, giving them back as warmth rather than fear. 
Orie's hands, though blithe among the roses, moved with solemn patience here. 
She untangled a final crimson knot, and the threads fell into a pattern like a compass rose. 
In that map she read the shapes of intentions: a spiral of regret, a straight line of duty, a closed circle of love. 
She saw how a mercy could bend like a river and how a grudge might harden into flint. 
The sovereign case, she realized, had been built to hold exactly these patterns, its velvet slots shaped like hollows in a hand. 
She placed the decoded threads inside, each strand finding its ordained channel. 
Outside, the chess clock in the chapel rang again, signaling a new game, but the silo stayed cobalt and still. 
Orie closed the case and pocketed it, humming a tune with no beginning or end. 
Tomorrow she would replant the lilies and straighten the wind-scattered pebbles, tenderly organized as ever. 
Yet she carried a secret now, the uncharted echoes no longer haunting her but humming in time with her pulse. 
She understood at last that intentions were not sharp arrows but soft, knotted fibers, waiting to be held without judgment. 
Each soul she tended left such shapes behind, and she had grown to love them. 
The cemetery slept beneath its quilts of moss, and the cobalt silo kept its vigil under the wheeling stars. 
In the velveteen gloom, Orie walked home lighter, her blithe step a quiet hymn to all she had untangled.
