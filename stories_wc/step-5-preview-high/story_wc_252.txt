Since violence became impossible, the people of the sublittoral fringe had learned to wound one another with words.  The harbor of Giltmarsh lay where the continents dipped into bright shallows, and its docks were crowded with graceful workers who swore like sailors and moved like dancers.  Kael was the most graceful of them all, loading crates of salt-cod and coil-rope with a liquid economy that made hauling look like ceremony.  They were lean, quick, and quarrelsome, and their oldest enemy was Wren, the boatwright who claimed she would strangle Kael if only violence were still an option.  You move like a cat on water, Wren would say, shoving a half-sanded plank under Kael's arm with a gentleness that betrayed her.  And you fight like a woman drowning in air, Kael would answer, grinning because the quarrel felt like being held.  Once every score of tides the disappearing island rose from the sublittoral fringe, its hump breaking the harbor horizon like a held breath.  The elders called it the Wanderer, for it would detach from its seafloor moorings and vanish without warning, returned by no chart and no tide.  They said it left only by the call of a whale from the deep, a song too low for lungs to hear.  In Kael's boot, wrapped in oilcloth, rode a pirate's whalebone scrimshaw, taken from a captain who had once used it to end a war.  Pressed to a bare chest, the scrimshaw unlocked guarded hearts, laying open whatever fear or love a person had chained in the dark.  Because of it, fists had softened and blades had grown useless, for no soldier could strike once the enemy's grief stood naked in his own ribs.  Kael had never dared use it, afraid of what naked thing might be found beneath the ribs.  But the Wanderer was surfacing early, tilting on its side, and Kael felt the same tilt inside, a hunger to detach from the dock and drift.  Wren found Kael at the waterline at dusk, boots sinking in the warm muck of the fringe.  You are leaving, Wren said, and her voice was all complaint and clutch.  I am already gone, Kael answered, combative and tender.  Why? Wren demanded, throwing up her hands. What could this rotting dock still owe you?  Not the dock, Kael said, unrolling the oilcloth so the engraved whale seemed to swim in the lantern-light. I want freedom.  Not from you, but from the fear that I was meant only to haul other people's cargo.  My true nature is not grace on wood; it is out there, deep, where I am not ashamed.  Wren crossed her arms, jaw set, the old wall rebuilt plank by plank.  If you go with that island, you will vanish, she said, and I will be left shouting at gulls.  That is why I brought this, Kael said, holding up the scrimshaw. It unlocks guarded hearts.  Then the sea went quiet. A shudder passed up through the silt, through the sublittoral fringe, into the bones of the dock.  The call of a whale from the deep rolled beneath the waves, and the Wanderer groaned as its moorings snapped.  It began to detach, grinding free of the shelf in a slow, terrible exhalation.  Kael caught Wren's wrist and pressed the scrimshaw to her heart before she could pull away.  It grew warm, and Wren gasped as her locked grief spilled open, not to keep Kael, but to keep herself from admitting the sea had always been kinder.  Her hands fell from their fists, wet and furious, combative tenderness poured out like water.  Go, then, she whispered, striking Kael's shoulder with an open palm. Be stupidly, gloriously yourself.  The Wanderer gave a final, grinding lurch and tore loose, water boiling white around its roots.  Kael looked once at Wren, then at the horizon where the deep whale still sang.  With the scrimshaw clutched tight, they stepped off the dock onto the drifting island's wet shore.  The earth tilted, the island slid seaward, and Kael laughed as the sublittoral fringe fell away beneath their feet.  Somewhere in the dark water the whale called again, and Kael, wild and unashamed, answered with a voice that had always belonged to the deep.
