The Whispering Gallery had become a waiting room for the end of an empire, its curved wall carrying secrets from one side of the dome to the other while below, in the interregnum, no one dared to claim the throne. 
Mira had been a crisis actor for three years, hired to weep convincingly at municipal drills, to clutch fake bandages while officials practiced their responses. 
Then the siege came, and the staged horror had answered it, and she had not been acting anymore. 
Now she belonged to the nomadic penumbra, a shifting company of survivors who circled the capital's empty institutions, sleeping in the half-light of doorways and archives, neither ghosts nor citizens but the shadow cast by a vanished sun. 
They moved through the palace because the palace no longer belonged to anyone, and the Whispering Gallery, with its perfect acoustics, let them speak without being heard by the wrong ears. 
In the center of the rotunda lay a marble chessboard inlaid with black and white squares, but all the pawns had been removed. 
The nobles remained, arranged for a game that could not begin because the infantry of the board had been ground into dust, and Mira understood that history was always written by the pieces that refused to be sacrificed. 
She had come here to become the historian of small gestures, to record the way a stranger's hand trembled before sharing bread, the way her mother straightened a collar that no longer mattered, the precise angle of a bow offered to an empty room. 
The grand chronicles would praise the next dynasty and damn the last, but Mira sought the luminous absence between them, the brightness of things that had been quietly erased. 
The Keeper of the Gallery, an old woman named Ash, was benevolently ruthless; she had turned away three refugees because they could not walk the required silence, yet she had given her own coat to a dying child. 
"You must unbind what you know," Ash told her on the first morning, pressing a strip of vellum into Mira's hands. 
"The speeches are already recorded. Unbind the silence. Take the words out of the archives and let them live in the floor." 
Mira learned the method from the shadow-draped librarians of the penumbra: a pattern of walking that spells words. 
By stepping precisely along the marble seams, a walker could trace letters in cuneiform, in the old trade script, in the language of the siege. 
The gallery amplified the faint scrape of sandals, transforming footsteps into syllables that raced along the curve and entered the ears of those standing thirty feet away. 
For six weeks Mira practiced, her feet memorizing the geometry of grief, until she could inscribe a sentence across the rotunda without breaking her rhythm. 
She chose her first text from the pawnless board. 
Walking slowly, she spelled the names of the dead drill victims, the ones who had been paid to suffer and had then truly suffered, their names omitted from both the old rolls and the new. 
The words traveled the whispering curve and reached the nomadic penumbra gathered in the dark, and they wrote them down with chalk on broken columns. 
Ash watched, her face hard and tender, and when Mira finished she nodded once. 
"You are ready to unbind the rest," the Keeper said, leading her to the eastern alcove where the binding cords of the imperial archive had been knotted since the coronation that never happened. 
The cords were physical and otherwise, knots of red tape and iron, of decrees and debts, and Mira understood that to unbind them was to release not chaos but memory. 
She placed her foot on the first letter and began the long walk that would spell the truth of the small gestures across the dome. 
Her pattern grew wider, braver, encompassing the chessboard where the missing pawns had stood, and she felt the luminous absence fill with something warmer than light. 
When the heralds finally arrived to announce the new sovereign, they found no riot in the Whispering Gallery. 
They found only a woman standing still at the center of the board, breathing hard, and the curved wall still humming with the footsteps of everything she had preserved. 
The pawns were still gone, but their gestures had been spoken, and in the penumbra beyond the pillars, the historians of small things closed their books and smiled. 
Mira remained in the gallery through the first weeks of the new reign, not as a prisoner but as a custodian of the interval. 
The regime had no use for a pawnless board, so they left it untouched, and she walked her patterns whenever light entered the oculus. 
She was benevolently ruthless with comfort, sleeping little, recording much, knowing power would soon demand grand narratives.
