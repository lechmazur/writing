Mira the tidal-pool chronicler knelt on wet basalt inside the ancient grotto under stars. 
The tide whispered at the entrance, retreating from the limestone shelves she had documented for eleven years. 
Above the arched ceiling, winter stars glittered with indifferent clarity, scattering constellations across a black vault. 
She adjusted her copper lamp and tried to ignore the silence settled between her and her apprentice Kael. 
Their talk had stalled on a hard question: whether the chronicle should end after the final high-water survey. 
Kael wanted to burn the last pages as an offering to the sea, claiming the records belonged to water. 
Mira believed the opposite, that memory was the only anchor for people caught between land and tide. 
Now, in the pause, she felt the weight of transition periods hanging in the humid air. 
They were between one tide and the next, between argument and agreement, between death and rebirth. 
She was warmly observant by training, noticing Kael's fingers drumming a nervous rhythm against his knee. 
His boot rested near a basin where a single jade cicada sat half-submerged in salt water. 
The figurine was no larger than a sparrow egg, its wings carved with concentric rings mimicking tidal lines. 
Kael had fished it from the pool that morning without explaining its presence. 
Mira saw her chance to move through the impasse by a tangential approach rather than frontal assault. 
She lifted the object gently, feeling deep-water chill clinging to its polished surface. 
The carver knew the tide, she said, turning the jade cicada so starlight caught its milky veins. 
Kael blinked, his jaw loosening from its clenched set as he followed her gaze to the sculpture. 
Those rings are not decoration, Mira continued, tracing them with a calloused fingertip. 
They are a calendar of pauses, the intervals when the sea hesitates before turning. 
Kael leaned forward, curiosity replacing some of the anger in his eyes. 
He had expected her to defend the chronicle directly, to argue with his plan to abandon it. 
Instead, she had opened a side door, letting him enter the subject on his own terms. 
Through tangential approach she could foresee the shape of another person's resolve. 
Now she felt pressure behind her temples, the scent of ozone and old salt that preceded insight. 
She saw the grotto in a future season, its pools dimmed by algae, the chronicle ash scattered by wind. 
Then the vision shifted because she let the jade cicada guide her associations rather than forcing it. 
She saw a child crouching in this same grotto, reading her notes under a changed but continuing sky. 
The tidal-pool chronicler smiled, understanding that the future was not a wall but a membrane. 
Time has texture, she murmured, mostly to herself, though she meant Kael to hear. 
It is granular where water drags, smooth where stone is patient, and rough where people argue. 
Kael took the jade cicada from her palm, his thumb moving over the carved rings. 
You want to know what it feels like, he said slowly, the accusation gone from his voice. 
I want to discover the texture of time, Mira corrected, so that no one mistakes a pause for an ending. 
The grotto seemed to breathe around them, limestone groaning as the tide reversed beyond the entrance. 
She let the silence return, but now it was a working silence, a transition period holding possibility rather than defeat. 
Kael set the cicada on the flat stone between them like an offering or perhaps a treaty. 
The sea takes what it needs, he said quietly, but returns what we are careless enough to leave behind. 
Mira nodded, her warmly observant eyes cataloging his lowered shoulders and stars reflected in his pupils. 
She picked up her pencil and turned to a fresh page in the chronicle. 
The difficult conversation was not finished, but its trajectory had bent through a single tangential question. 
She wrote the date, the tide level, and a description of the jade cicada's concentric grooves. 
Then she added a private note: the future is legible to those who read spaces between words. 
Outside, the season's first true stars pierced the vapor rising from the ocean. 
The grotto held them both in its ancient curve, and Mira began to dictate the next entry. 
Kael listened, no longer burning to erase the past, but learning to carve new rings into the present. 
In that ancient grotto under stars, two people crossed the threshold of transition, guided by a carved cicada. 
And the chronicle, like the tide, continued.
