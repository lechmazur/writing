Mara Voss was a destitute philanthropist by choice, having spent the last of her family's fortune curing other people's bad luck.  The only inheritance she had kept was a bottled lightning bug collection, forty glass vials lined with copper, each one glowing faintly as if remembering summer.  She carried it across the sea to the island of Vesk, whose southern shore was a black sand beach at the foot of an active volcano.  Every seven years, during the slow dance of celestial bodies, the volcano sighed, the tide withdrew, and the air became a whispering hush, a silence so bright it seemed to murmur.  The islanders believed that at that hour, if a person brought a light that was once alive, the mountain would speak the texture of transformation.  Mara had crossed the harbor that week with less than a week's bread in her pocket, having given away her berth money to a ferryman whose wife was ill.  She arrived in Vesk with only her clothes, the case, and the habit of seeing strangers as unfinished stories.  Mara had never been motivated by prophecy.  She wanted to understand the texture of transformation itself, why people changed, why the poor became generous and the rich became cruel, and she thought the answer might be like grain or grit, something she could feel in her own hands.  On the morning of the alignment, a rockslide closed the only road to the village, so the driver unhitched his mule and pointed Mara down a goat path, a detour that would take her along the cliffs.  She did not argue, because Mara had learned that the best way to help a stranger was to let them change her plans.  Halfway along the detour, she found a girl weeping beside a broken cart of fish.  Mara set down her case and spent an hour repairing the axle with wire from her coat, then carried the fish panniers so the girl could walk the mule home.  The girl's mother, a baker, insisted on feeding her and, when Mara mentioned the beach, warned that soldiers would turn back anyone without a pass.  Mara had no pass, only her case and a mind that orchestrates coincidences.  Years before, she had arranged a merchant's carriage to break near a widow's farm, and the two had discovered they shared a need for salt and paper, and the resulting friendship had saved a village from famine.  She trusted such chances more than money, so she left the baker's bread at a barracks gate, knowing the sergeant's lonely daughter collected insects.  By evening the sergeant came himself, returned the bread untouched, and offered an escort to the shore, saying a woman who fed soldiers should not die alone on a cursed sand.  Mara followed him through basalt ribs and steam vents as the first planet rose, and the black grains beneath her boots began to ring like struck crystal.  At the waterline she opened the case, and the lightning bugs rose from their jars in a slow spiral, not flying away, but singing with green-gold light.  Green light pooled on the black sand, and for a moment the whole coast looked like a held breath wearing jewels.  The whispering hush deepened, and she heard it then, not words, but a hush with a texture, rough and silken, like the moment a debt is forgiven or a stranger becomes a friend.  She had been fulfilling prophecies accidentally, as she fulfilled most things: she had fed the hungry, mended the broken, and given away her last coin, not knowing the island required exactly that.  The volcano did not erupt.  It exhaled, and in that vibrant hush Mara finally understood that transformation was not a single event but a series of small hands letting go.  The bugs drifted back into their jars one by one, and when Mara left the beach, she carried the collection not as treasure but as proof that light could be given away and still return.  She never became rich, but the coincidences she kept orchestrating moved through the island like a current, warm and invisible, changing everyone who touched it.
