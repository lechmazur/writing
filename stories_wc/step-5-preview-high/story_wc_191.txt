Lore keeper Mira adjusted her cracked spectacles in the lamplit archive, where obsolete machines hummed like bees preserved in amber. 
She was the last archivist of the Sunken Metropolis, trained to weigh wonder against risk and to distrust easy maps. 
The council praised her as wisely adventurous whenever they wanted a dangerous errand completed quietly. 
Tonight the errand was personal. 
The unclaimed throne of the High Arcology had stood empty for thirty years, and every claimant had vanished before the orbital lifts would carry them upward. 
Mira had spent her life cataloguing the wreckage of that vanished procession, and she had come to believe the stairway of ascension was not lost but concealed. 
Her only clue was a ruby flake pried from the shattered crown of the final queen. 
It was no jewel but a pigment key, and under ultraviolet light it still carried the chemical signature of ancient orchard paint. 
A dying courier had gasped that the hidden orchard behind a kaleidoscopic waterfall had been built around the first stair. 
If Mira did not reach it before dawn, the Foundry Council would weld the throne shut and call the search treason. 
She had until sunrise to find the stairway of ascension, and the city clocks were already bleeding blue midnight. 
Mira packed the ruby flake in a lead case, chose a coat lined with obsolete foil, and prepared to sally into the rain-slick tunnels. 
The tunnels remembered the old world in dripping neon and rusted advertisements for products no factory could ever build again. 
She moved with the calmly urgent rhythm of someone who had read too many disaster journals to panic and too many legends to stay home. 
At the valley gate she pressed the flake against a corroded scanner, and the locks sighed open like a sleeper remembering childhood. 
Beyond it, spray painted the air in revolving bands of color, and she stepped into the hidden orchard behind a kaleidoscopic waterfall. 
The falls were not natural but a curtain of recycled light and mist, engineered by ancestors who loved beauty more than efficiency. 
Inside, genetically old apple trees grew in curved rows, their fruit glowing faintly with phosphors that had been fashionable decades before her birth. 
The orchard smelled of rain, warm metal, and a futuristic nostalgia that felt like a memory of tomorrow. 
At its center stood a small observatory, its dome flaking with a century of celebratory murals and repair overpainting. 
Mira unrolled her tools and began the slow work, the method recorded in her mother's field notes: through the peel of paint layers revealing hidden murals. 
Each peel lifted a decade, and beneath the patriotic parades she found star charts drawn in forbidden pigment. 
The ruby flake warmed in her palm, and when she held it near the wall, invisible ink bloomed into a spiral staircase ascending into clouds. 
But the stairway was only a painting, a promise of engineering, and Mira knew promises had to be read carefully. 
She peeled deeper, past a layer of factory slogans, until the final mural showed the orchard itself as a machine. 
The apple trees were diodes, the waterfall a coolant veil, and beneath the roots a service stair climbed toward the unclaimed throne. 
The final queen had hidden it not with walls but with nostalgia, trusting that only a patient keeper would dig through sentiment to find the mechanism. 
Mira set the flake into a socket disguised as a painted sun, and the mural floor opened with a smell of ozone and crushed berries. 
Cool air rolled up the stairway of ascension, and for a moment she understood why claimants vanished. 
The climb did not test strength but memory, and it demanded that a ruler remember every name the city had swallowed. 
Mira had those names, in tapes and paint and stories, and she carried them up the stairs like a lantern against the dark. 
She reached a chamber where the unclaimed throne sat veiled in dust, simple and enormous and quietly waiting. 
Instead of claiming it, she placed her mother's field notes and the ruby flake on the seat, then activated the orchard's public transmitter. 
If the throne belonged to anyone, it belonged to the people who had preserved the murals, the tapes, and the trees. 
The first sunrise in thirty years found the city arguing, listening, and climbing the valley road together. 
Mira watched from the orchard, wisely adventurous to the last, and finally let herself sleep.
