Elias Thorne had once guided ships by starlight, but after the wreck he was called a broken navigator by everyone from the harbor master to his own reflection. 
Now he stood at the coastal kayak launch with a cracked astronomical compass pinned to his chest, its brass rim biting through his oilskin. 
The morning forecast had promised stillness, but by noon the sky bruised purple and the expectations of every angler on the dock collapsed into frantic rope-coiling and shouts. 
Elias merely smiled his merrily melancholic smile, the one that made grief look like a dancing ghost, and dragged his yellow kayak into the surf. 
He did not paddle away from the shore; instead, he gravitated along the breakwater, where a sagging row of utility poles marched into the mist like tired sentinels. 
His method for the day was simple, perhaps insane: navigating by utility poles, counting each concrete shoulder until the rhythm lulled the tremor from his hands. 
The astronomical compass was a relic of who he had been, when he believed true north existed and could be held in a velvet-lined box. 
Now it only served as a paperweight of guilt, each hairline fracture a map of every night he had steered by ego instead of stars. 
The backlash of regrets began as a low hum beneath the hull, then swelled into a chorus that echoed off the incoming tide. 
He heard the shipowner's daughter asking why he had ignored the barometer, heard his own laughter from a tavern before the storm, heard the silence of radios going dead. 
Wave after wave of memory battered him harder than the squall, each crest a verdict, each trough a brief mercy. 
When expectations collapse, he thought, you learn whether your spine is built of oak or driftwood, and he paddled harder toward the fourth pole. 
The wind tore at his hood and the rain came sideways, yet Elias kept his eyes locked on the utility poles, their insulators glinting like dull coins through the grey. 
His motivation was not escape but alignment; he had come to the coastal kayak launch to align heart and action, to stop lying about the shore he had wrecked upon. 
A flickering faith wobbled in his chest like a candle in a lantern, guttering but never fully dying, urging him onward. 
By the seventh pole, the backlash of regrets grew almost physical, a riptide of faces pulling at his rudder feet. 
He saw his younger self in the spray, all clean charts and unbroken confidence, and he spoke to that ghost with a merrily melancholic tenderness. 
"You meant well," he called into the gale, "and meaning well is not enough." 
The astronomical compass swung against its cord, and he realized the needle no longer sought magnetic north but instead quivered toward the sixth utility pole, as if the instrument had finally learned humility. 
Elias laughed then, a sound like wind through cracked bamboo, because he understood that broken things could still point somewhere true if you admitted the break. 
The storm did not relent, but he no longer fought the current; he let it gravitate him between the poles, each stroke a confession, each breath a penance accepted without flinching. 
By the tenth pole, the coastal kayak launch reappeared as a thin silver scar where the water met the sand, and he turned the bow toward it not in defeat but in return. 
The backlash of regrets still followed him, but it had changed pitch, from shriek to psalm, because he had stopped fleeing the wreck and started carrying it. 
When his hull grated against the shingle, Elias dragged the kayak above the tide line and stood in the rain, his merrily melancholic eyes wet with salt and something dangerously close to peace. 
He unpinned the astronomical compass and held it out toward the last utility pole, its cracked face reflecting both the storm and the stars hidden behind it. 
"True north," he whispered to the collapsed afternoon, "was never a place." 
The remaining fishermen watched from the parking lot, unsure whether to summon help or salute, as the broken navigator walked inland with a flickering faith that no longer needed the dark to hide its light. 
Behind him, the utility poles hummed their electric hymn, and Elias understood that navigating by utility poles was not the act of a lost man, but of one who had stopped pretending the heavens were always visible. 
The backlash of regrets had not vanished; it had become ballast, steadying a boat that had once floated on hollow certainty.
