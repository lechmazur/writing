After the birds fly south, Marek returned to the palace courtyard where his grandmother had once shown him a secret tea house. 
He was a creeping normality noticer, and he had watched the city lose the bakery in increments so small that no one sounded an alarm. 
The shop had not burned; it had faded from bright to dim to gone, leaving only a round loaf in Marek's coat pocket. 
That bread from the vanished bakery had hardened into a stone he could neither eat nor discard. 
Marek carried it to the overgrown palace each gray morning, hoping to find the sacred in the ordinary. 
The secret tea house in an overgrown palace courtyard had been swallowed by wisteria and winter rain. 
The courtyard had become a tangle of rogue tomatoes, self-seeded plum trees, and broken tile, so Marek began to work through guerrilla gardening. 
He carried compost in a wheelbarrow, pruned dead growth, and left seed bombs along the fence, trusting neighbors to tend what sprouted. 
His approach was heuristically gentle; he read each plant's wishes from the angle of its leaves and each neighbor's from their hesitation at the gate. 
Slowly the garden uncloaked paths that had been buried under ivy, and the paths led to the tea house's tarpaulined ribs. 
One frozen dawn, after the birds fly south, three neighbors helped Marek lift the canvas and uncloak the small room beneath. 
Inside, a soot-stained kettle still hung above a stone hearth, and a wooden sign read, "For the tired." 
Marek set the bread from the vanished bakery on the warped table as if it were a relic. 
He had carried it for two years, unable to eat it or throw it away. 
The loaf was his indictment and his promise: proof that he had loved something after it was too late to save it. 
When the old baker's daughter climbed through the broken gate, Marek felt the tenuous absolution he had come for slipping away. 
She looked at the stale loaf, then at the tomatoes fruiting in the palace cracks, and laughed without cruelty. 
"My father closed the bakery because he was tired, not because you failed to notice," she said. 
The bank wanted the lease, and he wanted mornings in the garden. 
Marek tried to apologize, but she raised a hand and touched a sage plant still green after frost. 
"You undo what smothers things," she said. 
"That is the only absolution the city offers." 
Her words did not erase his grief, but they loosened its knots, and that was enough to keep him working. 
They swept the hearth, filled the kettle, and brewed tea from lemon balm harvested in the courtyard's abandoned beds. 
Marek broke the bread at last, crumbling it for the birds that would return in spring, and the crust fell like brown earth. 
He realized he had not been feeding the past; he had been feeding the ground that refused to die. 
The neighbors named the room The Second Oven, not for the bakery, but for the heat of people gathering after loss. 
The secret tea house in an overgrown palace courtyard opened whenever someone brought milk, sugar, or silence. 
Marek kept noticing slow things: rust bleeding down a drainpipe, moss erasing a step, a child outgrowing the fence. 
He no longer believed attention could prevent every disappearance, but he believed it could sanctify what remained. 
The garden taught him that mercy is not a verdict; it is a habit practiced with dirty hands. 
He was heuristically gentle with the ruins, with the newcomers, and with his own younger certainty. 
In the lean months after the birds fly south, the tea house became a chapel of steam and crumbled bread. 
People came not for grand miracles but for the sacred in the ordinary: a warm cup, a bean sprout through tile, a chair that did not break. 
Marek stopped calling himself a failure of noticing and began calling himself a keeper of slow returns. 
When the first green shoots pushed through the palace gravel, he understood that tenuous absolution is a seed, not a door. 
It does not close the past; it grows forward, season by season, through the hands that choose to plant. 
He set out cups on the stone bench and waited, quietly, for the ordinary world to become enough.
