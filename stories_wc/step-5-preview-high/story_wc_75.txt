The grotto city of Aldervale breathed behind a monumental waterfall whose roar never ceased.  Mist gathered on the pale roses that grew along the terraces, and their scent was steeped into rosewater that masked the city's deeper damp.  Ilse, a cultured museum guide with a historically oriented mind, walked these terraces each dawn, cataloguing the relics that the displaced persons had carried through the falls generations ago.  They had lost their homelands to war and frost, and now they preserved dying arts with the desperate care of people who believed memory itself might starve.  Among the collection was an emerald-tinted opera glass lens, its rim of tarnished silver still sharp enough to cut a thumb.  Ilse had always suspected the lens was more than a gentleman's curiosity.  Since the beekeeper's last visit, when his final hive was hauled up the rope ladders to the outer world, the city's sourdough starters had begun to fail.  Without his bees to carry pollen from the cliff flowers, the family custom of baking bread shaped like initials had grown heavy and gray.  Each loaf was meant to bear the first letter of a dead ancestor or a lost province, a daily edible memorial that the displaced refused to abandon.  Now the bakers wept over their ovens because the dough would not rise.  Ilse climbed to the museum archives, determined to rescue the craft before it vanished entirely.  She laid the emerald-tinted lens upon a map stitched into a founder's ledger, and through its green refraction, faded ink blossomed into a diagram.  The diagram showed an ice cellar beneath the falls, sealed since the first exiles arrived.  There, she read, the original yeast cultures had been preserved by frost, waiting for a thaw that no one living had dared attempt.  The ledger warned that the cellar breathed with rosewater, a fragrance that meant both preservation and decay.  Ilse felt the rosewater dread settle in her chest, sweet and suffocating.  She gathered her coat and the lens and descended through the grotto's narrow stairways.  Behind the last curtain of water, the air was crystalline and cold.  The ice cellar opened like a ribcage of blue stone, and inside, frozen loaves shaped like unfamiliar letters stood in silent rows.  These were the true initials of the displaced, baked before the city had a name.  Ilse knelt and held the emerald-tinted lens to the torchlight, watching the beam concentrate into a warm green point.  She applied that point to the oldest loaf, and a slow thaw began, hissing as ancient frost surrendered.  The scent that rose was not merely rosewater but the ghost of every bakery the exiles had fled.  She worked for hours, thawing culture after culture, her gloves soaked in meltwater that smelled of gardens no one would see again.  When she returned to the upper city, the bakers received the revived starters with trembling hands.  Within days, bread shaped like initials rose golden and fragrant, each letter a small defiance against oblivion.  The displaced persons gathered in the museum square, breaking loaves whose crusts spelled hope and mourning in equal measure.  Ilse mounted the emerald-tinted lens in a new display, not as a curiosity but as the key that had unlocked survival.  Yet she kept a fragment of the thawed ice in a vial beside her desk.  The rosewater dread lingered because she understood that the cellar had not been a pantry but a heartbeat.  The arts were dying not for lack of flour or fire, but because the city itself was slowly thawing from its long grief.  Still, each morning she guided visitors past the lens, teaching them that to preserve an art is to keep a people from dissolving.
