The antique desk seal had been wedged beneath a loose drawer in my new apartment, its brass handle cold as river stone and its face engraved with a spiral that hurt my eyes if I stared too long. 
I had moved into the building because the bricks had spoken to me, or so I told the landlord, who only smiled, lowered the rent, and refused to explain why the previous tenant had left in such a hurry. 
He said her name was Ms. Corvin, a dead language tutor who had spent her life teaching the dead tongues of antiquity, and who had died quietly between semesters in the apartment directly above mine. 
Her unit had been cleared, but the rooftop garden remained exactly as she had left it, and on the night the Perseids blazed overhead, I climbed the iron stairs to see if the falling stars could teach me what the crumbling mortar already seemed to know. 
A shadow moved among the tomato cages, and I saw patiently nimble fingers tending a copper kettle that should not have been boiling on a cold night. 
I asked if she were real, and Ms. Corvin laughed with the mysterious familiarity of someone who had been waiting a very long time for me to climb those stairs. 
She said the building did not speak in ordinary words but in pressures, temperatures, and settling dust, and if I truly wished to learn the language of old buildings, I would need to interrogate every creaking joint, every swelling beam, every root that cracked the cellar stones. 
My sugar had run out that morning, so I borrowed a dented cupful from her rusted tin, a small debt that turned into weekly tea sessions beneath the meteor shower. 
Each week we sat in the rooftop garden as streaks of light bled across the sky, and she taught me to read the Latin etched into the foundation, the Greek hidden in the cornice, the scratches in the pantry door that predated English. 
The seal, she explained, had once stamped official ledgers in a tongue no living mouth could pronounce, and it retained the weight of every contract, confession, and curse it had ever closed. 
I turned it over and over, feeling the spiral catch the light like a slow eye that measured my patience. 
To survive the lessons, we constructed agreed upon lies. 
I pretended her hands were warm, and she pretended my tea did not taste of ash and iron. 
We agreed the building was merely old, not hungry, and that the voices in the walls were autumn drafts, not the confessions of tenants who had never moved out. 
But the lies frayed the night I tried to use the seal to press my own questions into wax. 
The wax hissed, the garden tilted, and the rooftop fence became a lectern of black oak that groaned under the gravity of forgotten syllables. 
Ms. Corvin's eyes went hollow, yet her voice stayed kind as she warned me that old buildings only answered those who had something precious to surrender. 
I asked what the walls wanted, and she told me to ask again in Sumerian, in Hittite, in the dead grammar of joints, keystones, and rainspouts. 
So I interrogated the brickwork with her patiently nimble guidance, pressing the antique desk seal into softened wax until the building recognized me as a student rather than an intruder. 
The meteor shower peaked above us, each falling star a syllable in the long sentence of the sky, and I felt the floorboards answer beneath my feet. 
We wrote our conclusions in wax, sealed them with the brass stamp, and hid them where the next resident would find them, continuing the agreed upon lies that made habitation possible. 
Ms. Corvin faded at dawn, not dead but departed, leaving the kettle warm and the sugar tin half full. 
I still climb to the rooftop garden during the meteor shower, borrowing sugar from the empty tin, brewing tea for two, and speaking aloud the language of old buildings until the mortar answers in kind.
