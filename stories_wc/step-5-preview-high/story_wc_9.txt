The birth had left Marrow hollowed and glowing, a new mother whose hours slipped like water through the cracks of her fatigue.  She was an onyx-ink cartomancer, skilled at reading the black rivers that flowed from her stained fingertips.  Since the birth, she had begun to lose time, not minutes but whole afternoons that vanished while she rocked the crib.  The loss was a silent theft, and she resolved to find where lost time accumulates before the thief took her self.  So she climbed the cloudstairs at midnight toward the glade that hovered above the world.  The midnight glade above clouds was a suspended cathedral of grass and starlight where the air tasted of iron.  It was a place of choking beauty, so lovely it squeezed the breath from her lungs and left her gasping.  In her pack she carried the dolls' tea set her mother had given her, its porcelain saucer split by a hairline crack.  She had come to perform cloudbound repentance, the rite her order required when life grew tangled with absence.  On the glade's flat altar stone she spread a linen cloth, part of a family custom of tracing hands on birthday tablecloths.  She traced her own hand, then the tiny hand of her infant daughter, the outlines darkening as if hungry.  Marrow was willingly perplexed by the way the ink refused to dry, pooling into runnels that defied simple meaning.  She had learned that certainty was a blade, and confusion a shield that kept the heart searching.  The cartomancer laid her cards upon the cloth, but they too were lost in the spreading darkness.  The saucer crack caught her eye, its jagged line mirroring a fracture in the ink.  When she pressed the saucer to the cloth, the crack aligned with the gap between her hand and her daughter's.  It was there, she realized, that the stolen hours had gathered, thick as sap in the negative space between touch.  To reach it, she had to unfasten the cord that bound the saucer to the tea set's tiny handle.  The crack widened with a sigh, releasing a thread of silver mist that curled into the shapes of lullabies.  The cloudbound repentance demanded she speak her loss aloud, so she whispered every hour she had missed.  She confessed the fogged mornings and drowsy afternoons, the way the birth had blurred the edges of her purpose.  The glade absorbed her words, and the choking beauty deepened, pressing the sweetness of regret against her ribs.  Yet she remained willingly perplexed, refusing to name the shape of the mist, knowing names could cage the truth.  The silver thread pooled above the saucer, condensing into droplets that fell onto the traced hands.  Where the drops landed, the ink shifted, revealing maps of rooms she had paced and chairs she had rocked.  The lost time had not vanished; it had accumulated in the sacred geometry of care.  She saw that her family custom had been a ledger all along, measuring love by the spaces between hands.  The dolls' tea set saucer crack was the flaw that let the time back into the world.  Marrow wept, not from sorrow but from the pressure of so much beauty that she could barely breathe.  She gathered the tablecloth, now bearing wet luminous outlines, and began her descent from the clouds.  She carried the saucer in her pocket, its crack warm against her hip, a reminder that broken things can pour.  The cartomancer understood now that she need not hoard the hours that motherhood consumed.  They had gone to the glade to ripen, and she could drink them back in the quiet moments between feeds.  At dawn, she traced her daughter's hand on a fresh cloth, though no birthday had yet arrived.  The ink dried clean that time, and the saucer remained whole, its purpose fulfilled in the midnight above.  She kept it close, and when visitors asked about the crack, she smiled.  She told them it was where lost time accumulates, not as a thief, but as a garden grown in the dark.  The choking beauty of that night remained with her, a fragrance of clouds and milk and black ink.  She was no longer afraid of empty afternoons, for she knew where they went and that they returned.  Every year, when the birthday candles guttered, the family traced hands on the cloth with solemn joy.  They never sealed the saucer again, but left it cracked upon the cloth as a gateway for time to pour.  It was their custom, their repentance, their cloudbound vow to let beauty overflow until it almost choked.  In that breath, between the unfastened past and unwritten future, she found her home.
