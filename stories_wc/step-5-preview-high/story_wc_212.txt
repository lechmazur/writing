The egg had hatched at moonrise, and the icebound atrium under starlight had begun to sing. 
Maren stepped through the broken door with an oak acorn cap in her pocket. 
She was the heirloom seed saver of the Cinder Atrium, sworn to keep its vault of forgotten harvests breathing. 
Her family had called their legacy a vortex inheritance, though it was no storm to ride. 
It was a spiral of duty, narrowing toward the order hidden beneath chaos. 
The newly hatched thing was no larger than a sparrow, with eyes like wet coals. 
It had cracked the frozen chronometer at the heart of the atrium and then gone still. 
Maren felt furiously tranquil, her breath slow while her mind raced through seven generations of notes. 
The vault would not open for force, only for a reversal. 
She had to reopen the sealed seed hall before the frost finished its slow feast. 
Her method was forbidden and precise: she would decrease entropy. 
Scattered warmth had leaked from the world for decades, and she intended to gather it back into patterns. 
She set the oak acorn cap upon the chronometer's frozen face, cupping it like a listening shell. 
The hatchling tilted its head and breathed once, and the cap began to turn. 
Frost drew inward, spiraling in a low, patient vortex around the rim. 
Maren had inherited this motion in her bones, a family trick of unspilling what time had knocked over. 
She did not merely want to save the seeds; she needed to discover the texture of borrowed time. 
Every elder had spoken of it, but none had returned from the winter with words that satisfied her. 
Was borrowed time smooth as river glass, or serrated with the mistakes of the dead? 
The cap turned faster, and the air above the chronometer thinned into a hush. 
Starlight poured through the cracked dome and lay across the ice in bands of blue fire. 
Maren held her ferocious calm in both hands and fed it into the spiral. 
Memories she had never lived flickered past her: planting in drought, singing to sprouts, refusing a king's grain. 
They were not ghosts but heat returning to its proper work. 
Entropy is only confusion, she thought, and confusion can be combed. 
Under the turning cap, melted water rose into beads and hung like ripe fruit above the frozen dial. 
The oak acorn cap trembled, then locked against a notch older than language. 
The sealed seed hall began to reopen with a sound like a long exhale. 
Ranks of glass jars emerged from the ice, each holding dust that had once been a summer. 
Maren walked down the aisle, wild serenity rising in her chest like weather over a plain. 
Then she felt it, the texture she had chased through every borrowed hour. 
Borrowed time was not smooth and not sharp; it was fibrous, stubborn, full of small knots where other hands had held on. 
It had the grain of an acorn's own cradle and the chill of a star that would not be rushed. 
She understood, finally, that the vortex inheritance had never been about hoarding the past. 
It was about lending forward with both hands open. 
The hatchling hopped onto her boot and chirped, and the atrium's frost retreated from the door in a green wave. 
Maren tucked the oak acorn cap into her pocket and began the long work of sorting seeds by starlight. 
Outside the glass, the frozen world waited, but the air inside carried the smell of thaw and risk. 
She did not promise the garden would return by dawn. 
She promised only to keep the turn steady, one seed and one unwound minute at a time. 
Above her, the stars wheeled in their old wild serenity, and she worked on without hurry. 
In the quiet, a first root pushed through ice and remembered its name.
