The tidal library above clouds did not open its doors until the third moon of the season. 
Elara pressed her palm against the atomically reflective glass of the entry vault, watching her reflection scatter into a thousand ghostly copies that rearranged themselves into creatures she had outlived. 
She was a contemporary of extinct species, the last human voice that still remembered the roars of the thunder-lizards and the songs of the sky-whales. 
After the reincarnation memory had unlocked in her chest during the last equinox, isolation had become a physical weight, pressing behind her sternum each morning. 
She climbed the spiral stair to the astronomy mezzanine, where the brass sundial sat upon its marble pedestal. 
Its face was atomically reflective, a polished disk that caught starlight even when no stars were visible above the perpetual cloud sea. 
Elara ran her thumb along its rim, feeling the familiar tick of heat that marked it as more than mere metal. 
The library had been built around the ancient stone circle in its lowest tidal chamber, a ring of standing stones that hummed when the high tides of cloud-water rushed beneath the foundations. 
That hum was the only method by which the dead could be reached, and by which the living could coexist with them without drowning. 
For three centuries, she had refused to descend. 
But brittle awakenings had begun to crack across the city of her sleep, hairline fractures that forced her to remember what she had been avoiding. 
The extinction had not been a sudden wall but a slow erosion, and she had remained, the final contemporary, cataloging their bones while pretending she was content among paper and silence. 
Now, in the aftermath of the reincarnation memory, she understood that her loneliness was not a sentence handed down by fate but a language she had forgotten how to translate. 
Elara lifted the brass sundial from its pedestal and felt the hum rise through the metal, a resonance that matched the thrumming in her own rebuilt bones. 
She descended through the vertical stacks, where books were shelved in tidal columns and volumes floated upward on currents of humid air. 
At the base of the tower, the ancient stone circle waited in a basin of saltwater that pulsed with the rhythm of distant oceans. 
The stones were worn smooth by the passage of species no longer living, and their collective hum vibrated in the marrow of anyone willing to listen. 
Elara stepped into the center, clutching the sundial. 
Instantly, the atomically reflective surface flared, catching the sound-waves of the stone circle and bending them into visible light. 
Shapes emerged around her, translucent and trembling: the thunder-lizards, the sky-whales, the hummingbird-moths that had once darkened the dusk. 
They did not attack or mourn; they simply were, held in the amber of the hum. 
Elara realized that to coexist did not mean to join them in death, nor to wall them up in grief. 
It meant to stand within the same current, acknowledging their silence while sustaining her own pulse. 
The brass sundial grew warm, its reflective atoms multiplying the circle's song until the water in the basin began to rise in a slow spiral. 
She understood then that the brittle awakenings were not meant to harden into permanent wounds. 
They were openings, fragile and luminous, through which a single survivor could grow sturdy enough to stand alone. 
Elara closed her eyes and let the extinct species surround her, not as ghosts of her past lives, but as witnesses to her present choice. 
She would remain in the tidal library above clouds, but she would no longer be its prisoner. 
The hum settled into her heartbeat, steady and independent, a private music that belonged to no era and no extinction.
