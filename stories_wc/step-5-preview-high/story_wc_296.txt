The drowned orchard had stopped screaming by the time Maren arrived, and that silence was the worst kind of warning.  The swirling orchard vortex at the heart of a cursed lake turned apple, pear, and blackthorn trees in slow rings, their roots clawing upward like drowning men.  She stood on the shore, a guarded druid who had spent thirty years refusing to ask questions about the things she recovered.  Lost coins, runaway hounds, and wayward children had found their way back to her camp, yet no mirror had ever shown her why.  After the magic trick failed, the court's last illusionist fled, leaving only a scorched circle and a debt.  He had promised to reveal a hidden king through a polished glass, but the glass had cracked and the reflection had refused to move.  Maren had come to discover what mirrors don't show, not to comfort the grieving or to settle the court's panic.  She stepped onto the frozen marge, where the ice hummed with a warlike serenity, calm as a drawn bow.  The orchard vortex opened before her, expanding circles of churned water and bark circling inward toward a heart of black light.  She waded in, and the lake accepted her weight as though it had been waiting for a soldier rather than a witness.  At the second ring, the water became a corridor of turning trees, and she saw the seed lodged in a knuckle of root.  It was a flesh-eating plant seed, pale and folded, the size of a sparrow's heart, wrapped in a stranger's blood.  Lost things called to her by weight, and this one pulled at her palm like an anchor asking to be lifted.  She took it, and the orchard groaned, branches bending toward her with the patience of an army that knows its orders.  At the fourth ring the water thickened, and the frozen confession hung in the ice above her, suspended in a sheet of unmelting rime.  Words were locked inside the frost, a sentence that would not fall, spoken by the lake's drowned choir and never released.  Maren read the shapes through the glaze, and her guarded heart tightened because the confession named the mirror that had refused to move.  It had not failed; it had been forbidden to show a face that the orchard had swallowed.  The trick had failed because truth had been frozen here, bent out of shape by the curse that kept the vortex spinning.  She understood then that to free the words she would have to unbend the orchard itself, not cut it, not burn it, but straighten the warp.  The seed in her hand was the instrument, for its hunger could eat the knotted grief that held the confession rigid.  Kneeling on a root that circled like a bridge, she pressed the flesh-eating plant seed into a cleft where the bark had grown crooked.  Her voice was low, warlike in its peace, an instruction rather than a plea.  Open through expanding circles, she said, and the seed split.  From it grew a spiral of tendrils, each ring wider than the last, eating the blight in measured rings.  The orchard shuddered, but the tendrils did not rage; they consumed the knot of the curse in perfect order.  With every circle the trees slowed, and the frozen confession began to drip, not as water but as syllables.  Maren listened as the frozen confession confessed that the hidden king had been drowned by his own reflection.  The mirror had not refused to move; it had been turned away so that no one could accuse the water of theft.  As the last tendril reached the black heart, the seed's flower opened and devoured the rime itself.  The frozen confession finished, the vortex unbent, and the orchard settled into a still and terrible quiet.  The trees no longer turned, but their roots remained raised, like spears planted in a field after battle.  Maren walked out through the widening rings, no longer merely a finder of lost things but a keeper of what they had cost.  She carried the truth to the court, where mirrors were veiled that winter, and she never spoke of the seed.  Yet the orchard stayed, and in its center a single white flower opened each dawn, hungry and serene.  She had come for what mirrors don't show, and she left with a quiet war inside her chest.  Some confessions, she knew, were not meant to be mirrored, only carried.  And so the druid remained guarded, not because she feared the world, but because she had finally seen it.
