The shadowed library at dawn held its breath, as if the dark had not yet decided to leave. 
After the last dictionary was printed, the city had declared language complete and closed the printers' halls. 
In the reference hall, Rhea had not spoken aloud since the binding ceremony, and people called her the reticent scholar. 
She swept dust into constellations and listened to the rafters because listening was safer than speech. 
The final dictionary filled the lower shelves, sealed in cloth, its pages listing every word that would ever be allowed. 
Above the reading table hung a chandelier of false stars, one prism already missing. 
Rhea had found the fallen crystal from the chandelier three winters ago and kept it wrapped in linen. 
She believed it had been cut to catch not only light but sound. 
Every morning she carried it to the long table at dawn, when the glass walls still held the night. 
The room kept a suspended hush, not empty but waiting, as though the building itself was holding a note it refused to release. 
Rhea had come to the library to decode the pattern of growth. 
A vine had crept through the broken clerestory window and wrapped the card catalog in slow green script. 
Its leaves did not sprawl without reason. 
They climbed by angles that matched the spiral of pressed ferns in a botany text, then split into pairs, then pairs again. 
Rhea traced the stems with the crystal, and the stone proved sonically reflective. 
It gave back whispers from the walls: footsteps, water in pipes, a printer's final hammer, the rustle of the vine itself. 
The crystal fractured those echoes into sequences, brief broken memories that arrived out of order. 
In one fragment she saw herself as a child in this same room, naming seedlings before the lexicographers came. 
In another she heard her mother say that a word is a seed and a dictionary is only a fence. 
The fragments overlapped until Rhea understood the growth pattern. 
The vine was not invading the library; it was writing. 
Its stems followed the stress and spacing of the last spoken sentence ever printed, and every new leaf extended a word that had been closed. 
Because words had been locked in books, life had begun to speak in another script. 
That morning two clerks arrived from the Ministry and found her at the table. 
They carried an order to burn the vine, because growth without a printed name frightened them. 
They expected Rhea to sign as a witness, but she had learned to testify through fractured sequences. 
She set the crystal on the order, and the paper trembled under its reflections. 
At first no sound filled the room, only a suspended hush more absolute than silence. 
Then the crystal began to speak with the voices it had collected: a printer swearing over a jammed type, a gardener humming, a child asking why no more words would be added. 
The clerks stepped back, for the evidence was not in documents but in growth. 
Rhea's voice came out rough, unused, but it did not waver. 
She said that the dictionary was complete only for the dead. 
She said the vine had extended the margins of meaning, and every leaf was a syllable the city had forgotten it needed. 
Her testimony was not loud, but it held a weathered innocence, the stubborn gentleness of someone who had been disappointed and still chose to hope. 
The older clerk looked at the vine and saw not a threat but a library of leaves he had never learned to read. 
He set the burning order beside the crystal and let the vine keep its place among the books. 
At dawn the suspended hush finally eased, and Rhea, the reticent scholar, listened to the world grow.
