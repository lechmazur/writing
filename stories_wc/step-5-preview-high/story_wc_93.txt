The tidal bluff had grown slick and treacherous after friction disappeared, leaving the herder to cling to basalt by will alone.  He was the elusive eel herder, a figure the coastal villagers spoke of only in hushed tones because seeing him was said to invite a fate not yet chosen.  His curse was precognitive, each dawn arriving before its light, each sorrow rehearsed in the dark behind his eyes.  For years he had avoided the village below, for his glimpses of their futures had curdled into silence, and his presence made mothers clutch their children close.  He could harness nothing of his own future, yet he could channel the slippery currents that now carried silver eels down from the glacial rivers.  His method was exact and strange: placing painted rocks in mathematical sequences along the bluff's wet lip, each stone a variable in an equation older than the sea.  The paint was mixed with collapsed star dust, a heavy grit that refused to float, drawn from a supernova's cooled remnant and ground until it shone like bruised embers.  Only this dust gave the stones enough teeth to hold their places against the glassy surf.  Each painted rock held a number, a hue, and a silence that the eels read as law.  He had learned that the eels swam the same paths the stars once burned, and if he could trace those motions correctly, he might finally understand the sacred patterns woven into every tide and current.  But the promise lingered, a debt of breath and memory owed to the first herder, who had whispered that the day the patterns completed would be the day the world regained its grip.  The herder had seen that completion in visions that tasted of salt and iron; he knew the exact sunrise when the last stone would be set.  He also saw the waves that would take him before he could step back from the line of painted stones.  He had wept for that sight once, but grief had no traction in a frictionless age.  This was the cruelty of his attribute: he worked not toward hope but toward certainty, every act of creation already mourned in advance.  On the night the aurora hung low and green above the bluff, he carried the final rock in a satchel lined with meteor scales.  The sky did not brighten toward morning, for the sun was a rumor the aurora mocked, casting a borrowed dawn that belonged to no hour and no god.  He knelt and pressed the stone into the sequence, its painted face showing the ninth prime, its weight settling through the slick film of the vanished world.  The eels rose as one, their bodies aligning with the line of rocks, and the aurora pulsed in answer, a light that almost seemed solid.  The pattern completed itself in their wake, a proof written in muscle and silver.  He harnessed their motion, letting the collapsed star dust drink the aurora's charge and turn it into a lattice of force across the water.  For one held breath, friction returned; the stones ceased their sliding, and the sea remembered how to hold still.  He saw the promise complete, not in vision but in the sudden roughness of the world against his palm.  The village would never know his name, only that the tides had grown kind and the rocks stayed put.  Then the wave he had foreseen arrived, not cruel, simply punctual, and the herder walked into it without surprise, leaving only the painted rocks to mark where understanding had cost him everything, and the borrowed dawn faded with him into the sea.
