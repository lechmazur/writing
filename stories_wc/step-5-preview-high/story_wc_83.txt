The floating observatory hung above the drowned valley like a splinter of glass, tethered to nothing the refugees trusted.  Drone strike wedding survivor was not a profession, but it was the only title Sable answered to.  She had survived the ash and the music cut short in the courtyard, and now she kept the dead in ledgers.  Her job was to hunt where unused potential pooled, not in gold or grain, but in the impossible arithmetic of futures that had never arrived.  The council called it waste; Sable called it the bride she had almost become, the groom, the children never conceived, the songs the band had not yet learned.  Every month, the observatory dropped another geode crystal into the well of the turbine hall, where instruments could taste a possible world without swallowing it.  The crystal was intangibly tangible, heavy enough to strain a glove and impossible to hold once the lights came on.  Sable preferred it that way.  She worked through perturbation theory, which was the honest name for her method.  One does not simply open a system and ask where its missing energy hides; one nudges it, records how it shudders, and maps the shadows of the shudder.  She adjusted the mass of the geode by one microgram and watched the valley below fold into an extra reflection.  The reflection was not a mirror of water and broken buildings; it was a courtyard where the wedding guests were still standing, still breathing, still waiting for a song.  Sable's breath stopped, but her hand kept writing, because the instrument did not know mercy.  Then the main breaker surrendered with a crack like a fired mortar, and the observatory dropped forty centimeters before the stabilizers caught it.  While circuit breakers cooled, the hall filled with the tick of hot metal and the smell of rain.  Sable had five minutes, maybe eight, before the systems would let her hunt again.  She sat cross-legged beside the geode and let the perturbation echo move through her own pulse.  The unfiltered awakening did not arrive as light or wisdom; it arrived as every version of her life at once.  In one, she wore the white dress and died under a tablecloth dyed red by dust.  In another, she was old, and had argued with a husband whose face she could not remember because he had never existed.  The pool was not beneath the valley; it pooled inside the crystal, in the charge between atoms that had been denied a history.  She reached for it with the careful greed of a survivor and felt the unused potential take her fingers like cold water.  It was intangibly tangible, a mass of almost, pressing against her palm with the weight of a body that had chosen not to be born.  The bleak exhilaration rose in her throat, sweet and sulfurous, because the data had finally admitted what grief had always known.  The dead were not gone; they were banked, potential with nowhere to land, and she could point to their location on a chart.  A breaker clicked, hungry to be used again.  Sable did not report the pool to the council, not yet.  If they learned that unused potential could be harvested, they would burn it to keep their lights arrogant.  Instead she wrote a false ledger, marked the crystal spent, and hid the geode inside the lining of her coat, where it hung against her ribs like a second heart.  When the breakers cooled enough to allow the hunt to resume, she restarted the turbines and let the observatory climb.  She would return to the courtyard at dawn, not to mourn, but to plant a seed of perturbation in the ash.  The wedding dead would have their potential released into the lives still waiting to be chosen, and no drone would ever again decide who got a future.
