The tide knocked against the rocks like a debt collector who had finally found the right door, and Marina Wu counted the drops of condensation on her beer glass because numbers were the only fortunes she trusted. 
She wrote fortunes for the Golden Dragon on Harbor Street, where the owner paid her in rice and room and never asked why her slips of paper kept predicting deaths, foundlings, and fire. 
The islanders called it a gift, which was their open secret, and they left her coins on the counter while pretending not to notice her hands shaking over the ink. 
After last orders, when the pub door swung shut and the fog swallowed the last fishermen, she folded a napkin into a crane and stepped out into the salt wind. 
The mysterious lighthouse on Skull Rock had not guided ships in forty years, yet its beam still turned, patient as a metronome, and Marina had learned to read its rhythm. 
She climbed the iron stairs with the microdot film specs taped beneath her left breast, the spycraft so small it hid inside a period at the end of a recipe. 
The specs detailed a new cipher that would transpose every intercepted whisper into music, and the men who wanted it had no intention of changing the key. 
Marina had stolen them not for country or cause, but because the cipher was built from her own fortunes, her accidentally prophetic lines transcribed into enemy algorithms. 
At the top, the lamp room smelled of kerosene and old lemons, and a man in a naval coat stood with his back to the sea, waiting for the woman he knew only as the Cookie. 
"You are late," he said, and she realized he was right because she had lingered to direct the flow of drunks away from the path, guiding them home by the long route through the chapel. 
"I direct flow," she said. "Water, talk, you. All the same." 
He did not smile, but he nodded, and in the rotating light she saw the same familiar enigma she saw in her own mirror: a person half-known even to himself. 
The specs were not enough; the cipher needed a human key, and that key was a mind that could hold a secret in plain sight, an open secret the size of a fortune cookie. 
"Give me the film," he said, and she pressed her palm to her chest, feeling the paper ridge like a rib. 
"Not until you transpose my life into a new key," she said. "I am done being an oracle in a paper wrapper." 
He reached for his holster, but the beam swept between them, and in that flash she saw the trap, the open secret that the lighthouse itself was the drop site, not the exchange. 
She had orchestrated worse with a dinner table and a sharp pencil, arranging seating charts until enemies became lovers and informants vanished into fortune and fog. 
Now she needed to orchestrate the next three minutes by directing flow: the man, the tide, the beam. 
"Look down," she said, and he looked. 
The microdot specs were already gone, slipped into the pocket of his coat while he slept at the pub three hours ago, tucked inside a fortune that read: The moon remembers what the tide forgets. 
His face changed, not with anger but with the horror of a man discovering he has been the instrument all along, and she stepped back toward the stairs. 
"That is why you are accidentally prophetic," he said. "You never know which line becomes true, so the enemy cannot predict you." 
"And neither can I," she said. 
Below, the boat she had summoned with a single coded fortune to the harbormaster growled against the current, arriving exactly when she had promised, which surprised even her. 
The man lunged, but she was already descending, and she let the flow of the stairwell take her, weight shifting, palm on the rail, a body in a new key. 
On the shore, the harbormaster did not ask why the Cookie woman needed passage before dawn, because on this island open secrets were the only currency that never devalued. 
As the boat cut through the black water, Marina opened her hand and read the last fortune she had written that night, accidentally prophetic as ever: You will transpose silence into a song no one can decode. 
She laughed, and the lighthouse beam turned behind her, steady and mysterious and familiar, a riddle she had finally answered by leaving it behind. 
The cipher would fail without her mind to complete it, and somewhere in the fog a submarine commander would curse an open secret that had changed key mid-measure. 
Marina sailed for the mainland, smiling at the open secret of her escape.
