They called me the luminous skeptic, half as compliment and half as warning, because my skin still glowed faintly from the childhood dyes of a fever vaccine and I trusted instruments more than omens. 
I had come to the lavender bluff under aurora while forests hold elections, when the root parliaments opened their polling paths to anyone carrying a question and enough salt to bargain. 
My question had cost me seven years, three failed grants, and one marriage: to learn why some people carry weather with them. 
My mother had carried rain the way other women carried perfume. 
She entered rooms and left them humid; her laughter thinned the air, and her grief sat in the air like a waiting front. 
When she died, she left me the tin compass ring, a band of pitted metal that had stopped pointing north and trembled whenever a storm-carrier came near. 
I told myself it was faulty alloy and inherited sentiment, but I kept it. 
My method was equally stubborn: sensing through taste changes. 
Since a fever in my teens, I had read electric tension as copper, ozone as burnt lemon, and approaching snow as cold iron. 
On the bluff, the aurora was not merely overhead; it was terrestrially cosmic, green fire folded into the smell of wet lavender and old stone. 
The whole place had a futuristic nostalgia, as if tomorrow had been repaired with heirlooms and no one had thrown anything away. 
The forests were voting, and I could taste the season in resin, wet bark, and the faint metallic sap rising in the roots. 
Three other paths reached the clearing as the sky brightened, and the people on them seemed to converge with the pulse of the ring. 
A boy arrived whose breath would taste of hail. 
A woman in a pilot's coat carried thunder in her jaw. 
An old rootwarden leaned on a staff and exhaled the dry sweetness of distant dust. 
We were strangers, but our flavors braided in the cold air, and the tin needle of the ring began to spin. 
I tried to dismiss it as barometric coincidence. 
Then the needle stopped, not toward magnetic north, but toward a low dish of stone at the edge of the bluff. 
We dug until our fingers bled into the lavender and found a seed-case of woven fiber and gold leaf no larger than a plum. 
Inside were pollen grains stamped with electoral marks, each one dense as a planet. 
When I touched it, my mouth flooded with the exact taste of my mother's kitchen before rain: hot bread, iron, and bergamot. 
The woman with thunder laughed and said the forests had opened their voting to humans for the first time in a century. 
The old rootwarden disagreed. 
He said the forests had never stopped voting, only stopped waiting for people to listen. 
The seed-case, he explained, was an emergent compass: not built, but grown by the alignment of carriers, witnesses, and soil. 
It used our bodies as instruments and our senses as its bearings. 
The boy's hail, the woman's pressure, the warden's dust, and my copper doubt converged into a single direction. 
Some people carry weather, he said, because the land lends it to them and asks them to bring it back while forests hold elections. 
My mother had not been ill. 
She had been chosen to carry a climate too large for roots alone, to teach it patience, and to remember water for the trees. 
I, luminous and skeptical, had wanted a mechanism, and the land had answered with one. 
The tin compass ring had never been broken; it had been waiting for four tongues to taste the same change. 
As we sat on the bluff while the forests finished their tally, the aurora flickered and the air tasted of lilac and ozone. 
I did not stop doubting. 
I recorded the taste, the direction, the seed-case, and the storm each of us carried like a lantern. 
Some inheritances are not objects, my mother used to say; they are climates you agree to carry until the election is won. 
The forests would ask again, and next time I would not need a ring to know which way to turn.
