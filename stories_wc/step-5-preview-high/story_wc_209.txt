Mira had been called a vivid mural artist since the summer she painted a field of poppies across the town reservoir wall. 
Critics praised her colors, but she remained quietly driven by a question no one could paint. 
She wanted to understand why flowers bloom at night. 
Across the melting of winter's last snow, she followed rumors from thawing towns toward a valley of old glass. 
At the amber pavilion in autumn, she found a long glass house left behind by a botanist who had loved shadows. 
Its benches held a collection of old postcards, each one curled like a dried petal. 
The postcards showed moonflowers, evening primrose, and a cathedral whose window had been photographed in every kind of weather. 
On their backs the botanist had traced each bloom to a particular hour, a particular moon, and a particular kind of waiting. 
One card held only a single line: ask the glass. 
The cathedral still stood beyond the river, empty and locked since the flood. 
Mira went there with her sketchbook and a permit, then climbed into the narrow space behind the altar. 
She had come to reckon with her old belief that beauty needed daylight to be real. 
For years she had made flowers enormous and unmissable, as if she could shame the night into color. 
The work had left her exhausted, and the more vivid her murals became, the less she saw. 
Now she studied the cathedral window, whose upper panels were veiled with grime and lead. 
The glass appeared ordinary until rain began to move across it. 
In the slipping light, faint lines emerged beneath the surface, layer under layer, like handwriting sealed in amber. 
Mira realized she was looking through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window. 
The etching was almost invisible by day, but at dusk it gathered a gray shine and began to speak. 
She cleaned a corner with a soft cloth and found the first hidden mark, the long throat of a moonflower cut by a steady hand. 
Beneath it lay a second image, and beneath that a third, each one smaller and more delicate. 
The layers formed a calendar of opening and closing, not of hours but of shadows. 
Moths were drawn in the margins, their wings mapped to the petals they visited. 
A notation near the base read that night was not a hiding place but a slow room where certain flowers could unfold without hurry. 
Mira traced the lines with her fingertip and felt the same patience in the grooves. 
She saw then that the flowers did not bloom at night because they feared the sun. 
They bloomed because the dark held fewer demands, because their scent could travel farther in cool air, and because the moths came when the world grew quiet. 
There was no shame in it. 
The understanding did not arrive like thunder. 
It came as a dawning hush, a soft clearing in her chest that let her set down the need to be witnessed. 
She returned to the amber pavilion with the postcards spread across a long table. 
The snowmelt had swollen the river and then subsided, leaving the valley brown and tender. 
Mira mixed a new palette of indigo, ash, rust, and a pale green like stem light. 
She painted the night blooms as they actually were, half closed, unhurried, their petals turned toward the moths rather than the viewer. 
She gave each flower room, as though the wall itself were the slow room the glass had described. 
The amber light through the pavilion roof warmed the darker colors without erasing them. 
Visitors came and asked why the murals did not glow. 
Mira told them that some beauty opens only when the eye stops demanding more. 
She no longer tried to reckon her worth by how loudly her work could hold a room. 
Instead she reckoned with the quiet, and found it full of movement. 
On the last evening she set her brushes beside a bowl of evening primrose and waited. 
The flowers opened slowly, one white fold at a time. 
The last snowmelt had drained from the high fields, but the cool air still remembered winter. 
Mira sat in the amber pavilion in autumn and let the umbral tenderness of the hour settle over her. 
She had wanted to understand why flowers bloom at night. 
Now she knew they bloomed because the dark asked nothing of them, and she could finally do the same. 
In the dawning hush, she covered her brushes and left the flowers to close.
