During the unlearning, the world had begun to forget its own grammar.  The whispering grotto in mist breathed like slow milk, and the lilac unease of that season clung to every stone.  Pomeline, a whimsical chalk artist who drew blooming whales on alley bricks, entered the grotto with her pockets full of pigment dust.  She was socially perceptive enough to read the way travelers now clutched their mouths, as if language were a coin they feared to spend.  The unlearning had started quietly, first with names, then with recipes, then with the promises that held neighborhoods together.  What remained was an emergent hush, not the silence of empty rooms but the silence of crowded rooms where everyone had agreed to stop speaking.  Pomeline believed that if she listened closely enough, the hush would reveal a new alphabet.  In her left palm she carried a whispering gallery tile flake, a shard of blue ceramic she had pried from the ruined rotunda at Meridian Park.  Centuries ago, whispers had traveled around that dome with perfect clarity, but now the fragment only hummed when the fog pressed close.  She had come to the grotto because the acoustics were said to bend sound into spirals, and she needed to interject something into the growing quiet.  The grotto floor was already littered with the detritus of abandoned thought: torn letters, broken abacuses, and chalk outlines of people who had forgotten how to stand still.  Pomeline knelt, socially perceptive eyes tracing the patterns in a sand mandala that a previous visitor had half-completed before losing the will to finish.  Its concentric rings were asymmetrical, trembling with the same lilac unease that perfumed the air, and she understood at once that the maker had tried to map the hush.  To discover meaning in the mess, she would have to continue the design.  She uncorked her pigment jars and began to trace new lines through the patterns in a sand mandala, letting crushed lilac and gray quartz sift between her fingers.  The whispering grotto in mist accepted her work without judgment, its breath rearranging the sand into temporary galaxies.  As the mandala grew, Pomeline placed the whispering gallery tile flake at its center, where the circles converged.  She leaned close and whispered a word she had not spoken since childhood.  The flake caught the sound and flung it outward, so that her voice seemed to interject itself from every wall at once.  For a moment the emergent hush recoiled, trembling like a held breath, and then it answered.  It was not language, not exactly, but a pattern of pressure and pause that moved through the mandala like wind through wheat.  Pomeline, socially perceptive as she was, recognized the shape of that reply: an invitation to keep listening, to keep arranging the chaos into forms that could hold a voice.  She worked until the mist turned from lilac to deep violet, drawing chalk sigils on the grotto walls that mirrored the mandala below.  Each mark was an interjection against the erasure, a claim that some things could still be said even when words were fleeing.  When travelers stumbled into the grotto that evening, they found no written language, only a whimsical chalk artist asleep beside a sand mandala that seemed to hum.  The whispering gallery tile flake had gone dark, its charge spent, but the air around the mandala carried a fragile resonance.  One traveler, a former linguist who had forgotten her own thesis, knelt and pressed her ear to the sand.  She heard not sentences but the emergent hush itself, now organized, now willing to be shaped by patient hands.  The unlearning had not ended, yet the grotto had become a place where meaning could be rediscovered one grain at a time.  Pomeline woke and smiled, knowing she had found her answer in the mess.
