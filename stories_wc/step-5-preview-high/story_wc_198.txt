The morning fog saxophonist arrived before light, carrying an instrument older than the village that had banished him.  The radiant grotto in mist waited, its wet stone glowing with the last coal of yesterday's sun.  This was the hour as dewdrops form, when the world held its breath and the cave walls grew quiet.  He had returned to hush a prophecy spoken in code, one the grotto shaped from every drip and echo into a warning no one else could stop.  The old players called the remedy the vested hush, a charge passed down through musicians who understood that some songs must end before they were heard.  Only a morning fog saxophonist could hold that office now, and only because he had learned the fog more intimately than he had ever learned people.  He stepped into the prism atrium in mist, where panes of cloudy glass and crystal split the half-light into colors that were quietly vivid.  Each hue moved without heat, staining the air with memories of summers he had never been young enough to trust.  He thought of the old bandstand, of snowmelt, of the way applause could feel like rain on a tent, and the nostalgia made his fingers tremble.  The prophecy rose again, whispered by water along the grotto's ribbed ceiling, and the words were wrong in a language he almost knew.  He lifted the saxophone and breathed a low note, letting it hang among the columns until the mist bent toward it.  The vested hush demanded a terrible economy; he could not drown the code with sound, for every loud note gave it shape.  Instead he began to whisper between the phrases, threading a counter-code through negative space, through the gaps where the prophecy could not survive.  The words were old, plain, and merciless: sleep, hollow, keep, forget.  The mist swallowed the syllables before they could settle, and the atrium's prisms threw them back in colors no lantern had ever made.  He remembered the first teacher who had brought him here as a boy, the one whose breath smelled of pine tar and winter apples.  She had said the grotto was not a cave but an ear, and that the radiant stones listened for anything louder than truth.  He had laughed then, because boys laugh at what they fear, but the laughter had died when he saw the dewdrops forming on her motionless hands.  Now the same water gathered on the prisms, each drop a lens, each lens a patient eye, and the prophecy sharpened as the lenses multiplied.  He played softer still, letting the horn's voice become a suggestion rather than a sound, and in the pauses he whispered the silence that held the grotto.  That was the method the order had trusted: not to shout down a curse, but to erase the space it needed to speak.  The negative space was not emptiness; it was a room with no corners, a road with no end, a promise that no answer would come.  He felt the prophecy twist against him, furious and cold, trying to bind his name into its verses so he would have to finish them.  He gave it nothing but the negative space, and in that absence his own name became a stranger he no longer needed.  The grotto dimmed, and the mist withdrew from the walls like a tide leaving a shore it had loved too well to drown.  In the hush that followed, he could hear his own heartbeat keeping time with a song that had not been played in thirty years.  He lowered the saxophone and wiped the mouthpiece with a cloth that still smelled of pine tar, and he smiled without knowing why.  The prism atrium in mist stood empty and radiant behind him, quietly vivid even in ruin, as if the light had learned to grieve.  Outside, the village was waking, unaware that its morning had been bought with a silence no one would ever hear again.  He walked down the path through the fog, an old man in an old coat, carrying an instrument that had finally finished its song.  Behind him the dewdrops fell one by one, and the grotto kept the vested hush, as grottos do, until the next coded warning came.  But for now the mist was only mist, and the nostalgia in it was sweet, and the world went on speaking in voices it could understand.
