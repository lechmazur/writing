The nocturnal arbor breathed above the clouds, its vast boughs silvered by a moon that did not reach the earth.  Mira stood on the observation deck, a midnight pattern seeker whose eyes had adjusted to a world without sunlight.  For eleven years she had traced the wind currents that braided through the canopy, reading the invisible script that kept the floating city aloft.  Tonight the script was wrong.  Static had swallowed the radio for three hours, a grinding hiss that made her teeth ache, but now the channel lay silent and clear.  She pressed the transmit key and heard her own voice return as a soundless echo, flat and distant, as if the clouds were swallowing her words.  "Deck Six, this is Mira," she said.  "The resonance is climbing the western trunk."  The answer came instantly, sharp with fear.  "We see the lights flickering, but the engineers say it is only a cooling surge."  Mira closed her eyes, not in doubt, but in the familiar weight of the watcher's burden, the crushing knowledge that only she had seen the geometry of the coming shear.  If she cried wolf and they evacuated a thousand sleepers into the thin air, the trust she had spent years building would shatter.  To preserve trust under pressure, she had to be exact.  She was attentively inventive by nature, but invention alone would not save them; she needed proof.  Her fingers found the frayed edge of her flight harness, and she began to revise the night's drop coordinates by touch, remembering through muscle memory the exact tension of each strap and clasp.  Her hands knew the equipment better than her mind, a knowledge stored in tendon and joint rather than thought.  She had packed the chute herself, had rehearsed the buckle's release a thousand times until the motion was a reflex she could perform in absolute darkness.  That darkness was now literal; the power had failed on the west span, and only starlight pooled on the metal walkway.  Mira moved toward the launch cradle, feeling for the guide rope that ran along the bough like a vein.  The bark beneath her boots was cold and humming, a subsonic thrum that spoke of forces gathering above.  She reached the cradle and found the parachute buckle loose in her palm, brass gone green from salt air, the only piece of hardware strong enough to lock the release pin.  It was a relic from an old rig, incompatible by design, yet her attentively inventive eye saw what the engineers had missed: if she seated the buckle sideways, its curved lip would catch the vibrating pylon and dampen the resonance before it snapped the trunk.  She worked quickly, her fingers remembering through muscle memory the precise torque needed, though she had never performed this exact repair.  Below, the radio crackled with panic.  "Mira, the bough is groaning," the voice said.  "I know," she replied, her voice steady.  "Trust me and hold the cradle."  She jammed the buckle into place and felt the vibration change from a scream to a sigh.  The soundless echo of the wind died in her ears.  The canopy settled, and the clouds below parted to reveal the first gray breath of dawn.  Only then did Mira allow herself to tremble, the watcher's burden lightening just enough for her to breathe.  She stood alone in the nocturnal arbor, high above the sleeping world, her hands still curled around the parachute buckle.  They would ask her tomorrow how she had known.  She would revise her report, of course, making the logic clean and the timing precise, because a pattern seeker's credibility was a fragile instrument.  But the truth lived in her knuckles, in the muscle memory of brass and bark and braided wind, a silent testimony that no logbook could hold.  Above the clouds, the echo returned, soundless and sure, and she listened.
