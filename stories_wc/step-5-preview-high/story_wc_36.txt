After the guardianship ended, the driven relic keeper returned to the Thunder-echo range with a worn case strapped across her back. 
She had failed to weave between worlds when the seam collapsed, and she intended to harvest insights from failure instead of grieving it. 
The canyon hush settled over her like a held breath, and somewhere above the rim thunder rolled once before dying into stone. 
In the dry wash below, she began arranging driftwood as directional markers, angling each bleached piece toward the invisible seams she had once ignored. 
They were clumsy tools compared to the star-charts of her order, but they were honest, and honesty was what she lacked when she last tried to save her apprentices. 
She set the case on a slab of sunwarmed rock and paused, letting the silence deepen until it felt almost avalanchingly quiet. 
Then she began to uncase the gilded reed flute, unwinding silk that had not smelled of cedar since her teacher's death. 
The instrument had six fingerholes, a mouthpiece of pale bone, and a shine that seemed to borrow light from elsewhere. 
Her order had taught that weaving between worlds required a steady hand and a louder voice than fear, but her first attempt had ended with the world-thread snapping. 
The flute could open the seam only if the player matched the canyon's hidden rhythm, and she had tried to dominate that rhythm instead of listening to it. 
Now, after the guardianship, the order would send a new keeper unless she returned with proof that the range could be crossed without loss. 
She lifted the flute, felt its weight settle into her palms, and blew a single low note that drifted out over the driftwood markers. 
The canyon answered with a tremor that had no direction, and the dry wash filled with a canyon hush deeper than silence. 
She played again, and the air in front of her began to weave like a loom thrown open, showing a pale road under a bruised sky. 
It was the seam, and beyond it lay the world she had failed to reach, its edges frayed by her own impatience. 
She stepped forward, but the flute grew hot, and a vision rose of the day the thread broke and took four young keepers into the dark. 
In the memory, she had forced the seam, demanded passage, and treated the other world as a prize to be taken rather than a voice to be heard. 
The failure had not come from weakness but from refusal, and that understanding arrived with the avalanchingly quiet certainty of falling snow. 
She lowered the flute and sang instead, matching her breath to the slow pulse of stone, letting the seam open at its own pace. 
The pale road steadied, and the bruised sky softened into dawn as she walked across the threshold without touching the flute's power at all. 
On the other side, she found the remnants of her apprentices' last camp, their journals left open beside cairns of river stone. 
They had not died; they had been stranded in the pause between heartbeats, waiting for a keeper patient enough to follow their markers back. 
The driftwood she had arranged in the wash had pointed the way, and now she saw that each marker mirrored one they had left behind. 
She gathered the journals, retraced the path, and led the stranded keepers home through a silence that no longer felt like an accusation. 
When they emerged, thunder rolled again, but the canyon hush held them all in a gentler grip, as if the range itself had finally exhaled. 
She returned to the order not with conquest in hand but with a single lesson, that weaving between worlds begins with unmaking the urge to control them.
