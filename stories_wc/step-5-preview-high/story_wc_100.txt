The mysterious package arrived after the last bell had drowned, sliding through a rusted chute into the forgotten underwater city library as if the sea had finally decided to deliver its own mail. 
Elias Vane, the library's weary undertaker, carried it to a dry alcove that existed only because he had wedged it open with rusted bookshelves. 
For eleven years he had buried the drowned in impossible spaces, because the ocean refused to let them stay anywhere else. 
The package contained a broken lighthouse lens wrapped in oilcloth and lead, and Elias recognized it by the cracked starburst at its heart. 
Inside the oilcloth he found a map marked with seven rooms, each one a place where the drowned refused to drift, and a single word stamped beneath: revitalize. 
He was not a superstitious man, but he turned the lens until the fracture caught his lamp and threw a fan of distress signals across the vaulted ceiling. 
Each flash marked a gap in the water, a hollow where the flooded architecture had folded back on itself, and Elias felt the old restlessness of someone being watched. 
He had not asked to become the keeper of the drowned, but he had kept them anyway, and that inadvertent stubbornness had become his only virtue. 
Surface salvage crews called such pockets suicides; Elias called them rooms. 
He meant to give rest a permanent address, one shelf at a time. 
The lens was dead, he told himself, merely a reminder of the lighthouse that had guided ships onto the rocks. 
Still, he climbed with it through staircases that rose into salt and opened into lecture halls full of gray fish. 
The library occupied impossible spaces, and now the lens seemed to fit them, as though it had been ground to a different geometry. 
He had spent years trying to make that word true with wedges and ropes, but the ocean had undone every grave it found. 
He had learned to keep them by occupying impossible spaces, pockets where the sea could not push him out. 
To keep an impossible space, someone had to inhabit it, not as a corpse but as a fixed point, and Elias was the only fixed point left. 
He set the lens in the central reading room, where the dome had cracked open to the sea, and he stood beneath it on a pedestal of drowned encyclopedias. 
The first pulse went out as a question, three short, three long, three short, and the water answered with a trembling hush. 
The second pulse traveled down a corridor that should not have connected two towers, and a door of silt and coral unsealed itself. 
He ignored the knocking at the outer hatch, the tapping that had followed him since the mysterious package arrived, because he had learned that rescue sometimes sounded like a command. 
The lens did not burn bright; it bled its broken light into every crevice, and the forgotten machinery of the city began to revitalize. 
Pumps the size of cathedrals coughed in the walls, and the library inhaled a breath it had held for sixty years. 
The dead in the alcoves did not move, but they no longer rose, and Elias understood that he had anchored them by standing where they could not be reached. 
His knees ached, his hands were black with brine, and every muscle in him begged for a chair he did not own. 
He was too weary to argue with the silence, and too inadvertently stubborn to leave it unfinished. 
At the hatch, a voice demanded entry, speaking of contracts and unauthorized preservation, but Elias did not turn. 
He kept the lens moving in the old undertaker's pattern, three short, three long, three short, until the walls learned it. 
Doors of barnacle and bookboard sealed behind him, not to trap him, but to hold the stillness he had stolen from the sea. 
It was a chased stillness, the kind that arrives only when pursuit has stopped to listen. 
In the morning that no longer existed below the tide line, the library opened its dry shelves to the drowned like a final room. 
He gave each one a number, a name, and a shelf, so that no current could carry their memory beyond the catalogue. 
Elias would remain their undertaker, their witness, and the stubborn pulse that kept impossible spaces from collapsing.
