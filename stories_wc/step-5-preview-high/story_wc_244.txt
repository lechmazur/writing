When the story changes, it often begins with a train that no longer runs. 
I came to the disused railway station overgrown with ivy because I had run out of maps for my own life. 
I had come to find the cure for loneliness, though I expected only rust, pigeons, and a cold bench. 
What arrived instead was a woman with a clipboard, a tin badge, and a laugh that sounded desperately jocular. 
She introduced herself as Vera, the elusive disaster-relief logistician whom governments could not station and neighbors could not dismiss. 
Her arrival had been announced through improbable rumor chains, beginning with a tea seller and ending with the pigeons. 
"The bridge is down, the road is sulking, and I am here to catalyze a parade," she said, handing me a paper cup. 
I took the cup because refusing a gift in that place felt like breaking a law. 
Vera opened her satchel and showed me a fragment of moon rock wrapped in gauze. 
She called it collateral, a loan from a night that had once belonged to everyone. 
"We are bound by reciprocity," she explained, as if she were reading me the weather. 
"Nothing moves in a disaster unless something moves the other way." 
"You gave me a cup, so I owe you a seat; the town owes you a say." 
She asked me to carry the fragment to the old ticket gate and give it to the first person with empty hands. 
I obeyed because the ivy had already made anarchists of the benches and polite thieves of the sparrows. 
At the gate stood a boy with a lantern and a question. 
I gave him the rock and told him Vera wanted something in return, but not money. 
He said he could trade a secret: the old freight line was passable if nobody asked for tickets. 
The rumor moved again, politely, from the boy to a woman with flour on her sleeves. 
She took the fragment, weighed it like a debt, and gave us bread and a whistle. 
Then came the stationmaster's ghost, or perhaps merely a retired man named Otto who refused to be arrested by routine. 
He knew every scheduled train and no actual ones, which made him perfect for Vera's plans. 
Under her direction the station became a disaster-relief depot disguised as a street party. 
People arrived with bandages, soup, a tractor, and apologies for arriving empty-handed. 
Vera kept count in a ledger whose columns were gifts given and gifts promised. 
She joked that she had never found a catastrophe that could not be calmed by accurate paperwork and one honest cake. 
Her smile was desperately jocular, a bright scaffold built over exhaustion. 
It was the kind of jocularity that dared the room to give up before she did. 
The trucks could not pass, but the rumor could, so supplies moved along the tracks in a long courteous riot. 
That was the polite anarchy of the evening: everyone broke the rules, but everyone said excuse me. 
A rumor from a librarian reached the hospital; a rumor from the hospital reached the mayor's wife. 
The mayor's wife arrived with keys, a permit, and a reluctant order to ignore both. 
The freight line opened, not by authority, but by the pressure of a hundred small debts. 
Vera asked for the fragment again, and the boy returned it without being asked twice. 
She placed it on the dispatcher's scale, where it weighed exactly as much as the promise of a return. 
"The rock did not save you," she told the crowd. 
"It only gave you a reason to notice that you need each other." 
Then she turned to me, and the noise around us seemed to step aside. 
"You came looking for a cure for loneliness," she said. 
"Every relief worker does." 
"I organize relief for floods and fires because attention is a supply chain, and I am terrified of running out." 
I asked her what happened to the moon rock, and she said it had been sold to a museum for the price of one good meal. 
"The curator paid the town in light bulbs," Vera added, "which is how a rock learns to be useful." 
As she left, she gave the ledger to me and asked only that I keep the columns moving. 
The train that came at dawn was not the same train, because it arrived on time and full of flour. 
I still go to the disused railway station overgrown with ivy, though now the ivy is full of notes. 
Strangers leave requests, and strangers fill them, and no one is allowed to leave without owing someone else a small kindness. 
That is the cure I found: not the end of solitude, but the end of believing it was deserved.
