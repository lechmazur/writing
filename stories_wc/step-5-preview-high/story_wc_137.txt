The Stargazer's Open Tower clung to the basalt cliffs like a ribcage opened to heaven, and during the annual whale migration, Mara swept salt dust from its circular floor while the leviathans sang below. 
She did not believe in ghosts, spirits, or haunted heirlooms, but she believed in grief with a ferocity that surprised the mainlanders who sometimes rowed out to her. 
Mara was a sympathetic skeptic by trade and temperament, an archivist of tides for the maritime academy, and she had learned that the most merciful response to another person's sorrow was to withhold easy lies. 
So when the young woman named Ilse arrived wrapped in a wool coat too thin for the wind, clutching a bundle of oilcloth, Mara offered tea before questions. 
"My grandmother built the mirrors in this tower," Ilse said, her voice thin as gull-cry. 
"She said the whale song was a score no one could hear, and that if I ever needed to find her, I should bring the tile back." 
Mara set down her cup, her shoulders tensing with the familiar ache of witnessing hope she could not endorse. 
But kindness, she had learned, was not the same as credulity, and so she followed Ilse up the spiral stair to the open dome without protest. 
Ilse unwrapped the bundle on the central plinth. 
The object was a magic square tile, ceramic and glazed in abyssal blue, its edges chipped by centuries of salt wind, and the patterns pressed into its surface seemed to shift when Mara tilted her head. 
"Forgotten patterns," Ilse murmured. 
Mara leaned in, her skepticism bristling, and saw that the grooves were not mere decoration. 
They were charts. 
Star-maps interlaced with knotwork, geometric lattices that predated the telescope, drawn by hands that had known the sky before iron instruments named it. 
Mara's breath caught. 
She was looking at a method of transcribing celestial motion through mnemonic geometry, a language the old stargazers had used to bind memory to matter. 
When Ilse placed her palm flat against the tile, the tower filled with the scent of lavender and low tide, though nothing grew on the cliff but thrift and rust. 
Mara felt a memory that was not her own: a woman in oilskins inking similar patterns by lantern light while whale song rolled beneath the floorboards. 
The woman was grieving. 
The woman was hoping. 
The woman was courageously kind to a child who would outlive her, sculpting a space for reflection out of glass and starlight so that the child might one day return. 
"Entangled memories," Mara whispered, the phrase arriving from her own throat as if borrowed. 
Ilse nodded, tears tracking silently down her cheeks. 
Mara's scientific mind rebelled, offering refraction and pareidolia and the chemistry of grief, yet her hand was already reaching for her logbook. 
She could not give Ilse a ghost, but she could give her witness. 
"Show me where the patterns begin," Mara said, and she started to transcribe. 
She worked through the long afternoon while the whale migration passed beneath the tower, vast bodies moving in a dark procession that made the stone hum. 
Each line Mara copied seemed to draw the music of the whales into geometry, translating an unheard symphony into angles and arcs that her pen could hold. 
The forgotten patterns required precision; a tremor would break the mnemonic knot, so Mara breathed slowly, her kindness now a deliberate instrument, brave in its patience. 
As the spiral closed, the light in the dome changed, and the brass mirrors Ilse's grandmother had installed caught the sun in a precise ring. 
The tile grew warm. 
Mara stepped back and understood that the tile entangled memory, linking every soul who had sought reflection here during the whales' return. 
She used the geometry she had copied to adjust the shutters and mirrors, to sculpt space for reflection as the old builder had done, guiding light into a quiet column that illuminated the plinth without glare. 
Ilse gasped. 
In the pool of light, the tile showed her grandmother's hands aligning the mirrors, her voice humming against the sea, her choice to leave behind a tool of solace rather than a plea for belief. 
Mara said nothing. 
Her skepticism remained intact, a clean and honest thing, but it had expanded to make room for the possibility that some mechanisms were simply too complex to dismiss. 
The whales sang on through water and stone, a symphony the tower had been built to conduct though no human ear had ever been meant to catch the score. 
Ilse slept in the eastern alcove that night, and Mara kept watch, a guardian of mysteries she would never name in her reports. 
She never wrote of the symphony she had heard, the one that played in silence through every transcribed line.
