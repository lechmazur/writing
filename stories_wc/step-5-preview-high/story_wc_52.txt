In the rain-beaded skylight loft, the air smelled of wet iron, sawdust, and old canvas, and the late light lay over everything in a rust-hued peace. 
Marguerite waited until the kettle began to tick. 
Then she set the ash wood carving blank on the bench beside her chisels. 
She was the lion tamer's widow, though no one in the prop district needed the title to know why she kept the loft. 
Her husband, Arvid, had trained the cage cats for twenty years and had taught her that every act was only a way of making time stand still. 
While the kettle boils, she liked to say, grief must be given a fence. 
The routine was hauntingly practical: five minutes of carving, one clean lesson, then tea. 
Anything longer and memory grew teeth. 
She turned the blank until its pale end grain faced her. 
The ash had been cut from the support beam over the old practice cage, so every ring was a season of roars, rain, and sawdust. 
Arvid had believed that wood kept what passed through it. 
He had taught her to carve through crystallized time, following the rings until a single moment stood still and could be handled. 
Marguerite had never thought of herself as patient. 
Since the fire, though, she had learned to move like a woman measuring a trap. 
The circus had scattered; the loft had become a workshop for repair, for memory, for careful unfinished things. 
She had come there to harvest lessons from loss, not to worship it. 
Loss had taken Arvid on a dry Tuesday, while a fuel lamp fell and the night caught. 
What remained was the strange arithmetic of love: he was here in her hands and gone from the door. 
The first chisel cut made a sound like a held breath released. 
She pared the block into a shallow box, leaving the corners thick and honest. 
Ash was strong but forgiving, and she wanted a vessel she could contain without glue or nails. 
Her plan was to carve a lid that fit so tightly the wood itself would hold the secret shut. 
Outside, rain ticked harder against the skylight, and the kettle began its low song. 
She worked in the ring Arvid had named the year of the white lioness. 
The grain there was dark and close, almost resin-hard, as if the tree had swallowed a winter of pacing and made it into strength. 
She carved a lion's head into the lid, not as ornament but as a latch of attention. 
Its mane became ridges her thumb could find in the dark. 
Its eyes stayed closed, because Arvid had said a tamer never asks the animal to be anything but present. 
The kettle's tick quickened. 
Marguerite laid the brass stopwatch key in the hollow, set the lid, and pressed until the fibers sighed. 
She had left no room for ashes, only for one small weight of metal and the clear rule it carried. 
Arvid had used that key to time the cats' entrances, cue by cue, until the animals moved like sentences in a language he could almost speak. 
That key was the lesson she could carry: not the beast, not the blaze, but the steady human act of showing up on cue. 
Arvid had taught her that a lion was here and gone in the same breath, and that the tamer's art was to honor the breath without clutching it. 
She had spent her first months of widowhood trying to contain the uncontainable: his voice, the smell of singed canvas, the exact tilt of his smile. 
The ash box was better. 
It held the key and one clear rule, and nothing else could get in. 
The kettle shrieked. 
She set the box on the sill beneath the rain-beaded skylight and turned away to make tea. 
Steam rose, the loft darkened, and everything settled deeper into its rust-hued peace. 
The carving would keep. 
Grief, for five minutes at a time, could be shaped, held, and answered. 
Then she would open the window, let the rain-washed air in, and begin again.
