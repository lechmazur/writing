The obliging sky mechanic knelt on the frozen gantry of Kepler Station, a xenobiology research station anchored above the cloud layer, and watched the last amber light bleed across the horizon. 
Joss had always been mechanically warm, her gloved hands quick to repair a fractured coupling or share a ration of heated tea with shivering researchers, and she carried that same steady heat into every engine she touched. 
Tonight, within the hush of beating wings at rest, the aerolith colonies folded their translucent sails into the magnetic nexus at twilight, seeking the geomagnetic cradle that let them dream in suspension. 
The station's xenobiologists recorded these subdued awakenings each evening, noting how the creatures' metabolisms dimmed to a whisper, but Joss had come for a different purpose. 
For three years she had hidden equations beneath her tool belt, driven by a single desperate need to prove that time moves sideways, flowing not forward but laterally through layers of folded causality. 
She approached the magnetic nexus, a cathedral-like spire of charged basalt grown by station botanists to interface with the aeroliths, and placed her palm against its mechanically warm ribs. 
The basal filaments glowed with residual energy, and she knew the creatures resting inside could not see her, yet she lowered her voice anyway, an icy warmth softening her tone as she spoke to the dark. 
Kepler Station had no asphalt runways, only ice-sheathed spires where mechanics tethered themselves against katabatic gusts, and Joss had patched every gasket in the observatory dome to earn the trust of the science team. 
She had discovered the anomaly by accident while recalibrating a weather drone, noticing that the nexus returned its hum before she struck its shell, a discrepancy that no linear clock could explain. 
Her device was crude, a rig of salvaged projectors wired to the nexus core, but it operated through holographic principles, casting interference patterns that could reveal where temporal currents branched. 
Joss gripped the lever at the base of the console, its metal worn smooth by her nervous thumb, and waited until the aeroliths' wings fell utterly still. 
The hush deepened, that sacred pause when the station itself seemed to inhale, and she threw the lever. 
Basalt teeth in the spire engaged, translating her mechanical force into harmonic resonance, and the frozen air around her shimmered with latent heat that did not burn. 
Holographic light spilled across the nexus, not as a simple image, but as a lattice of sideways moments, each one breathing in parallel with her own. 
In one layer she saw herself pulling the lever, while in another she had not yet arrived, and in a third she knelt there years from now, old and gentle, tending a wiser station. 
Here, in the sideways flow, she understood that the station existed as a single chord struck across eternity, every repair and every kindness resonating simultaneously. 
The aeroliths stirred, their subdued awakenings rippling outward as the nexus transmitted a warmth that was neither quite flesh nor machine, and one great wing brushed her shoulder in gratitude. 
Dr. Ansel emerged from the airlock, her frostbitten cheeks flushing as she studied the readouts, and for a long moment she said nothing. 
"You were right," she whispered. "The nexus is breathing in every direction at once." 
Joss released the lever, and the holographic patterns faded, leaving only the magnetic nexus humming beneath the returning stars, proof enough that time had never been a straight road. 
Not every awakening needed to be loud, she thought; some truths arrived softly, like dawn on the tundra, inevitable and warm. 
The creatures settled again into their twilight sleep, and Joss returned to her workshop, her hands cold but her heart full of that quiet, defiant warmth. 
In the morning she would file her report, but for now she stood in the dark and let the impossible settle over her like a familiar blanket.
