The hush just before a condemned building is demolished has a taste like cold metal on the tongue. 
Ilke tasted it as she stood inside the tree trunk library hollowed by a scholar's guild. 
Three centuries of readers had worn the floor into a shallow bowl around a packed-earth hearth. 
She was a face-blind portrait artist, and she navigated the room by the angles of shelves, the smell of tannin, and the particular creak of the west stair. 
No one expected her to recognize the guild warden who waited beside the circulation desk, and she did not. 
She knew him only by the tapped cadence of his cane and the tallow smoke that clung to his sleeves. 
His request was strange for a portraitist: to mirror the sky inside walls before the crew brought the condemned tree down. 
Ilke had accepted because she trusted measured angles more than expressions. 
Faces had always shifted under her gaze, but stars held their distances with a patience she could map. 
The warden led her to a shelf of damaged almanacs and withdrew a leather volume bound in bark-gray cloth. 
He pressed a hidden seam, and the front cover lifted to reveal a secret book safe hollowed from the spine. 
Inside lay a centuries-old coin, dark and warped, with no portrait, date, or mint mark on either face. 
He said the guild founders had struck it as an instrument rather than as money. 
Ilke turned it beneath the lamp until faint indentations caught the light along its rim. 
She had spent her life translating unreadable surfaces into lines, so she understood what those marks were meant to hold. 
Through faint indentations on a centuries-old coin, she read the star field recorded above the guild on the night the library was planted. 
Her method was fractally precise, each printed ridge repeating its shape at every smaller scale she could resolve. 
She laid the coin on vellum and rubbed graphite sideways across it, building a negative sky from its dents. 
Then she transferred those dents to the curved bark with calipers, plumb lines, and brushes worn to single hairs. 
The tree's amber seams interrupted several marks, and a fire seam swallowed a whole curve of the record. 
Still, she painted, because the work asked for stillness and the condemned building gave it to her. 
The ceiling opening framed the same air the coin remembered, and dust turned slowly in that light. 
She painted the horizon first, then the known stars, each one placed at the intersection of measured lines. 
As the image spread, she began to notice where the coin's record simply ended. 
She tried to restore the missing arcs by symmetry, but the wood refused her invented stars. 
The discovery arrived quietly, like dust settling: her craft could approach the sky only as far as the instrument and the room allowed. 
She had come to mirror the sky inside walls, and now she had to decide what kind of truth a wall could hold. 
A face-blind painter understands the danger of asking a surface to promise more than it can carry. 
Clients had wanted her to flatter them, to invent eyes and mouths they had never owned. 
She had refused, because her distance from faces had taught her that no likeness survives pretending to be complete. 
So she chose the honest limit. 
She left the lost star field as bare bark and painted only what the coin and the ceiling opening could prove. 
Around that emptiness the surviving stars shone with terrible accuracy. 
The warden returned at the hush, his cane silent on the earthen floor. 
He studied the blank arc, then the painted sky, and Ilke watched his shoulders lower as if he had set down a weight. 
"You have left the sky unfinished," he said. 
"I have left it exact," she answered. 
Outside, someone called the ten-minute warning. 
The library seemed to inhale, holding dust, amber, and starlight in one suspended breath. 
Ilke understood that she had finally made a portrait no changing face could betray. 
She had discovered her limit and, within it, crafted a truth that would outlast any complete lie. 
Then the first distant engine coughed, and the hush ended.
