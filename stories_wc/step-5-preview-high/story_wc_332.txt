Ilva was the odd-hour librarian of the Tidewrack Library, the only reading room in the whispering archipelago that opened in the hush of improbable beginnings.  Her shift ran from three until five, when insomniacs, lighthouse keepers, and widows came to borrow receipts for weather and directions home.  Ilva shelved their sorrows by scent and tide, but she could not shelve her own: she wanted to capture a runaway dream.  It had slipped from the vault beneath the reading room on the night of an improbable new moon, and since then the islands could not remember what they were missing.  In the lost-and-found drawer sat a watercolor palette stamped with a crescent moon and nine unnamed islands; its pans darkened at dawn and shone at midnight.  The older librarians had called the archipelago a cipher of longing, because every whisper between the skerries was a sentence someone had been too afraid to finish.  Ilva had been taught to read it one gull-cry at a time, but after the Long Quiet the whispers thinned and even the maps forgot their coastlines.  She suspected she had left the vault open, because her memories of that morning were the same colors as the palette.  When she mixed rainwater into the pans, she discovered that she paints with light, laying strokes of gold on the dark that dried into soft, breathing dawn.  The strokes did not illuminate the paper so much as reveal the shape of something gone, a luminous absence that made readers lean closer and weep without sadness.  In the attic she found a brass plate camera and a note from her mother, the last odd-hour librarian before the dreams began running.  The note read: To vitalize what longing has scattered, learn to see through sunrise photography.  For eleven dawns Ilva carried the camera to the east quay and photographed the sun climbing out of the whispering archipelago.  She developed each plate beside the palette, and in the silver grain she saw a path that curved around every unlit window, held letter, and name the tide had taken.  The cipher of longing had a grammar, and the grammar had a verb.  The verb was vitalize, and Ilva understood that the runaway dream could not be chased; it had to be given a body of light strong enough to hold it.  So she ground the palette's dawn-cakes into a paste and brushed them across the glass plate, turning sunrise photography into an act of painting.  Each exposure became a doorway; each doorway asked the same question in light.  The archipelago answered by whispering the coordinates of the vault, not as words but as a chord of gulls and hinges.  On the twelfth morning, in the hush of improbable beginnings, Ilva set the palette on the east quay and waited for the sun to finish its sentence.  When the first ray crossed the water, she pressed the shutter and felt the dream turn toward her like a boat hearing its name.  She did not hunt it; she painted.  With a brush dipped in refracted sunrise, she drew a harbor on the dark plate, then a door, then a threshold of salt and mercy.  The image rose from the plate as a luminous absence, a shape of light with a hollow center, and the hollow was the exact size of the dream.  Ilva let the last pan empty into the hollow, vitalizing the threshold so the dream could step back into the world without burning.  It did not return as a person or a ship, but as a sudden hush that filled every lit window and made the archipelago legible again.  The whisperers found they could speak their longing aloud without finishing it, and that was enough.  Ilva kept the watercolor palette on the return desk, empty but warm, and in the margins of every overdue slip she wrote the same instruction.  If you lose a dream, do not chase it; photograph the sunrise, paint the absence, and leave the door unlatched.
