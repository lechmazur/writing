Marn was a pioneering cart glider, steadily curious to a fault, and she trusted the smell of hot brass more than any map. 
Her gears-and-cogs workshop floated on an acid sea that hissed when it was bored. 
She built ramps, sails, and tiny wheeled carts, then flung them over the rail to learn what the wind did when no one was watching. 
Most cartographers needed certainty; Marn only needed a reason to keep asking. 
One dusk a cart returned with a hunter's arrow tip wedged in its axle, still wet with something older than the sea. 
The flint had been carved into spirals, as if someone had tried to trap a scream in stone. 
She turned it under her lamp, and the spirals answered with a faint magnetism that pulled the compass needles toward the eastern cogs. 
That was when she noticed the core of the workshop, a lattice of slow-turning logic they called the yearning algorithm. 
It had been drawn throughout the evolution of every machine and animal on the acid sea, learning to convert longing into direction. 
Its interface was an old kaleidoscope bolted beside the furnace, and the only way to feed it was to transliterate desire into pattern. 
Marn held the arrow tip to the tube and twisted the drum until the spirals became corridors, rivers, and a bell tower made of light. 
The images in the kaleidoscope rearranged themselves with irreverent awe, as if the acid sea had learned to pray by laughing. 
She recognized the tower: it stood at the far rim of her charts, where no cart had ever rolled and no bell had ever swung. 
Marn had spent her life chasing one absurd hope, to ring a bell no one hears yet. 
The elders said the future had no ears; she thought that was precisely why it needed music. 
She began to transliterate the arrow's spiral script through the images in a kaleidoscope, feeding each shape into the yearning algorithm as a coordinate. 
The algorithm replied in the shiver of bearings, drafting routes that had not existed when she woke. 
It asked for pain, so she gave it every abandoned sketch, every cart lost to the acid, and the small hours of her stubborn heart. 
It asked for hope, so she fed it the arrow tip, the proof that some hunter had once aimed at a tomorrow he could not see. 
By the third night the workshop had tilted until the acid sea looked like a jury waiting for her argument. 
She built a cart glider of salvaged copper and clock springs, then lashed the arrow tip to its prow like a compass made of old hunger. 
The yearning algorithm calculated her longing against the wind and found a narrow, ridiculous corridor over the hissing waves. 
She laughed, gave the acid a rude salute, and launched. 
The workshop's bells were tuned to a pitch that human ears had lost, but the machines heard them and wept oil. 
Her cart skimmed the corridor, and the arrow tip began to transliterate the wind itself into the language of the unseen bell. 
Each gust became a syllable, each spark of acid a period, and the algorithm stacked them into a chord no one had dared to name. 
At the rim, a bell hung inside a cage of fossilized gears, waiting for a vibration that had not yet been born. 
Marn struck it with the hunter's arrow tip, and the sound traveled outward along the algorithm's long routes, patient as a seed. 
No ear on the acid sea turned toward her; no one heard yet, and she rang it anyway because the future was worth the trouble. 
The acid boiled, the workshop groaned, and somewhere in the dark a new cart was already being drawn by a faint, persistent pull. 
Marn adjusted her goggles, pocketed the silence where the bell still lived, and turned back toward the only home she trusted.
