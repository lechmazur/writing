Milo adjusted the surgical lamp over the embalming table, its brass arm groaning like a tired mourner.  The funeral home prep room smelled of formaldehyde and lilies, a deathly vitality that made the air feel both ended and electric.  He hated this basement chamber, yet its sterile warmth kept his hands from shaking.  His father Silas called himself a consultant, which meant he sold false hope in exchange for real money.  Silas had promised a breach, not of the law but of a locked supply cabinet where the county stored fresh asylum files.  Milo, the only honest child a con artist ever produced, intended to stop him by waiting actively rather than running or arguing.  He knew Silas would arrive when the corridor lights dimmed, because criminals kept the schedule of frightened men.  The body on the table belonged to a woman named Desta who had applied for political asylum before her heart failed.  She had been a mathematician, and her file contained a proof on the mathematics of a fair trade, a theorem that allocation without coercion was possible.  Her personal effects sat in a plastic tray: a rosary, a transit pass, and a vintage ink pot that the county clerk had missed during inventory.  Milo had learned from a retired coroner that the old metal liquefies rigid thinking when held during contemplation, a rumor whispered over autopsies.  He gripped it now, feeling the strange warmth spread through his certainty until his rigid hatred of his father's trade began to soften.  To find the mathematics of a fair trade, he reasoned, one must weigh dignity against desperation, and never let grief be the currency.  He picked up the embalming needle and worked with methodical patience, letting the arterial fluid carry deathly vitality through Desta's veins.  The compressor hissed, and the room's quiet pulse seemed to argue that endings were only arithmetic awaiting a solution.  Desta's face, half restored by his careful hands, seemed to listen, and he felt the unreasonable hope of the dead press against his ribs.  By waiting actively, he monitored the cabinet's magnetic lock while pretending to sketch the layout of the woman's bruised knuckles.  He noted that the lock buzzed when the refrigeration compressor paused, a gap of forty seconds that arrived each quarter hour.  When the compressor paused a second time, Milo slipped the file beneath his coat and wrote the address of the immigration court on his palm.  Silas would come at the first pause, Milo predicted, because the man trusted luck more than clocks.  The corridor lights dimmed exactly as the compressor sighed into silence, and Milo set the vintage ink pot atop the cabinet as a signal.  When Silas slipped inside with a pry bar, his eyes were wild with the deathly vitality of the room, alive and predatory.  You said a fair trade, Milo said, his voice steady, but you brought a pry bar to a conversation about justice.  Silas laughed, yet the laugh faltered when Milo explained the equation he had solved while working the needle.  Asylum is not a commodity, Milo said, and the only fair trade is to return what was stolen from the dead.  He had already mailed Desta's untouched file to the immigration court with a letter confessing his father's scheme and forfeiting every bribe.  The breach was complete, achieved not by force but by waiting actively until the lock's rhythm betrayed itself.  Silas stared at the ink pot, then at his son, and something in his rigid greed finally liquefied into shame.  He set down the pry bar and asked what the exchange rate was for a second chance.  Milo did not know, but he was willing to calculate it slowly, one honest day at a time.  Outside, sirens sang their thin hymn, and the prep room held them both in its deathly vitality, neither fully lost nor entirely saved.
