The salt flat waited in the lull of distant thunder, a white expanse ribbed with cracks that looked like handwriting. 
Vira stood at its edge, her heels clicking once against the crust before she chose to walk barefoot. 
She had donated three children's hospitals to this region, and in doing so, she had learned that gratitude is a currency that depreciates. 
What did not depreciate was the egg in her satchel, the last relic of the Loomwing, a bird whose extinction she had witnessed not as tragedy but as opportunity. 
The drought that had cracked this valley had been manufactured, cloud by cloud, through her aerially deployed hygroscopic dust. 
Critics called it climate warfare; the grateful called it Vira's Water Accord, since the rainfall she rerouted had filled reservoirs in the lowlands. 
Yet her true ledger was private, a single entry: the echo of extinct birds had to be preserved, and for that she needed a map only desiccation could reveal. 
The cracks in the dried riverbeds were that map, a deliquescent script converging at the flat's heart where an ancient geothermal vent whispered beneath the crust. 
She was secretly sentimental, a fact she had buried beneath surgical precision and shareholder meetings, but the memory of her mother humming a Loomwing lullaby had anchored her to this work. 
Thunder muttered again, far off, and then the air went still in the lull between charges, when barometric pressure dropped enough to sing through the salt. 
Now, she thought. 
She walked the white script, reading the fractures as directions, her bare feet leaving damp prints that the salt greedily drank. 
The salt flat had been a seabird rookery ten thousand years ago; now it was an archive, its mineral lattice capable of holding sound like amber holds light. 
At the confluence of three great cracks, she knelt and opened the velvet case. 
The egg was small, moon-pale, its shell etched with fractal patterns that mimicked the cracks around her. 
She had kept it for decades, moving it from vault to vault, an amoral philanthropist hoarding one soft thing. 
But preservation without purpose was merely theft from time. 
The action required was release. 
She lowered the egg into a fissure that breathed warm, mineral-scented air, and let it go. 
It slid into the dark, settling into the resonance chamber below, where the dried seabed would carry vibrations through its crystal bones for centuries. 
Above, the thunder returned, but its voice was changed, layered now with a ghost of birdsong that had not existed in living memory. 
The echo would not die; it would wait in the salt, a fracture in the silence, preserved by the very drought that had silenced the valley. 
Vira stood, her eyes dry, her face composed for the drones she knew would eventually catalog her trespass. 
She was cruel enough to have caused this aridity, and sentimental enough to mourn what it had cost. 
That dissonance was her only honest prayer. 
As she walked back along the cracked map, the salt flat seemed to hold its breath around her, a fractured grace made of loss and mineral light. 
She had given the world hospitals, water, and silence. 
Now she had given the dead a voice that would not fade. 
The lowland towns had named her a savior when the aqueducts swelled, but the upland shepherds had cursed her name into the wind. 
She had signed both letters of thanks and threats without reading them, because she had long ago traded empathy for outcomes. 
Yet outcomes were lonely architecture. 
She thought of the Loomwing's call, a cascading arpeggio that her mother had once sung into her hair. 
The recordings in archives were thin, compressed, flawed; the living echo required more than audio files. 
It required a body, or at least a vessel, returned to the acoustic geometry of the nest. 
The egg was that vessel. 
Its shell contained no embryo, but its inner membrane had been cultured with piezoelectric salts harvested from this very flat. 
When the thunder lull bent the air, the salts would resonate, imprinting the stored harmonics into the vent's basalt walls. 
It was science disguised as ritual, and she had wept when she designed it, alone, in a lab smelling of ethanol. 
The tears had surprised her then; she had checked her pulse as if diagnosing a stranger. 
Now the flat sang, low and crystalline, and the song rose through her soles into her sternum. 
She did not look back, for she knew that grace, however fractured, could not bear witness to itself. 
Behind her, the salt kept its counsel, holding the dead in its white hands. 
The thunder rolled on, indifferent and eternal, while beneath the crust an extinct bird sang its one, long, preserved note.
