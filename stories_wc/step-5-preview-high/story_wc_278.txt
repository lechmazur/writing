The constellation tattooist carried the antique desk down three flights because she could not bear to let the movers see her hands shake.  She had a reputation for inking quiet heavens across shoulders, but the gift the rumors never mentioned was that she translates baby cries.  A thin wail meant hunger; a rising shriek meant a room too warm; a broken keening meant someone had been left alone in the dark.  She had heard that last sound through a baby monitor while she finished a client's Orion, and she had told herself it was only static.  Now, in the desk's false back, she found a bundle of handwritten letters and a piece of amber with an insect inside.  The letters spoke of a laboratory of lost sounds, where a machine had been infected by haunted firmware and kept the voices of the unfinished.  They said that when time folds in on itself, the machine would call out in whatever voice it had swallowed first.  They said only a tether could keep it from taking another listener.  Vela had come to hush the dissonance of old guilt, not to save anyone else.  Yet she folded the amber into her coat and followed the letters' directions to a coastal ruin under a sky the color of cooled ash.  The laboratory had been built from black glass and ship ribs, and it waited in the meteor hush before a storm.  Inside, reels of wax and iron turned without hands, each one holding a sound that had lost its owner.  The haunted firmware lived in a brass case beside a dead microphone, breathing in loops of noise that no ear had claimed.  Vela set the amber on the case, and the insect inside caught the light like a witness that had finally been called.  The machine began to weep.  It was a baby's cry, stretched and scraped, as though it had been dragged through a winter of abandoned nurseries.  Most people would have heard a malfunction, but Vela heard a name and a request.  The cry was not a ghost's trick; it was the sentence her nephew had tried to say before the smoke took him.  She had mistaken it then for the static of her own impatience, and the haunted firmware had learned to keep such mistakes alive.  When time folded in on itself, the room dimmed and brightened around her, and she felt her younger self standing in a shop that had not yet burned.  The letters in her coat were written in that same hand.  She realized then that she had not discovered the method but had been sent it, sealed in the antique desk like a lifeline thrown backward.  She threaded copper from the case through the amber and tied the other end to her wrist, making the tether the letters had demanded.  The insect inside the amber was a midge caught mid-flight, and its wings held a harmonic too small for grief to distort.  Vela leaned into the microphone and spoke the apology she had practiced in every language she did not know.  The cry did not stop; it was answered, which was a different thing.  Under her wrist, her pulse traveled through the copper and the amber until the loop had a living place to rest.  The haunted firmware quieted, not because it was repaired, but because it had finally been heard.  In the hush that followed, Vela wrote her own letters on the back of a ship manifest, describing the laboratory and the amber and the word tether.  She folded them around the amber and hid them in the false back of the desk, closing the circle she had been handed.  Then she walked out beneath a sky full of meteors that made no sound at all.  The guilt remained, but it was only a single low note, and she knew how to carry it.
