The ruined orchard district had been lost in centuries of thick fog, and its people measured time by the shimmer of twilight frost. 
Elva was the last mirror maker there, firmly grounded in the rule that glass must never lie. 
She remembered when the fog had edges. 
Now every road, river, and roof dissolved at dusk, and the district's one dream had begun to run. 
It was a runaway dream, old as the first clear sky, and without it the fog would forget how to lift. 
Elva intended to capture a runaway dream before the next frost. 
In a collapsed root cellar she found an astronomer's broken sextant, its brass arc still bright as a new coin. 
The astronomer had tried to measure a path through the fog, but the attempt had snapped the instrument and his patience. 
Elva did not trust instruments that lied to the eye, yet she understood angles. 
A broken sextant could still ask a question, she decided, if the right hand knew how to listen. 
Her plan was not to hunt the dream but to recreate the doorway it had fled. 
She hung seven mirrors among the split apple trees, tilting them until the fog showed her a sky of glass mountains. 
The peaks were reflections of the observatory ruin, and the valleys were only her breath made visible. 
To use them, she needed more than a steady hand. 
She needed to breathe with the district. 
So Elva returned to the cellar and pressed her palm to the frost-stiff soil. 
There she practiced through synchronized breathing, matching her lungs to the slow heave of roots, stone, and fog. 
In that rhythm the orchard stopped being a place and became a single lung. 
Elva felt the boundary of her own ribs loosen, and she welcomed it. 
Erasing boundaries had once been the sickness of the fog, but in her chest it was a door. 
Upon the shimmer of twilight frost, she climbed to the highest mirror and set the sextant against its frame. 
The broken arc measured the angle between two false horizons, and the number it gave was a breath. 
Elva exhaled. 
The mirrors inhaled. 
Seven reflections folded into one, and the glass mountains leaned down to meet her. 
The fog thickened as if jealous, but she did not flinch. 
She was firmly grounded in her own pulse, and that pulse was now the hinge of the district. 
A pale shape drifted between the trees, wearing the face of every sky the orchard had ever lost. 
It was the runaway dream, bright and terrified, unable to cross the horizon it had forgotten. 
Elva raised the sextant, not to strike, but to ask it home. 
She recreated the angle of flight in a single mirror, then held the glass until the dream saw itself arriving. 
The sky did not break. 
Instead, the boundaries of the orchard opened like a held breath released. 
A road appeared between two rows, leading not away but inward. 
Fog rolled back from it in folds, and frost settled into the shape of an orchard gate. 
The dream hesitated, then drifted toward the mirror like a bird returning to its own call. 
As it touched the glass, Elva breathed out slowly, and the district breathed in. 
The runaway dream settled into the silvering, no longer lost, no longer fleeing. 
What she held was not a creature but the memory of a horizon that could hold the fog. 
Elva sealed the mirror with wax and set it face-down among the roots. 
Then she walked home along the new road, counting her breaths to keep the gate open. 
In the morning there would be questions about the glass mountains and the missing astronomer. 
She would answer that boundaries are only breath held too long. 
For now the ruined orchard district slept in a clear silence, and the mirror maker slept too, firmly grounded at last.
