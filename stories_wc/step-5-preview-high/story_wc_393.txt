Thunder muttered beyond the shuttered windows of the antique music box repair shop, yet inside the air held a vibrant hush. 
Chaplain Elias Voss removed his soaked hat and ran a thumb along the brass counter, aptly deliberate in every gesture, as though the world outside were a chapel he had not yet earned the right to disturb. 
He had come from the penitentiary chaplain's office three blocks away, where he had spent twelve years counseling men who measured time in iron clangs and fading letters. 
On the worn velvet between them sat a clockwork heart, its copper petals stilled mid-beat, salvaged from a music box that a prisoner named Marcus had rebuilt in secret over four long years. 
Elias had brought it here because he no longer trusted his own judgment, and second opinions had become the only currency he knew how to spend without shame. 
The repairwoman, an elderly artisan named June, adjusted her spectacles and turned the mechanism beneath a hooded lamp, her silence a vibrant hush of tuning forks and dust. 
Outside, the storm eased, and a shaft of sun forced itself through the breaking clouds; by the way light breaks through storm clouds, Elias saw the room shift from gray to trembling gold. 
It was the calm before realization, that suspended breath when a man stands at the edge of his own making and waits for the world to finish its argument. 
June set the clockwork heart on a bed of oiled felt and clicked her tongue, a sound both verdict and invitation. 
"The mainspring is true," she said, "but the escapement is worn." 
"Your man needs a second opinion from someone who knows metal the way I do." 
Elias folded his hands, the knuckles scarred from years of gripping prison gates he had sworn to keep open in spirit. 
Marcus had sent the heart not as a bribe but as a question, a small machine that asked whether beauty could deserve to survive in a place built to erase it. 
The chaplain had written Marcus a letter of apology after a disciplinary hearing he had failed to soften, words that felt thin as tissue until he realized an apology without motion was only a ghost of mercy. 
He had come to this antique music box repair shop to turn that apology into action, to find a way to carry Marcus's hope beyond the walls. 
June saw the way he watched her hands, aptly deliberate as they hovered over the gears, and she seemed to measure not only the brass but the man. 
"If you want to save the heart," she said, "you must decide whether the prisoner deserves the time it will take." 
The words landed softly, yet they carried the weight of gavels. 
Elias thought of the warden's skepticism, the board's repeated refusals, and how he had begun to doubt his own report that Marcus's transformation was real. 
A second opinion, he realized, was not merely a luxury for the guilty; it was a duty owed to the possibility of grace. 
The light through the window deepened, and the dust motes drifted like a congregation rising to sing without sound. 
In that vibrant hush, Elias made his choice. 
He would take the clockwork heart to the parole board, not as contraband but as testimony, proof that a man who could fashion such delicate life from scrap deserved a listener. 
June wrapped the mechanism in silk and pressed it into his palm with a nod that said she understood old things and older sorrows. 
When Elias stepped back into the street, the storm had passed, leaving the sky washed and clear. 
The calm before realization had given way to purpose, and he walked with the steady rhythm of a man who had finally matched his faith to his feet. 
At the penitentiary gate, the guard studied the parcel, then the chaplain's face, and saw something new there, a quiet authority that needed no sermon. 
Marcus was waiting in the library, hands folded, eyes hollow with the particular fear of a man who had hoped too loudly and been punished for the echo. 
Elias set the clockwork heart between them and said that second opinions were not for the doubting alone but for the courageous who dared to be seen twice. 
Marcus reached out, and the copper petals caught the fluorescent light, beating once, twice, as if the storm clouds had finally broken inside the room.
