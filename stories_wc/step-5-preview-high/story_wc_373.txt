The moon was a chipped plate above the harbor when Mabel slipped through the gap in the rotting pier. 
She was a moonshine runner's daughter, born to the smell of copper and corn, and she knew that hidden things kept better in the dark. 
Tonight she climbed down the barnacled ladder into the pirate market that breathed beneath the waves. 
Its stalls were rotting crates and stolen silk, and its lanterns swung from pier beams like trapped fireflies. 
Here the truce of hush held until sunrise: no shouts, no gunshots, no splashing, lest the harbor guards hear and the old pier collapse into the tide. 
The comfort of that silence wrapped around her like a quilt, even though every shadow held a knife. 
Mabel carried her father's antique watch fob, a brass loop etched with moonshine stills. 
It was also a recorder, a whisper-thin phonograph that could trap a sound inside its spring. 
She had come to capture the sound of trust breaking. 
Her father had told her that trust did not shatter like glass; it snapped like a watch spring, dry and final. 
She needed that sound to prove what Cobb was planning. 
Cobb was a fence with a smile like a closed trap, and he had begun trading glances with the guard boats. 
If he cried out before dawn, the truce of hush would die, and the market would be dragged into chains. 
Mabel was curiously steadfast; fear made her slower, not louder. 
She decided to thwart him through false starts, small failures that would not alarm the watchers. 
Her first false start was to bribe Toby, the boy who oiled the ladder, with a jar of peach mash. 
But Toby had already been paid in silver by Cobb, and he only shrugged and turned away. 
Her second false start was to scatter wet kelp across the north stair, slick and silent. 
A stray dog, half starved and bold, ate the kelp and licked her hand as if thanking her. 
Her third false start was to pass a warning note tucked inside a fish's mouth to the midwife, Old Saz. 
But Old Saz was deaf in one ear and read the wrong message, smiling and tucking the paper into her apron. 
Mabel sat on an anchor chain, the antique watch fob warm in her palm. 
The market murmured around her, a sea of whispers and wet coins. 
She understood now that Cobb's betrayal was not a thunderclap but a hinge, waiting for the right weight. 
At the stroke before sunrise, the watchers expected the signal bell to ring three times, soft as a heartbeat. 
Cobb would strike it early, hard enough to carry up through the pier planks, and the guards would come. 
Mabel climbed the bell post with the quietness her mother had taught her, the moonshine runner's daughter vanishing into shadow. 
She tied the watch fob to the clapper with a strand of her own hair, so the recorder would drink the metal's song. 
Cobb appeared at the base, a lantern in one hand and a pistol in the other. 
He raised the striker, and the market held its breath. 
Mabel whispered the old hush, a promise that the night would not bleed. 
Cobb's arm faltered; the false starts had seeded doubt, a doubt now blossoming into hesitation. 
Then a gull screamed outside, and Cobb flinched. 
He struck the bell. 
The sound tore out, not a whisper but a crack, and the truce of hush split like rotten wood. 
Inside the antique watch fob, the phonograph needle etched the exact frequency of trust breaking: a dry snap, a gasp, a final recoil. 
But Mabel had already loosened the clapper's pin. 
When Cobb struck, the bell did not ring to the harbor; it rang inward, the sound collapsing back into the market's throat. 
The guards above heard only the lap of water. 
Cobb's pistol hand was suddenly empty, Old Saz's deaf ear turned just enough to catch his wrist, and Toby remembered the taste of peach mash. 
The false starts had become a net. 
At sunrise, gray light dripped through the pier gaps, and the truce of hush healed into morning chatter. 
Mabel unknotted the watch fob and pressed it to her ear. 
The sound of trust breaking played once, sharp and small. 
She smiled, for she had captured it, and in capturing it she had thwarted the man who sold his neighbors for coin. 
The market returned to its comforting unease, a place where danger slept beside you and no one screamed. 
Mabel, curiously steadfast, slipped back up the ladder, the recorder warm against her heart.
