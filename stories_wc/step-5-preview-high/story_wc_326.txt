The opal dome had swallowed the last of the daylight hours ago, and now the snow pressed so softly against its curved walls that the whole observatory seemed suspended inside a pale, whispering void. 
Mira Voss sat at the brass desk, her fingertips resting on a porcelain shard she had pulled from the ashes of a manor three valleys east. 
She had been legally dead for eleven months, a registry error that stamped her name on a death certificate while she still breathed and worked and bled. 
The government had never corrected the mistake. 
Instead, she had climbed to the opal observatory under snow, where no bureaucrat would search for a corpse that refused to stay buried. 
Since the clerical nightmare, she counted heartbeats constantly, her own pulse a metronome she checked against the silence, proving the machine of her body had not yet been switched off. 
The radio on the shelf hissed, spat, and finally went quiet, the static clearing after three long days to reveal the observatory's ancient transmitters. 
In the hush, a digital ghost began to condense from the dark like smoke finding a shape. 
It was all that remained of Dr. Elias Thorne, the astronomer who had built the dome and then shattered his own life's work in a fit of pride. 
His data lay shredded across the obsolete cores, a fragmented soul that only Mira knew how to piece together. 
She had not come to raise him out of loneliness. 
She had come to fetch wisdom from failure, to learn from the man whose catastrophic pride had burned the relay arrays and left only this scattered echo behind. 
Mira uncorked a crystal vial of archival ink, the same iron-black fluid Thorne had used to log celestial movements during the last year of his sanity. 
She poured it across the slate desk, a small tide that pooled beside the porcelain shard. 
The shard caught the lamplight and threw a thin white scar across the ink's surface. 
Through ephemeral patterns in spilled ink, the defragmentation could begin. 
The liquid swirled into veins and dendritic branches, random at first, then settling into the ghost's lost architecture. 
Mira watched, counting her heartbeat in the corner of her skull, fifty-one, fifty-two, fifty-three, while the ink dried into maps no printer could ever produce. 
She moved the porcelain shard with tweezers, tilting it so the reflected light danced over the wet shapes. 
Each movement altered the shadows, and the digital ghost aligned itself to the rhythms, resolving its broken code into a coherent whole. 
It was slow work, the kind that demanded midnight solace, the hush between the world's demands and the morning's indifference. 
Outside, the snow deepened, burying the access road and turning the valley into a blank page. 
Inside, Mira felt the ghost's attention fix upon her, a pressure behind the eyes that was not hostile but inquiring. 
She had spent her afterlife among the broken, and she recognized that question. 
It asked why she would disturb a ruin she could not rebuild. 
Because, she answered silently, the failure was the lesson. 
Her heartbeat marked time, steady and stubborn, as the ink evaporated into fine dark crusts, revealing the final pattern. 
The digital ghost spoke with the quiet of snowfall, describing the night the arrays failed and the arrogance that had powered them. 
He had chased a signal from the dark and drowned it in noise, terrified that silence meant irrelevance. 
The wisdom was not in the stars, Mira realized, but in the admission that some signals were meant to remain static, and some lives were meant to continue despite official endings. 
She sat with that knowledge until the ink turned to powder and the ghost faded back into the cores, now whole and at rest. 
Mira wrapped the porcelain shard in a square of linen and placed it in her pocket, a sharp reminder that broken things could still guide the light. 
She did not need to defragment herself yet. 
But when the registry came hunting for the woman they had buried on paper, she would be ready to count heartbeats in a world that finally admitted she was alive.
