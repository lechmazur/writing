The alchemical orchard greenhouse shrouded in endless night stood over the hush of winter-cracked fields, breathing warm mist against the frost. 
I was its last apprentice, keeping glass panes warm with a coal stove and old alchemical salts. 
My grandmother had taught me the trees grew on resonance, not sunlight, and that every winter they asked to be sung awake. 
She called those nights the resonant awakenings, when root, sap, and memory answered one another in a chord no throat could hold. 
In the greenhouse, warmth meant life; outside, the hush of winter-cracked fields meant hunger for villages I never saw. 
So when a shadow moved among the plum trees without bending a branch, I told myself it was only steam. 
The shadow had an ashen veil, a bridal train that smoked into ice, and eyes like lanterns behind wet glass. 
She was the ashen-veil ghost bride, and she had been ghosting the orchard through ephemeral cameo appearances since before I was born. 
She never stayed long enough to frighten me: a hand at the stove, a mouth against a watering can, a reflection among the silver leaves. 
Her attention was lovingly venomous, a tenderness that numbed my fingers and made the coal stove burn blue. 
I should have been afraid, but I had been lonely so long that her poison felt like being known. 
One duskless morning she appeared beside the cedar box and nodded at the carved stone bead inside. 
Her veil shifted, and I heard her voice as if it came through the roots themselves. 
"Your grandmother promised me a witness," she said. 
"I have come to repay a debt across lifetimes, not to take one." 
I asked what debt a ghost could owe the living, and she lifted the bead from its box without touching the wood. 
"In my first life I was the orchard's bride, and in my last I was its keeper," she said. 
"I carved this bead from the heart-stone of a man who saved the trees, then I used it to keep him awake too long." 
"He begged me to let the fields rest, but I loved him with a lovingly venomous grip, and so the sun never rose again." 
The greenhouse creaked as if in agreement, and frost bloomed across a window in the shape of a ring. 
"His blood is your blood," she said. 
"Every keeper since has carried his patience and my theft." 
"If you place the bead in the root cradle tonight, the trees will wake and the night will end." 
"But the awakening will need a voice to hold the chord, and that voice will not survive the spring." 
I looked out over the hush of winter-cracked fields, thinking of my grandmother's cough and the empty villages beyond the frost. 
Then I looked at the ghost bride, whose veil was woven from every farewell she had ever delayed. 
"You were supposed to ghost me through ephemeral cameo appearances," I said, "not stand here offering yourself." 
Her smile was mournful elation, grief and joy braided so tightly that neither could be named alone. 
"I have haunted enough," she said. 
"Let me be the bridge instead." 
I carried the carved stone bead to the oldest apple tree and set it into the root cradle, a bowl of petrified sap. 
The moment it touched the cradle, the greenhouse became an ear, and every tree began to answer. 
These were the resonant awakenings my grandmother had described: root against root, bud against starlight, memory against memory. 
The ghost bride opened her mouth, and the chord rose, a note that was also a door. 
Her veil lifted into the song, and her cameo face became whole, no longer a fragment smuggled between breaths. 
I held the low note with her, though it burned my throat and turned my blood to warm sap. 
Through the glass, the endless night cracked, and a gray dawn walked slowly over the hush of winter-cracked fields. 
Snow melted from the orchard roof in long silver threads, and somewhere beyond the frost a blackbird tested its voice. 
The ashen-veil ghost bride turned to me one last time, lovingly venomous even now, warning and blessing in the same breath. 
"The debt is paid," she whispered, and her voice became the first real warmth of spring. 
I stood in the greenhouse while the trees sang around me, alive and grieving and glad. 
Outside, the fields thawed into mud and green thread, and I carried my mournful elation into the morning like a lantern. 
I would replant what she saved, and I would never again let love hold a season too tight.
