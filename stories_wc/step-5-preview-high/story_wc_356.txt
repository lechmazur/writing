The mysterious treetop village of Larkspur Canopy woke to mist that morning, its rope bridges swaying like quiet metronomes.  Veya was an ambitious herbalist, and ambition had taught her to listen to leaves the way other people listened to sermons.  Her cottage sat on the third platform, where the air smelled of wet cedar, turmeric, and the salt-thick fog rising from the bay.  Veya was olfactorily sensitive to a degree that made the village elders uneasy; she could smell sorrow in smoke and decision in damp rope.  That morning, at the turning of the tide's thought, the estuary drew back as if reconsidering every promise it had made to the roots below.  A courier left a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, its outer surface stamped with the herb guild's seal and nothing else.  Veya's name had been written in her grandmother's slanted hand, and beneath it, in the same hand, three words: keep it moving.  Inside the oilcloth lay a single corpse flower seed, dark as a closed eye, resting on wrapping paper whose interior had been covered in doodles.  Her family had a custom of doodling on wrapping paper interiors, turning discarded coverings into maps, recipes, and quiet arguments across generations.  Veya would journalize everything, so she set the seed on her worktable and began to record its scent, weight, and the temperature of the air around it.  The seed smelled of iron, rain, and old libraries, but under those notes she detected the undercurrent of wisdom that her grandmother had always insisted lived beneath strong things.  Her ambition rose like sap; if she could germinate the seed in this place, she would prove that rare stillness could be grown rather than merely waited for.  Other growers called the corpse flower a vulgar spectacle, good only for frightening children, and their scorn had sharpened her resolve.  She wanted, above all, to cultivate serenity within motion, to give the village a calm that swayed with the bridges instead of fighting them.  But the village was restless, and the turning tide carried arguments up the trunks: merchants feared lost trade, priests feared omens, and children mimicked both.  Veya closed her shutters against the noise and studied the wrapping paper by lamplight, letting her nose guide her through the doodles.  A spiral of inked roots wound around a sketch of a bridge; at its center, her grandmother had drawn a small open mouth breathing out, not in.  Veya traced the lines with a fingertip, then lifted the paper to her face; the ink smelled of myrrh, and the back of the paper smelled of crushed ferns.  She understood then that the doodles were not decoration but instructions: the seed needed to travel, to be rocked by the village's motion until its hard casing cracked.  Veya crafted a sling from willow bark and old hymn string, then tied the seed inside against her chest where the sway of her walking would mimic the tide.  She walked the rope bridges at dawn, noon, and dusk, past the market platform, the rain cistern, and the shrine where fog collected like milk in a wooden bowl.  At each pause she journalized what her nose found: the seed's iron note softening, a green thread of smell appearing, the village's fear thinning into salt and pine.  On the seventh evening, when the tide's thought had fully turned and the water lay silver and still beneath the platforms, the seed split.  Veya felt the crack through her sternum before she saw it, a small sound like a bud deciding.  She did not cheer.  Instead she carried the seed to the compost ring at the village's heart and pressed it into warm, dark loam that smelled of patience.  Within a week, a hooded leaf unfolded, not the monstrous flower yet, but a green breath rising through the bark.  The leaf gave off a scent like wet stone and opening windows, and the villagers who came to scoff stood a little straighter, then a little stiller.  A wildflower hush moved through the treetop paths, not silence, but a shared willingness to let the bridges swing without fear.  Veya sat with her journal open and wrote only one line before the tide changed again: wisdom grows downstream of noise, and serenity must learn to swim.
