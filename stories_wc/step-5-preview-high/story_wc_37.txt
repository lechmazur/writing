In the mirror world, an alchemical orchard greenhouse shrouded in endless night stood like a ribcage of iron and clouded glass. 
Its trees were grafted with silver bark and bore fruit that held no sweetness, only smoke and old chemistry. 
Ora was a scattered memory keeper, hired or perhaps born to guard the orchard's fading thoughts. 
Her own memories had come loose during the long darkness, lodging in soil, dew, and broken glass until she could no longer name the mornings she was meant to save. 
She kept working because the realm was dying, and she wanted to rekindle it more than she wanted to remember. 
The greenhouse methods had failed: lamps, distillation, and chemical grafts could produce heat, but they could not produce dawn. 
In the coldest aisle, between rows of unripe pears and alembics that dripped starlight, Ora found a mica window beneath a tarpaulin. 
The pane was thin as breath, rippled with age, and veined with colors that did not belong to the greenhouse night. 
When she touched it, a thread of warmth slipped through her fingers, and the orchard shivered as if recognizing an old command. 
Behind the glass lay woven dawns: strands of sunrise braided into tapestries so fine that each thread hummed with the voices of many households. 
Their work knits communities together, binding farmers, bakers, lamplighters, and even quarrelsome neighbors into one shared morning. 
Ora understood then that the mica window was not merely a window but a loom's face, a place where dawn waited to be finished. 
A song rose in her, broken and hesitant, the kind sung in orchards when hands worked side by side. 
It was one of the forgotten songs, kept alive only by the greenhouse's remembering wood and the mica's patient heat. 
As she sang, memory returned in pieces, not as a flood but as careful stitches: her village, the loom, the night the window cracked. 
She had hidden the woven dawns to keep them from being torn apart by fear, but hiding them had left the realm without morning. 
That had been her first binding, and now she needed its release, a bound release that would open without shattering. 
The song demanded more than her voice, so she gathered every keeper in the greenhouse: roots, mirrors, jars of dew, and the workers' names she still recalled. 
She set the mica window upright in the compost and soil and sang through forgotten songs of planting, pruning, harvest, and mourning. 
The air thickened, and threads came loose from the glass, weaving themselves around her arms like a child asking to be carried. 
Ora realized she was not meant to force the dawn open but to swaddle it, to hold the new light gently until it was strong enough to rise. 
She tore strips from her keeper's cloak and wrapped the luminous threads until they became a small warm bundle in her hands. 
It pulsed against her chest, not burning but breathing, and the orchard's dying leaves turned toward her like faces recognizing a relative. 
She carried the bundle to the mica window and held it against the glass while she sang the last forgotten song, the one about coming home after a long winter. 
The glass warmed, and the woven dawns loosened their knots, spreading outward into the mirror world as threads of rose and gold. 
Where they touched the orchard, the alchemical trees stopped producing smoke and began to flower, their fruit swelling with clear light. 
The greenhouse's endless night did not vanish all at once; it thinned at the edges, allowing a single breath of morning to enter through the mica. 
Ora felt her scattered memories settle, not into a single perfect picture but into a chorus she could hold without losing herself. 
She stepped back, unbound at last, and watched the dawn climb the iron ribs of the greenhouse like a climber returning to a family table. 
In the villages beyond the glass, people who had forgotten the songs began to hum them, and their voices rewove the morning across the dying realm. 
The woven dawns bound the far farms to the city kitchens, the quarries to the river, the children to the elders, until no community stood outside the light. 
Ora remained in the greenhouse as its keeper, no longer scattered, sweeping mica dust into a jar where new songs could sleep until winter. 
She had rekindled the realm, not by stealing dawn but by swaddling it, singing it loose, and letting its threads knit the world back together. 
The next evening, she planted a seed and closed the mica window with a woven latch. 
Its release was complete, yet the orchard remained bound to song, and that was a binding she finally loved.
