Cobb was a meek blacksmith in the quarry town of Loom, on an orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches. 
Groves of quince and plum grew in terraces between arches that drifted like patient sailboats. 
Beneath the fruit lay abandoned mine shafts his grandfather had sealed after the rock began to rise. 
Cobb was quietly determined, though his voice was soft and his shoulders had learned to flinch. 
The orchards were failing because crushed stone had crept into their roots, and Cobb knew it. 
He understood that trees needed grit to grow strong, but not grit that refused to change. 
That was where his mind hovered, between understanding and confusion, each night after the forge cooled. 
In the deepest shaft he found a bundle wrapped in magician's silk, its fibers the color of storm-lit skies. 
The cloth whispered when touched, and words rose like heat: I trade in bartered awakenings, but I accept no coin. 
Cobb had no coin, so he offered patience, one hour of stillness for every length of cloth. 
The silk tightened around his wrist, and he felt a bartered awakening settle behind his ribs like a coal. 
His motive was not glory; he wanted to coax resilience from rubble, to teach broken stone to hold life. 
He began deliberate work, using abandoned mine shafts as veins for a strange graft of root and stone. 
First he wrapped seed bundles in the silk, then packed them into cracks with powdered copper and ash. 
Then he set his smallest anvil above the shaft and hammered iron pegs through the cloth into the rubble. 
The silk carried the bargain downward, turning his patience into a slow green heat. 
Roots found the pegs, chewed the copper, and drank the ash as if it were rain. 
Where roots touched stone, the stone softened, not into mud but into something stubborn and porous. 
Other smiths laughed, but Cobb only nodded and returned to the shafts with more seed and more silk. 
Weeks passed, and the floating arches above the eastern grove began to sink a finger's width each dawn. 
He understood the danger: the arches were quarried from the same veins he hoped to heal. 
If he healed the shafts too quickly, the stone might fall and crush the orchard he meant to save. 
Confusion pulled at him, yet he kept his temper quiet and his hands steady. 
He laid silk through the lowest tunnel, then climbed out to watch the arches against the sky. 
By midnight the silk had made another of its bartered awakenings, this one from the sleeping rock to the waking roots. 
A quince tree split a boulder, white flowers ringing the fracture like a crown. 
The ground shuddered, and one arch tilted, shedding gravel onto the terrace roofs. 
Cobb ran into the mine with his hammer, not to fight the rock but to listen. 
He struck the walls in careful order, hearing where the silk had bound and where the stone still slept. 
The answer came as a pattern, and he followed it, using the abandoned mine shafts as channels for water. 
He redirected a spring so it would feed the roots without flooding the hollow places beneath the arches. 
Then he wrapped the largest silk banner around the keystone of the sinking arch. 
He whispered his trade: take my sleep, give the stone rest, and let the orchard keep its sky. 
The cloth drank the words, and the silk turned from blue to deep violet. 
The arch groaned, settled, and stopped, held by roots that had learned to grip. 
Cobb collapsed in the mud, but the trees stood in azure defiance beneath the brightening sky. 
By morning the arches were steady, and the mine mouths breathed green shade. 
Villagers found seedlings growing from broken stone, their tendrils shining like cooled metal. 
Cobb returned to his forge, meek as ever, quietly determined, with silk tied around his wrists. 
He did not claim magic or mastery, only the slow grammar of exchange and growth. 
On the orchard planet, between understanding and confusion, he kept teaching rubble to wake.
