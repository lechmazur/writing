The wise orchard pruner known as Sela walked the verdant parapet beside a waterfall at the hour upon the hush of budding hope. 
Mist rose in silver ropes, and the stone beds were furred with moss, but the fruit trees above the falls had forgotten how to blush. 
Their buds were gray as ash, and Sela knew that when wonder loses its color, both the near world and the far world begin to starve. 
She carried a leather satchel against her hip, and inside it lay a single heirloom seed, smooth as a river stone and warm as remembered laughter. 
It had been passed to her by her mother, who had pruned orchards on the other side of the veil, where blossoms grew in midnight and ripened by starlight. 
Sela was insatiably creative, and she could not bear to stand idle while the trees died. 
She studied the waterfall until she saw what she always sought: the bright threads of light that trembled in the spray. 
If she could capture essence, she thought, she could spin it back into the buds and succor the roots that drew breath from both worlds. 
She worked through the long night, laying supple willow wands across the parapet as a loom. 
From her satchel she drew silver twine, and she tied each strand to a bud, to a stone, to a drop of water that had not yet fallen. 
Then she began the weaving between worlds, pulling the twine through the air until the night around her shimmered like a held breath. 
The waterfall slowed to a whisper, and beyond the spray she saw the mirrored orchard, its trees leafless and pale, waiting. 
Sela did not fear the crossing, for she had learned that every cut she made was also a bridge. 
She reached through the loom and gathered the last bright essence of the far orchard, the taste of impossible pears and the smell of moon-dark pollen. 
It moved like syrup through her fingers, and she sang low to keep it from scattering. 
Back in her own world, she opened the heirloom seed with a thin blade, though she took nothing from it. 
Instead she pressed the captured essence into the seed's sleeping heart and sewed the seam shut with a strand of her own hair. 
The heirloom seed drank the light and began to hum, and the hum answered the hum of the waterfall. 
Sela set it in a crevice of the parapet where the moss was deepest, where roots from both sides of the veil could reach it. 
She pruned each tree with three careful cuts: one to release the past, one to open the future, one to welcome the unseen. 
The first bud to feel her hands trembled, then split, and a petal unfurled in a color that had no name in either world. 
It was not merely red or gold; it was the color of wonder itself, preserved and returned. 
One by one the branches followed, until the parapet was crowned with blossoms and the air rang with a radiant hush. 
In the mirrored orchard beyond the veil, a matching bloom opened, and its light crossed the water like a bridge. 
The people of that world came at dawn, thirsty and gray, and Sela gave them cuttings to carry home. 
She taught them how to listen for the hush of budding hope and how to keep it from dying. 
Her reward was not thanks but the sight of children pressing seed packets to their chests as if they were stars. 
Sela remained on the parapet until the falls resumed their song, pruning, teaching, and dreaming new grafts between worlds. 
The heirloom seed became a tree in time, and every fruit it bore held a different captured essence. 
Travelers said the orchard glowed on certain mornings, and that the waterfall itself seemed to pause in a radiant hush. 
But Sela only smiled and returned to her trees, insatiably creative, forever weaving succor into the seams of the world.
