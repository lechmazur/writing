The otherworldly sentimentalist drifted through the nebula garden, cradling a calculating machine gear worn smooth by the thumbprints of twelve ancestors. 
Around her, silver petals fell in spirals, each one a quiet sigh released from the breathing rocks, and she measured their descent with a patience that had become inherited rather than chosen. 
She was the thirteenth keeper of the transit routes, and the garden's generational patterns had etched themselves into her bones as surely as the gear's spiral groove approximated infinity. 
Her motivation, passed down through twelve lifetimes of horticultural memory, was to share light without using fire, for flame had once blackened the first garden into ash and sorrow. 
Instead, she cultivated luminescent moss on the undersides of petal-platforms and taught the translucent fungi to hold starlight like water in cupped hands. 
But the fog between the terraces had grown thick with the season's molting, so she prepared to transit the central aisle by sonar pinging, tapping the calculating machine gear against a tuning rod to send soft chimes into the gloom. 
Each returning echo told her where the rows of night-blooming lilies stood rooted in the dark, their roots drinking from comet tails that had been trapped in the soil generations before her. 
The chimes struck the leaning monoliths of compressed hydrogen and came back altered, revealing safe corridors and hollow places where gravity folded in on itself. 
She walked across the quiet sigh of falling petals, her bare feet kissing the phosphorescent walkways, and the gear in her palm hummed with the same rhythm her mother and grandmother had walked. 
In the garden's center waited the Seed Archive, a lattice of frozen light where every bloom that had ever lived was catalogued by a single tooth of the calculating machine gear, which was machined so finely that its curve approximated infinity. 
The generational patterns were visible there in the arrangement of the seeds: spirals within spirals, a horticultural inheritance that demanded she plant only what her predecessors had planted, in the same rows, under the same dim stars. 
Yet as she fitted the gear into the Archive's crank, she felt the troubling serenity of the place settle over her like a veil woven from lullabies and warnings. 
The gear turned, and the sonar pings she had sent earlier returned as harmonics, unlocking a panel of pearl that had not opened since her grandfather's era. 
Inside lay not seeds, but a mirror of oxidized silver, showing a young woman with the same cheekbones, the same longing, the same calculating machine gear already weathered in her hand. 
The sentimentalist understood then that the garden did not preserve flowers; it preserved keepers, and her transit had been a circuit through a closed corridor of time. 
Each generation approximated infinity by repeating itself, and the falling petals were the only clock that mattered, marking the quiet sigh between one keeper's lifetime and the next. 
To break the cycle would have been a violence against every hand that had ever tended these rows, and she was, after all, a sentimentalist who remembered the dead as vividly as the living. 
She could have shattered the gear, scattered the moss, let true darkness swallow the aisles, but the memory of her first dawn in the garden still warmed her, and that warmth was the only fire she had ever needed. 
She knew the pattern would call her back again in some other skin, some other century, and the thought filled her with a sorrow so gentle it felt like love. 
So she set the mirror back, aligned the seed spiral according to the old design, and continued her transit along the sonar-lit path, humming a lullaby her predecessor had hummed. 
The nebula garden exhaled, and across the quiet sigh of falling petals, the next buds opened in pale green light, fed by no flame, offered freely to the dark.
