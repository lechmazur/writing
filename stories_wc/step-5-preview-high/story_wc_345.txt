The dawn came bruised purple over the orchard planet, riddled with floating rock arches that hummed like struck glass. 
Marek had stopped fearing them only after strangers had become family, when the crash camps grew into kitchens and names. 
In that wary peace, the first messages appeared, chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn. 
Bring water, they said, and a bell that remembers wings. 
The colonists gathered in patched coats, arguing about trespassers, but the chalk was strangely trustworthy. 
No mud marred its edges, no wind scattered the lines, and every arrow pointed toward terraces they had ignored. 
Ilva, an old crewman, insisted the planet was empty, yet her hands shook as she traced the letters. 
Marek had seen the chalk writer once: a translucent figure kneeling at the curb, shoulders bent in labor rather than menace. 
The ghost, they decided, was the lost caretaker, an altruistic ghost whose quiet motive was to orchestrate collective effort. 
Still, trust came slowly, thorned by hunger and the memory of dead engines. 
The second morning brought one command: Reexamine the cistern maps. 
They reexamined everything, peeling moss from water ledgers and finding the hidden garden in a valley the arches concealed. 
The butterfly garden had been paved over by debris, its paths cracked, its bronze fountain dry. 
Beneath the fountain lay the butterfly garden watering bell, a copper vessel with petal holes and a clapper shaped like a cocoon. 
It was too heavy for one cart, too delicate for a crane, too precious to abandon. 
Above them the arches groaned, and the sky tore open with soft violet light as the nebula of respite began its slow passing. 
The elders called it a resting place for travelers, a calm that could heal a world if met with an open hand. 
The ghost wrote longer instructions each dawn, chalked onto deserted sidewalks at the settlement edges where no one walked. 
They were orders and apologies both, as though the writer knew the cost of every blister. 
By the fifth morning the colony rose before the twin moons set. 
They came with ropes and rollers, grain sacks and cupped handfuls of water. 
Marek directed the excavation while Ilva sang the counting songs the strangers had invented before they trusted one another. 
The ghost stood on the ridge, faint as breath, guiding not with command but with the angle of its folded hands. 
When the bell was freed, its copper skin was green, and the clapper rang once against the wind, low and sweet. 
They carried it in a sling of woven vine, stepping in the rhythm the chalk had taught them, twelve steps on, three back. 
The path crossed a sinkhole and a thorn grove, and twice they nearly dropped the bell into darkness. 
But the chalk marks waited at each turn, strangely trustworthy in their certainty. 
They reached the garden at noon and set the bell on its cracked pedestal. 
The ghost moved among them, mending a strap, steadying a child's bucket, leaving no fingerprints on the living. 
Ilva asked the empty air why it helped them. 
The ghost raised the chalk and wrote on flat stone: Because respite is wasted unless it is shared. 
That answer settled into them, brambled hope taking root alongside fear. 
They repaired the fountain, cleared the petal holes, and filled the bell with water from the cisterns they had reexamined. 
As the nebula deepened overhead, the ghost showed them a final spiral that meant ring. 
Marek struck the clapper, and the watering bell sang across the orchards in waves. 
Water spilled through the petal holes as fine mist, catching violet light, and butterflies rose from the hedges where they had waited. 
The mist drifted toward the floating rock arches, and their hum changed from warning to chord. 
The arches tilted, slow as drifting boats, forming a corridor of stone that opened toward the nebula. 
The colony stood together, hands linked, no longer strangers, no longer merely survivors, but a family holding its breath. 
The ghost raised both arms and thinned into the music, scattering like chalk dust in dawn wind. 
They never found its body, only a ledger of kindness and a bell that still rings for rest.
