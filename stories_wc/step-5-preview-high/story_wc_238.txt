During the hush before the orchard's trees were cut down, the sheet music archive smelled of glued paper and dormant moths.  Maribel Vane, gossip-column exorcist and reluctant archivist of other people's sins, locked the basement door behind her.  She had spent eleven years casting out demons with ink, exposing the possessed in society pages until their secrets had nowhere left to hide.  Now she carried the one miracle she had botched in an antique perfume bottle, its glass cloudy as watered-down absinthe.  The bottle had belonged to Ines Okafor, the cellist who once tuned her heart to the archive's humidity and hum.  Maribel had come to deliver an overdue apology, though she suspected the dead preferred silence to confessions.  She set the bottle on a piano bench beside a copper induction coil salvaged from the building's dead intercom system.  Through induced currents she could wake the residue left on paper, she told the empty stacks; a lie is a current too, and it travels best along a wire you have already grounded.  Maribel advised through metaphors because directness had never been her trade; she had learned that a congregation would forgive a riddle long before it would forgive a verdict.  She clamped the coil's leads to the bottle's brass collar and to a stack of Ines's unfinished scores, then closed her eyes.  The archive's lights flickered as the current hummed, low and blue, turning dust into a galaxy of slow sparks.  Ines appeared not as a body but as a pressure behind Maribel's sternum, the way a held note waits in the ribs.  The imperfect miracle was exactly this: no resurrection, no absolution, only enough voltage to carry one honest sentence across the dark.  "I wrote the article because I was afraid," Maribel said to the empty bench, "and I made your haunting into a column instead of making you into a friend."  The current spiked; a nearby fan began to turn without wind, pushing the scent of iris and old cigarettes through the coils.  That smell had been Ines's signature, but in the archive it now carried a grit that perfume alone could never fake.  Maribel reached for the bottle and found it warm, as though the glass remembered being held by living hands.  "You want to reconnect," she whispered, "but reconnection is not repair; it is only a splice between two frayed wires."  The fan slowed, and the stacks released a soft rustle like pages turning themselves to a final chord.  Maribel understood that the apology had been received, imperfect and incomplete, like every exorcism she had ever performed.  She had not banished Ines; she had merely stopped pretending the haunting was somebody else's story.  Above them, the hush broke as a saw bit into orchard wood, and the vibration traveled down through the archive's floorboards.  The first tree fell with a sound like a cello string snapping, and Maribel felt the current in her chest answer in kind.  She gathered the scores and the bottle, but left the coil humming, a small shrine of induced grief between the shelves.  "Imperfect miracles only clear the throat," she told the dark, "so the next song can be sung without choking."  In the stairwell she passed the superintendent's chalk marks, numbers and crosses that tried to inventory the building's ghosts.  She had read about herself in those columns, how she had "exorcised" a tenor and "cleansed" a conductor, as though people were drafty rooms.  But a building only stays warm because somebody keeps the windows cracked, and Ines had been the only one who let her breathe.  Outside, the orchard lay in fresh stumps and sawdust, the air sharp with sap and the metallic taste of severed roots.  Maribel pressed the warm bottle against her palm and walked toward the streetlights, already drafting the only kind of column she could now write.  It would be an apology in metaphor, imperfect as current, honest as dust, and finally hers to sign.
