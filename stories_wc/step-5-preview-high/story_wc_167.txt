Mira knelt at the eastern edge of Pearl Bay, where the dawn mist clung to the water like threadbare gauze. 
She was a harbor glazier by trade, a cutter and mender of windowpanes who had spent twenty years reading the stress lines in cracked glass. 
Tonight she had come before everything shifted. 
The night was her last covenant with the old harbor, a final chance to weigh what the dawn would scatter. 
The tide would turn at seven, and with it the city council's plans would pivot away from the old wharves toward the steel cranes downstream. 
She needed to finish her noctilucent reckonings while the night clouds still smoldered overhead. 
High above the bay, those luminous streamers burned with a cold blue fire, defying the sun, casting skeletal geometry across the pier. 
Mira moved toward the prism atrium in mist, a forgotten conservatory of angled glass at the harbor's western rim. 
Its facets had held darkness for decades, breaking it into spectrums, a cathedral raised by some Victorian dreamer who believed that light itself could be weighed. 
She had always read between lines, in ledger margins, in the stories of dockworkers, in the particular silence of gulls before a storm. 
Now she needed to commune with something older than the harbor, something patient and unmarketable. 
From her coat pocket she withdrew a smooth grey stone, still warm from her clenched palm. 
Inside the atrium lay a hidden pond, fed by a spring that refused the bay's salt and chop. 
The air smelled of wet slate and mineral cold, a silence deeper than the bay's tides. 
She cast the stone. 
The rings expanded, perfect and transient, crossing the black water in slow mathematics. 
Mira watched them collide with the refracted light and read the pattern the way she read a glaze's hidden flaws. 
She sought the value hidden in the mundane: the worth of ordinary labor, of salt-eaten beams and warehouse windows clouded by a century of steam. 
The rings deepened, and in their intersection she saw the harbor not as the council saw it, but as it longed to be. 
Before everything shifted, she needed to know what deserved to be preserved. 
The mist curled around the atrium, blurring the boundary between glass and air, between reflection and truth. 
Mira traced the outermost ring with a calloused finger, feeling the cold promise of the water. 
The noctilucent clouds thinned, and dawn began to bleed gold along the eastern skyline. 
The blue fire retreated, its reckoning complete, leaving only the honest grey of morning. 
She understood then that the reckonings were not a tally of debts, but an accounting of light. 
Every cracked pane she had ever replaced had trapped a fragment of morning for someone who could not afford more. 
Every warehouse window had held a family's supper glow against the dark. 
The mundane was not empty; it was luminous, if one knew where to look. 
The pond stilled, the rings fading into the black like arithmetic erased from a slate. 
Mira rose, her knees stiff, her heart clear and complicated all at once. 
Her reflection in the water had shown her a woman made of intersections, neither wholly past nor future. 
She turned back toward the harbor as the gulls began their argument with the incoming tide. 
Before everything shifted, she would illuminate value in the mundane. 
She would show the surveyors that the old wharf was not obsolete, but a treasury of accumulated dawns. 
She would restore the lighthouse lens not because it was efficient, but because its prism asked a question the town had forgotten how to answer. 
The cranes would come, she knew, but they need not erase what had already shone. 
Walking out of the atrium, she left the mist to its secrets. 
Behind her, the prism atrium dissolved into pale vapor, its purpose fulfilled. 
She carried the rings' lesson in her chest like a kept promise, warm and unbreakable. 
The dawn was full now, sharp and ordinary and entirely alive. 
Mira stepped onto the pier, ready to meet the shift with open hands and a glazier's quiet fire.
