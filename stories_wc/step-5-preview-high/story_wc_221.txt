The observation deck had been sealed after the shutdown, but the lock still remembered Elara's fingerprints.  She stepped into the long chamber where gravity felt optional and the air hummed with an echoed silence that had outlived the machines.  Once, the time dilation observation deck had been the pride of the colony, a lens through which scientists watched hours stretch into seasons.  Now it stood dark and orphaned, its great windows frosted with the breath of dead instruments.  Elara was the blessed pariah, chosen in youth to endure the dilation and cursed afterward by those who feared what she had seen.  They called her blessed because she had walked through slowed time and returned with her mind intact.  They called her pariah because she no longer matched their seconds, and shared time had become an accusation.  Tonight marked the anniversary of the shutdown, so she carried her grandfather's sketchbook beneath a coat of insulating foil.  Their family custom was simple: on every anniversary, they sketched the night sky from wherever they stood, mapping the stars as a way of holding the year still.  Elara had kept the ritual through exile, through the cold shoulder of the station, and now through the silence of the deck.  She set the book on the central console and watched dust motes hang in a beam of starlight, each mote temporally stretched into a tiny silver thread.  The shutdown had warped the deck's remaining field, so seconds pooled in the corners and minutes dripped from the ceiling.  She had come to turn confusion into exploration, to read the distortion instead of drowning in it.  Her fingers found the edge of the console, where a gilded compass rose applique still gleamed, its points sharp enough to draw blood.  The metal was warm, and when she pressed it, a hairline seam hissed open to reveal a rusted eyepiece.  Elara leaned in, expecting darkness, but instead she caught a glimpse of the sky as it had been before the shutdown.  Stars wheeled in reverse, and the constellation of the Navigator hung low and huge, guiding her eye toward a fixed and patient place.  True north, she realized, was not merely a direction but a promise that some coordinates survive even when time forgets itself.  She opened the sketchbook to a fresh page and began to draw, her pencil moving in time with the slow tides of the room.  The family custom had always been about orientation, she saw now, not navigation.  To sketch the sky was to admit that the world turns, and that the turner remains.  She worked through the night, which in the deck might have been a minute or a decade, and the echoed silence kept her company like a choir of absent voices.  Each line she drew mapped a distortion, each shaded nebula marked a pool of trapped seconds.  The gilded applique grew hotter beneath her palm, as if the deck were offering her its last remaining axis.  When she looked up again, the eyepiece showed not the past but a future in which the windows were clean and the instruments breathed.  In that glimpse, she saw herself no longer pariah but pioneer, and the word blessed settled back into its original shape.  She sketched that sky too, folding the future into the family custom until past and possible converged on the page.  The shutdown had taken the colony's clock, but it had left her the stars.  Elara closed the book, tucked it beneath her coat, and placed her palm once more on the compass rose.  The deck whispered around her, an echoed silence that was no longer empty but expectant.  She walked out into the corridor, no longer a woman displaced from time but a cartographer of it.  Behind her, the gilded points of the applique held their line toward true north, steady as a promise kept.
