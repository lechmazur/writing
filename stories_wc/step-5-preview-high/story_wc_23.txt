The starlit signal station crouched on the ridge like a rusted animal, its windows black after the hydraulics failed.  For eleven years, the great arms had swung freight cars away from the gorge, but now the ropes hung slack and the levers only whispered.  He had come after the last train left, after the crews walked down the mountain, drawn by reverse migration, the slow tide of people moving back toward what they had lost.  They called him the patchwork navigator, a name stitched from rumor because no one remembered which scars were his and which belonged to the dead he had guided home.  His patchwork mind fluctuates with moon phases, sharp as broken glass at the full turn, and dissolving like ash under the new.  It was during the waxing crescent that the first phone rang.  At midnight, the cracked receiver on the office wall shuddered, and a voice like gravel under tide told him to build.  He did not ask what.  He had come to navigate the geography of loss, and he knew enough of grief to understand that such a country required instruments and maps.  In the station's storage room, beneath coal dust and rodent nests, he found the patina bronze censer foot, its once-holy base crusted green with salt and time.  It had been salvaged from a chapel that drowned when the valley flooded, and its weight felt like an anchor for his uncertain hands.  He set it upon the plotting table and watched it settle, its shadow pointing east while the wind outside turned the antenna wires into harp strings.  The midnight calls came every night after that, cryptic and patient.  "One rail, one breath," the voice said.  "The moon remembers what the water took."  He began to build, though his hands shook under the gibbous moon, when his memory swelled with names he could not place and streets he had never walked.  He laid planks across the broken signal platform, fashioning a walkway that led not to the gorge, but back along the ridge where the reverse migrants would come.  The hydraulics were gone, so he built a simpler machine, a counterweighted arm that could swing a lantern across the dark, using the censer foot as the pivot.  Its patina absorbed starlight and released it in a dull green pulse, visible from the switchbacks below.  Each midnight, the phone confirmed his progress.  "You are making a place to stand," the voice said, which he understood meant a place to fall.  By the full moon, his clarity peaked, and he saw the station whole, its angles sharp as a knife, its purpose clear.  He saw that reverse migration was not madness but arithmetic: the living subtracting themselves from empty rooms until only the outline of sorrow remained.  He built benches from rail sleepers, not for rest, but for confession, so that travelers could set down their burdens before climbing.  The first arrivals came on foot, families with carts and children who had never seen the valley before it vanished.  They spoke of lost addresses, of kitchens swallowed by reservoir water, of a language that no longer had listeners.  He guided them not with maps, but with the censer foot, which he set upon each junction where the path forked.  Its shadow shifted with lunar light, stretching long at the dark of the moon and pulling tight at the full, revealing which routes were safe and which led only to deeper absence.  On the night of the new moon, the voice on the phone changed.  "The final rail is yourself," it said.  He understood then that the midnight caller had been his own echo, or perhaps the station itself, speaking through copper and silence.  He built the last section alone, nailing a simple wooden sign above the platform: Reverse Migration, All Welcome.  Then he sat with the censer foot in his lap and waited.  The hydraulics never returned, but the lantern swung, and the path held.  People came and wept and continued onward, and the patchwork navigator offered them what raw solace he could, which was not comfort but direction.  He could not heal the geography of loss, yet he could keep its trails clear, one plank, one shadow, one cryptic midnight call at a time.  Under the starlit signal station, the reverse migration moved like a slow river, and he stood at its source, patchwork and whole, finally home.
