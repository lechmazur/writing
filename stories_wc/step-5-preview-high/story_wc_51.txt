After the first snowmelt, the bronze grotto breathed with a sound like slow water remembering ice. 
Above its ribbed mouth, the starlight came down in thin spears, touching the wet stone where a fractal weaver sat cross-legged. 
She had no name that could be spoken aloud, because the grotto did not echo but rather absorbed every syllable into its shingled hush. 
The hush was layered there like slate on a roof, each stratum of silence overlapping the last until the quiet had depth and texture. 
She was boundary-lessly trapped, able to wander the cavern for as long as her legs would carry her, yet every path curved back to the same starlit center. 
The weaver collected the weights of different burdens, not her own, but those left behind by wanderers who climbed down after the thaw to wash their grief in the spring-fed pool. 
Each burden arrived as a strange density in the chest, a heaviness that could be peeled away like wet wool and set upon the altar stone. 
Her tools were simple: a coil of silver thread that split endlessly into smaller copies of itself, and a kerosene lamp burner that held a flame she never had to feed. 
The burner was her anchor, its brass collar crusted green with age, its wick burning with a light that did not consume fuel but rather distilled intention from the dark. 
To make a weight permanent, she worked by orienting properly, turning each burden until its hidden axis aligned with the grotto's deeper geometry. 
When the alignment came, the burden would begin to calcify, hardening from soft sorrow into a pearl of dense, mineral truth. 
Then she wove it into the fractal pattern on the grotto wall, where each calcified stone repeated the shape of the whole, smaller and smaller, until the eye could no longer follow the recursion. 
It was a whalebone reverie that governed her days, all pale arches and remembered tides, a dreaming that rose from the mineral ribs of the cave like a leviathan's song slowed to the pace of moss. 
The snowmelt water dripped from the ceiling in regular intervals, each drop carrying a different weight she had measured before: a mother's fear, a soldier's regret, a lover's silence. 
She recorded them not as stories but as mass, feeling the gravitational pull of each one in her wrists as she held them against the flame. 
Some weights were light as breath and calcified into hollow shells that sang when the wind moved through the grotto's fissures. 
Others sank her hands to the stone and turned to anchors of black marble, immovable, their density making the air around them tremble. 
The fractal weaver did not judge which burdens deserved preservation; she only oriented, weighed, and wove, letting the shingled hush decide what should remain. 
Over years the walls became a map of human sorrow, a repeating filigree of calcified burdens that caught the starlight and threw it back in patterns of cold comfort. 
Sometimes a traveler would return, seeking the weight they had left behind, and the weaver would point to the wall where their grief had become a single tessera in the infinite mosaic. 
They would touch it, and the shingled hush would rise around them like a tide of felt, and they would understand that some burdens could only be carried once they had been given form. 
On the night the last snowmelt drained from the high peaks, the weaver found a new weight waiting on the altar, heavier than any before, and knew it was her own. 
She held it against the kerosene lamp burner and began to turn it slowly, by orienting properly, until the burden's axis locked into place with a sound like a rib settling into a spine. 
As it began to calcify, she saw that its shape was the grotto itself, the endless paths, the starlight, the layered hush, all of it folded into a density she had carried without knowing. 
With reverent hands she wove it into the final empty place on the wall, completing the fractal, and in the whalebone reverie of that completed pattern, the boundary-lessly trapped weaver finally became the architecture of her own peace.
