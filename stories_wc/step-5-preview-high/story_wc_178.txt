In the ancient Babylonian palace reimagined by a dynasty that trusted geometry, the cedar halls stood quiet between regimes. 
A frugal astronomer named Tabni had stayed behind because the library paid in barley and no one else could repair the water clocks. 
She patched cracked conduits, measured lamp oil in spoonfuls, and ignored rumors of spirits in the empty throne room. 
One dusk she uncovered a baked floor tile whose glazed face was divided into a black-and-red square grid. 
The magic square tile was warm, as though it had been waiting for someone patient enough to read it. 
It had no royal seal, no inscription of victory, only a worn groove along one edge where fingers had pressed it a thousand times. 
Tabni knew the spacing of its dots as a star clock, but the strokes between them hid something older than royal propaganda. 
They were encrypted devotion, the private language of palace astronomers who had loved the silent sky more than the kings they served. 
Each sign was a threshold: a cuneiform wedge for distance, a mirrored glyph for silence, and a repeated notch that Tabni understood as a name she was not meant to pronounce. 
Lacking money for wax tablets, she worked through schematic representations, sketching the palace on the backs of ration lists with ash and reed ink. 
She had learned geometry from accountants, not priests, and now she used their dull arithmetic to translate a prayer. 
Her diagrams aligned courtyards, drains, and stair angles until the palace itself appeared as an instrument and the tile as its key. 
For three dusky evenings she labored between regimes, when absent priests left the chalk jars unguarded and no guards asked about closed drains. 
She had remained not to crown a successor but to seal a covenant with the unknown, asking why celestial order survived every broken empire. 
When she pressed the tile into the seam her diagram had marked, the floor hissed, and a slab pivoted back to uncloak a stair winding down toward blue light. 
Dust drifted up in the lamplight, bright as slow snow, and for a moment the empty palace seemed to inhale. 
The chamber below held no treasure, only a domed room lined with silver mica and star-shaped sockets cut into the walls. 
At its center stood a bronze astrolabe, rusted yet calibrated to a measure more honest than any decree the palace had ever proclaimed. 
Tabni realized the tile was a password left by astronomers who had built the entire palace as a machine for devotion. 
Beneath the mica were alcoves for oil lamps that never smoked, proving the designers had planned for generations of patient readers rather than one glorious night. 
The empirically ironic truth was that the empire had spent fortunes begging for divine favor while the exact answer hid beneath an empty court. 
She cleaned the astrolabe with vinegar and a scrap of wool, since frugality had taught her that precision mattered more than abundance. 
Setting the magic square tile into its socket, she watched schematic constellations rise through the mica and cross the vault like slow rivers of dusk. 
Tabni spoke the only vow she knew, promising measure, honesty, and care to whatever intelligence listened beyond the visible stars. 
The chamber answered without thunder, in a dusky harmony of gears settling, water resuming its ancient rhythm beneath the floors. 
Outside, the river went on moving as it always did, indifferent to crowns, yet in that chamber every dripping joint seemed to agree with the turning stars. 
A covenant with the unknown had been sealed, not by wealth or sacrifice, but by a careful mind reading love encoded as geometry. 
Nothing in the courtyard changed that night, yet the silence there afterward felt less like abandonment and more like an agreement. 
When the new regime finally entered the city, they found a working water clock and a modest astronomer asleep at her inked desk. 
They never noticed the hidden socket in the court or the unfamiliar star burning above the palace in a pattern only Tabni understood. 
She woke at first light, counted her remaining oil, and smiled because the sky had answered her exactly as her charts predicted.
