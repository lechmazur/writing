Mira had spent thirty years shaping hedges into labyrinths that obeyed the restless angles of hyperbolic geometry. 
Her workshop stood at the heart of the living bridge garden, where roots had been trained into suspension spans and willows wove themselves into handrails over black water. 
She did not build for visitors who might admire the view, but for the silence between leaves, because Mira wanted to discover the texture of time. 
Every dawn she walked the garden's curves, collecting the fading photographs that tourists left on benches like molted skin. 
The images showed brides, children, a bridge that no longer existed; yet as the colors bled away, each picture seemed to reveal not what had been lost, but what had always lain beneath. 
She believed the garden remembered more faithfully than paper, and so she grafted her mazes to follow the same branching logic that hedgerows used to outgrow boundaries. 
One mist-thick morning, while pruning a hornbeam arch, she found a halo fragment wedged between two roots that had swallowed an iron bell centuries ago. 
It was no larger than a thumbnail, a crescent of cold gold that hummed when she turned it toward the light, and in its curve she saw the same hyperbolic curve she had spent a lifetime teaching to yew and box. 
Mira pocketed the relic and returned to her drafting table, where she spread the fading photographs across vellum and began to measure their warp. 
Each crease corresponded to a node in her garden; each fold marked a place where the living bridge had crossed not water but duration. 
She understood then that the halo fragment was not a saintly relic but a key, and that the garden itself had been waiting for someone fluent in its crooked grammar. 
To chart the deeper folds, Mira wove the fragment into a pendant of willow fiber and hung it at the center of her newest maze, a hyperbolic labyrinth whose paths doubled back through themselves every eleven paces. 
On the first night the pendant began to glow, the hedges exhaled a green fog, and Mira stepped not into the next corridor but into a version of the garden that had stood three hundred years before. 
She saw the original bridge gardener, a woman with soil under her nails and the same hunger in her eyes, laying the first root-bridge across a ravine while singing to the water. 
Mira reached out to touch the woman's shoulder, but her hand passed through as though the past were smoke, and in that instant she realized she was retrocognitively trapped inside the loop of the garden's memory. 
The fading photographs in her satchel grew hot, then began to disintegrate, their fibers curling into ash that drifted upward instead of falling, as if gravity had become a local opinion. 
Each mote of ash carried a fragment of scene, and Mira perceived that the garden did not exist in a single timeline but in a non-Euclidean weave where hyperbolic geometry let adjacent centuries touch. 
She was not lost; she was nested. 
The quaking reverie of the place settled into her bones, a trembling awe that made every leaf seem a metronome counting a rhythm older than fruit or frost. 
With each step backward through the maze, Mira felt the texture of time directly, not as a smooth stream but as a coarse fabric of root hairs, of rust, of light caught in the sticky teeth of bud scales. 
She wept, and her tears did not fall but hung in the hyperbolic air, becoming small lenses that showed her the bridge garden's future, where her own hedges would outlast stone and iron. 
Yet the halo fragment could not hold so much attention without cost; its gold skin cracked, and it began to disintegrate into the same upward ash that had consumed the photographs. 
As the crescent dissolved, the corridor of centuries folded shut, and Mira found herself kneeling in the wet loam of the living bridge garden, alive and shaking, with no relic in her palm. 
The maze around her had changed; the hedges now leaned in ways that suggested both embrace and vertigo, and she knew she had planted them so. 
She rose, brushed the soil from her knees, and walked toward the oldest root-bridge, where she would sit until dusk and teach new apprentices to listen for the quaking reverie hidden inside every green wall.
