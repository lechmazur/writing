Odile arrived at the drift-ice marina as a euthanasia tourist, carrying a sealed appointment card and the heirloom lace parasol tip her grandmother once used to test thin ice. 
The clinic above the harbor promised a quiet, legal ending, but the sea had other plans. 
That night a pressure ridge split the inlet, and the floor of her room became a moving raft. 
She woke in a rescue canteen after waking up changed, her memories loosened and bright, as though the accident had folded them into new shapes. 
Her old certainty was gone; she grips reality loosely, the way a child holds a balloon string in moving wind. 
When the coordinator asked for volunteers, Odile stepped forward. 
She was sent to the salvage deck through disaster relief volunteering, sorting crates of tinned milk and salt-caked rope. 
It was honest work, and it kept her hands busy while her mind relearned the harbor. 
The veteran rescuer, Sander, taught her to trawl the shallow channels for trapped boats, dragging a weighted listening line beneath the ice. 
He said the floe spoke before it broke, and that a rescuer had to learn its grammar. 
Odile thought of the appointment she had missed, and of her own wish to learn the sound of approaching endings. 
At first the ice sounded only like distance. 
Then, holding the heirloom lace parasol tip against a frozen piling, she felt a faint tremor travel up the lace's steel rib. 
She began tapping the harbor each dawn, counting the silences between knocks. 
Sander called the rhythm a fold in patience, a place where waiting bent without breaking and the heart could rest. 
The phrase stayed with her, and she folded it into her shifts like a warm stone in a pocket. 
Volunteers came from flooded farms and broken ferries, and they did not ask why she had come. 
They only saw that she arrived early, stayed late, and wrote down every echo she heard. 
In the evenings she catalogued the sounds: gull cries, hull seams groaning, generators coughing, water moving under the blue roof of ice. 
Each ending she recorded was small, and none of them belonged to her. 
Yet the harbor taught her that an ending could be attended without being claimed. 
She stopped visiting the clinic stairwell where her appointment card had been filed. 
Instead she trawled deeper water, where the drowned hulls of a fishing fleet lay scattered like dropped coins. 
The work made her calmer, though her thoughts still drifted. 
Some mornings she woke believing the marina was a ship, and she would grip reality loosely until the deck steadied. 
Sander did not correct her; he simply handed her gloves and told her the tide would tell her the truth. 
One storm afternoon the wind came off the pack with a sound like a held breath released. 
Odile felt it in the parasol tip, a humming that matched the ache behind her sternum. 
She followed the listening line until it caught, and she called the coordinates without hesitation. 
They pulled two brothers from a capsized skiff before the dark closed. 
Their shivering gratitude was a windborne solace, not loud, but enough to warm her hands. 
After that she no longer measured her life against the clinic's quiet door. 
She still wanted to learn the sound of approaching endings, but now she wanted to hear them early enough to offer help. 
Winter set the harbor in glass, and the volunteers grew quiet with exhaustion. 
Odile kept to her rounds, tapping the ice, trawling the channels, waiting in the fold of patience she had been given. 
When a floe groaned near the outer breakwater, she recognized the low summons and sent the sleds out. 
They reached a tourist caught on a melting slab in time, and the rescue cost no one a life. 
That night she sat with Sander on the salvage deck, the heirloom lace parasol tip resting between them like a key. 
He asked if she was still a euthanasia tourist. 
She said she had come to end a sound she could not name, and had found a different music underneath it. 
The wind moved through the rigging, and the marina answered with a long, forgiving hush. 
She did not pretend the ache was gone. 
But after waking up changed, she had learned to hold the world lightly and still choose the work. 
In the morning there would be more channels to trawl, more cracks to listen for, more patience to fold. 
She would be there.
