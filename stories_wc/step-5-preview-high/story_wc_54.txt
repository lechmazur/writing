The carnival had left town the week before, and the abandoned spaceport passenger terminal was Mara's job again. 
She was a curious civil engineer intern, three weeks from her degree, assigned to survey the concourse for water damage before demolition crews arrived. 
The terminal had once launched ships to the outer rings; now its ribbed ceiling sagged over empty seats beneath dark departure boards. 
Mara walked the length of the main hall with a clipboard, a headlamp, and a notebook filled with the private reason she had taken the job: to puzzle out new equations for hope. 
The old engineers had built flow into every surface, and she loved them for it, but she could not yet write what she felt as something that could bear load. 
Behind Gate Nine she found a heap of carnival trash and a bent piece of brass stamped with the worn numerals of an explorer's sextant piece. 
It was the lower arc of an instrument, its degree scale intact, and someone had used it as a coaster for somewhere hotter than this. 
She pocketed it, telling herself it was salvage. 
That night, in a trailer smelling of wet cement, she set the brass arc on her structural drawings and saw what it wanted to measure. 
The terminal's original guidance spire still stood outside, a skeletal pylon aimed at the empty sky, but its power feed had been spliced decades ago by festival wiring. 
Mara returned at dawn with her tools, conduit, and the carnival's abandoned lighting rig, which cleanup crews had judged too tangled to bother with. 
She was functionally creative in the way of people who have grown up fixing things with what the household could afford. 
The concourse itself was a maze of intentions, its corridors bent by three generations of plans that had never agreed. 
The builders had wanted arrival; the carnival had wanted spectacle; the town had wanted escape. 
Mara began to read the building the way she read shear diagrams, by following forces instead of signs. 
In the subfloor chase she found old coolant lines, still pressurized, their flow redirected by the carnival's hasty plumbing into decorative fountains. 
She followed the ventilation trunks, the cable trays, and the runoff grates, and she mapped them as vectors. 
Where they crossed, she imagined variables; where they dead-ended, she imagined constants. 
The sextant piece gave her angles no digital level could trust, because it had been calibrated by someone who once navigated by starlight and stubbornness. 
With it she aligned the lighting rig so its reflectors threw dawn light down the service shafts, and the terminal seemed to wake a little. 
Then she began to redirect flows. 
She bled pressure from the old coolant loop into a turbine scavenged from the carnival's portable power plant, then sent its output through the lighting rig into the guidance spire's battery bank. 
She split the ventilation stream to cool the batteries, and rerouted runoff into a heat sink that kept the circuit from cooking itself. 
Each correction was small, and none of them were heroic, but the building answered like a held breath released. 
The terminal had been built to move people, she realized, and hope might be a current that refused to stay in the wrong channel. 
She wrote that in the margin, then crossed it out because it sounded like a poster. 
Beneath the concourse she found the passenger tram's control spine, its logic board corroded, its governor clutch seized. 
The tram was not meant to fly, only to carry travelers from gate to ship, but its motor could turn the old beacon. 
Mara worked through the night with the stoic wonder that had drawn her to engineering, a calm delight in forces doing exactly what they must. 
She redirected the tram's auxiliary power through the conduit nest, using the sextant piece to set the blade pitch of a fan that drove the final stage. 
With the carnival gone, she stood in the empty terminal and watched the guidance spire take a slow, amber breath. 
She had not resurrected old glory, but through redirected flows she had resurrected one honest function: the ability to tell the sky someone was still here. 
The beacon pulsed once, twice, a rhythm old freight pilots would have known. 
Mara sat on an empty bench, the brass arc warm in her hand, and let the redirected flows settle into a steady hum. 
She did not know who would answer, but the equation in her notebook had changed. 
It was no longer a proof that hope would arrive; it was a system for bearing its weight while it did.
