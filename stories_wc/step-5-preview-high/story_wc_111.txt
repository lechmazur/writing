The garden maze had outlived the era when luck could be guessed.  After the invention of the luckometer, every harvest was assigned by measured fortune.  The withered vine dresser named Cass remained because he was wisely pragmatic about what numbers could not price.  He kept his cracked marbles in a tin that had once held harvest cake.  The tin rattled when he walked, and the maze corridors carried the sound like rain.  Each marble had been a lottery ball for the old maze races, and each still carried a hairline scar from the counting chute.  Before the Bureau, children rolled them through hoop mazes to wager on which sprout would ripen first.  Cass did not call them broken.  He called them evidence.  A crack was not damage but a record of pressure survived.  Every dawn he polished the collection with oil and a square of wool, working until the cracks glimmered like deliberate seams.  The wool darkened, and the marbles emerged warm, their flaws transformed into lines of silver.  Then he walked the green corridors and chose one small triumph to commemorate.  He never hurried, because haste was a luxury for those who trusted luck to outrun neglect.  A rescued shoot got a blue marble set beneath its leaves.  The garland was simple, only ribbon and clover, but it was made with the precision of a coronation.  A vine that had clung to a dry wall earned a green one pressed into the soil at its roots.  He spoke to the wall as if it were a tired elder, then left the marble gleaming in the dust.  He decorated each victory elaborately, weaving garlands around the marker and singing the plant's name until the hedges seemed to lean closer.  The officials from the Luck Bureau considered such rituals harmless, but they also considered Cass unprofitable.  They did not see that his methods were a deliberately mended deception, not denial.  His maze had a measurable luck quotient so low that no investor would risk seed.  The charts showed no fortune, only stubborn roots.  Cass wanted to prove that gardens know their gardeners, so he designed a mended deception that the Bureau could measure without seeing.  He would let the Bureau measure the garden as a machine while the garden answered him as a living partner.  He began grafting withered rootstock onto thriving vines, then training the healthy growth along dead paths so the maze appeared prosperous only where he had walked.  It was a lie in shape and a truth in care, a mended deception that required more honesty than luck had ever asked.  The hedges bloomed in ribbons and withered in shadow, exactly as his hands arranged them.  Visitors photographed the ribbons and ignored the shadow, so his small victories became the maze's public record.  When an auditor arrived, Cass greeted her with a polished marble held out like a coin.  It was cracked, and he told her so before she could inspect it.  A lie would have hidden the flaw; a mended deception made it the centerpiece.  He said the garden had given it to him, scar and all, and he was wisely pragmatic enough to keep what still rolled true.  She measured the rows by luck score and found only failure in the dry sections.  Cass asked her to follow him to a withered arch he had repaired with hidden grafts.  There, in the poorest quadrant, a single vine had burst into blossom around a red marble.  He commemorated it that evening with lanterns, honey wine, and a speech praising its stubbornness.  The auditor returned the next week with instruments and found the same blossom, then another beside it, blooming exactly where the polished marbles rested.  Luck metrics insisted those spots should have withered.  The instruments could measure probability, not devotion.  Yet the vines knew the hands that pruned them, and the Bureau's own charts recorded an anomaly they could not dismiss.  The anomaly had a pattern, and the pattern was a trail of polished marbles.  Cass never claimed magic.  He claimed reciprocity.  He had simply commemorated every small victory elaborately until the garden understood that its efforts were witnessed.  The garden knew its gardener because he answered it in attention, in water, and in the small ceremonial care that made survival worth recording.  In the end the maze was not saved by fortune but by proof that a withered place can bloom in the exact shape of the one who tends it.  Some leaves withered at the edges, but the center kept blooming, and the blooming was his signature.  In a world of measured chance, that answer was the wildest variable.
