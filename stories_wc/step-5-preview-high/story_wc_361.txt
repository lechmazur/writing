Kest adjusted the strap of the relic satchel for the tenth time and tried to ignore the familiar tremor in the hands.  The aurora-lit ridgeway unwound above the cloud line, a ribbon of pale stone and singing ice that connected the last outposts of the waking world.  Each night Kest carried sealed vials, cracked icons, and ash across that high road, a jittery relic courier whose nerves never quite settled no matter how many deliveries survived the wind.  The work itself had grown routine, a sequence of checkpoints and signatures, yet Kest walked with a secret motivation to coax wonder from the routine.  The means lay against the chest, a dragonfly-wing pendant strung on silver thread, the last gift from an absent mentor who had vanished into legend before the season turned.  Master Orlen had not died, exactly; he had stepped across the fade from memory to myth, leaving behind only lessons and the pendant, which hummed when Kest's pulse raced.  The ridgeway was a corridor of thresholds, where brief encounters sparked and died like matches in the storm.  Travelers passed in flurries of frost, exchanging nods, coins, and warnings, and then the dark swallowed them.  Kest could not bear such vanishing, and so had learned to use the pendant's hidden attribute: it extends brief encounters, stretching a nod into a conversation and a warning into a shared story.  That night, near the spine of the ridge, Kest found a woman kneeling beside a split seed pod, her breath clouding the air.  She was a cartographer, she said, mapping roads that were being forgotten even as she walked them.  Her voice was flat with exhaustion, and Kest recognized the dull weight of repetition, the way wonder starves when the world is reduced to routes and tariffs.  Without thinking, Kest opened the collar and let the pendant swing free, its translucent wings catching the green wash of the aurora.  The dragonfly-wing pendant began to vibrate sympathetically, answering the tremor in Kest's hands and the slower shiver in the woman's shoulders.  It did not sing aloud; rather it resonated at a frequency that made the frost crystals on the stones dance and the woman's lantern flame lean inward.  Time stretched around them, the brief encounter extending into a pocket of impossible silence while the aurora rippled overhead.  In that suspended space Kest placed the split seed pod upon a flat rock and held the pendant close.  The vibration traveled through silver and wing, down into the husk, and the dormant grain began to germinate.  A pale shoot uncurled, drinking the green light, and the woman gasped as if waking from a long sleep.  She touched the sprout, then the pendant, and her eyes cleared into a brightness that no map could hold.  "I had forgotten," she whispered, "that stone was once a seed, and the road was once a wandering."  Kest nodded, throat tight, because this was the work Orlen had entrusted across the fade from memory to myth.  Not delivery, but transfiguration: to coax wonder from the routine until the world remembered how to dream.  The aurora flared, and the extended moment began to close, the shoot folding back into seed with a soft chime.  The woman tucked the pod into her coat, her step lighter, already sketching what she could not yet name.  Kest continued along the ridgeway, satchel heavy, hands still jittery, but the tremor now felt like a tuning fork rather than a fault.  Near the ridge's end, where the path narrowed to a thread of snow, an old man waited with a lantern and a sealed urn.  His face was a ledger of miles, and his eyes held the resignation of one who believed all crossings were mere transfer.  Kest took the urn, and for a moment their fingers brushed; the pendant warmed.  Again the attribute engaged, extending the brief encounter into a breathing eternity of ice and light.  The old man set down his lantern, and in the pause Kest opened the urn just enough to release a handful of ash mixed with seed.  The pendant's wings beat in a silent rhythm, and by vibrating sympathetically they made the ash and grief resonate as one.  The seeds caught the resonance, and one by one they began to germinate in the frozen crust, sprouting filaments of silver that anchored the path.  The old man watched wonder germinate from loss, his shoulders dropping as the ash gave way to rooted light.  "Orlen said you would come," he murmured, naming the absent mentor as though he were a season rather than a man.  Kest blinked, stunned, but the encounter was already fading, the old man walking on with a stride that seemed to buoy the stones themselves.  Kest smiled and walked into the waiting aurora.
