After the empathy virus, the villagers of Gallowstep measured trust in flinches and held their grief like cut glass.  The hanging orchard in the ravine battered by storms had become a second nervous system, roots gripping terraces while rain worked the soil loose.  Wren was a rational truffle forager who read spore maps and rainfall instead of prophecies.  Since the virus she had been involuntarily truthful; every false word scraped her throat like a burr.  That made her useless at bedside comfort and invaluable at rationing seed.  Her motive was not romance or revolt but a stubborn wish to cultivate psychological safety in a town that had forgotten privacy.  The virus had made everyone transparent, and transparency without shelter had become another kind of storm.  She kept a ledger of which terraces held, which trees wept resin, and which neighbors could not yet be left alone.  The council wanted total candor enforced by ritual, but Wren had watched candor break people who needed time to heal.  One storm-dark evening, hunting truffles beneath a fallen support beam, she found a dead courier.  His pack had split open on truffle-scented roots, and inside the oilcloth lay an antique glass eye.  The milky blue iris hid a hard black pupil.  She knew at once that it was a smuggled relic, forbidden because it predated the Board of Candor.  Her honesty would not let her pretend she had not seen it, so she wrapped it in her own coat and said nothing.  Silence, she reasoned, was not the same as a lie.  For three nights she descended into the drowned seminary, where the storm had cracked the floor and left the shelves swollen.  The approved medical books taught breathing drills and mandatory confession, but the discredited textbooks whispered of older, rougher mercies.  Their footnotes spoke through rumored cures: cold iron on the sternum, honey poultices, and a prosthetic orb placed in an empty socket to occlude the empathic flood.  Wren had lost her left eye to a flying branch years before, and the antique glass eye fit the socket as though it had been cut for her.  She called it an experiment, not a miracle.  At dusk she pressed the glass into the socket and felt the orchard's chorus narrow, not vanish.  Fear still reached her, but it arrived like a letter instead of a flood.  For the first time since the virus, she could decide when to speak and how much to carry.  The next morning the council learned of the antique eye from a seed merchant who had seen her lift her patch.  They came to the orchard chapel with lanterns and called the relic a sacred instrument of exposure.  Wren could not deny it, and she did not try; she set the eye on the table and told them exactly where she had found it.  They asked if she wore it, and the truth left her mouth before caution could reach it.  Yes, she said, and the word cost her the chance to hide the method from them.  The elder magistrate wanted the eye mounted above the cistern, where every supplicant would be seen and known without defense.  Wren argued that exposure was a tool like fire, not a god.  That night the storm returned, battering the ravine until the hanging orchard shuddered and a retaining wall gave way.  Water poured down the terraces, and a child's terror went through the crowd like a live wire.  Mothers clutched their heads; men who had faced hail began to weep with a stranger's memories.  Wren ran for the cistern with the antique glass eye warm in her pocket.  She pressed it into place, occluding the channel that let every cry in the ravine take root in her chest.  The world narrowed to ropes, mud, and the names of people she could still reach.  With that hard clarity she directed the weakest to the high shelves and the strongest to the broken wall.  When the water fell back, the council stood in the mud, silent for once, and looked at her with something nearer to shame than awe.  Wren gave them the method she had copied through rumored cures in discredited textbooks: smoked glass, wax, leather straps, and careful instruction.  The relic would stay in the chapel, but not as an idol; it would be the pattern from which anyone could learn to occlude feeling and restore it at will.  They made occluders for the mourners, the couriers, and the night watch, and they practiced intervals of privacy like grafting.  Wren still could not lie, but she could choose when to speak, and the orchard held with fractured grace.
