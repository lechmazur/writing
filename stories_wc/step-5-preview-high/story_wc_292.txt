The experimental weather station had been spongey since the jet stream shifted; its corridors were padded with damp insulation that swallowed footsteps and returned them as a soft, waiting dread. 
Mara Quill, an idealistic map librarian, had come north to honor an ancestor's overlooked bravery by questioning the verdict that had erased him from every reliable chart. 
She was nervously confident, the way a held breath is sure of its own trembling. 
The station's directors called it reversible exile now, a punishment of damp weather and drifting air, not execution, and they kept careful ledgers of who might be brought back. 
Mara did not trust their carefulness; the building itself felt like a sponge squeezed around her thoughts, so each idea arrived soaked and blurred. 
She had found the shipwrecked journals in a pressure locker, waterlogged volumes from a balloon tender that had broken apart above the shifted jet stream years before. 
The entries were verse, not weather data, and Mara had spent the last three nights decoding them through rhymes that turned like barometric wheels. 
Each stanza was a coordinate and a warning, and in the third volume she found her ancestor's initials scratched beside a line about a coffin nail. 
Anselm Quill had not abandoned his post, as the official charts claimed; he had flown into the new storm corridor to warn a downwind town that the directors were willing to let drown. 
They had reversed the story, then reversed him, sending him out past the ridge where the air behaved like wet wool and no instrument held a steady reading. 
The journals insisted that reversible exile could be turned backward if the exile's name was questioned aloud and anchored by copper from his coffin. 
Mara had taken the copper nail from the station's reliquary, a blackened spike pried from her ancestor's coffin after the sea sent it back. 
It was cold and light in her palm, as if it had been waiting for someone foolish enough to finish the pattern. 
Director Hale found her at midnight, his boots making no sound on the spongey floor. 
He said the station was a kindness, that the atmosphere after the jet stream shifts had become unlivable and that only controlled removal kept the coastal towns from panic. 
Mara looked at the coded stanzas spread across the table and felt the old dread soaking through her sleeves, spongey and patient. 
Then she questioned him, quietly, about the balloon tender's final transmission and about why Anselm's rescue orders had been filed under weather loss. 
Hale's mouth tightened, and the station's walls seemed to absorb the silence and swell with it. 
He reached for the alarm strip, but Mara pressed the copper nail into the chart table, using it to mark the name Anselm Quill in the wax seal of the last journal. 
The coded stanzas had told her this was not magic but a reversal of the exile formula: question the false record, anchor the true one, and let the shifted air return what it had borrowed. 
Somewhere beyond the ridge, a horn answered, and the pressure in the room dropped so suddenly that the dread around her pores opened like a sponge drinking rain. 
Hale staggered, saying it could not be reversed, that the record was set, but his voice was absorbed by the spongey dark and came back thinner. 
Mara kept her hand on the nail and read the final stanza aloud, each line a question she had been storing since she first learned her ancestor's name. 
The words were damp and simple: Who flew east? Who refused to lie? Who carried the town's warning into the teeth of the new wind? 
As she spoke, the station's instruments began to fail in an orderly way, needles sinking as if into soft ground, and the corridors gave up their swallowed footsteps all at once. 
A figure began to form at the threshold, not quite solid, as though the air were remembering a shape it had been forced to misplace. 
Mara knew that reversible exile had a cost, that everything returned came back through the same spongey dread, and she was idealistic enough to believe it was worth paying. 
She lifted the copper nail from the table and held it out like a question, and the station seemed to lean toward her, waiting to answer. 
Behind the glass, the jet stream settled into its new course, and in the hush that followed, Anselm Quill took one spongey step toward the light.
