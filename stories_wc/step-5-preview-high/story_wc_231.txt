At nautical twilight, the opal dock in mist seemed less like wood and more like a breath held against the sea. 
Isolde Vane, eccentric lighthouse keeper, stood at its end with her coat collar turned up and her pockets full of unscrewed jar lids. 
She labeled everything, including silence, and argued with gulls as if they owed library books. 
The lighthouse did not burn oil. 
It burned borrowed memories, traded by sailors who wanted to survive one more crossing and would leave behind a mother's face or the smell of first snow. 
Isolde kept those recollections in blue glass jars, labeled with the year borrowed and the exact shape of the emptiness left behind. 
She had learned, softly persistent as tide-water working a crack in stone, that a missing piece kept the outline of its absence. 
That was why she investigated the stranger not by his words, for he had none, but by observing gaps. 
He had come to the opal dock without oars, lantern, or name. 
His thin coat was pinned with a dried Edelweiss boutonniere, its woolly heads faded to old paper. 
Edelweiss grew above timberline, not near the coast, and Isolde had seen it once before on a boy who swore to return before the pass closed. 
The stranger looked at her with the exhaustion of a man who had walked a long way through his own mind. 
Then he pressed the boutonniere into her hand with fingers cold and precise. 
The gesture felt witnessed by the fog itself. 
Before she could ask his name, he stepped backward off the dock into water that did not splash. 
The mist closed behind him like a ledger shutting, and Isolde was alone with the boutonniere and the need to understand. 
She wanted, more than sleep, to decode a stranger's final gesture. 
She returned to the lamp room and laid the boutonniere beside the memory ledger. 
The lamp was dark because the borrowed memories had run low, and the lens looked like a hole punched through the room. 
Isolde lit a candle and began to investigate. 
She started by observing gaps. 
In the ledger, the last entries were not missing but inverted, as though someone had copied the absence instead of the memory. 
One space on the jar shelf between 1893 and 1894 held no dust, so something had been removed recently, not decades ago. 
On her chart, the pass above the village was marked with a white gap, and she could not remember whether the boy had returned. 
The softly persistent doubt she had carried for years pressed against that gap like a tongue against a missing tooth. 
She opened the nearest jar and borrowed the memory inside: rain on a slate roof, a harmonica, and the silence of a house where someone waits up. 
It was not hers, yet it fit the shape of her chest so well that she had to sit down. 
She held the dried Edelweiss boutonniere and let the borrowed memory move through it. 
The flower was not a token of love, nor a payment. 
It was a bookmark. 
In the memory it carried, she saw the boy placing edelweiss in a jar and whispering that Isolde had traded away her own memory of him to keep the light burning through the winter of the great storm. 
The gap on her chart was not an error. 
It was the place where her own life had been borrowed. 
The stranger had followed the one thing the lighthouse could not consume: a flower dried by altitude, not by the sea. 
His final gesture had been an instruction: give back what is missing. 
Isolde carried the boutonniere and the borrowed memory down to the opal dock, and the fog leaned away from her lantern. 
She knelt where the mist still held the shape of his withdrawal. 
She placed the boutonniere in the empty space between 1893 and 1894, and spoke the boy's name aloud until it stopped feeling like a rumor. 
The lighthouse shivered, and the lamp filled not with strangers' recollections but with her own: frost on a pass, a boy's laugh, the sting of a promise, and the long gray loneliness she had kept at bay. 
It was a hard light, and it burned clean. 
By true dark the fishing boats found their way home through thinning mist. 
The villagers would say the keeper had stopped collecting other people's pasts. 
They were wrong. 
Isolde still kept the borrowed memories, but now they were guests rather than foundations. 
She walked the opal dock at nautical twilight as though looking for someone, yet when the fog came in she stood inside her own light, softly persistent against the sea, and let her isolation be witnessed rather than borrowed.
