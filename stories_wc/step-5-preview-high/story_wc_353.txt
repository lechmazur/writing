The siskin alder grove lay in an intensely quiet hush, as if every leaf were listening for a sound it had never been allowed to hear. 
After the hedgehog uncurled at the base of the forgotten sculpture, Mira Vane stepped over the moss and began her work. 
She was a tree-song sculptor, and her trade was older than the silence that had settled over the valley. 
Mira had come to the grove to create music in a silent world, but she knew the grove would not yield a melody to anyone who merely demanded one. 
Long ago, engineers had hung porcelain insulators along the alder limbs to carry voices through wire, and when the wires fell, the grove had kept the shards like dull teeth. 
Mira believed those objects remembered resonance. 
She lifted a blue-gray porcelain insulator from her satchel and fitted it against a split in an old alder, testing the tree as another musician might test a string. 
Her mind was symphonically minded; she heard single notes as movements waiting to be assembled, and silence as a chord with its parts hidden. 
Yet inspiration would not come by wandering. 
It came by analyzing stress cracks in a long-forgotten sculpture half buried near the stream. 
The sculpture was a carving of two hands cupping water, though the water had cracked away from the palms years before. 
Mira traced the fractures with her fingertips, reading them the way a healer reads a pulse. 
One crack ran upward in a panic; another widened slowly, as if the wood had been trying to breathe through a wound. 
She understood then that the maker had hurried, sealing the grain against its own seasons, and that the sculpture had never been allowed to rest. 
This was her method and her ethic both: empathy in motion, the discipline of entering another body's strain and moving with it rather than against it. 
She set the porcelain insulator against the split limb and began to strum, softly, so the first note rose like steam from wet bark. 
The sound was small, yet it traveled. 
Siskins flickered among the alders, not in alarm but in attendance, as if the grove had been waiting for a signal it could trust. 
Mira did not force the tempo. 
She let the cracks tell her where the tree was tender, and she placed her fingers there, strumming around the knots that still held rain and winter. 
A low hum gathered under her hand. 
Then another, and another, until the insulators on the limbs formed an instrument the valley had forgotten it owned. 
The hedgehog had uncurled fully now, unbothered by the vibration, and crept toward the warmth of the sun on the sculpture. 
Mira felt the tree's stiffness as her own; she shifted her angle, offering the alder an easier way to sing. 
The response was a chord so tender it seemed to bend the air without breaking it. 
In that intensely quiet grove, the music did not fill the silence so much as teach it how to carry meaning. 
Mira's motivation had never been to defeat the quiet, but to give it a voice capable of grief, patience, and return. 
She worked until light moved across the carving. 
By afternoon she had mapped a whole movement for the siskin alder grove, every phrase anchored in a stress crack she had learned to honor. 
She did not sign the sculpture, for she was not its author; she had only listened until it could speak. 
When the first evening star appeared, she placed the porcelain insulator in the cupped hands of the old carving and left it there as an offering. 
The hedgehog had gone to sleep in the roots, but its trust remained in the way the grove did not flinch when she stepped away. 
Behind her, the alders held the last note like a secret. 
It was empathy in motion, passing from cracked wood to human hands and back into the quiet, where it would wait for someone symphonically minded enough to hear it whole.
