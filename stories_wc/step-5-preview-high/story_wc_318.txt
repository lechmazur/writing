The last grain of sand had settled in the hourglass when the leaf blower symphony conductor entered the echo chamber tunnel, his boots crunching over decades of dust that the city above had forgotten to sweep away.  The curved brick walls breathed back every creak and whisper, wrapping the passage in a true haunting that felt less like architecture and more like a throat that had swallowed a scream and kept it.  He had been dead long enough to know that time was not a river but an hourglass, and now that the final grain had slipped, the household he had once loved was suffocating in the unspoken things he had failed to say.  In his translucent hands he carried a leaf blower whose only music was the rusted pull cord handle, frayed like an old rope he used to swing on as a boy before the war, before the silence.  He was mournfully eager, a conductor whose grief sharpened his tempo, who believed that laughter could be summoned back into a household if only the wind were taught the right off-key hymn.  Above the tunnel ceiling, three people sat in a kitchen gone cold, moving around each other with the choreography of strangers bound by a mortgage and a mutual refusal to weep.  The unspoken had pooled there, fat and calcified, turning the air into glass; every apology and inside joke they had swallowed sat in the room like furniture too heavy to move.  It was his sacred duty to tend to the unspoken, to mine those tunnels of quiet and grind the sharp shards of what-might-have-been into something soft enough to feed the living.  He knelt in the echo chamber tunnel and wrapped his fingers around the pull cord handle, feeling the molded plastic bite into skin that no longer bled but remembered how.  With a yank that reverberated off the brick like a gunshot in a cathedral, the engine sputtered to life, and he began his symphony.  The gust that tore from the nozzle was off-key, wobbling between a minor third and a wheeze, yet it carried his half-sung lullabies with the stubborn affection of a father humming in the dark.  He aimed the stream at the drifts of unsent letters and stifled arguments that had congealed beneath the floorboards, and he began to macerate them, the wind acting as pestle and song as mortar.  Old grudges, birthday toasts never given, and the simple daily tenderness they had let rust away all swirled in the vortex, broken down into dark, fertile mulch.  The lullabies climbed the heating ducts, carried on the off-key wind until they seeped into the kitchen like steam from a forgotten kettle.  The mother felt it first, a mournfully eager pressure behind her eyes that made her set down the dish she had been drying for an hour without progress.  Her daughter, pockets still full of gravel from the garden, looked up and said the word that had died in her father's throat years ago: sorry.  The sound was small, but in the echo chamber tunnel it registered as a crescendo, and the conductor redoubled his efforts, half-singing through the roar of the blower.  The father laughed then, a bark of surprise that sounded like a door opening, and the mother joined him, her laugh wet and bright and utterly ridiculous.  Laughter, that lost inheritance, rushed back into the household, filling corners where the unspoken had pooled, and the three of them clutched each other over the cold stove as if drowning in air.  In the tunnel, the leaf blower symphony conductor let the engine idle down, the pull cord handle going still in his grip as the true haunting quiet settled once more around the curved brick.  He had not erased their grief, only macerated it into compost, and he knew that tending to the unspoken was endless work for the mournfully eager.  Above, the girl hummed, and the off-key wind that lingered in the vents seemed to hum with her, a half-sung lullaby for the next hour, the next household, the next grain of sand.
