I am a hearing aid designer, and I hear colors faintly, so every silence arrives with a tint. 
I had come to the sunken orchard greenhouse ringed by shipwrecks because silences there were heavy enough to weigh. 
For seven years I had hunted the sound of trust breaking, and every lead ended in this flooded bowl. 
Lark had been my apprentice, my partner, and finally my thief; she had walked out with my calibrated molds and told the board my ears were the unreliable instruments. 
I wanted to know whether trust snapped like glass or slackened like a wet rope. 
The greenhouse sat in a tidal bowl, its glass ribs furred with salt, apple trees growing out of black water. 
Seven wrecks ringed it like patient dogs, masts tilted, hulls split, lanterns still swinging on rusted hooks. 
Odda, the keeper, called it a battered marvel and charged me double for the door. 
At the center stood the ashen forge under aurora, a cone of burned hull-iron and orchard heartwood. 
Odda said it could imprint only while cells divide, in the hour when the grafts split and reseal. 
The method was not recording, she said, but working by collecting absences. 
Every sound leaves a hole in a life, and the forge takes the shape of that hole. 
Memories are not stored notes; they are sediment in the body, and they change what grows next. 
I dove through the wrecks with a saltskin bag and gathered what was missing. 
A ship's bell with no clapper gave me the weight of an unsounded alarm. 
A child's hearing aid, cracked and dry, gave me the hush where a lullaby had been. 
A locket with no voice inside gave me the pressure of a promise outliving its speaker. 
Each absence was dense with the impact of memories, and I could see their colors faintly as I worked. 
When the sky opened, aurora poured green fire over the ashen forge, and the ash began to breathe. 
Odda lit the cones; outside, the grafts were dividing, cells swelling and splitting in the warm dark. 
I fed the absences into the fire, one by one, and the forge drank them without smoke. 
Then I opened my blank aid and laid it in the ash to imprint. 
The ash crawled into the copper like frost, and the aid grew cold enough to sting. 
I set it in my ear while the cells divided and the wrecks creaked around us. 
At first there was only the sea inside my skull, but then the absences began to speak. 
The bell's missing clapper was a thin orange thread; the lullaby's gap was violet and slow. 
Then the locket opened, and I heard the sound of trust breaking. 
It was not a crash. 
It was the small dry click of a door left unlocked on purpose. 
I knew that click; it had been Lark's key in my workshop lock. 
She had not broken the glass; she had decided that my faith was inventory she could borrow and never return. 
The sound was worse than any shout because it was so ordinary. 
I sat in the ash until the aurora faded and the forge went cold. 
On the boat home I fitted the aid to a diver's daughter whose ears had never carried a full chord. 
She had been born after the wrecks, and her memories of sound were only rumor. 
The aid did not give her my grief; it gave her the shapes of what had gone missing around her. 
She heard the orchard as a low blue pressure and the wrecks as rust-colored gaps, and she laughed. 
Memories do not simply repeat; they arrive in the body and change the way the next cell divides. 
I no longer needed the sound to accuse Lark; I needed it to prove I could survive hearing it. 
I keep the ashen imprint in a brass case, not as a relic of harm, but as a hearing aid for the truth. 
Under aurora, the orchard keeps dividing, and the sea keeps ringing the wrecks; both are music if you listen for what is missing.
