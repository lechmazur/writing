The counter-clockwise clock maker arrived on the rocky island as the sea began its slow exhale of low tide.  For twenty years she had repaired timepieces that ran backward, believing that fate was merely a mechanism wound too tightly.  Her atelier inside the mysterious lighthouse was orderly to the point of devotion, each gear sorted by size and sorrow, each escapement pinned to velvet like a butterfly.  She was neatly inventive, able to build a marine chronometer from driftwood and salt if necessity demanded such terrible elegance.  The lighthouse had stood empty for a century, its lamp dead, yet sailors whispered that the tower still shone when every other beacon failed.  Salt had eaten the iron rails, and guano whitened the stairs, but the lens remained immaculate, as if preserved by a force that resented decay.  At the black waterline, the retreating waves uncovered the hunger stone, its carved warnings older than the masonry above it.  In older centuries, villagers had knelt before it during drought, reading its messages of thirst, famine, and waiting.  She had no thirst for famine, but she understood the language of thresholds.  She saw instead a gauge, a measure of how far the world would fall if the sea kept withdrawing its mercy and the sky forgot rain.  Beneath the wet glyphs, a faint luminescence pulsed with the patience of glaciers, a latent radiance waiting for the correct sequence to unlock it.  She had carried the ancient yoyo from a drowned monastery, convinced that its toy-shape concealed a deliberate design rather than childish amusement.  For three sleepless nights she studied it, tracing how the weighted halves spun against the salt-corded string.  Then she began reverse-engineering the puzzle of its construction, measuring the axle's hidden grooves and the asymmetrical weight of its wooden discs.  The axle, she discovered, was a cipher ring; the string, a meridional map; the sleep, a countdown measured not in seconds but in tides.  Each clockwise turn of the puzzle tightened a spring inside the stone; each counter-clockwise turn loosened the bars of a cage she could not see.  It was a key shaped like a game, meant to initiate the hunger stone's buried engine before the ocean reclaimed it for another age.  Her motivation was not treasure but escape: to step beyond fate's design, she needed to unmake the forward pull of causality itself.  The island wind tasted of iron and rotting kelp as she climbed the spiral stair, the hunger stone wrapped in oilcloth against her hip.  In the lamp room, she fitted the yoyo's bronze axle into a slot no human hand had carved, a void shaped like a question.  The mechanism accepted it with a sigh, and the great lens began to rotate counter-clockwise, grinding against the rust of decades.  Gears that had slept for a hundred years bit into fresh motion.  She worked by moonlight and muscle, aligning the prism with the exposed stone below, where the tide had bared its ancient ribs.  The slow exhale of low tide had become a held breath; the sea hesitated at the shoreline, unwilling to witness what came next.  When she tripped the final escapement, the latent radiance surged up through the hunger stone and into the lamp, violent and silent.  It was not fire but possibility, a cold blue light that showed every path not taken, every door fate had locked, every clock stopped at the wrong hour.  She felt the bleak exhilaration of it, a joy so sharp it bordered on grief, for she was about to break the world or herself.  To be free was to be orphaned from certainty.  The clock maker reached into the light, her neatly inventive fingers finding the seam in destiny's weave and tearing it wider.  She pulled.  The horizon cracked like an eggshell, and beyond it she saw not darkness but an unwritten morning, chaotic and absolute.  For one impossible instant, she stood outside the design, a creature of reverse hours with no past to haunt her.  The lighthouse went dark behind her.  The hunger stone crumbled into ordinary rock.  And the tide, reversing its ancient habit, began to rise in reverse, as if unsure which direction belonged to the future.
