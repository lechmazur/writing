Queen Marisol had grown disenchanted with a realm where every smile was rehearsed and every threat wore velvet. 
Yet she moved through the throne room with a softly commanding grace that left no room for question. 
For thirty nights, the same recurring dreams had burned through her sleep: a cobalt grotto above clouds, where starlight bled from ancient fractures in slow, silver threads. 
The dream left her waking with frost on her eyelids and a hunger she could not name. 
The astronomers begged her to ignore the vision, and after the final warning, when the chancellor sealed the observatory and hid every star chart, Marisol chose the mountain. 
Her motivation was simple and terrible: to unravel the mystery of recurring dreams before they unraveled her. 
She took only Sena, her rider, who knew the ridge trail better than any map and feared nothing on horseback. 
They rode until the horses refused the scree, then began to climb. 
The ridge was a spine of black granite, and every handhold felt like the judgment of the council she had left behind. 
Halfway up, Marisol paused on a shelf of wind-scoured stone, and she and Sena used a shared exhale spotting the first fireflies of summer to steady their nerves. 
The insects rose from the crevices in flickering couples, as if the mountain itself were practicing a language of tiny lanterns. 
Sena whispered that fireflies were omens of safe passage, but Marisol saw in their pulse a mimicry of the light that bled in her dreams. 
They climbed until the clouds pooled beneath them like milk in a basin, and the air turned thin and sweet with altitude. 
At the summit waited the ember grotto on the ridge, its mouth breathing a low orange heat that did not warm. 
Lacework of ash clung to the entrance, delicate as cobweb, menace in every thread. 
Marisol stepped inside; the blue was deeper than ocean and older than sleep. 
This was the cobalt grotto above clouds, a cathedral of mineral filigree hung over the vanishing sky. 
Above, fractures in the ceiling leaked the bleeding starlight, not in rays but in drops, each drop a wound of gold that sizzled when it struck the stone. 
Marisol understood then that her dreams were not prophecy but invitation. 
The grotto fed on disenchantment, spinning it into lace, turning weary queens into decoration for its vaulted dark. 
She reached toward a falling bead of light; Sena caught her wrist. 
"We should leave," Sena said, but Marisol's voice was softly commanding as she answered that mysteries unraveled only at their source. 
A shape resolved from the stalactites, a figure draped in the same lacy menace as the ash at the entrance, with no face save a swirl of starlight. 
It extended a hand, offering the final thread of the pattern she had chased for thirty nights. 
Marisol realized the figure wore her own crown, bent and luminous, and that the dreams were the grotto breathing against her mind. 
If she took the thread, she would become the grotto's heart, forever lit and forever still. 
But she had not climbed to be consumed; she had climbed to unravel. 
With softly commanding precision, she drew a dagger and cut the thread, not the air, and the starlight shrieked as it withdrew into the cracks. 
The ember mouth gasped, and the cobalt walls dimmed to ordinary stone. 
Sena pulled her free as the first fireflies of summer, now liberated from the crevices, rose around them in a spiral of living light. 
They descended without looking back, though the mountain still hummed with a lacy menace beneath the skin of the rock. 
In the court below, Marisol no longer sighed; she ruled with a wonder sharpened by warning, her dreams finally quiet. 
But on clear nights, if one looked closely at the ridge, a single thread of silver still glowed above the clouds.
