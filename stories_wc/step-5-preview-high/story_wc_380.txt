After the whale beaches, the harbor settled into a gleaming silence. 
The black bodies lay half in the surf, and the tide refused to take them. 
The council posted notices about gas buildup, but no one lit fires. 
I knew it was the gravity, because I was the dock-side lantern lighter. 
My job was simple: fill the lamps, trim the wicks, and keep the piers from swallowing the dark. 
On the ninth night after the stranding, a lamp swung against the wind and pointed inland. 
I followed it to the old salt works. 
The salt crystal growing chamber stood behind the warehouse, breathing cold white steam. 
Inside, racks of brine dripped onto glass tables, and pale cubes grew in perfect silence. 
The forewoman, Senn, ground coffee beans and studied the wet grounds at the door. 
She said the patterns in coffee grounds could show where deleted memories were sinking. 
I did not laugh, because I had come to find where deleted memories go. 
Three winters ago I had sold the clerks my sister's name, her laugh, and the sound of her coats. 
The clerks promised relief, but they left a page in my pocket: pressed wildflowers, brittle as moth wings. 
Senn looked at the flowers and said they were heavier than paper. 
A memory with its feeling cut out should weigh nothing, but grief keeps mass. 
The chamber had been built over a pocket of gravity that fluctuates mysteriously beneath the quay. 
When the pocket sank, the trays grew cloudy; when it rose, salt dust floated upward like snow. 
Senn poured my last coffee into a saucer and turned it three times. 
Through the patterns in the coffee grounds, she traced a spiral beneath the central grating. 
There was a plug of salt under the floor, she said, where the first deletion had been buried. 
The whales had beached on the same night the plug loosened. 
We waited until the pumps quieted, then pried up the grating with a lantern hook. 
The air below smelled of low tide and old rain. 
I went down to exhume what the town had dropped into the dark. 
My lantern showed a slab of salt crusted over a wooden box. 
The pocket tugged at my ribs, and the walls gleamed without any light. 
I pulled the box free and set it on a drying tray. 
Inside were packets of gray salt, each labeled with a person's date and a stolen hour. 
Beneath them lay a bundle tied with dock twine, and I knew the knot. 
I cut it open and found the pressed wildflowers from my pocket, doubled, as if they had been copied. 
Under the flowers was a salt lens no larger than a coin. 
Senn whispered that deleted memories did not die; they were pressed into whatever would hold them. 
The lens held my sister's voice humming a lantern tune I had taught her. 
I remembered the tune first, then her name, then the way she braided salt-stiff rope. 
The memory was cold, but it was whole, and it fit the empty place like a key. 
Above us the chamber groaned as the pocket of gravity fluctuated mysteriously, loosening another tray. 
Senn said the memories were not lost; they pooled where the world forgot to hold its weight. 
The beached whales were only the first warning of so much buried grief. 
I carried the lens and the flowers back up into the gleaming silence. 
The harbor lay empty and bright under a moon that felt too near. 
I climbed the seawall and lit every lantern until the flames stood straight and still. 
In the morning the council would ask for explanations, but I had one. 
I would tell them the deleted memories go into pockets of gravity, and grief has tides like the sea. 
Then I would ask them to stop selling quiet and start exhuming the past before it beached again. 
Senn took the coffee saucer and scraped the grounds into the brine. 
She said patterns were only warnings, and warnings were useless without hands. 
We left the chamber with the salt growing behind us, white and flawless and terribly quiet. 
I kept the pressed wildflowers in my coat, not as a relic, but as a seed. 
If deleted memories could be pressed into salt and lifted back into the light, then grief was not a wall. 
It was a door that only needed someone to knock. 
That night I walked the piers, and for the first time after the whale beaches, the silence did not gleam with loss. 
It gleamed with work still to be done.
