Prince Aldric had not bathed in months, and the grime of the mechanical world had settled into his skin like a bitter-tinged garment.  His suspended palace, anchored to giant stalactites that pierced the cavern roof like the roots of some inverted cathedral, groaned beneath him in a constant quaking reverie.  Above, the great engines of the Citadel ground without pause, turning the sky to ash and the rivers to oil while the people below forgot the names of flowers.  Below, the abyss swallowed every prayer for grace that the world still remembered how to whisper, returning only echoes of iron.  Aldric had become a scholar of grief, hypothesizing about everything from the rotation of gear-moons to the chemical composition of forgotten rain and the weight of unspoken regret.  He believed the world suffered from a flawed genesis, a factory of creation where beauty had been omitted from the blueprint and no engineer had thought to file a correction.  His chambers were filled with diagrams scrawled in lampblack and wine, each one a desperate argument against the tyranny of iron and a plea for the return of wings.  At the center of his obsession sat a velvet cushion holding an extinct bird's egg, pale as moonstone and veined with gold, warm to the touch though no living heart beat within.  It was the last proof that the earth had once known flight, and he guarded it as though it were a crown more legitimate than the one rusting in his trunks.  Every morning he woke with the quaking reverie still trembling in his chest, a dream that shook him awake and reminded him that awe and terror were siblings.  He told himself that to reawaken beauty in a mechanical world he must assemble the right conditions, or the shell might forget how to break and spill light instead of yolk.  So he gathered copper wire from dead automatons, silk from unravelled tapestries, and crystal lenses that had once projected state lies into the public squares.  Through the long, lamplit hours he worked, hypothesizing about everything: temperature, vibration, the precise frequency of a lullaby now extinct, and the possibility that stone could learn to sing.  He spoke aloud to the egg as though it were a courtier, confessing his bitterly sweet hope that beauty might forgive the world its factories and its ash.  The palace swayed, anchored by only those ancient teeth of stone, and he steadied his apparatus with trembling hands while the chasm winds rose from below.  He had learned that the flawed genesis was not merely a lack of design but a severance, a tearing away of organic wonder so complete that men mistook numbness for peace.  The mechanical world had replaced flight with propellers and song with steam-horns, calling the exchange progress while children learned to draw only right angles.  But Prince Aldric, bedraggled and half-starved, still kept faith in the memory of feathers brushing against a warm sky, a sensation he had felt once as a boy before the smog closed in.  On the fourteenth night of assembly, the chandeliers flickered as a subterranean wind rose from the abyss, carrying with it the smell of soil that had not known a boot in centuries.  He placed the extinct bird's egg into the nest of wire and silk, adjusting the lenses to focus every available spark of light until the chamber hummed with potential.  His hypothesis had been mad: that warmth was not a chemical but a language, and that the egg still understood the dialect of dawn and the grammar of petals unfolding.  As he wound the final spring, the quaking reverie became a physical thing, a shudder that passed from stalactites to stone to bone, shaking the very idea of machinery apart.  A hairline crack appeared, then widened, and the bitterly sweet scent of ozone and ancient pollen filled the chamber, making him weep without knowing why.  From the fissure emerged not a chick of flesh, but a resonant chord of color that painted the rusted walls in vermilion and gold, visible music given a body.  It was sound made visible, the ghost of a birdsong crystallized, hovering where the machine had lain and tracing spirals no forge could manufacture.  Aldric fell to his knees, weeping at the discovery, feeling the flawed genesis tremble under the weight of this impossible grace that he had willed into being.  The palace itself seemed to inhale, the giant stalactites singing as the color spiraled up through the vaulted dark and out toward the mechanical sky.  He realized then that beauty had not been lost, only dormant, waiting for someone mad enough to assemble its scattered grammar.  Yet through the discovery, a door had opened that no mechanical lock could close again.  He smiled, bitterly sweet, and worked on.
