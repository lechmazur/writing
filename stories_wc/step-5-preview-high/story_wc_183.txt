I had come to the abandoned carnival ship as a chimera twin discoverer, a profession few still remembered. 
The vessel drifted in a foggy bay with its lights dead, its carousel half sunk, its music box lungs rusted shut. 
My purpose was to find the garden of forgotten names, a place my missing twin had charted before the letters stopped. 
I carried an emerald swatch card cut from the awning of a funhouse we had entered as children. 
It had no value except as proof that we had chosen different lives. 
Every mirror aboard showed me not as myself but as two bodies with one genome, chimera twins split into separate persons. 
The ship called this doubling a sanctuary, though it was only unkindled solace, comfort that never caught fire. 
The ship kept its clock by the hour as memories alter, so the prize booths changed stock and ticket stubs rewrote names. 
The bay was silent, so the creaking hull sounded like applause from an audience that had forgotten how to leave. 
I pulled a thread from a clown's painted sleeve and found my sister laughing in a garden gate made of ticket wire. 
She had been hunting the garden of forgotten names too, believing our mother had hidden there after the twins in her womb vanished. 
I was blissfully aberrant enough to trust the wrong version of her, because every thread gave me the freedom to choose another ending. 
Yet each choice bound me tighter to the ship, so my escape felt like a door opening into another locked room. 
I followed the ship through narrative threads, and the emerald swatch card grew warm wherever memory had been stitched and restitched. 
Those stitches led to the captain's gallery, where a carousel horse held a brass key in its mane. 
The key unlocked a chest of unsent letters, each one addressed to a name I had spoken only in sleep. 
As memories altered, the letters changed from pleas into invitations, and I could no longer tell which twin had written them. 
I recaptured the moment of our separation by pulling every thread back to the womb-dark corridor where the ship had first dreamed us. 
The corridor was the garden of forgotten names, not a field but a ledger, its paths made of every label our family refused to speak. 
Names hung like fruit from rusted hooks, and picking one erased a memory to make room for another. 
I saw ours waiting on the highest branch, but the ladder was made of the same threads I had used to navigate. 
Unkindled solace wrapped around me, the peace of knowing that some losses are kept safe by never being resolved. 
If I took the names, I might free my sister from the ship, but she would forget why she had ever searched. 
If I left them, the carnival would keep us drifting forever, which was a kind of trapped freedom neither of us had asked for. 
I decided to recapture not the garden but the thread that led away from it, thin and frayed and almost invisible. 
With the emerald swatch card, I spliced that thread into the chest of letters, making a new story where the twins chose to be found. 
The foggy bay pressed against the portholes like a hand testing a lock, and the hull groaned as the narrative threads tightened. 
My sister appeared in the gallery wearing the captain's coat, her face changing as memories altered with each step. 
She said the garden had been a trap of comfort, an unkindled solace that warmed no one, and asked me to burn the ledger. 
We carried the names to the carousel and fed them into the motor, where sparks finally caught and lit the midway. 
The ship did not sink, but it turned, slowly, toward open water, its freedom still bounded by rails and anchors. 
I remained a chimera twin discoverer, though now I discovered only what we had chosen to keep. 
The emerald swatch card faded to gray, its work finished, and I let it fall into the bay. 
Somewhere behind us the garden of forgotten names closed like a show between seasons, waiting for the next pair of twins to misread its gates. 
We stood at the rail, blissfully aberrant and unkindled no longer, two names in one body of story. 
The carnival drifted, but the threads we had recaptured led home, and for once home did not move as we reached it.
