The orderly seed sorter knelt in the ashen skyborne arbor. 
Above, the sky hung gray and particulate, but below the roots, moss drank deeply and hummed with green contentment. 
Each morning, according to regional folklore, the arbor exhaled seeds that had once carried entire cities. 
The sorter arranged them by weight, by lullaby, by the angle of their dormant wings, and filed them in cedar drawers. 
It was meticulous work, and the sorter loved it, yet a deeper hunger pulled at their ribs whenever autumn arrived. 
During the hush where past meets prophecy, the leaves did not fall downward but drifted sideways, each one a folded letter of ink and chlorophyll. 
The sorter's motivation was not hoarding but reading. 
To decode the message in falling leaves, they had learned a method from grandmothers now interred in the soil pockets. 
Their method was building tiny monuments to forgotten things. 
A broken thimble became a cenotaph; a nail clipped from a hanged man's coffin became an obelisk; a sheep's knucklebone became a chapel where beetles could kneel. 
Each shrine stood no taller than a molar, arranged in spirals along the widest branch. 
The folklore insisted that memory was a nutrient, and when the forgotten were honored, the air sweetened and the leaves confessed their cargo. 
On the first evening of the hush, a leaf shaped like a crescent settled onto the sorter's open palm. 
Veins branched across it in a pattern the sorter had never catalogued, pulsing faintly with silver intent. 
They placed it upon the smallest monument, a cairn for a whistle lost in the flood of three centuries prior. 
Beneath the leaf, almost invisible, clung a moonstone cabochon dust speck no larger than a gnat's eyelash. 
Curiosity overruled procedure, and the sorter chose to prod it with the tip of a silver sorting needle. 
The speck split into seven motes that orbited the needle before implanting themselves in the leaf's veins. 
In that instant the sorter felt the old rule awaken: those who touch the arbor's mineral dreams emerge transformed always. 
Moss ribboned up their wrists; their fingernails became lacquered jade; their pupils bloomed into tiny seed-green moons. 
It did not hurt; rather, it felt like the first cool breath after a long fever, a mossy delight flooding every limb. 
The leaf's message blazed open: not a warning, but an invitation to remember what the ash had tried to erase. 
The sorter saw, through transformed sight, that the skyborne arbor was itself a monument, grown by ancestors to shelter every name the lowlands had abandoned. 
They moved among their tiny monuments, adding a shard of moonstone dust speck to each, so that every forgotten thing gained a star of its own. 
As they worked, more leaves fell and folded themselves into envelopes around the new monuments. 
The sorter sorted no seeds that night; instead, they catalogued the silence between rustles, giving each hush its own cedar drawer. 
Prophecy, they realized, was only memory with the dust blown off, and the past was simply a seed awaiting its proper drawer. 
When the hush lifted, the sorter descended the arbor's spiral root, emerging transformed always into a world that had forgotten how to listen. 
Villagers stared at the green-shot eyes and moss-silked hair, but the sorter only smiled and pressed a leaf into each open hand. 
Read the veins, they said, and build something small to hold what you have lost. 
Soon the lowlands bristled with thimble cairns and feather arches, and the ashen sky began to bruise with dawn. 
Each year, when the hush returned, the sorter climbed again into the ashen skyborne arbor, carrying new seeds and older memories. 
The leaves fell sideways, whispering of prophecies that were only the past wearing moss, and the sorter, orderly still, greeted them like old friends. 
In the drawers, seeds slept beside monuments; in the sky, ash danced with pollen; and in the sorter's jade hands, every falling leaf became a song. 
The sorter emerges transformed always, and always with the same mossy delight, sorting eternity one small, glowing monument at a time.
