The blackout had silenced the pumps that kept the lower archives dry, and silence was the only map Elara trusted. 
She was the logical page indexer of the Drowned Athenaeum, a forgotten library suspended beneath the collapsed towers of a sunken city. 
Her task, assigned by no one and expected by fewer, was to trace honest origins in a collection that had spent decades drowning in convenient lies. 
During the blackout, the emergency lamps painted the water-stained ceiling in shades of amber, and the light came through the cracked dome in dappled resolve. 
Elara moved along the reading alcoves with tranquilly perceptive patience, her fingers trailing the spines of books that had swollen with salt and time. 
She did not panic when the currents shifted the silt outside; she readjusted her stance and let the stillness settle around her like a second skin. 
In a brass drawer beneath the master catalogue, she found an antique ink stamp, its oak handle blackened by age and its rubber face stamped with a seal no ledger recognized. 
The stamp bore the sigil of the Tidewrights, a guild the city records had dismissed as a myth invented by feverish cartographers. 
Elara set it beside her ledger and wiped her glasses, preparing to form an alternative hypothesis rather than force the artifact into the approved narrative. 
If the Tidewrights had truly built this library, then the honest origins she sought lay not with the merchant patrons but with the engineers who had raised the shelves above the waterline. 
She tested the stamp against a blotter of pressed kelp paper, and the ink left a perfect, unapologetic mark. 
That small act of readjustment, realigning the ink face so the seal read true, felt like an answer to a question she had carried since her first descent. 
The reclaimed stillness of the library was not empty; it was a deliberate quiet reclaimed from the panic that had drowned the streets above. 
In that quiet, she could hear the building confess. 
The beams remembered the axe. 
The mortar remembered the tide. 
The catalog cards remembered the hands that had written truth before the censors learned to swim. 
Elara opened the tidewright manifest she had reconstructed from marginalia and barnacle scars. 
It told a story of builders who had not merely survived the sea but had negotiated with it, raising halls where knowledge could rest in brackish dark. 
The official records called them smugglers. 
The alternative hypothesis named them archivists of depth, and the stamp in her palm was their signature of consent. 
She lifted a volume of cartography, one whose frontispiece had been scraped clean by a cautious clerk long ago. 
With the antique ink stamp, she restored the Tidewright seal to the title page, reclaiming the page from its falsified silence. 
The ink dried quickly in the cold, damp air, and she readjusted the book upon its shelf so the seal faced outward like a lantern. 
Outside, a school of silver fish passed beyond the dome, and their shadows made the room flicker with dappled resolve. 
She understood now that to trace honest origins was not to punish the liars but to free the drowned from their long embarrassment. 
The library did not need rescue from the water; it needed correction of record. 
Elara sat back on her stool, the stamp heavy in her palm, and let her tranquilly perceptive gaze travel the aisles. 
She was no historian, no hero, only a logical page indexer who had refused to let the index lie. 
Yet in that refusal she had found something rarer than fame: a reclaimed stillness that belonged to the builders and now, briefly, to her. 
The blackout continued, but the darkness no longer felt like an ending. 
It felt like a pause between tides, a moment to readjust the world before the water returned to test the walls. 
She dipped a pen and began the new entry in her ledger, her handwriting steady and plain. 
"Origin: Tidewright Collective," she wrote. 
"Method: alternative hypothesis accepted upon material evidence." 
"Condition: reclaimed stillness maintained." 
Below the dome, the sunken city groaned as a distant current shifted the silt against the western stair. 
Elara did not flinch. 
She stamped the ledger page beside her entry, sealing the truth with the same instrument that had once concealed it. 
The library held its breath around her, and for the first time in years, the breath was honest.
