In the lush orchard that spanned the rafters of the abandoned cathedral, where apple trees had burst through stone and pear vines dangled like chandeliers, the quartz-eye mender knelt in a cradle of moss and searched her empty hands for a solution.  Three weeks after the breach of contract, the orchard's ancient caretakers had refused to honor the deciduous vows that turned with the seasons, and now the trees grew wild enough to swallow the nave.  Her name was Bessary, though the townsfolk called her the mender, and she had come not to cut but to persist, because only she remembered that vows, like leaves, were meant to fall so roots could remember how to climb.  A sharp whistle cracked through the transept, followed by the endearingly feisty voice of her daughter, Mabel, who clung upside down from a branch above.  "The covenant is broken," Mabel called, kicking her heels, "and you're stitching dead paper to living wood."  Bessary's quartz eyes, cloudy and glittering from decades of pruning resin, fixed on the girl with a patience that had outlasted three widowhoods.  She wanted to bridge generational wisdom, to show Mabel that a broken vow could be replanted rather than discarded, but the girl had spent her life in the world below, where contracts were iron and orchards were orchards.  Bessary reached into the leather satchel at her hip, hoping for twine or a grafting knife, but found only the forgotten bag contents of years: a rusted key, three glass marbles, a folded tally sheet, and her daughter's hair ribbon.  The ribbon was faded crimson, frayed at the fold, a relic from the last autumn Mabel had let her braid it without complaint.  She had forgotten it there after a quarrel about seeds and promises, and now it seemed less like cloth and more like a question.  Above them, a branch groaned, heavy with fruit that glowed like lanterns, and Bessary realized the orchard was not choking on the breach but on its own stored silence.  The deciduous vows had always been a shedding, a deliberate release each equinox, but the contract's breach had tricked the trees into holding fast.  They clung to old commitments like frost to iron, and the orchard had become a cathedral of stuck things.  Bessary lifted the ribbon, letting it catch the green light that filtered through the apple canopy.  "If vows must fall," she muttered, tying one end to a bending limb and the other to her own wrist, "then let this knot be the lesson we practice."  Mabel dropped onto a lower branch, her endearingly feisty temper giving way to reluctant curiosity as the ribbon fluttered between mother and tree.  "You're using that scrap to bargain with the orchard?"  Bessary ignored the jab, instead drawing the rusted key from the bag and scratching a channel in the bark where sap still moved.  She packed the wound with the tally sheet, then pressed the marbles into the soil as anchors, using forgotten bag contents not because they were tools, but because their absurdity proved that broken things still had work left.  "The contract said tend forever," Mabel said, softer now.  "And the earth said renew," Bessary replied.  She guided her daughter's hand to the ribbon's knot, letting the girl feel the pulse of sap against the fabric.  "Your grandmother wore this to her first grafting, and I wore it to my last argument with the council," Bessary said, her quartz eyes warm.  "But wisdom isn't a rope, child; it's a season."  At that moment the ribbon loosened, slipped from the limb, and drifted down like a leaf, carrying with it the orchard's first honest release.  A breeze of perfume rolled through the rafters, and fruit began to fall, not rotting, but planting itself in cracks along the stone floor below.  Mabel stared, then laughed, an accidental wisdom dawning as she understood that the breach had not destroyed the vow but freed it from rigid forever.  "So the contract was a cage," she whispered.  "It was a branch you outgrew," Bessary said, gathering the ribbon from the moss.  Together they planted the marbles in the cathedral's eastern corner, where apple saplings would later climb the columns, bridging old roots with new hope.  The quartz-eye mender and her feisty daughter worked until dusk, their voices low, their hands stained with sap and soil, no longer mending a broken past but tending a deciduous future.
