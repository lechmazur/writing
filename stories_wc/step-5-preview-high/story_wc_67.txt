Wren had not heard the gallery doors close behind her, only the faint tick of cooling stone as the old rocking chair finally slowed to a stop. 
She counted twenty-three paces from the entrance, enough to let her eyes adjust to the vanishing point gallery, where corridors folded into one another so that distant walls seemed close enough to touch. 
In her coat pocket she carried a mask made from shattered porcelain shards, its edges still sharp from whatever violence had unmade it. 
It was not her face she intended to hide, but the face she had yet to draw. 
Wren was known in low districts as a charcoal portrait ash scatterer, a woman who returned people to their families as smudged lines and grey dust. 
She worked with the powder of the dead because living memory refused to hold still, sliding like water through careful fingers. 
Tonight her commission came through incomplete information: a first name, a lullaby hummed off key, and a photograph burned at the edges. 
She was to crystallize a fleeting emotion, the specific sorrow of a mother who had outlived her lullabies. 
The fee had been paid in advance, tucked inside an envelope that smelled of lilies and wet iron. 
Wren walked the gallery until the perspective squeezed her shoulders, until she felt the architecture breathing against her spine. 
This place was built to feel like sanctuary, a soft trap where the eye surrendered to the comfort of converging lines. 
She understood better than most that safety was a mirage drawn with straight rules and vanishing ink. 
At the far end sat the rocking chair, its runner worn pale by decades of imagined occupants. 
It had been moving when she arrived, a gentle arc like a metronome counting backward. 
Now it stilled, and the silence arrived not as peace but as a held breath. 
She set her satchel on the floor and unpacked the tools of her trade: compressed ash, a bundle of vine charcoal, and a fixative she had learned to brew from resin and river water. 
The porcelain mask she placed upon the gallery bench, its shard face turned toward the wall. 
Each fragment had been salvaged from a broken doll, from a hospital floor, from a wedding gift dropped in anger. 
She did not know which piece had belonged to the grieving mother, and that not-knowing was itself a kind of draft. 
Wren had learned to weave truth carefully, threading lies between facts until the fabric held a recognizable shape. 
She mixed ash with a drop of linseed, grinding until the pigment became a soft, yielding powder. 
Then she began to powder the paper, tapping the mixture through linen so that it settled like frost on a window. 
The first strokes were not a face but a weather system, the barometric pressure of loss. 
She worked from the outside in, because inward was a country she could not map. 
The lullaby fragment returned to her, incomplete, missing its final note, and she realized that the mother had not died singing it. 
She had died trying to remember the words, and the forgetting was the emotion to be preserved. 
The mask on the bench caught the gallery light and threw a prism onto the floor, thin as a nerve. 
Wren lifted it, feeling how the shards shifted against one another, loose as teeth. 
She brought it to her own face and peered through the gaps, seeing the portrait bifurcated into fragments. 
Through this broken lens, the charcoal woman on the page seemed more honest than any photograph. 
The mirage of safety shattered further: the shard mask began to powder against her palm, its dust joining the ash already on her skin. 
It did not cut her; it simply consented to becoming what it had always been. 
She let the last of it fall into her palm, then scattered it across the wet portrait like a final benediction. 
The rocking chair had stopped, and in that arrested motion the gallery felt less like a room and more like the inside of a held note. 
Wren understood that she had not captured a ghost but the precise vibration of air displaced by one. 
She signed the work not with her name but with a smudge of her own thumbprint, another incomplete mark. 
When she left, she did not look back, trusting the vanishing lines to rearrange themselves behind her. 
Outside, the city noise returned, but she carried the stillness with her, a somber marvel tucked beneath her arm. 
The portrait would hang where mothers stood to remember, and the ash would do what ash does best. 
It would hold the line until the next breath disturbed it.
