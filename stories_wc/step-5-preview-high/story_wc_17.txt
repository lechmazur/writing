The ivy-veiled passage had swallowed the east wing of Kettle House whole, its green ropes thick as ship's cable after three decades of relentless rain.  Maren adjusted her recording collar and stepped inside, her sensitively perceptive ears sorting the fresh drip of water from the older drips still trapped in plaster and pine.  As a corridor echo cartographer, she mapped the acoustic ghosts that lingered in places the climate shifts had left to slow ruin.  The trade had become vital since the great thaw, when old estates turned to mossy labyrinths and surveyors needed to know which corridors still hummed with memory.  Her satchel held tuning forks, wax cylinders, and brass gauges that measured how long a footstep survived in granite.  But the work was also her family's legacy, a burden passed down through four generations, and she had never found the courage to refuse it.  Her mother had charted these same halls until the silence swallowed her whole, or so the neighbors whispered behind gloved hands.  Tonight, however, Maren was not tracing the usual reverberations.  She was hunting for something more elusive, a question that had haunted her since she inherited the collar: what the walls dreamed about.  The ash in the main chimney had gone cold years ago, but the fireplace in the north passage still held a little heat from yesterday's brief sun.  She approached it slowly, her boots crunching on glass and acorns that had blown in through the broken gable.  Maren knelt and sifted through the charred brick until her fingers brushed half-burned diaries.  The leather covers were scorched, but the pages inside had survived, curled like dry autumn leaves.  She unfolded one fragment and recognized her grandmother's cramped, urgent handwriting.  The entry described a method for reading a wall's sleep through half-burned diaries found in a fireplace.  It explained that stone recorded more than sound; it kept images, and that a film negative strip could expose those images to anyone willing to look.  Maren pulled the strip from her satchel, where it had lain beneath her calibration tools since the estate sale.  The celluloid was no wider than her thumb, frames dark and shimmering with a faint, impossible silver that seemed to breathe.  She carried it deeper, past the servants' stair, to the central hall where the ivy had cracked the window glass and let in a blade of moonlight.  Here the walls were thickest, layered with centuries of paint and voices, and the air felt heavy as held breath.  Tarnished sconces still clung to panels that had once known candlelight and argument.  Maren pressed the negative strip against the wainscot, aligning its first frame with a knot in the oak.  Her perceptive nature had always made her an outsider, too aware of every creak and sigh in the old houses she surveyed.  But now that same sensitivity became a lens.  She felt the wall inhale.  The temperature dropped, and for a moment she smelled rain on dust that had been dry for thirty years.  Through the negative's ghosted frames, she caught a glimpse of the room as it had been before the climate shifts, before the rot.  Not a memory, but a dream: the walls longing to be a forest again, their mortar dissolving into loam, their bricks softening into seed.  She saw dancers turning in mist, a child pressing a hand to plaster, and beneath it all a patient, verdant hunger.  Faces she did not know watched from doorframes that no longer existed.  Maren realized the burden her family carried was not a cage but a conversation.  Her mother had not been swallowed by silence; she had simply stopped shouting long enough to listen.  The diaries, the collar, the negative strip were all instruments for listening.  She stood there for a long time, sensitively perceptive and unafraid, finally hearing the walls dream aloud.
