After the carousel halts, the kinetic lagoon above the clouds holds its breath in a fractured stillness that feels like a warning. 
There is only one errand that brings a resolute scavenger to that altitude: to negotiate with one's own ghost. 
Her method is strange, but it has sustained her, and it works by collecting fragments of overheard stories from travelers on the cloud rails. 
She keeps the fragments folded in her coat like paper money, because she has learned that a ghost cannot be paid in promises. 
In the kinetic lagoon above the clouds, time is chronologically confused, so the carousel's last song might have ended an hour ago or forty years before she was born. 
She cannot tell whether she is returning to her failure or leaving it behind, only that the water moves in a way that refuses to settle. 
From her pocket she draws a disposable camera wheel, its plastic spokes worn pale by a thumb that kept trying to wind back the past. 
It is a useless thing now, a fragment of some forgotten amusement, yet its ridged edge matches the spiral scars on the floating docks. 
She turns it once, twice, and the lagoon answers with a low hum that makes her teeth ache in an order she cannot place. 
The ghost arrives without footsteps, rising from the water as a shape she recognizes from mirrors she has avoided for a decade. 
It wears her younger face, but the mouth is set in an ambition so sharp it seems to cut the mist. 
She does not flinch; the resolute scavenger in her has survived worse silences than this. 
"You kept the wheel," the ghost says, and its voice is assembled from every overheard story she has collected. 
She lays the fragments on the dock between them: a laugh about leaving, a confession about staying, a question about where the horses went. 
These are not confessions of her own; they are currency she has gathered so she does not have to speak first. 
The ghost touches the fragments, and the lagoon begins to move in earnest, kinetic currents pulling the words into a slow, wheel-shaped spiral. 
"I want what we promised," the ghost says. 
"I want the sky to be ours, permanent and loud." 
She remembers that promise, or she will remember it, because regret arrives in this place before the event that caused it. 
Her eroded ambitions have been stripped to a stubborn thread: the wish to be worth the height she once claimed. 
The ghost leans closer, and she smells old cotton candy and rust, the perfume of a carousel that has stopped but not ended. 
"You are chronologically confused," it says, almost kindly. 
"You are negotiating with a version of me that has not happened yet." 
She grips the disposable camera wheel until its teeth bite her palm, and she speaks the terms she has practiced in empty rooms. 
"I will carry your ambition without serving it," she says. 
"I will keep collecting stories, but I will stop pretending they are my life." 
The ghost studies her with an expression that fractures, the way ice fractures on a lake that is still somehow moving. 
"Then take the regret," it says, "and let it teach you where to stand." 
The water surges once, and a cold weight passes into her hands, heavy as a roll of film she does not intend to expose. 
She turns the disposable camera wheel one final time, feeling the plastic snap as it surrenders its last notch. 
The ghost dissolves into mist, not defeated but finished, and the lagoon settles into a stillness so complete it feels fractured. 
She stands there after the carousel halts, no longer collecting, no longer bargaining, simply breathing in the cold altitude. 
The eroded ambitions of the sky do not disappear; they become ballast, and she walks down the cloud stairs with a steady, unborrowed weight. 
In the fractured stillness, she finally hears the lagoon not as a demand but as a pause she has earned. 
She is a resolute scavenger still, but now she gathers only what she can set down. 
Her ghost remains in the clouds, and she carries it forward as a quiet companion rather than a creditor. 
After the carousel halts, there is no music, only the clean sound of a woman walking away from her own echo.
