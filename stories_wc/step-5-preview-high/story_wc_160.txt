Juno Fenn was an ingenious floodgate machinist who maintained the stone throat of Mount Aster. 
Her workshop stood in the crumbling amphitheater inside a dormant volcano, where broken seats curved around a vent the townsfolk called the Old Mouth. 
Each morning, as the building breathed, she carried an antique surveyor's chain along the tiers, measuring the slow swelling of warm stone. 
The chain had belonged to her grandmother, and its worn brass tags still clicked against the rock like a code. 
The engineers from the low terraces wanted the floodgates opened wide before the storm season drowned their fields. 
The keepers of the mountain wanted the gates sealed forever, certain that any opening would wake the sleeping fire below. 
Juno had been hired to harmonize conflicting values, and she believed the two demands could share one answer. 
One gray afternoon she found a once-sealed envelope lodged beneath a bronze seat, its letter gone to mold. 
The flap remained, faintly sticky, ridged in a way that ordinary age did not explain. 
Juno's curiosity was slyly heartfelt; she hid her tenderness for the dead behind sharp questions and careful hands. 
She lifted the envelope to the lantern and studied the dried gum, certain that it remembered more than dust. 
By analyzing the leftover adhesive from a once-sealed envelope, she recovered a pattern of invisible ridges left by a stylus. 
The ridges formed an old shorthand of pressed dots and dashes, a private alphabet her grandmother had used during the First Thirst. 
What Juno held in that ruined paper was encrypted compassion, a warning disguised as housekeeping so no enemy would erase it. 
The message read: "As the building breathes, the seats inhale on the count of eleven, or the fire will taste the rain." 
She did not understand the count yet, so she walked the tiers with the antique surveyor's chain and listened. 
The amphitheater groaned as warm air rose through the caldera, and dust fell in slow ribbons from the dome. 
She laid the chain across the central ring and waited, feeling the stone change beneath her boots. 
The building breathed in, drawing a cool breath from the lower tunnels, and the chain went slack. 
Then the exhalation came, hot and long, and the links began to tremble against the floor. 
Juno counted the trembles as they climbed through her palms, and at the eleventh pulse she understood her grandmother's count. 
The keepers arrived first, robes stiff with rain, and demanded that she lock the gates before the storm. 
The engineers came next, mud to their knees, and begged her to drain the terraces before dark. 
They argued in the ringing air, and their voices cracked against the same old fear of losing either homes or heritage. 
Juno let the building breathe twice before she spoke, letting the rhythm steady her like a familiar hand. 
Her report was slyly heartfelt, praising the keepers' caution and the engineers' mercy while folding both into a single plan. 
She set the surveyor's chain across the gate wheel so the links would sing at the moment of release. 
When the seats inhaled, she opened the eastern culverts, and stormwater poured into channels the old masons had carved for the mountain's thirst. 
When the seats exhaled, she cracked the vent hatches, letting steam rise in thin white threads away from the terraces. 
The floodgates did their work without forcing the caldera to swallow the rain, and the dormant volcano remained asleep. 
Water ran clean to the fields, steam breathed free to the sky, and no one had to choose between the two. 
Above them the broken dome let down a gray dappled light, and Juno felt a dappled resolve settle behind her ribs. 
She lifted the chain, and the brass tags clicked once, like a heartbeat that had been waiting for permission. 
The building breathed around her, gentler now, and Juno knew that compassion could be engineered into stone.
