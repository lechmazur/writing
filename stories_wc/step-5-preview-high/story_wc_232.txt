Irvin Cobb was a lonely antique map dealer whose life had narrowed to a dusty back room, a kerosene lamp, and a stubborn collection of coastlines that no longer belonged to anyone he knew. 
His customers bought landscapes they would never walk, but Irvin kept one item not for sale: a type foundry matrix chip, no larger than a thumbnail, stamped with a symbol that appeared on no letterpress alphabet he had ever catalogued. 
His grandmother had pressed it into his palm when he was nine, and she had made him swear a lingering promise: that he would go back to the blooming cavern at midsummer and find the doorway of decisions before pride could close it. 
He had spent thirty years becoming empirically intuitive enough to trust such a sentence, testing old ledgers against creek temperatures, erosion patterns, and the habits of insects that never lied about weather. 
In late June, a county sign warned that as the creek rises, the low road would close, and the shop began to smell of wet clay and mineral green. 
Irvin studied the waterline against his notched walking stick and decided the rising creek was not a reason to wait but a map of its own, drawing him toward the limestone shelf above the village. 
That evening, while he was wrapping a shipment of sea charts, he noticed a column of ants crossing the counter, each one carrying a crumb that had fallen from his uneaten lunch. 
He set the crumb in a saucer, but the ants ignored it and continued east through the door, moving with the unhurried certainty of people returning to a house they had built long ago. 
Because he was empirically intuitive, he did not dismiss them; he matched their direction to a faded survey note about a drowned footpath and decided that following ants home was the most honest compass available. 
He filled a canvas satchel with lamp oil, rope, and the matrix chip, then locked the shop with the key he had stopped lending to anyone. 
The rain started before he reached the ridge, turning the trail into a slick tongue of mud and gravel. 
The ants were always a few steps ahead, climbing roots and crossing stones with a dark glimmer on their shells. 
When the creek began to roar behind him, he understood that they were not fleeing the water but using its pressure to guide them toward air pockets in the rock. 
At midnight, the path ended beneath a shelf where the limestone broke open into a blooming cavern at midsummer. 
Pale fungi and cave lilies crowded the ledges, and hundreds of moths drifted above pools fed by the creek. 
The air was warm and sweet, almost violently alive, and the sound of water seemed to come from beneath the floor rather than the storm outside. 
The ants entered a crack at the cavern's northern edge and vanished, as if the crack were a doorway they had always considered their own. 
Irvin set the chip on a flat stone, and in the lantern light the stamped symbol began to glimmer. 
He had assumed the chip was a printer's tool, but the glimmer came in pulses that matched his heartbeat. 
A groove in the rock accepted the chip, and the wall turned with a grinding sigh, revealing a corridor of stepped stone. 
Beyond it was a chamber where light rose from the floor, and every surface seemed to carry the faint outlines of unmade choices: roads not taken, names not spoken, letters never cast into words. 
At the center stood a low threshold, no taller than a child, carved with the same symbol and with the phrase his grandmother had repeated as a lullaby. 
The doorway of decisions was not locked, but it was still, and he realized it had been waiting not for a key but for someone willing to remember why he had promised to return. 
He thought of the maps he had sold and the years he had spent alone, afraid to choose. 
The creek rose in his ears, and the blooming cavern trembled as water found new passages in the dark. 
He knelt and placed the matrix chip into the threshold socket, completing a sentence begun long ago in a type foundry. 
She had taught him that every decision was cast once, like a letter, and repeated until it became a life. 
The doorway showed him two images: himself safe in the shop, and himself walking into the storm with maps he had never dared to read. 
He stepped through. 
Before dawn the creek fell, and Irvin walked home beside the ant line, carrying the map he had finally chosen.
