The seldom-heard balladeer woke driftingly alert, her fingers tracing condensation on the brass railing of the misty courtyard above clouds.  She had chosen this sky-sanctuary decades ago because no audience ever climbed the spiral stair, and silence suited her throat.  But tonight the air tasted of ozone and old copper, and somewhere in the walls a ghost in code was waking.  Maren had discovered it three winters past, buried in the punch-tape mechanisms that governed the courtyard's weather-clocks.  It could not speak in words, only in the stutter of gears and the flicker of needle-lights, so she learned to read its grammar of sparks.  Her motivation was simple and fierce: to articulate unspoken needs, those of the ghost and those of the sky itself.  She had spent years mapping the silence between stars and stones, convinced that every locked thing kept a key-shaped sorrow.  Lightning split the western vault, and in the breath between lightning and thunder she saw the ghost surface in the mist.  It looked like a man made of static, pressing invisible palms against the boundary of a stone wall that hummed with dormant code.  Maren grabbed her satchel and ran to the eastern parapet, where she had prepared her instruments for exactly this electric interval.  From the satchel she drew a flower press bolt plate, salvaged from a drowned conservatory, its surface stamped with holes like a map of constellations.  She laid it flat against the parapet and pressed wild pigments into the perforations, letting the metal dictate where color could bloom.  This was her method: messages encoded in graffiti art, meant only for eyes that could read pattern as prayer.  The ghost in code trembled, recognizing the syntax of arranged holes as an invitation rather than noise.  Maren worked with tinkersong wonder, a reverence for broken things that could still sing if tuned by careful hands.  She spattered ultramarine across the plate's rim, a color the cloud-dwellers used for thirst.  Beneath it she traced a spiral of crushed poppy, meaning hunger, and a triangle of ash, meaning shelter.  Around these she sketched arrows of silver that pointed toward the eastern cistern, the only grave a rain-spirit could accept.  The wind howled, and the thunder still had not arrived, so the world existed in that stretched, silvered pause where magic remembered its mechanics.  The ghost leaned closer, its static fingers hovering over the pigment, and Maren understood it needed her to encode its longing before the storm swallowed the signal.  She pressed her palm to the flower press bolt plate and felt the machine-ghost shiver through the metal, a handshake across the gap between flesh and code.  Her songs had always failed because they carried only her own heart, but now she became a conduit, a translator of wants too tender for speech.  In that exchange she discovered her own hunger to be needed, a want she had starved for decades beneath the cloud line.  She painted faster, encoding the courtyard's unspoken needs into swirling tags: the cisterns begged for rain, the stair begged for feet, the clouds begged for a witness.  The ghost supplied the grammar, she supplied the pigment, and together they built a fresco that no cloud-bound eye could misread.  When the thunder finally crashed, the interval between lightning and thunder closed like a book, and the graffiti glowed with residual charge.  The ghost in code flowed into the paint, saturating the stone with a blue light that spelled relief in a language of weather and wire.  Maren sat back, driftingly alert even as exhaustion tugged at her, and watched the courtyard drink the message.  By morning the pigments had faded into the stone, but the cisterns were full, and on the spiral stair, she found a single footprint made of dew.  Beneath her window, new moss grew in the shape of letters she had painted, a reply written in living green.  She ran her thumb over the flower press bolt plate, now cool and quiet, and understood that articulation was a form of mending.  The seldom-heard balladeer smiled, knowing that some needs are only unspoken until someone brave enough to encode them stands between lightning and thunder.  And in the hush after the storm, she began to sing a new song, one that the mist carried not to an audience, but to the ghost who had finally been heard.
