The fever broke on the third day above the cloud line, leaving Ren hollow and ringing like a struck bell. 
She had climbed the switchbacks to the mountain weather station wrapped in wool and silence, seeking the oracle who tolerated her own errors. 
Mira had lived there for eleven years, recording temperatures and laughing when the instruments lied. 
"Come in," Mira called before Ren knocked, her voice cracked by altitude and disuse. 
The station's windows were frosted with rime, but the aurora leaked green and violet across the glass, casting a sorrow so bright it felt like grief wearing a wedding dress. 
Ren set down her pack and unfolded the botanical illustration, its paper yellowed, its ink still sharp. 
The plant had been drawn from a specimen found near the summit, its petals mapped with obsessive care. 
"It grows in the margins," Mira said, peering over Ren's shoulder. 
"Not the meadows, not the sheltered hollows. 
It roots in the grit between rock and ice, where nothing should thrive." 
Ren touched the lower corner of the page, where a charcoal smudge blurred the Latin names. 
"My mother made this. 
She swallowed something true before she died, and I need to know how heavy it was." 
Mira's eyes, pale and patient, held the peripherally aware quality of someone who watched storms from the corners of her vision. 
"Fault-tolerant," Mira muttered, as if explaining herself. 
"I predict, I fail, I adjust. 
The mountain does not care for my precision." 
She moved to the brass scales on the wall, the ones used to weigh snow samples before melting them. 
"Truth has mass," Mira said. 
"A swallowed one changes the body. 
We will find its weight by occupying margins, the way the plant does." 
Ren slept that night in a bunk that smelled of kerosene and old wool, her dreams swaying like certainties caught in a downdraft. 
When she woke, Mira had laid the botanical illustration on the observation deck, pinning its corners with stones. 
The sky was clearing, the aurora retreating into a pale sorrow along the northern rim. 
"Watch the edges," Mira instructed. 
"The center is a lie the artist told herself. 
The margins hold what she could not say aloud." 
Ren stared until her eyes watered, and only then, peripherally aware, did she notice the faint second sketch beneath the botanical illustration. 
It was a map, not of the mountain, but of a body, with a dark knot where the throat would be. 
Mira produced a stick of willow charcoal and handed it to Ren. 
"Smudge the margin," she said. 
"Let the boundary dissolve. 
Certainty must sway or it snaps." 
Ren drew the charcoal across the map's edge, blurring rock into flesh, altitude into artery. 
The paper blackened under her thumb, and a fine dust fell onto the brass scales. 
They weighed the smudged page first: four ounces. 
Then they weighed the original, unblemished illustration from Ren's pack: the same four ounces. 
Ren felt the swallowed truth shift in her chest, a stone that would not settle. 
Mira adjusted the scales, her fault-tolerant mind refusing to declare the experiment a failure. 
"The weight is not in the paper," she said. 
"It is in what the page displaces. 
Air. 
Memory. 
The space between breaths." 
Ren looked again at the smudge, now a soft grey cloud eating the margins of the map. 
She understood that her mother had not died of altitude sickness, but of a truth she could not exhale. 
The botanical illustration had been her attempt to speak, to plant the secret where only a daughter would look. 
Ren took the charcoal and wrote along the margin in small, fierce letters: I know. 
The certainties of her life swayed then, not breaking, but bending like the aurora bending around the magnetic pole. 
Mira placed a hand on the glass, her fingers tracing the path of a satellite that would never arrive. 
"We record weather because it refuses to be recorded," the oracle said. 
"We tell truth because it refuses to be still." 
Outside, the wind rose, and the mountain weather station groaned in its bones, tolerating the storm as Mira tolerated error. 
Ren folded the smudged illustration and pressed it to her sternum, feeling the exact, terrible weight of what she had swallowed. 
The aurora sorrowed above them, green and immense, and the fever-cool air tasted of iron and petal. 
She would descend in the morning, carrying the weight down to the village, planting it finally in the margins of her own life. 
The station would remain, fault-tolerant and peripherally aware, weighing the unweighable in the swaying light.
