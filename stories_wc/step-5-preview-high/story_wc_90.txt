The recycled spaceship called Dandelion had outlived every nation that built her, and Mira maintained her with prayer and solder. 
It was thirty years after the last human was born, and Lio, that final child, was the only future the ship still possessed. 
Mira had carried Lio into the world with her own hands, and she had made a vow there would be no guns aboard her decks. 
The old crew had called her a steadfast pacifist, half in admiration and half in worry. 
She accepted both names, because her vow was not soft. 
It was a blade she kept sheathed against her own throat. 
Since childhood she had been gravitationally lighter when happy, a quirk of her mother's long radiation pregnancy. 
Laughter made her drift; joy turned her bones to thistledown. 
Grief, anger, and guilt kept her boots on the deck. 
In a ship made of salvage, that mattered more than anyone admitted. 
The resident psychologist had called it shadow work, not magic. 
A person had to sit in the dark with everything she had disowned and stitch it back into the pattern of her life. 
Mira practiced in the Dandelion's deepest holds, where her own rage waited like a second spine. 
She had wanted to burn the raiders who killed her sister and to silence the council that had ended the breeding program. 
She sat with that hatred until it spoke, then stitched it into her chest where it could watch without ruling. 
Her needle and her lens were Kwan Yin's tear, a bead of translucent salt larger than her thumbnail, recovered from a drowned temple. 
Her mother had pressed it into her hand on the day the birth ward burned. 
It would not cut flesh, but it would cut lies, and it could guide a thread through the seam between what was and what had been refused. 
The locked dimension had begun as a whisper in the recyclers: a folded place, a stalled womb, a chamber where the unborn waited. 
Lio heard it at night, calling in the voices of children who had never drawn breath. 
The ship's priests claimed the door could only be pushed open by a soul that faced the dark without trying to conquer it. 
Mira meant to push open a locked dimension not for escape but so that the last human might not end in sterile silence. 
The navigators had no coordinates. 
The only maps were faded photographs salvaged from dead quarters, curled with age and dried tears. 
Mira read the folded coordinates via the faint shimmer of long-dried tears on faded photographs. 
She hung the pictures from the ribbed ceiling and waited for Kwan Yin's tear to catch their shine. 
On the night the recyclers screamed, the tear bloomed with a pale thread no thicker than spider silk. 
Mira climbed into the dark hold, following that shimmer, and found the air torn in a straight seam like a badly mended wound. 
She took a needle of shipwire, threaded it with light from the tear, and began to stitch the seam wider. 
Every stitch was a refusal to tear. 
Every pull asked the locked dimension to open instead of forcing it to bleed. 
The shadow of her rage coiled up from the floor and tried to seize her wrist. 
It showed her the birth ward again, the smoke, the small bodies. 
Mira grew heavier with sorrow until her boots were anchors, then lighter with a compassion that made her want to float. 
She did not banish the shadow. 
She stitched it to her own ribs and said, "You may watch, but you may not steer." 
The seam opened with a sound like a held breath released. 
Beyond it lay a field of folded cradles and children waiting to be born into a kinder archive of time. 
Lio stepped through first, and the ship's dying lights steadied as if the dimension had given them breath. 
Mira remained behind, ghostly and resolved, mending the threshold with her shining thread. 
She had not pushed the door with force. 
She had persuaded it open with sorrow, with joy, and with every stitch she could make.
