The lost soul collector climbed into Echo Gorge before the hidden sunrise had bled through the mist. 
She was modestly fearless, a trait born from counting other people's grief in ledgers for thirty years. 
Her coat smelled of river mud and old paper, and her fingers gripped the crocodile's tooth like a cold promise. 
It was not a trophy; it was a tuning key, a splinter of a beast that had once swallowed echoes and refused to digest them. 
The gorge yawned around her, terraced with pale stone that drank the darkness and gave back faint hums. 
In the hush of a hidden sunrise, the dead grew talkative, and their voices pooled in the hollows like cold milk. 
She had come to pursue one voice among them, a boy who had died choking on a laugh that never finished leaving his chest. 
His spectral confession had lodged in the rocks, turning every morning into a rehearsal of the same sharp inhale. 
The standard ritual required solemn hymns and kneeling, but she had a different theory. 
Healing without laughter was only rearrangement, she believed, and she intended to prove it. 
At the gorge's heart stood the misaligned harp, its frame warped by decades of damp and sorrow. 
No one played it correctly; even the wind produced distorted notes that made listeners weep without knowing why. 
The collector stepped beneath the strings and lifted the crocodile's tooth to the lowest chord. 
She did not strum it so much as argue with it, dragging the tooth across the wire until the note bent into something almost like a question. 
The sound traveled through the distorted notes of a misaligned harp and shattered into spectral confessions that took visible shape. 
Pale mouths opened in the air, whispering betrayals, apologies, and recipes for bread long since extinct. 
She walked forward, modestly fearless, letting the confessions brush her cheeks like moth wings. 
Some clutched at her sleeves, begging to be carried out, but her ledger was reserved for a single name. 
The boy's confession was tangled near the eastern wall, a knot of sound that hiccuped between tears and giggles. 
She pursued it along the ledge, leaping over fissures that exhaled frost and stale applause. 
The hidden sunrise strengthened, sending blades of amber light down the stone to meet her. 
Each beam carried the luminous ache of a world waking up while something in the gorge remained asleep. 
When she reached the knot, she saw him: a translucent figure doubled over, trying to laugh and apologize at once. 
His confession was unfinished, a punchline that had turned into a riddle he could not solve. 
She set the crocodile's tooth against the harp's bridge, tuning the misalignment just enough to let joy pass through. 
The next notes were imperfect, cracked at the edges, but they carried a wobble of warmth instead of pure grief. 
The boy's mouth opened, and instead of a haunting, out came a startled, dusty chuckle that bounced off the canyon walls. 
The echo returned to him changed, carrying laughter back into the wound until it began to close like a smile. 
Other confessions stirred, confused by the sound, then curious, then brave. 
She moved along the gorge, modestly fearless, guiding each lost soul to release its burden through joy rather than sorrow. 
A widow confessed to stealing oranges and laughed until her ghost turned the color of sunrise. 
A soldier admitted fear and found that his confession, set to the harp's crooked melody, sounded like a lullaby. 
The crocodile's tooth grew warm in her palm, no longer a splinter of a dead predator but a key to living sound. 
By the time the hidden sun fully crested the ridge, the gorge held no lingering echoes. 
The lost soul collector sat on a sun-warmed boulder and opened her ledger to a fresh page. 
She wrote not of the dead, but of the living who might still learn to laugh while healing. 
The misaligned harp stayed behind her, still crooked, but now its distortion carried a hidden sunrise inside every note. 
And somewhere in the valley below, a child laughed for no reason, continuing the work she had started. 
The luminous ache of the gorge did not vanish; it simply changed key, becoming something she could carry. 
She closed the ledger, tucked the crocodile's tooth into her pocket, and began the descent home.
