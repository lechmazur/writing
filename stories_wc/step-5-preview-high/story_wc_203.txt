Elias Vane repaired elevators by trade and listened to ghosts by compulsion. 
The city had hired him to inspect a rusted freight lift inside the abandoned circus ticket office on Halstead. 
He preferred buildings like this, hollow, honest, and thick with the residue of people who had once believed in wonder. 
The ticket office still smelled of stale popcorn and cracked leather, and peeling posters advertised acrobats whose painted smiles had begun to flake like scabs. 
Elias was skillfully observant, a trait that kept his clients nervous and his ghosts relatively honest. 
He knelt at the freight elevator threshold and ran a gloved finger along the jam, noting rust rings and a hairline fracture that had nothing to do with metal fatigue. 
Dust, gray and velvety, had settled in even layers across the floorboards. 
But near the ceiling, the patterns broke. 
He analyzed dust accumulation patterns the way other men read newspapers, searching for headlines in the smudges. 
A clean arc swept above the ticket window, as though something had risen through the plaster again and again. 
Cracks in the facade had spread from the crown molding like veins of ash, and he traced them upward until they disappeared behind a water-stained acoustic tile. 
The overhead fluorescent buzz had been dying since he arrived, a tired insect hum that now stuttered and surrendered to dark. 
He knew the timeframe mattered. 
When the overhead fluorescent buzz died off, the veil between breath and silence wore thin enough to touch. 
She appeared beside the turnstile. 
A woman in a sequined leotard, her ankles wrapped in gauze that did not move, her face fixed in the weightless burden of a performer who had forgotten how to rest. 
Elias did not flinch. 
"You have been climbing," he said quietly. 
The ghost pointed toward the ceiling with a hand that ended in translucent smudges. 
He had come to discover what ceilings hide; it was the reason he still worked shafts alone at night. 
Elias dragged a folding ladder beneath the widest fissure and pried at the acoustic tile with a pry bar. 
The gap exhaled cold air and the smell of machine oil. 
Inside the crawlspace, wrapped in oilcloth, sat a horologist's mainspring barrel. 
It was brass, thumb-sized, and inscribed with the circus's old maker mark, its coiled spring still holding tension after thirty years. 
He lifted it into the dim light. 
The ghost drifted closer, her eyes fixed on the barrel with a hunger that was almost memory. 
"The high-wire clock," she whispered. 
"It regulated the trapeze." 
Elias understood then. 
The circus had hidden its true mechanism in the ceiling so that no ticket-buyer would ever know how precarious the flight truly was. 
The cracks in the facade were not merely structural; they were the building confessing its own lies. 
He engaged the ghost by holding the barrel aloft, letting the last gray light from the window catch its teeth. 
The brass teeth gleamed. 
She reached out, not to take it, but to engage with the weight she had abandoned decades ago. 
Her fingers passed through, yet the mainspring shuddered once, as if time itself had mis-stepped. 
Dust drifted down from the ceiling, rearranging itself into a new pattern above their heads. 
Elias set the barrel on the counter beside a ledger that recorded sold-out shows for a night that never came. 
"You kept it running," he said. 
She nodded, and the sequins on her shoulders dulled to gray. 
The weightless burden was not the dead that haunted him, but the living who chose to forget them. 
He left the ticket office at dawn, the barrel in his satchel and the ghost finally still. 
The freight elevator would remain out of service, its cab a shrine to a ceiling finally opened. 
But Elias would return to the next building, skillfully observant, ready to analyze dust accumulation patterns again. 
Some burdens, he knew, were heavy only because no one else would lift them.
