The lunar observatory above the clouds stood like a silver anchor, and inside it the sage camera repairer, Oona, listened to metal learning to scream. 
The time when foundations shake arrived at the third hour, a slow tremor traveling up from the buried roots of the station. 
Oona had spent forty years learning that machines tell the truth before instruments do. 
Her hands were changelessly constant as she climbed into the instrument shaft, and the staff left her alone because her silence had become a kind of law. 
The observatory had been built to photograph the ephemeral: moonrise over Earth, auroras, the blue shimmer of clouds warming toward dawn. 
Oona intended to harness the ephemeral, to hold the moments that fled from every lens she ever mended. 
She had come because the observatory's great camera had begun to weep light and no one else could hear the sound of its weeping. 
In the heart of the camera lay a regulator clock, a small brass coffin that measured exposure with a single hand. 
That clock hand would not stop spinning, a blur of metal that no spring could explain. 
It spun so wildly that the exposure windows became prayers whispered in a storm. 
The shaking had shifted the mount by a hair, and the mount had changed the load on the clock's governor. 
If the hand kept whirling, the shutter would open at the wrong instant, and the observatory would record a smear where a world should have stood. 
Oona set her toolbox on the vibration-damped table and closed her eyes, listening. 
On a shelf of specimens from a wetter world, a single preserved leaf had fallen onto a copper dish when the first tremor came. 
Now she lifted the leaf to the lip of the dish and learned what she needed by the sound of a leaf falling. 
Its whisper lasted less than a breath, and in that whisper she found a beat, a tiny unit of time that no machine had corrupted. 
The leaf told her the spin was not mechanical malice but a structural flaw. 
The governor had been mounted to the old frame in a way that made every tremor feed itself. 
To stop it, she would have to restructure the governor's housing and let the clock breathe in a new shape. 
But restructure meant opening the regulator during the quake, and the foundations were no longer pretending to be still. 
She removed her gloves, and the cold taught her fingers to hurry. 
The clock hand blurred past the number three, past the number seven, as if it were trying to escape the dial itself. 
That was when she understood that escape velocity was not only a number for rockets but the longing of every captured thing. 
The hand wanted to break the pull of the dial, and the trembling foundations wanted to break the pull of the Moon, and she wanted to break the pull of loss. 
She would give the mechanism its release, not by force, but by changing the shape of the path it traveled. 
She unscrewed the governor, unwound its bent spring, and laid the pieces beside the leaf in the copper dish as if preparing an offering. 
The leaf's faint rustle still seemed to hang in the air, marking the seconds that mattered. 
She fitted a new bearing and shifted the governor so that the shaking would cancel its own force. 
Then she remade the hand's seat so that a tremor, instead of driving the spin, would slow it. 
She worked by the same sound that had guided her, by the sound of a leaf falling, and she matched each click of the governor to that brief hush. 
The foundations shuddered one last time, and the clock hand stuttered, caught, and settled at the number four. 
A profound quiet returned, changelessly constant as the Moon outside, but different now, as if the station had finally agreed to remain. 
Oona mounted the camera's plate and watched the shutter open for exactly the length of a leaf's fall. 
The image rose into the lens like a small planet leaving its world, carrying Earth's clouds away from grief and into silver light. 
She had harnessed the ephemeral not by keeping it still but by giving it a true path. 
The spin was gone, and in its place was a luminous ache, a tenderness for everything that passes and everything that remains. 
On the viewing wall, the clouds turned slowly, and Oona smiled as if she had heard one more leaf fall.
