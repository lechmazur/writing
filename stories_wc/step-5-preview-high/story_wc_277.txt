The journalist's call had ended forty minutes ago, yet its echo still rattled inside Cornelia Vale's mind like a marble in a tin can.  She was the sole heir to the Argos Newsprint Trust, a woman who had spent her adolescence smelling of ink rather than perfume, and the reporter's insinuation that her late father had faked his celestial survey had roused her from complacency.  So she had taken the dawn funicular to the echoing observatory above clouds, a brass-and-glass cathedral built to chart what her father had called the Orbits of Regret.  The phrase had always struck her as solemn playfulness, a mathematical jest that assumed the dead still traced ellipses around the living, and perhaps begged forgiveness for every unchecked proof.  Cornelia was devotedly observant, a habit drilled into her by years of editing headlines at midnight; she noticed that the dome's eastern shutter had been forced open, and that a single rosewood pen nib lay abandoned in the dust beside the meridian wheel.  The nib was from her mother's desk set, the instrument that had corrected her father's proofs before the ink ever reached the plates, and it still smelled faintly of cedar and vetiver.  Its presence meant the intruder had not acted alone, or perhaps that her father had left it as a breadcrumb for anyone clever enough to see.  A cough erupted from the catwalk, where a gaunt figure in a reporter's rumpled suit stood adjusting the telescope's fine focus with theatrical care.  It was Julian Marsh, the same man who had called her an hour ago with a voice like a velvet threat, offering to bury the story if she confirmed the observatory was merely a tax shelter for pseudoscience.  Cornelia set her satchel down and adopted the gentle irony she used when facing boardroom wolves.  "You forced the shutter before dawn, Mr. Marsh," she said, "which means you believe the Orbits of Regret are visible only at first light, or that we are both fools."  He flinched, then smiled, his eyes calculating whether pretense still had any value.  "Your father documented a comet that returns to the same constellation every seventy years," Marsh said, "but there is no comet."  "There is only a gap in the star charts, a sentimental hole he paid astronomers to leave open."  Cornelia lifted the rosewood pen nib, turning it so that dawn light caught the worn initials carved into its side.  Her motivation to discover hidden truths had never been sentimental; it was arithmetic, a ledger that must balance before the world could be believed.  She set the nib on the logbook and began to reason aloud, her tone half dirge, half chess move.  "Suppose, as you claim, the observatory exists to fabricate data."  "Then a fraudulent survey would require a consistent sky, yet my father's charts contain deliberate errors that no forger would include."  "They align instead with an asteroid your employer's telescope logged last Tuesday, proving the ledgers are genuine and your premise is false."  Marsh stiffened, the silence of the dome pressing in like water.  "Through proof by contradiction," she said lightly, "your story collapses, and with it your leverage."  She had intervened, she realized, not to save her father's name, but to correct the orbit before another body was flung off course by the lie.  The journalist's shoulders dropped, the theatrical menace draining like mercury from a broken thermometer.  "The nib," he said, nodding toward the logbook.  "It was in the safe with the real ledgers."  "Your father hid them where only a devotedly observant daughter would think to look, and left the door unlocked for the rest of us."  Cornelia placed the nib in her pocket, feeling the weight of proof and pardon at once, though she suspected pardon was a rumor.  Outside, the clouds parted to reveal a pale dot tracing its long, elliptical regret across the morning, indifferent to human ledgers.  She smiled, solemn and playful, and reached for the telephone to fire her publicist before the journalist could change his mind.
