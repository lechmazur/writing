Mist coiled through the serpentine grotto like a slow breath.  Inside that damp curl of stone, an earthbound star watcher knelt at a basin of tidewater.  Her name was Lira, and she had not left the coast in nineteen years.  Yet every night she charted the stars through the grotto’s narrow throat, tracing their paths across wet rock.  Now, as memories resurfaced like silt stirred from the seabed, she sorted shards of sea glass upon a slab of slate.  Each piece was cloudy, frosted, and dull, the sea having ground away every sharp edge.  Lira’s hands remembered what her heart had nearly forgotten: the village above the cliffs had banned flame after the great drought, and the winter dark had grown heavy.  She had promised them light without fire, a gentler beacon born of patience rather than heat.  Her method was gem polishing, a slow alchemy of water, grit, and touch.  She lifted a cobalt fragment and let it weigh against her palm, feeling its density like a small verdict.  It was heavy enough to hold a reflection, light enough to keep a star.  With a leather lap and fine corundum sand, she began to work the curve of the shard.  The grotto breathed around her, a serpentine hush that uncoiled with every pass of her thumb.  Mist drifted inward on a zephyr bloom, carrying salt and the ghost of meadow flowers from the headlands.  It was as though spring itself leaned close to watch.  Another memory rose: her mother teaching her to read the night sky by the glow of phosphorus on the waves.  Lira had been earthbound even then, rooted by illness while other children climbed the lighthouse stairs.  But the stars had never required her ascent; they required only her witness.  She polished until the sea glass lost its frost and grew reflectively vibrant, a blue that seemed to inhale the dim grotto air and return it as luminescence.  She weighed the stone again, this time against her heartbeat, and knew the piece was ready.  It was not merely glass now; it had become a pearl of stillness, a drop of calm that held the sea’s long patience inside its curved face.  When dawn threatened beyond the rocks, Lira carried the gem to the grotto’s mouth and set it in a natural cradle of basalt.  The morning star met the surface, and the polished sea glass answered with a silent, rippling fire.  No fuel was burned, no wick was lit, yet the blue light pooled across the wet stones like a waking bloom.  Villagers came at first with suspicion, then with bowls to catch the glow.  They saw in the gem their own faces softened, their breath slowed, their fear of the dark thawing.  Children touched its surface and felt the hush of deep water.  Lira told them nothing of magic; she spoke only of tides and time.  Still, the pearl of stillness did its quiet work, turning the grotto into a chapel of reflected light.  As memories resurfaced in those early days, she remembered the old astronomers who believed the sky was a sea turned upside down.  Perhaps that was why sea glass could hold starlight: both were waters that had learned to glow.  The zephyr bloom of the coast carried her reputation inland, and soon travelers climbed the cliffs with lanterns doused, seeking the light that did not burn.  They brought their own shards, asking her to weigh and polish them into mirrors of calm.  Lira taught them to listen to each stone, to feel when the surface sang against the grit.  She taught them that to share light without using fire, one must first become still enough to receive it.  Seasons folded gently, and the serpentine grotto in mist became a sanctuary.  Lira remained earthbound, but her light wandered.  In distant windows, reflectively vibrant stones caught the dawn and returned it to those who had forgotten the sky.  The grotto keeper grew old, her hands steady, her eyes always turned upward through the mist.  And when at last she set down her tools, the next star watcher found a single sea glass resting in the basin.  It was unmarked, yet it held the entire night.  A pearl of stillness waited for morning, patient as the tide.  Outside, the wind stirred the cliffs, and somewhere a zephyr bloom opened.
