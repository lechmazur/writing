After the orchard grew silent beneath the moon, the secretive conjurer returned to the cavern orchard with a small tin of glass. 
The trees there were old and patient, their roots gripping broken mosaic, their branches heavy with fruit that never fell. 
Stalactites nourished the orchard drop by drop, and the water had taught the roots to listen instead of grow. 
The conjurer had always been gently inventive, preferring small repairs to grand sorcery. 
They came to understand the mathematics of emotion, to weigh what could not be held. 
For three years their partner had walked into the orchard at night and had not walked out. 
There was no body, no grave, and no word from the outer villages, only a silence that felt unfinished. 
The conjurer called it ambiguous loss, though the phrase felt thin against the weight of it. 
On the path lay a mosaic stepping stone, chipped in patterns that seemed almost deliberate. 
The partner had counted those chips aloud, heel and toe, turning a walk into proof. 
Now the conjurer learned to read the orchard via the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone. 
From their pocket they took the taxidermist's glass eye pair, two pale orbs no warmer than river stones. 
A taxidermist in the lower caves had made them for a customer who wanted dead eyes to remain bright. 
The conjurer set the eyes into two chipped hollows, and the moonlight passing through the glass turned the stone into a diagram. 
Through the eyes, the drips became numbers, the roots became curves, and the missing tiles became a proof waiting for a final line. 
The conjurer knelt and began to write, searching for the variable that would make sorrow resolve. 
Every equation failed in the same place, at an unknown that refused to become zero. 
The partner was not dead, yet not alive in any ledger the conjurer could balance, and that refusal was the cruelty of the thing. 
The moon moved, and the orchard's silence deepened, until even the dripping seemed to hold its breath. 
Then the conjurer noticed how the water worked, seeking each fault in the stone and widening it gently into a channel. 
It did not fill the cracks all at once, nor did it deny that they were there. 
The method arrived not as lightning but as a small correspondence between stone, drop, and the chipped pattern. 
The conjurer's own patient practice began to dovetail with the orchard's slower arithmetic of endurance. 
The fruit was sweeter near the broken places, as if the trees learned to answer loss with abundance. 
That observation felt like buried optimism, not a promise of return, but a reason to keep tending. 
The conjurer removed the glass eyes and set them on the stone beside a fresh equation. 
They wrote that emotion was not a single number but a field of relations, some measurable and some only survivable. 
The unknown partner became a variable in their work, not an answer, and not a thing to be solved away. 
Still, the proof waited, and the conjurer believed that a missing person could remain present in the structure of care. 
On some nights they thought the roots tapped in answer, though it might have been only water choosing the easiest fault. 
The conjurer no longer expected the orchard to give back what it had taken without naming. 
Instead they kept the path swept, the stone clear, and the equations open-ended, in case the missing returned as something other than grief. 
Ambiguous loss remained, but it no longer stood alone in the dark. 
A neighbor from the upper farm sometimes left a basket of grain at the cavern mouth, and the conjurer accepted it without explaining. 
In the quiet, the conjurer's hope was not loud; it was a small steady thing, like water finding its way through stone. 
And the orchard, patient beneath the moon, continued to grow around the space where someone had vanished.
