The forgotten underwater city garden had kept its secret in salt and silence until the living statue spoke into my mind. 
I had come to catalog coral, not to be guided by a living statue with telepathy. 
This was after revealing the secret, and every old answer in the water had begun to move. 
Its face was blank, but its thoughts arrived, incisively kind, and made me lower my lamp. 
The cascading revelations did not fall like a stone. 
They pulled apart every certainty I had carried down from the surface. 
The records said invaders had broken the gates and drowned Vellamaris in a single night. 
The statue said there had been no invaders. 
There had been a gardener, a queen with a failing heart, and a council afraid of poison. 
Foxglove grew along the eastern terraces in violet spires, beautiful and lethal. 
The court physician distilled it into medicine that could slow the queen's pulse. 
The same distillation, wrongly measured, could stop that pulse forever. 
I followed the statue's gaze to a cracked bowl at its stone feet. 
There lay a foxglove-stained glass ampoule, its neck sealed with wax and brine. 
The glass was clouded purple, as if the flower had been pressed against it for centuries. 
My first certainty had been that the city died by treachery. 
The statue's confession split that certainty open. 
It had threaded a copper wire through a structural weakness in the seawall lock. 
The weakness was a flaw only a gardener would notice, a gap worn by salt and patience. 
With that thin thread of metal, it had tripped the gates and let the ocean in. 
"You murdered them," I thought, but the words cracked as they left me. 
"I chose a slower mercy," the statue replied. 
It showed me, in the way memory moves through telepathy, the night of the burning order. 
The council had decided the foxglove was a curse because an overdose had killed the queen. 
They carried torches toward the conservatory to purge every root and seed. 
The statue knew the queen's death was not that simple. 
The physician had prepared a correct dose in the ampoule I now held. 
A rival had poured a second, lethal measure into the queen's wine after the medicine was given. 
The foxglove was blamed because it was already in her blood, innocent and guilty at once. 
The statue had faced an impossible choice between letting the garden burn and flooding the city that had misunderstood it. 
It chose water because roots could survive it. 
It chose water because fire would have made the garden ash and silence. 
I felt that choice settle into me, not as forgiveness, but as complication. 
The living statue did not want worship. 
It did not want absolution. 
It wanted me to welcome nuance into a certainty. 
Its thoughts were patient, precise, and almost gentle. 
"Take the ampoule," it said. 
"Let them see that the poison they named was also a remedy." 
I lifted the glass into my glove, surprised by how light it felt. 
The stain was warm, or perhaps my own blood had warmed it. 
The statue turned my attention to the eastern wall, where the dome had broken against the reef. 
A narrow fracture ran through the iron ribs, black and jagged. 
"Thread this line through the structural weakness," it instructed. 
"The current will carry you to the stair, and the ampoule will stay dry in your case." 
I unspooled a coil of kelp rope from my belt. 
My hands shook as I worked the fibers through the crack. 
The action felt like an echo. 
The statue had threaded metal through a broken lock to drown a city. 
I was threading rope through a broken wall to carry the truth back to air. 
Both acts had moved through damage. 
Both acts had trusted a flaw to become a passage. 
When the rope was fast, the statue placed one stone hand over the fracture, as if blessing the wound. 
Its thoughts were fading, not in fear, but in completion. 
"They will call me a monster," it said. 
"Let them," I answered. 
"Then let them call me something more difficult." 
The cascading revelations had finished moving through me, and what remained was not simplicity. 
It was a truer shape, with edges that did not match. 
I pulled myself through the structural weakness, into blue light. 
The garden fell away behind me, animate lifeless, bronze and bloom and bone. 
Fish darted through the ribs of a gazebo like nerves through an old hand. 
The statue did not follow. 
It had already become part of the place, a guardian no longer asked to choose. 
I surfaced at dawn, the ampoule held against my chest. 
The stain had faded to the color of old wine.
