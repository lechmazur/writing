After the final petal drops from the old rosebush, Marnie steps onto the fire escape and lets the door click shut behind her. 
She is the fire escape gardener of the Halden Building, keeper of cracked pots, climbing beans, and one stubborn old rosebush. 
The luminous harbor by moonlight holds a quivering hush, as though the water has paused between tides. 
Marnie is wistfully pragmatic about endings; the rose has dropped its last petal, so she turns from planting to the work she has prepared all summer. 
Her motivation is not magic but geometry: to reflect light into dark corners, to lend the sun's borrowed silver to rooms that never receive it. 
On the rail she has arranged three salvaged mirrors, a strip of oiled felt, and a battered tin box of brass pins. 
The most important tool is the smallest: a lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth, its edges dulled by decades of measuring the secret depths of locks. 
Her father had been lockmaster to the harbor warehouses, and when he died he left her the gauge tooth and the habit of precision. 
She presses its slender tooth against each mirror bracket to gauge the angle, a fraction of an inch at a time. 
Out on the water, a night boat begins to move, and she works with the rhythm of oars in water: lift, settle, tilt, pause. 
The stroke steadies her hands better than any prayer, and she lets the distant knocking of the oarlocks set the pace of her care. 
Her plan is simple enough for a gardener to manage, and she has tested it for three autumns with good results. 
The first mirror catches the moon and throws a wide pane of brightness across the alley wall, past the shuttered laundry and the rusted drainpipe. 
Down on the steps, a boy named Tib looks up from his homework, his pencil frozen above the page. 
He is eleven and wary, with a bruise of fear around his eyes that has not faded since the flood took his uncle's boat. 
Marnie knows the dark corners she is trying to reach are not only in buildings; some of them sit behind a child's ribs. 
She sets the second mirror lower, using the gauge tooth to measure the tilt, and aims a narrow beam under the tenement door. 
The light lands on the floor like a small silver fish, and Tib's mother stops in the doorway with a basket of mended nets. 
She does not ask where it comes from; the harbor teaches people to accept strange gifts without breaking them. 
Marnie moves to the last bracket, which will send a thin stripe of moon across the upper windows where the old tailor sleeps. 
He leaves a cracked pot of geraniums on the fire escape each spring, and she leaves him beans in return, so the exchange is already old and trusted. 
Tonight the tailor raises his window, nods once, and closes the curtain until only a soft rectangle of moon remains. 
It is a protected vulnerability that passes between them, a quiet offering that asks nothing and promises no more than light. 
The quivering hush deepens as the clouds thin, and the harbor seems to lean closer to hear its own silence. 
Marnie wipes the gauge tooth on her sleeve and wonders if her father ever imagined it would help her become a gardener of borrowed light. 
He had taught her that a lock is only a promise shaped like metal, and that every promise needs a key cut to fit. 
Now she cuts no keys, yet she still measures depths; she only wants to instill a steadiness in people who have learned to expect dark. 
A gull cries once over the water and is gone, and the rhythm of the oars answers from far down the harbor. 
Marnie checks the angles one last time, then sets the gauge tooth in the tin box beside a packet of lettuce seeds. 
She will plant the greens tomorrow, but tonight she leaves the mirrors aimed at the moon, trusting them to hold the light. 
Tib raises his hand from the steps, not waving, just holding his palm up to the brightness as if it were something he could weigh. 
The old rosebush stands bare behind her, but Marnie knows that after the final petal drops, a garden can still give light.
