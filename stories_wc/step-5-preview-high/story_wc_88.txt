After the procedure, Wren Calloway woke up with a mouth like borrowed linen and a past she was not allowed to own. 
The clinic called it a clean slate, and she had paid for it the way all clean slate seekers paid, with a dirty past documented in files she could no longer open. 
Her hands were steady, her reflexes expensive, and her conscience scrubbed down to a hum. 
The intake nurse gave her a sealed carton, one sentimental item permitted per erasure. 
Inside, wrapped in oilpaper, was a baseball signed by a time traveler. 
The signature read A. J. Wexler, first base, and the date was 2119. 
Wren had no memory of a ball, a player, or a century she had never seen. 
She only knew it felt delicately robust in her palm, worn leather holding a seam tighter than anything new. 
That was the first wonder in a year of jaded arithmetic. 
Because screens made blanks queasy, the Registry put her to work reading between newspaper lines for anomalies, contraband rumors, and weather lies. 
It was simple work if you treated print as code. 
The obituaries had the wrong initials; the crossword answers spelled reservoir coordinates; the classifieds begged for a woman who had lost her name. 
She read them in her narrow room while rain fell on a city that no longer flinched at storms. 
The procedure had made her patient, which she suspected was another word for emptied. 
But the baseball kept drawing her back, its signature smudged as if ink were still traveling through time. 
When she tilted it under the lamp, microscopic etchings appeared along the seams, coordinates and a warning. 
They matched the newspaper map exactly. 
The journey began at a shuttered pumping station, where she climbed past turnstiles into the old reservoir drain. 
She told herself she was hunting evidence, not redemption. 
The tunnel breathed cold mineral air and narrowed until she had to shuffle sideways with the ball pressed to her chest. 
Then the ceiling opened into an underground bioluminescent lake, and Wren stopped pretending she was unimpressed. 
The water glowed in slow pulses, blue veins running through black stone like a vast nervous system. 
Each pulse lit a different tunnel mouth, and each tunnel mouth waited, patient as a creditor. 
She had read enough to know this place was a labyrinth of choices, not a cave. 
The lake did not offer doors; it offered transactions. 
A passage of silver light promised her the return of every erased memory if she surrendered the ball. 
A green channel promised a new identity with no history if she walked away. 
A dark seam promised the names of the people she had destroyed, if she was willing to carry them. 
She understood then why the time traveler had signed a baseball instead of writing a letter. 
The ball was a compass for people who had forgotten how to want anything. 
It marked the one path that would not trade feeling for safety. 
Her dirty past came back in fragments, not pictures but weights. 
She remembered selling evacuation maps to developers who wanted tenants out of the floodplain. 
She remembered laughing when the levees failed because the check had cleared. 
The procedure had taken the memories, but the lake demanded the consequences. 
Wren stood on the shore and felt nothing, which was exactly the problem. 
She had come to kindle empathy where none remains, though she had not admitted it until the water asked. 
The silver light whispered that memory would hurt. 
The green channel whispered that forgetting would be clean. 
She set the baseball on the stone and chose the dark seam. 
It was not brave, she decided, only sturdy, and delicately robust, like the seam of a ball that survives a century. 
The dark tunnel filled with light as she walked, not from lamps but from the lake adjusting to her choice. 
At the chamber's end she found a pumphouse of dry wells and rows of blanks who had traded their grief for quiet. 
They sat with clean slates in their hands and no stories left to spend. 
Wren placed the baseball in the central well, where its seam split and the signature began to glow. 
The lake rose through the municipal lines, and the city's water started to carry a faint blue ache. 
It was not a cure; jaded wonder never was. 
But that night, in a thousand apartments, people woke and wept for strangers, and Wren heard the first sound she had believed lost. 
She sat by the underground bioluminescent lake, reading the seams of her own choice, and waited for the next seeker to arrive. 
The labyrinth was still there, but it no longer owned her.
