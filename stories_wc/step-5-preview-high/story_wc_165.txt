The tide came in like a slow exhale, filling the tidal arcade under stars with shallow mirrors that trembled whenever the gulls cried.  Between acts of the coastal theater troupe, the arcade emptied of spectators and filled instead with salt wind and the hush of wet stone.  Marguerite, an analytical street performer, stood at the edge of the colonnade with her pockets full of sea glass and her mind full of probability.  She had chosen this hour deliberately, for the interval between performances was when boredom festered like algae on the harbor walls.  Her motivation was never applause; it was to serenade monotony with creativity, to coax wonder from the most reluctant traveler.  From her coat she drew a bishop's chess piece, its felt base worn soft by decades of other hands.  She had recovered it not from a board but from a pawnshop wreck, sensing in its diagonal promise a tool for her peculiar craft.  The arcade was her board, the tide her opponent, and the wandering crowds her unwilling pieces.  Her method was orchestrated serendipity, a practice she perfected by ordering randomness into a geometry of chance.  First she marked the flow of pedestrians, counting footsteps, measuring the pause at each arch, noting who walked with eyes downcast.  She was lightly attentive to details others discarded: the limp of a tourist, the hesitation before a puddle, the glance toward the lighthouse.  Then she began the arrangement.  A silver coin appeared atop the tidal line, precisely where a red-haired violinist from the second act would step.  She nudged a crab into the path of a lost child, knowing the crustacean's sideways skitter would redirect him toward his mother's laugh.  She positioned the bishop's chess piece on the third bench from the west, tilting it so the starlight would catch the groove of its mitre.  Each object was a seed, each placement a stanza, and she waited for the music to happen.  The violinist saw the coin first and pocketed it with a grin, her step lightening as the tide rose around her boots.  A retired judge found the crab, knelt despite his stiff knees, and returned it gently to the sea.  Then the child, no longer lost, darted past the bench and struck the bishop with his knee, sending it spinning into a puddle.  Marguerite allowed herself one breath of doubt, for randomness could refuse to obey even the most elegant ordering.  But a mime from the closing act, silent until now, fished the piece from the water and held it up to the stars.  Around him, strangers leaned into conversation; a chemist explained tides to a painter, and the painter traded a charcoal sketch for a poem.  The arcade, wild with salt and shadow, had become a calculated wilderness where every encounter seemed destined yet secretly arranged.  Marguerite moved between them, lightly attentive, adjusting a scarf here and dropping a card there so the web would hold.  She did not ask to be seen; she only needed the pattern to happen, to bloom like phosphorescence in the wash.  When the theater bells rang for the third act, the crowd dispersed slowly, pockets heavier with small miracles.  The violinist found a second coin near the arch, exactly where Marguerite knew the light would miss.  The judge discovered a sea glass triangle that fit his palm as though carved for him.  The mime kept the bishop, later telling audiences it had swum to him from a wrecked chess set on the ocean floor.  Marguerite watched from the highest stair, her work complete, and let the tide erase her footprints.  Above her the stars turned in their fixed courses, the only randomness she had never learned to order.
