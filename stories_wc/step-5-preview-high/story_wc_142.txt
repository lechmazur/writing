The raft city had been a carnival for so long that nobody remembered a quiet morning, only calliope music braided with foghorns and gulls. 
Maribel Cardoso served as a culture shock absorber for the Transition Office, hired to soften every shock when the outside buyers walked the piers. 
She was orderly intuitive, able to read a queue by the tilt of shoulders and to file a chaotic neighborhood into workable rows in her head. 
In her coat pocket she carried a foundation stone chip, a splinter of granite pried from the original keel block when developers poured new pilings. 
The chip was heavier than it looked, and its edges still bore the mason mark of the first carnival families. 
Privatization was scheduled for the autumn auctions, when the floats and food stalls would become leased inventory. 
Her job, the Office said, was to navigate gentrification so that no one drowned in paperwork when the deed changed. 
Her allegiance, however, was split: she signed the consortium payroll and still ate bean soup at Aunt Iphe's barge every Friday. 
That dual allegiance sat in her chest like a loose coin, rattling at every meeting. 
The carnival was afloat, its streets lashed from painted pontoons that pitched under the crowds like patient animals. 
Music never stopped; the circus practiced through the night so visitors would never learn the hour. 
But the new lease maps sliced the rafts into marketable blocks, and the old routes would be reorganized by surveyors who had never balanced on a wet rope bridge. 
Maribel decided the carnival families had to reorganize first, before the Office could, and she had a method ready. 
She worked through messages encoded in graffiti art, painting sanctioned murals that carried hidden instructions in their layers. 
A stencil fish pointing left meant move stock after dusk; three red lanterns meant a meeting on the bridge of mirrors. 
The number of brushstrokes in each banner told the quarter where a family could claim standing. 
Buyers admired the colors and photographed the style, blind to the fact that the whole city was being quietly rewired beneath their eyes. 
Maribel laid her plan over the keel lines she remembered from the foundation stone chip, tracing the original community spine. 
It was an orderly intuitive sort of map, less grid than rhythm, so it could absorb change without breaking. 
In the survey office, her supervisor, Mr. Halloway, praised her progress and asked for the displacement ledger by Monday. 
He believed the carnival would benefit from a managed refresh, and he needed names to smooth the transition. 
That night Aunt Iphe pressed a folded notice into Maribel's hand, an eviction draft with her granddaughter's name typed in clean red ink. 
You know where the old families sleep, Aunt Iphe said. 
Burn the ledger, or feed it to me, but do not hand it to them. 
Maribel stood between them with the foundation stone chip warm against her ribs, feeling the full weight of her divided loyalty. 
She could not choose the quiet treachery of one side or the pure ruin of the other, so she chose a third path of desperate hope. 
Over the next week she painted the great canvas wall at the funfair entrance, a towering image of a woman standing on two boats at once. 
Critics would call it naive, but each boat held a lantern, and every lantern was drawn to hold a code. 
The codes told every block how to shift its traffic, its stock, and its sleeping quarters so no single lane would be lost to the new fences. 
Families followed her painted instructions because they trusted her pattern, and the raft city began to reorganize itself in full view of the consortium. 
When Halloway arrived to collect the ledger, he found instead a community plan stamped with every household's own seal, already too coordinated to evict without scandal. 
Maribel handed him the foundation stone chip and the survey marks that matched it, proof that the carnival had a deeper claim than the auction papers. 
Privatization still loomed, and the money would still come, but it would have to bend around a people who had learned to move as one. 
On the last night before the notices took effect, the carnival burned its lamps low, and Maribel climbed the highest mast to paint one final coded star. 
It meant hold fast, and it meant begin again, and in the salt dark she allowed herself to believe both.
