The claims adjuster arrived believing that grief was a currency people counterfeited. 
The attentive mystic had been hired to prove that objects absorb emotions, and she did not care what he believed. 
The estate kept the wrecked car in a quartz arbor in mist, where pale pillars sweated cold and she stood firmly grounded on the gravel. 
She was an attentive mystic of small sounds, not a performer, and she worked barehanded. 
Her only prop was a glass prism taken from the dashboard, still webbed with a hairline crack. 
She asked the groundskeeper to leave the windshield unsealed so the night could write on it. 
She told Doran that she would commune through the patterns in frost on a car window. 
He looked at the ferns and the fog and said it sounded like a parlor trick. 
She answered that she dealt in truthful lies: half-truths told exactly, the way a reflection tells the truth by reversing it. 
The mist moved until it seemed to listen. 
She set the prism on the defroster vent and tilted it until a red band lay across the ice. 
The frost patterns answered in fine branches, each line a path where warmth had traveled. 
A shifted hush settled over the arbor. 
It was not silence exactly; it was the absence where silence should have been, and it moved from pillar to fender to the nape of Doran's neck. 
She marked its passage in her notebook and told him that emotion leaves a residue like heat. 
He assumed the driver had panicked at a deer and jerked the wheel. 
The frost did not agree. 
It drew a small round shape, then a longer shape bent across it, as if someone had curled around a child's bicycle. 
The prism darkened as if it had drunk the color from the room. 
Doran stepped back and said that ice could make any picture if you stared long enough. 
She told him that was the honest lie of his trade, trusting only what could be counterfeited. 
Her motivation was not fame or money; she wanted proof that objects absorb emotions so that no one could call a widow's grief superstition. 
She wanted the ring, the prism, and the cracked window to be witnesses, not props. 
The arbor had grown so quiet that the dripping of meltwater sounded like footsteps. 
Then the shifted hush gathered in the windshield and stopped. 
She put her palm flat against the cold glass and whispered that the pattern was turning. 
The frost erased the deer Doran wanted and wrote a different assumption. 
It showed a steering wheel turned hard to the right, away from the road, and a hand lifted from the wheel to shield a passenger. 
This was when assumptions shatter, not with thunder but with the small finality of a rewritten pattern. 
The hairline crack in the prism split further, and the glass fell in two pieces. 
When the lower half struck the gravel, every assumption in the arbor broke as plainly as a mirror dropped in a dream. 
Doran said the driver's name, and it sounded like a question. 
The mystic told him that the prism had absorbed fear, yes, but also the steady calm that follows a chosen sacrifice. 
She said that this was why she was firmly grounded in facts; she had no use for pretty hauntings that could not be recorded. 
She packed the two halves of the prism and the photographs of the frost without ceremony. 
Before he left, Doran asked her to tell him something true that he could put in his report. 
She said the window remembered warmth, the prism remembered color, and the silence remembered where it had been pushed. 
It was a set of truthful lies, because no report would ever hold what the shifted hush had done. 
Yet he wrote it down anyway, and the words held. 
The mist closed behind him, and the quartz arbor kept its cold counsel. 
The mystic stayed a moment longer, firmly grounded, attentive to the faint tick of cooling glass. 
She knew she had not communed with a ghost. 
She had listened to what objects keep when people are too frightened to say it. 
That was enough.
