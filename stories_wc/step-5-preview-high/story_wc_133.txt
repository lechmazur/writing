The tide had finally pulled back from the causeway, exposing the bruised ribs of the old bridge that the water wars had drowned.  Mara, who had not slept through a full night since the levees broke, stepped onto the slick stone with her canvas satchel clutched against her chest.  She was a painter of drowned things, though the phrase felt too romantic for a woman whose hands were permanently stained with cheap charcoal and salt.  The island rose ahead of her like a barnacle-crowned whale, accessible only twice a day, and she had come to it because the mainland no longer offered anything worth looking at.  Last night's storm had left silver veins across the mudflats, and Mara had learned, through three seasons of scavenging, to find her way by following rain trails wherever the runoff cut channels toward the sea.  The water always knew the geometry of the land better than maps did, and she trusted it the way she trusted the honest ache in her shoulders.  Her motive was simple, though people rarely understood it: she needed to thresh meaning from experience, to separate the dry grain of truth from the flood of noise that the wars had left behind.  Halfway up the beach, where the shingle turned to black sand, the rain trails converged in a shallow bowl between two boulders.  Something caught the weak morning sun, and Mara knelt, her joints popping in the cold.  It was an amber brooch, perfectly preserved, its golden depths holding the fossil of a bee that had died long before the first desalination pump ever turned.  She turned it over with a wryly earnest reverence, half-laughing at herself for the superstition of touching it only after wiping the mud from her fingers.  The brooch was not valuable in the way the old markets had measured value, but it was heavy with the specific gravity of lives interrupted.  "You're standing in my drawing," said a voice.  Mara turned to find a gaunt man seated on the higher boulder, his sketchbook balanced on one knee, his eyes carrying the same sleepless gloss she saw mirrored in every puddle.  He introduced himself as Elias, a former hydrologist who had stayed behind when the evacuation ships took the healthy and the useful.  "I map the place where things surface," he said, and his mouth twitched in an expression that was both wry and earnest, as if apology and defiance had learned to share a room.  Mara understood.  They were both excavators of a kind, sifting through the sediment of a broken century.  The storm had unearthed more than amber; it had uncovered a convergence of paths that should have remained separate.  Elias showed her his drawing, not of the island but of the exact moment of their meeting, sketched from memory the night before, before she had arrived.  "I dreamt the light on your satchel," he said.  It should have frightened her, but the haggard awe of survival had stripped away her capacity for ordinary fear.  She understood that the island kept ledgers of its own, balancing the dead against the returning, the buried against the found.  Mara opened her satchel and took out her most recent canvas, a portrait of the drowned bridge painted in the gray hour before dawn.  She offered it to him without ceremony, an exchange that required no words because both of them had outgrown the currency of promises.  Elias accepted it, weighing the paint and salt in his hands, and gave her his sketchbook in return, filled with cross-sections of the seabed and the names of currents that no longer ran.  The trade was uneven by any rational measure, yet it completed something between them, a circuit closing across the tide.  Mara pinned the amber brooch to her collar, feeling its warmth against her collarbone, and understood that it had not been lost but planted, waiting for the right convergence of rain and retreating water to deliver it.  She would paint again before the tide returned, not to capture the island but to release it, to let the canvas hold what her memory could not.  When the water came back, it would cover the bridge and the boulders and the bowl of black sand, but it would not erase the exchange.  Mara walked toward the causeway, following the rain trails backward as they narrowed into threads, and for the first time in years, she felt the thin, wryly earnest possibility that sleep might find her.
