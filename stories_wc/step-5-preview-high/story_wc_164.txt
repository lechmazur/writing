The sea-foam recollectionist, whose name sounded like a wave retreating from wet pebbles, sat cross-legged in the iridescent cave inside the giant crystal geode. 
Light bloomed across amethyst and aquamarine facets, painting the walls with colors that had no names in any coastal language, and the air shimmered with a hush that felt both like a lullaby and a warning. 
They were a recollectionist, a keeper of borrowed memories, though sea foam had made them soft-edged, translucent, and prone to dissolving at the touch of certainty. 
Their present memory was already thinning at the edges, and the timeframe pressed hard: before memory fades, they had to understand why some paths disappear. 
In their lap rested a shard of crater glass, dark as a question and veined with sudden heat from a sky that once fell. 
It was more than a pretty stone; it was an instrument passed down through generations of foam-born archivists. 
Because the recollectionist transliterates emotions into light, they held the crater glass to their temple to sharpen the translation, and feelings curled into ribbons of visible script through the cave air like luminous smoke. 
Grief read as jagged runes; wonder read as spiraling loops; regret read as knots that no careful finger could undo. 
But today, the glass revealed only blankness where a feeling should have been. 
Even the recollectionist's own reflection seemed to hold back a word. 
The missing pieces of their own past left gaps in the translation, and gaps invited conjecture. 
So the recollectionist began to conjecture, aloud, in the cheerful gloom that had become their constant companion. 
They spoke to the seeds scattered across the cave floor, dropped by a migrating bird that had flown through a fissure during the last storm. 
The bird, a swallow with salt crystallized on its wings, had carried the seeds from an island where no one remembered the way home, and each seed still hummed with the bird's longing. 
Each seed was a tiny compass of vanished routes, and the recollectionist arranged them in spiral patterns on the crystal floor, guessing at the geometry of lost directions. 
"Why do some paths vanish?" they asked the seeds, their voice bright despite the sorrow humming beneath it like a second heartbeat. 
"Do they end, or are they simply forgotten by the feet that walked them?" 
The seeds, cool and glossy as dried tears, reflected the cave light in trembling points. 
The recollectionist turned the crater glass until a thin beam struck the seeds, and suddenly they read a buried emotion: not loss, but invitation. 
The glass showed that every disappearing path left behind a doorway of absence, a hollow shaped exactly like the traveler who once filled it. 
The missing pieces were not errors in the world; they were spaces reserved for return, carved by the salt and the silence. 
In every hollow, a possibility nested like a pearl in an oyster shell, waiting for the right grain of sand to arrive. 
The recollectionist's chest tightened, then loosened into a laugh that echoed like rain on the geode's outer shell, a sound of cheerful gloom made visible. 
They understood now. 
The paths disappeared because the recollectionists of the world, keepers of sea-foam memories, were meant to follow them into blankness, not to map the shore or fence the tide. 
They were foam, after all, and foam was never meant to build monuments but to cradle the shore until the tide returned. 
Some paths disappeared so that new ones might be grown, like seeds from a bird's passage, like crystal from a wound in the earth, like memory from forgetting. 
As the last of their current memory began to blur at its center, the recollectionist carefully pocketed a single seed and the crater glass, tucking them against the foam that made up their chest. 
They would carry the missing pieces outward, past the cave's iridescent hush, before memory fades completely and the question becomes a shape without a name. 
The geode's inner light dimmed to a rose-gold dusk, as if the crystal itself were holding its breath in anticipation of the answer. 
Outside, the ocean roared its cheerful gloom against the rocks, and somewhere a bird sang of routes yet to be drawn by feet that had not yet learned to vanish.
