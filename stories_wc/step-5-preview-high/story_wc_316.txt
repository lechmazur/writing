Wren was an ego death survivor, and she had come to the floating rock dojo to defeat emotional numbness.  The monks there taught through complementary opposites: stone to stillness, breath to flame, grief to laughter.  Each morning the plateau drifted through the season the monks called the unraveling, while history rewrites itself.  Judges, heirs, and crimes were different by dusk.  Wren could copy every form perfectly, yet praise arrived like weather, touching nothing.  Then a courier staggered onto the lowest rock carrying a brain in a jar labeled duplicate.  The tissue had been copied from Wren's own skull during the rite that dissolved her first self.  She believed the copy still held the feelings that had been cut away to keep her alive.  A clone named Ossa claimed the jar as her birthright, and the case was known everywhere as the clone custody battle.  Ossa had been grown from the parts of Wren that the rite discarded, and she wore those parts like armor.  Magistrates hired by the sky argued that the jar was property, evidence, or an heirloom, depending on the version of history that ruled at noon.  The abbot refused them all and set a trial on two circling rocks.  Whoever could vivify the duplicate brain would gain custody, because the dojo recognized ownership by completion rather than paperwork.  Wren wanted the brain to defeat emotional numbness, but Ossa needed it to prove she was not a leftover.  During the first round, Ossa struck with theatrical grief, while Wren answered with an empty calm that enraged her.  Wren's wooden sword had one attribute that made the abbot trust her as arbiter: it sliced through pretense.  When Ossa's tears quickened, the blade cut the air and exposed her performance as hunger for a mother who had never existed.  When Wren spoke of duty, it likewise opened her chest and showed the abbot that she feared feeling more than she feared death.  History rewrote itself again during the recess, and for an hour the law said the brain belonged to a man who had not yet been born.  The abbot ignored the new writ because the rocks had already begun their second circuit.  He taught that a dead self could be vivified only through complementary opposites, never by one will conquering another.  Wren had to supply the numbness that could endure silence, and Ossa had to supply the sorrow that could break it.  They knelt over the jar on a bridge of stone no wider than a blade, and the clone custody battle became a single question of how to hold one mind.  Ossa poured her rage into the fluid until the preservative smoked.  Wren poured her stillness over that rage, not to smother it, but to give it edges and a place to rest.  The brain twitched, and a voice arrived without ears, saying that the duplicate had waited long enough.  It asked them to stop asking which of them was the continuation, because the label duplicate had become a lie that even history could not sustain.  Wren felt the jar's attention turn toward her, and the sensation was not triumph but grief, warm and intolerable.  She began to shake, and Ossa held her, and the dojo did not correct them for touching an enemy.  The abbot ruled that the brain would remain in shared custody, split between the survivor who had endured ego death and the clone who had inherited what was discarded.  He also ruled that history could rewrite itself forever without being allowed to rewrite this verdict.  Wren walked to the edge of the floating rock and looked down at the valley where her old name was being reassigned to a stranger.  She felt the wind cut her face, felt the stone tremble, felt the shame of having been hollow, and she welcomed all of it.  Her numbness had not been killed by a blade, though the blade had sliced through every pretense that protected it.  It had been unmasked by the opposite she refused to love, and then vivified by the unbearable weight of being alive.  On two small rocks above a rewriting world, the survivor and the clone kept watch over the duplicate, and Wren finally wept without knowing why.
