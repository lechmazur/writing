The twilight garden had been abandoned after the nostalgia ban, and so it welcomed her back. 
Kaelin, a skilled tide dancer from the northern terraces, had chosen this elective exile rather than forget the rhythm of the drowned coasts. 
Here, beneath the unraveling constellations, she could still remember how water moved when it was allowed to weep. 
The city-states had scrubbed the old hymns from the air, yet they had missed this place, tucked behind a fault line where the stars seemed older and less obedient. 
She had carried only one relic across the border: a moonstone cabochon dust speck, no larger than a tear, ground from a gem her grandmother had swallowed during the Silent Tides. 
It was ink enough for a single line, and Kaelin intended to use it to pen a cosmic verse on mortal skin. 
The law had outlawed longing itself, yet it could not repeal the body's memory of salt, nor the way a dancer's feet ached for the pull of the moon. 
To fledge from the cage of sanctioned forgetting, she knew she must learn again how to rise, and she would do it by observing gaps, not by filling them with noise. 
The stars overhead offered instruction; she studied the dark spaces between them, the pauses where forgotten tides had once pulled at her ankles, and the hollows where the censors had scraped away the names of the dead. 
Each absence became a ladder. 
On the seventh night, a wind with no direction lifted the hem of her coat, and she felt the old bones of her shoulders begin to answer with a slow, terrifying bloom. 
But the garden did not want her to leave. 
The roots beneath the roses stirred, and a sudden gravity, irrational and intimate, pinned her heels to the soil; she had become spontaneously anchored. 
She tested the hold, flexing arches that had once walked on water, but the earth held her fast, as if the plot itself remembered her promise, and the roses turned their faces toward her in recognition. 
Kaelin laughed, a sound with obsidian grace, dark and polished by grief, and she understood that the exile had not ended at the border. 
The threshold had chosen her instead, and the only escape was through. 
She sat upon the cold bench of lattice and lichen and opened her palm. 
The dust speck gleamed, not with borrowed light, but with a patient hunger, a fragment of the moon's own refusal to be governed. 
If she could not dance the tides, she would write them. 
She pressed the grain to her inner wrist, where the vein mapped a coast the ban had tried to erase. 
The moonstone scratched a silver furrow, and the skin, mortal and willing, drank the stardust like rain falling on a drought-cracked bed. 
Across her forearm she traced the verse, not in words, but in the grammar of gaps: the space between wave and shore, between breath and sorrow, between the self she had been and the exile she had chosen to become. 
The letters deepened from silver to black, an obsidian grace written in her own blood's light, and the garden shivered as the first banned wordless hymn in years rose from the soil. 
When the last curve closed, she felt the anchor release, not outward, but inward, fastening her spirit to the pulse of the place. 
Her shoulders unfolded. 
They were not wings of feather, but of edge and moonlight, shaped by everything the world had tried to censor and every silence she had refused to fill. 
She had fledged through the negative space of forgetting, and now she rose, not above the garden, but into it, a tide dancer anchored to the twilight beneath the stars, forever singing the banned nostalgia in skin and starlight.
