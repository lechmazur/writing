The cobalt lagoon lay still after rain, holding the sky in its depths without a ripple. 
On the wet sand, a quiet shoreline poet sat with knees drawn up, watching the water wake slowly from the storm. 
She was warmly attentive to every glint and trickle, as though the lagoon were speaking and she owed it her full patience. 
In her lap rested a drake's tail feather, its iridescent tip frayed by wind and missing one barb. 
She had found it half-buried in driftwood, incomplete, yet exactly what she needed to tend the morning. 
The feather was not whole, she thought, but wholeness was a rumor she had stopped chasing. 
Her craft was the making of woven dawns, thin silks of light and sound that she stitched along the shore so the day would open gently. 
The storm had left its threads snarled, dripping discord into the hush, and only her hands could coax them into a single chord. 
She worked by harmonizing discord, listening past the gulls and the gutter-drains until the lagoon's clatter softened into breath. 
Puddles on the boardwalk mirrored the bruised violet of the retreating clouds. 
As a kite string snapped in the cove, a sharp cry split the burnished hush, and a paper bird tumbled from the gray. 
The poet looked up, the drake's tail feather caught in her fingers, and saw a child racing along the boardwalk toward the fallen sail. 
It landed in the shallows with a torn corner and a broken spar, its bamboo spine jutting from the silk. 
The child reached it first, sobbing quietly, and cradled the wreck as though it might still remember the sky. 
The string's frayed end dangled from a small fist like a question with no answer. 
She knelt beside them, warmly attentive to the grief that shimmered in the air like heat off pavement. 
The poet did not offer comfort that erased the loss; instead she offered the feather, its broken barb glinting like a small blue flame. 
She told the child that her own mornings were built from such damage, that she tended the shore by harmonizing discord into something wearable and whole. 
Her motivation had never been perfection, she explained, but the opposite: to discover beauty in the incomplete, the snapped, the frayed, and the left-behind. 
The child touched the feather, then the kite's torn silk, and the two incompletes seemed to recognize one another in the salt air. 
Together they carried the kite to the poet's workbench, where coils of dawn-thread waited in sea-glass jars. 
She tended the tear with slow stitches, not to hide the rip but to let it sing in a lower key beside the mend. 
The feather served as her needle, its hollow quill drinking the dawn-thread, guiding each pass with a warmth that asked nothing in return. 
When the last knot was tied, the kite was not new, but it breathed with a strange completeness that came from being honestly mended. 
They raised it over the lagoon, and the cobalt water held its reflection as though the storm had never been. 
The paper wing dipped and rose, its torn corner trailing like a flag of truce between rain and sun. 
A gull cried once and settled on the pier, folding its wings as if to listen. 
The poet watched the child run, the repaired kite pulling against a breeze that smelled of wet pine and salt. 
She returned to her woven dawns, threading the cry of the snap and the silence after it into the next panel. 
In time, others would speak of dawns that did not blaze but simply stayed, unhurried and gold. 
The lagoon kept its burnished hush, but now it held a new note, low and resonant, the sound of things made whole by their scars.
