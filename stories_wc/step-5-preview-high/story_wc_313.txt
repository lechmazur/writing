After the wind chime fell silent, Warden Ilse Vane climbed the midnight ridgeway at twilight to inspect the levee. 
The reservoir had dropped so far that the roofs of Lower Kessel broke the surface like gray reefs. 
Ilse was vigilant by habit and precisely humane by training, and she measured every warning in days rather than minutes. 
She had told the downstream farmers when to move their pumps, and she had told the gawking tourists when to keep back. 
What she had not told anyone was that the reservoir town resurfacing felt less like a disaster and more like a confession. 
In the old warden's shack at the ridge bend, she found a coffee can of photographs. 
Each image showed a street she had never seen and a boy she almost recognized. 
The trail of photographs led across the mudflats to the drowned square, then to the church steps, then to a general store with its windows blown out. 
Inside, the last photograph lay weighted by a brick, and in it a girl held up a bottle for the camera. 
The label was sapphire-hued, deep blue, and lettered with the name of her grandmother's winter liqueur. 
Ilse turned the photograph over and found a date, a street number, and three words in her grandmother's hand: "Do not forget." 
The sight of that sapphire-hued liqueur label opened a forgotten memory like a sluice gate. 
She remembered being six, remembered the wind chime over the shop door, remembered water rising in the streets while adults argued about the levee. 
Her grandmother had not fled upstream with the others; she had stayed to guide the last boat, and Ilse had been handed into that boat with a photograph sewn into her coat. 
The town had not died empty, as the county ledger claimed. 
It had been emptied too late, and the people who survived had been told to be grateful and silent. 
Ilse carried the photographs to the ridgeway as the light failed and the water went glassy. 
The mirrored hush of the reservoir made the resurfacing town seem doubled, once in mud and once in reflection. 
She thought of the council's plan to expunge Lower Kessel from every map and memory, to bulldoze the ruins before the reservoir refilled. 
They called it sanitation. 
She called it a second drowning. 
Her motivation had always been to spin stories into shared meaning, and now she had the proof to do it. 
She woke the archivist, the schoolteacher, and the families who had inherited the silence. 
They came with lanterns and coats, suspicious and hungry, and she laid the photographs along the ridgeway in order. 
When they reached the image of the sapphire-hued liqueur label, the old women began to name the children in the street, and the men began to name the boats. 
Ilse stood beside the levee and read the false line aloud: all clear, no loss, no names. 
Then, with the archivist watching, she took a pen and expunged the lie from the official copy, striking it through and writing the true list beside it. 
The act was small, but it was precise and humane, and it gave the resurfacing town a shape that could be carried without shame. 
By midnight the wind chime in the shack gave one soft knock, though no wind had reached the ridge. 
Ilse hung it again above the door, not to mark an ending, but to call people back to the work of remembering together. 
The reservoir kept its mirrored hush, but the town was no longer only beneath the water. 
It was in the photographs, in the names, and in the shared story that would outlast the drought.
