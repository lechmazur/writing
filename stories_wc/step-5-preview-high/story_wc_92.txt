At twilight the luminous fort should have shone like a controlled constellation, but its lamps guttered out during an unexplained blackout. 
I was Odalie, the fort's vigilant lock sentinel, and my post was the highest sealed stair where the council kept its most obedient secrets. 
They expected panic; instead, the corridors held a winking hush, as if the stone itself had learned to keep a private joke. 
No gates had been forced, no horns had sounded, and the guards below were pretending not to notice that the dark was listening. 
I carried my lantern to the north courtyard, where a work crew had left the half-painted sundial stripped of its second coat. 
The foreman claimed rain had ruined the paint, but the uncovered primer formed a set of lines that only someone trained in wards would draw. 
My mother had taught me that every honest sundial admits its latitude, so I read the false angles with an appetite for trouble. 
Each painted line bent a degree or two from the true hour, and each error matched a chamber number in the locked registrar. 
I copied them into my notebook and solved the map via the coded angles in a half-painted sundial, as though the courtyard had become a single long dial. 
It led not to the treasury but to a wall of black slag behind the eastern chapel, a wall no key on my key ring could rouse. 
I was calmly inventive by nature, so instead of calling for help I took down the bronze quill holder my mother had left me. 
It held three brass quills no scribe used anymore, and the base unscrewed to reveal a narrow compass of blue glass. 
The glass needle did not point north; it swung toward the slag wall and settled with a small triumphant click. 
The sentinel's key ring was meant to lock the council's doors, but my mother's compass was meant to open what the council had buried. 
I held it against a keyhole hidden under the chapel step, and the slab rose without the grinding complaint of a secret exposed. 
The stair beyond smelled of dust and old lightning, and at its bottom waited a chamber of pale machinery the old texts called the cradle of sparks. 
Glass vessels cradled ribbons of soft fire, and copper conduits climbed from them to feed the fort's forbidden towers. 
My mother had been a registrar who believed the light should belong to every street, and she had died in an accident the council never explained. 
Now I understood the blackout was not an accident either; it was a slow clamp placed on the cradle while the councillors debated how much dark they dared sell. 
My hands shook once, not from fear but from the sudden clarity that I had been guarding a door rather than a lamp. 
Here was an intrigue larger than theft: a conspiracy of locks, ledgers, and light, and it had been waiting for a sentinel patient enough to read it. 
I could have run to the captain, but his patrols were the slow hand of the clamp, and I had no promise that dawn would find the cradle intact. 
The painted lines had shown me a second sequence, the private order of valves and lamps by which the cradle could be persuaded to share its fire beyond the council's ration. 
With the blue glass key I turned the hidden taps in that order and let the stored heat move through the old conduits, slow and silent. 
The lamps did not flare; they breathed, as if the whole fort were inhaling a secret, and then every street below woke in the same warm hush. 
I had not come to smash the council or crown myself; I only meant to spark a silent revolution by returning the light to the people who had paid for it. 
No crowds shouted; no soldiers charged; instead a hundred windows filled with gold, and bakers lit ovens, and printers opened shutters without needing a reason. 
By morning the council would call it a fault in the copper, but the coded angles in the half-painted sundial would still be there, winking at anyone who knew where to stand. 
I returned the holder to my desk, set it beside my logbook, and waited with the calm patience of a woman who had finally chosen her side. 
Outside, the half-painted sundial held its secret in the new light, and the luminous fort at twilight no longer belonged to the council alone.
