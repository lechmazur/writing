The cuneiform translator arrived at the magnetic arbor three days after the spirit left Ilya Venn. 
The magnetic arbor floated above the clouds on a lattice of magnetized trunks, and every leaf carried a small blue spark. 
Nerin had spent twenty years coaxing meaning from clay wedges, and she was kindly, methodical, unwilling to let a sign remain mysterious. 
Ilya had been the opposite, a physicist with haunted ambitions who believed death was not an ending but a transition that could be measured. 
His last message asked her to meet him in the arbor and to bring nothing but patience. 
She brought instead a linen satchel containing a knowledge fruit seed, taken from the arbor's oldest mother tree before her own departure. 
The seed had been pressed into her palm by a dying keeper who said it remembered every mind that had ever ripened beneath the magnetic boughs. 
In the reading room, where the walls were lined with baked tablets, Nerin set the seed beside Ilya's final tablet and began to work. 
The signs were not Sumerian or Akkadian but a private shorthand Ilya had built from them, and they made no sense until she stopped reading them as words and remembered that she had come to understand the physics of transition. 
She began to translate through analogical thinking, comparing each wedge to the instruments around her. 
A sign like a gate became a threshold potential. 
A sign like a descending bird became the release of charge from a higher state to a lower one. 
A sign like a star with a missing corner became a seed, dormant until the field around it turned sympathetic. 
When she spoke the analogies aloud, the arbor answered; branches shifted, and the magnetic field reorganized itself into the geometry she had described. 
Ilya had spent his life trying to reestablish the bridge between body and whatever waited beyond it, and his notebooks were haunted by the ambitions of everyone who had died mid-equation. 
The seed warmed in her hand. 
She understood then that the fruit was not food but a recording medium, and its seed could be planted only where transition had recently occurred. 
Nerin walked the arbor paths with kindly precision, choosing a plot where Ilya's instruments still stood and his spirit had passed through. 
She dug with a trowel, set the knowledge fruit seed in the loam, and covered it without ceremony. 
Above them the clouds pressed against the invisible floor, and the air had the tenderness of a room where two people stand at touching distance. 
The spirit had left the arbor, yet the place still leaned toward her, as if attention could bridge the final inch. 
For six days she watered the soil with magnetized rain and read Ilya's tablets aloud, translating each sign into field, each field into sign. 
On the seventh morning a pale shoot rose, not green but silver, and its leaves were shaped like cuneiform marks. 
It grew into a small tree that bore one fruit, and inside the fruit was a single pit etched with the physics Ilya had chased. 
The pit described transition as a change of address rather than a cessation, a movement from one grammar to another, and Nerin saw that his haunted ambitions had been questions he had refused to leave behind. 
She placed the pit in the arbor's central compass, which had spun wildly since his death, and the needle settled. 
The magnetic arbor had been built to keep such transitions stable, and she had reestablished its purpose by giving Ilya's work a form that could be read after the spirit was gone. 
That evening, as the clouds below turned gold, Nerin stood at the edge of the grove with her hands hovering just above the silver bark. 
She did not touch it, and she did not need to. 
She felt the nearness of everything he had wanted to know, close enough to warm her palms, and she began, at last, to write it down.
