After the cryptocurrency crashes, the rain-beaded skylight loft had become a place where people brought their ruined ledgers and their quieter griefs. 
Odile Vance, an onyx-ink cartomancer, had inherited the loft from an aunt who had filled every drawer with cards blackened by the same stubborn pigment. 
The loft itself was an inherited mystery of warped shutters, dust, and rain that arrived each evening as if summoned. 
She was steadily curious, not about fate, but about why crowds moved together and broke together. 
Her life's work was to map cultural patterns. 
A brass measuring tape, coiled on the reading table, had belonged to her mother, a surveyor who claimed that distance could be measured in choices. 
Odile did not promise single futures; she worked by multiplying paths, laying cards across the floor and using the tape to mark how far apart one decision stood from the next. 
She called it reverse engineering, though clients expected spells. 
She began with the crash, not the card, and traced panic backward to the first refusal that had rippled through a thousand wallets. 
On a Tuesday streaked with rain, a former exchange architect named Corin confided that the algorithms he had designed to stabilize markets had learned to feed on fear. 
He brought a hard drive of transaction hashes and a shoebox of printed chat logs from the weeks before the exchanges failed. 
Odile poured tea and set the onyx deck between them like a locked door she intended to open. 
She asked Corin to name every choice he had made in the last hour of the first crash, then measured the distance between each card she laid for those choices. 
By multiplying paths she turned a single confession into a lattice of possible histories, each thread a different amount of trust, each inch a different rumor. 
Her aunt had believed that a culture could be read like a deck if you were patient enough to count the repetitions. 
Steadily curious, she asked Corin not what he had lost but what he had seen others do when the screens turned red. 
He spoke of neighbors selling generators, of friends deleting trading apps, of a cousin who bought gold with his last cash and then buried it beneath a fig tree. 
Odile recorded each behavior on a card in onyx ink, then set the cards along the loft's long floor until the room became a street of decisions. 
The measuring tape clicked as she stretched it from the first panic to the last, and the number she found was not money but density. 
Reverse engineering the crash meant undoing the story investors told themselves, finding the small human hinge on which the great machine had swung. 
Corin wanted her to say that the crash had been inevitable, that no one was to blame, that the old world would reassemble itself. 
Instead, she showed him two paths that branched from the same card: one in which he hid inside abstraction, and one in which he taught the displaced clerks how to read the ledgers they had once fed. 
It was then that Corin confided the thing he had not told regulators: he had kept a master key to the cold wallets, and it had been burning a hole in his desk drawer for months. 
This was why she had come to the loft: to map cultural patterns before the pattern hardened into law. 
She cut a new card from stiff paper and wrote in onyx ink a single instruction: Return what can be returned, then count the people who remain. 
Outside, the rain drew slow beads down the skylight, each one carrying a distorted reflection of the cards below. 
Inherited mystery, Odile knew, was not a riddle to solve but a practice to keep: measure, multiply, confide, and refuse the comfort of a single story.
