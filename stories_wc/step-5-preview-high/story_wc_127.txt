The amber grotto kept the last copper light of dusk, and its walls released a dry electric warmth that raised the fine hairs on Marek's forearms. 
He was a counterfeit emotion dealer, and he was professionally thorough in the way that other men were religious. 
Every evening before the transformation completed, he arranged his tools on a slab of fossilized resin: glass wands, split quills, and blotter petals borrowed from a fragrance chemist. 
The petals were thin, absorbent, and pale as moth wings, and Marek used them to test the counterfeit emotions he sold. 
He did not deal in joy or sorrow themselves. 
He dealt in half truths. 
A grieving widow might buy a vial of pipe smoke and rain, not because it was love, but because it let her stand among memories without screaming. 
A nervous heir might purchase warm almond approval, just enough to carry him through a will reading and the night after. 
Marek's gift, if it could be called that, was sensing through static electricity. 
Amber preserved more than insects and leaves. 
It preserved the charged silence of every confession, bargain, and goodbye that had ever happened in the grotto. 
When a visitor entered, static twisted around his fingers like a cat seeking attention, and he read hunger, fear, and false courage as small shocks. 
He told himself this distance was kindness. 
If he felt everything his customers felt, he would have nothing left to sell. 
Yet his private motivation, written in a ledger he showed no one, was to fuse insight with empathy. 
Insight alone had made him rich and lonely. 
Empathy alone, he suspected, would make him a fool and a beggar. 
He wanted the two braided together, and he believed the grotto could teach him the knot. 
Tonight the air tasted of ozone and pine resin, and the transformation that had been building in the amber for seven years was finally completing. 
Marek had first felt it as a pressure behind his eyes after a customer died clutching a bottle of his false courage. 
Since then, every emotion he counterfeited had left a residue in him, not as feeling, but as an ache for feeling. 
The grotto was turning his art into a crucible. 
A woman named Ilse stepped from the passage, her boots scraping static from the stone. 
She wanted to buy forgiveness. 
Marek tilted his head and let the static move through his fingertips. 
It told him she had not come to apologize. 
She had come to end an argument she was losing. 
He could have sold her the usual half truth: a blotter petal dampened with chamomile, dust, and the memory of a reconciled breath. 
It would have worked for a night, perhaps two. 
Instead, Marek found himself choosing a different petal, one he had been saving for the completion of the grotto's work. 
He was professionally thorough, so he did not rush. 
He dampened the petal with amber oil and the scent of her own skin, which the static had pressed into his awareness. 
Then he folded it into a small paper envelope and held it out. 
"This will not forgive you," he said, "because I cannot sell what you have not given yourself." 
Ilse's chin lifted. 
The static around her crackled, angry and ashamed. 
"You are a liar," she said. 
"Yes," he said. 
"But I am a thorough liar." 
The grotto chose that moment to finish its long work. 
The amber brightened, then dimmed, and the electric silence folded over Marek like a held hand. 
He felt the transformation complete not as a lightning strike, but as a rising stillness that began to subsume every half truth he had ever spoken into one clear, aching whole. 
Insight and empathy fused in him, not gently, but with the inevitability of resin sealing a wing. 
He looked at Ilse and, for the first time, did not calculate what she needed. 
He simply saw her. 
She opened the envelope, and the petal released not forgiveness, but the exact weight of her own regret, undiluted and unbearable. 
Her eyes filled. 
Marek did not look away. 
That was his affection, detached enough to give her room, close enough to keep her company. 
Ilse left without the comfort she had wanted, carrying instead the harder gift of being known. 
Marek closed his ledger, wrote a final entry, and extinguished his lamps. 
The grotto darkened around him, humming with static and something almost like peace. 
He was still a dealer, but he no longer sold what was false. 
He sold half truths that opened toward wholeness, and charged only what a person could learn. 
Outside, dusk finished its own transformation, and the amber kept the record of a man who had finally felt enough to stop.
