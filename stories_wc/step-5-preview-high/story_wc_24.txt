After blowing the whistle, Rurik the hydraulic clock shepherd stood in the fjord quiet while the last tremor left his fingers. 
The horizon measurement station had been built on a shelf of black rock, and every clock in it answered to water rather than spring or weight. 
Rurik was an eminently peculiar caretaker, narrow-shouldered and patient, with forearms scarred by thin cuts from the razor edges of calibration gates. 
He had chosen this post for one reason: to understand what clocks really measure. 
The officials who funded the station wanted coordinates; Rurik suspected they wanted a boundary between yesterday and tomorrow and were calling it an angle. 
Below the terraces, the fjord lay so still that the mountains had two reflections, one in the water and one in the glass faces of the clocks. 
The whistle marked the end of the official day, but it also started the shepherd's true work. 
He carried a brass mantle clock key on a cord around his neck, its teeth worn smooth by three generations of hands. 
It was the only key that could open the central clock at the station, and without that clock the hydraulic gauges drifted into metaphor. 
Tonight the gauges disagreed by four seconds, which was too many for a horizon that had to be measured against the night tide. 
Rurik began his repair through counting backwards. 
He started at the farthest channel, where water dripped over a razor-edged gate and dropped into a graduated stone basin. 
Ten, he whispered, counting backwards as the gate released a silver sliver of water. 
Nine, and the basin rose; eight, and the next gate answered; seven, six, five, until the numbers pulled him back along the line of clocks. 
At the seventh clock the sequence failed, because its wheel had stopped mid-tooth and the water had begun to pool behind it. 
Rurik squinted at the horizon, where a pale seam still divided sea from sky, and he compared that seam with the chalk marks on the station wall. 
The chalk line said the sun had set a half-second earlier than the clock allowed. 
He knelt, threaded the cord from his neck, and fitted the mantle clock key into the frozen wheel. 
The key turned with a sound like a held breath being let go. 
Inside the wheel, he found the razor edges he had dressed that morning had caught a fiber of wool, and the fiber had bowed the gate by the width of a hair. 
That was the four seconds: not lost, but shaved away, sliced into the gate and carried out with the tide. 
He cut the fiber free and set the wheel, then counted backwards once more from ten to test the repaired line. 
This time every clock answered at its number, and the water fell in silver slices that divided the dark into equal parts. 
Still, his obsession did not leave him, because he knew a working instrument was not the same as an honest answer. 
He climbed to the central clock, inserted the mantle clock key, and opened its brass face. 
There were no gears beneath it, only a shallow trough and a series of razor edges arranged like a fan. 
Water from the highest reservoir traveled down the fan, and each edge cut the stream into a separate moment. 
The hands of the clock were moved by nothing but the weight of those moments pushing against a float. 
Rurik understood then that the station had never measured the sky. 
It measured the interval between an act and its consequence, between the whistle and the last clock's reply. 
A clock was a device for catching the thin bright line where cause became memory, and holding it still long enough to be counted. 
He reset the central clock, locked its face, and hung the key against his chest. 
The fjord quiet returned, but it was not emptiness; it was the sound of water deciding what to keep. 
Rurik descended the terraces with his scarred hands open, ready to be useful and ready to wonder. 
Tomorrow the officials would read their horizon in numbers, and they would believe they had measured distance. 
He would let them, because he now carried the stranger truth without needing to say it. 
Somewhere behind the black rock, the tide leaned against the gates and made its small, exact sound. 
That sound was the only clock Rurik trusted, and he had learned to listen to it through counting backwards to the first cut of the light. 
He blew out the lamp, and the station settled into its long, patient, hydraulic sleep.
