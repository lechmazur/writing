The clean slate vendor unlocked his stall during the lull on the store's very first opening day, when the glass orchard library floated upon a swirling sea of clouds and no readers had yet climbed the spiral stair.  The glass trees bore books in place of fruit, and their pages turned in a wind that smelled of rain that had never fallen.  He had leased the stall from librarians who catalogued silence, because he needed a room quiet enough to hear the moment before a tale begins.  His sentence was glimmering parole: he would remain free only if he found the place where stories begin and wrote nothing there.  The parole shone at his wrist like frost, tightening whenever he reached for a pen.  So he learned to inventory what was missing rather than what he had made.  His shelves held blank ledgers, gray erasers, and phials of bottled moonlight that pooled like patient silver.  He arranged the phials in a pattern that seemed careless but was mindfully scattered, each gap calculated so that the whole row measured a silence.  During the lull, he practiced his method, by quantifying absence, filling a notebook with the exact dimensions of holes.  He would choose a page, decide what had never been written on it, and then incise the border of that emptiness with a fine needle.  The cut did not bleed; it opened a thin seam of shine, as if the paper had been concealing a door.  By noon, the empty ledger on his counter held the shape of a threshold.  He pressed one vial of bottled moonlight against the seam, and the liquid crawled into the incision like a word returning home.  The stall brightened, glass roots descended through the floorboards, and he saw the clouds revolving below the orchard in slow constellations.  He had been told that stories begin in minds, mouths, or markets, but the ledger suggested a humbler origin.  They began where a person admitted a lack.  He imagined the readers who would climb into the orchard after the lull, carrying unfinished thoughts like open wounds.  They would come to him for clean slates because they wanted to be remade, and he would sell them blankness priced by the ounce.  But selling blankness was dangerous, for a clean slate can either release a person or erase them entirely.  That was why his liberty was glimmering parole and not a full pardon; he could touch absence, but he was forbidden to own it.  A woman in a coat of catalog dust arrived as the first readers crossed the orchard and set a torn novel on the counter.  She asked him to remove the ending, which had become a wound.  The vendor saw that the story had not failed at its end but at a gap in its middle, a missing chapter no one had noticed.  He took his needle and began to incise around the absence, marking its length, its depth, and its refusal to be filled.  He measured the missing chapter by quantifying absence, by the space the reader's breath did not take when she turned the page.  Then he uncorked a vial of bottled moonlight and poured it into the measured gap, not to hide the hole, but to let it shine.  The novel repaired itself around the light, and the painful ending fell away like a scab.  The woman paid with a sentence she had never spoken aloud, which he bottled beside the moonlight.  As she left, the band of parole cooled and loosened, and he understood that the place where stories begin is not a location.  It is the honest gap between what is known and what is almost said, the clean slate a person carries before language.  The clean slate vendor swept his counter through the last of the lull, setting his mindfully scattered wares for the afternoon.  He left one shelf deliberately empty, measured to the size of a first word, and waited for someone brave enough to want it.  Above him, the orchard drifted on its sea of clouds, and every glass leaf held a small, unwritten dawn.
