The esoteric realist knelt in the verdant grotto above the clouds, where moss drank the mist and stone remembered rain. 
She had chosen this altitude because the lowlands kept no honest records, only ledgers that listed what could be sold. 
She was transiently rooted, anchored to the cliff only until the season turned, her cabin of woven reed and cloud-bleached bone destined to dissolve back into the mountain. 
The grotto was her archive, its dripping ceilings and pale ferns more trustworthy than any published book. 
Her name had faded from the towns below, which suited her, for she had come to honor overlooked histories. 
She believed that every unmarked grave and vanished orchard still hummed beneath the noise of the present, waiting for someone to build them a signal. 
Each dawn she practiced her method, building temporary structures as signals that could not outlast the fog. 
She raised arches of fern and spine, cairns of mica, and hanging gardens tied to stalactites with knots of horsehair and silver wire. 
She worked to the rhythm of dripping water, a slow cadence of place, tie, and step back. 
Her esoteric realism demanded no witnesses, only evidence that someone had remembered. 
She trusted erosion more than monuments. 
Then she saw it. 
A single monarch butterfly drifted through the cloud wall, the last of its kind, its orange wings tattered from a journey that would not be repeated. 
It settled on her newest structure, a cradle of lichen meant to signal the memory of extinct meadowlarks and the orchards they had once policed. 
The esoteric realist went very still, and the grotto held its breath in a blushing stillness of dawn light. 
She understood that the migration of the last monarch butterfly marked an interrupted cadence, a song whose final refrain had been swallowed by silence. 
Her heart beat against the pause, urging her not to let the ending pass unmarked, for history was not only written by the victors. 
From her pouch she drew a moth wing, pale green and veined like an old map, its scales still holding the moon of some vanished night. 
With careful fingers she wove the wing into the cradle beside the trembling monarch, letting the delicate membrane rest against the lichen like a page. 
She tied a knot of green thread, the loop cinched just enough to hold without harm, binding the wing to the cradle with a tenderness she had never shown to anything living. 
The knot was her answer to the interruption, a way to bind the lost past to the fragile present without pretending either would endure. 
She built more signals in the hours that followed, small domes of moss and bone that would decay by dusk, each one a temporary monument to a history the world had forgotten. 
The monarch did not leave. 
It rested through the pink morning, and when the sun warmed the grotto, it opened and closed its wings once, as if bowing to the quiet monuments. 
She sat beside it, transiently rooted in that shared stillness, no longer waiting for the cadence to resume, but keeping time with a different pulse. 
Instead she kept working, placing a final stone atop a cairn dedicated to the butterflies who would never migrate again, and to the farmers who had once named their children after blossoms. 
The grotto blushed around her, rosy mist curling through the arches, honoring the overlooked with a patience deeper than grief. 
When at last the monarch stirred and rose into the clouds, she did not mourn the interrupted cadence. 
She simply touched the moth wing, warm in its cradle of knots, and turned to build again.
