Marit Vale had negotiated eleven prisoner exchanges, and none had begun in such a furrowed hush. 
The storm-worn windblown parapet in mist belonged to no kingdom; it was neutral territory, and both armies had agreed to treat it as sacred ground. 
As a prisoner exchange negotiator, Marit had learned that silence could be as sharp as any blade. 
She was uncommonly thoughtful, a trait that had once saved a village and once, she believed, killed a boy. 
Tonight, between apology and forgiveness, she stood waiting for Renn of Corrath. 
Five years earlier, she had found an amended prisoner list inside a quartermaster's desk. 
The desk drawer had stuck, and an antique drawer pull had come away in her hand. 
She had kept the pull on a leather cord ever since, a small weight that tethered her to her error. 
Her motive now was not to be absolved but to recycle mistakes into wisdom. 
Renn arrived with two lanterns and a guard, his face carved by the same storm. 
"You look older," he said. 
"So do you," Marit said. 
"I received your letter." 
His apology had been careful and complete, yet she had not answered it with forgiveness. 
They were suspended in that narrow country between apology and forgiveness, where every word had to carry its own weight. 
"The moon is waning," Renn said. 
"By the old rule, we have until it clears the spire." 
Both kingdoms measured the seasons differently, so they had long ago adopted synchronizing through moon phases to time every exchange. 
A moon could be seen from either valley, while calendars could not be trusted. 
The method had failed once, when a delay of one moon phase had hidden a name from the ledgers. 
That failure was why Marit had come. 
"There is one name still on your side," she said. 
"Tamas Brone." 
Renn's jaw tightened. 
"You know he lives?" 
"I know because of this." 
Marit drew the leather cord from her collar. 
At the end hung the antique drawer pull, brass gone green, its back plate still marked by a quartermaster's thumbprint. 
She set it on the parapet stone, where it clicked like a small tooth. 
"When the drawer jammed, I found a second list rolled behind it," she said. 
"It named Tamas." 
"I thought the first list was correct because the quartermaster was indeed your father." 
Her thoughtfulness had become hesitation; she had waited to be certain while a cart left without him. 
Renn stared at the pull. 
"You never told my father." 
"I was afraid the truth would end the truce," Marit said. 
"That fear was its own kind of war." 
Wind pushed mist across the stones, and for a moment neither of them spoke. 
"I have brought him," Renn said quietly. 
"He is at the third bridge, under the new moon." 
The exchange was to happen in two phases: first the aged and wounded, then the young, as the moon changed. 
But a storm-worn rope still tethered the signal gate to the parapet; if they could not untether it, neither side would see the light. 
The rope had frozen stiff, and the wind made the parapet feel like the deck of a sinking ship. 
Marit climbed to the windward post and worked the knot with bare fingers. 
The cold bit her, but she loosened the final loop and felt the rope untether from the iron ring. 
She hauled the signal line free and tied it to the antique drawer pull, letting its weight hold the rope against the gust. 
The lanterns below flared twice, and the gates on both sides opened. 
Renn watched the pull swing. 
"You brought my father's drawer to a battlefield." 
"I brought my mistake," Marit said. 
"I am done carrying it alone." 
"Then let it stay here," Renn said. 
"Neutral ground should hold what we cannot yet forgive." 
He did not offer his hand, and she did not ask for it. 
The first prisoners crossed in the thinning mist, their breath rising like small prayers. 
Tamas Brone came last, thin and blinking, and Marit felt something in her chest loosen without breaking. 
By dawn, the exchange was complete, and the parapet was empty except for wind and the pull. 
Marit had not forgiven Renn, and he had not pretended to be forgiven. 
They had agreed only to meet again at the next full moon, synchronizing through moon phases until trust became habit. 
As she descended, she left the antique drawer pull on the stone, no longer a tether but a lesson. 
She had learned that wisdom was not the absence of error, but the patient work of rebuilding after it. 
The mist closed behind her in a furrowed hush.
