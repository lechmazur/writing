The rails hummed a fractured lullaby as the last freight crawled across the ash flats. 
After the last forest burns, the wind has no leaves to fold into songs, so it invents new ones from wire and sand. 
Jorran was the line's only dreamless freight guard, hired because sleep made men careless and dreams made them liars. 
He kept no dream of his own, only a ledger of routes and a chess clock frozen at midnight. 
The clock had two faces and no hands; its flags were raised in a tie that refused to end. 
Jorran rode at the tail of the train, where the cars rattled most and the dark pressed closest to the seams. 
In the fourth car slept the cargo he was sworn to carry: a divergent archive of every forest that might have survived. 
Each ledger in the archive recorded a different burning, and each unburned page was another world breathing beneath the weight. 
The archive had been made after the last forest burned, when the survivors tried to save possibility itself from a single decision. 
The train stopped before the bloom, as it always did, at the edge of the glowing dune in bloom. 
Its flowers opened only at midnight, each petal a small lantern cupping a spark of green memory. 
The railmen called that place a graveyard and a cradle, and they would go no farther than the railhead. 
Jorran climbed down with the clock in his fist and walked into the blue haze that rose from the sand. 
He was not always entirely on the siding; the archive had given him one attribute the company could not explain. 
He was occasionally everywhere, seen at the caboose and the engine at the same instant, and sometimes not seen at all. 
They called it a symptom, but Jorran had learned to use it as a way of listening to every unlit corner. 
He followed the petal-light until the archive crate sat before him, bound in seals that drank the moon. 
The first seal was a chessboard carved into the lid, its pieces set to a game no one had finished. 
Jorran set the chess clock frozen at midnight beside the board, and the flags stopped quarreling with the dark. 
The lid clicked open, and the divergent archive unfolded like a wounded animal made of paper and starlight. 
Ledgers bled into one another; trains ran on rails that had never been laid; forests stood whole in one page and cinders in the next. 
Jorran's head ached with the lullaby of all those worlds, each verse ending before it could comfort him. 
He knelt and opened his hands, because force would only collapse the archive into one burning forest. 
To open the archive, he had to charm it; to charm it, he had to sing through wave functions. 
He did not command the wave functions to choose a branch, but let them overlap and harmonize like strings in a chord. 
His voice was low and broken, a fractured lullaby sung by a man who had never dreamed and therefore never lied. 
The crate brightened, and the archive answered with a chorus of possible mornings, none of them whole. 
In the margins, where no keeper had written, he found the sparks he had been sent to save. 
They were not fire, exactly, but seeds of stubborn light, huddled in the unlit corners of every canceled spring. 
Jorran's work, his quiet reason for walking the ash flats, was to cultivate the sparks in the unlit corners. 
He carried them to the glowing dune in bloom and pressed each one into the sand beside a lantern flower. 
The ground warmed under his boots, and the petals turned toward him as if he were a sun they had only heard about. 
But the archive fought to close, afraid of being narrowed to one world; it tried to fold him into a branch where the train never stopped. 
Jorran felt himself pulled toward a sky with two moons and no desert, a road where he was not dreamless. 
He gripped the chess clock frozen at midnight and remembered that midnight is the hour when every branch agrees to wait. 
He charmed the collapse away, not with a spell, but through wave functions, teaching the archive to sing in counterpoint instead of one voice. 
The wave functions settled into a pattern that allowed many worlds at once, and his body returned to the dune. 
For a moment he was occasionally everywhere, and everywhere he touched, a spark took root. 
The flowers closed like fists around the seeds, and the sand turned the color of old amber. 
Jorran rode without dreaming, awake in every siding at once, a quiet keeper of the sparks that refused to be forgotten.
