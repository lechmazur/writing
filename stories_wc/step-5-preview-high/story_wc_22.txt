The gloom-hill coachman walked the blooming jetty at twilight, his boots silent on planks split open by white flowers.  Behind him the coach road vanished into salt marsh, and the bridge to the battlefield had crumbled into the tidal flat.  Ahead, the water had gone strange after the reef died.  The coral skeletons no longer broke the swell, so the sea lay flat and black, holding the last red light without reflection.  This was the submerged concord, the quiet agreement between the drowned battlefield and the dead reef.  Only at twilight could a living man stand inside it without drowning.  The coachman knew this because he spoke dead languages, the actual tongues of the drowned that rose from the reef when the tide turned.  He knelt where the jetty narrowed to a single rail and set down his collection of dried seed pods.  They rattled softly, brown and veined, each gathered from the grave gardens of Gloom Hill.  His mission was to let a garden reclaim a battlefield.  The battlefield lay beneath the flat water, a meadow of silt and broken halberds.  But silt alone would not grow anything, for the dead had poisoned the loam with iron and salt.  So the soil needed to be fertilized.  The coachman took out a small iron spoon, its bowl worn thin by decades of this work.  He carried no manure or lime; his fertilizer came from understanding bird calls.  At first light the terns had shrieked over the bar, and at noon the oyster catchers had whistled in minor thirds.  Now, in the violet dusk, the nightjars began their churring, and he understood that the eastern shoal was ready.  The birds were the last translators of the submerged concord, their throats carrying messages between the air and the drowned kingdom.  He spoke back in the dead language of the reef-builders, a sound like stones rubbed together underwater.  A heron answered from the third piling and stepped onto the blooming planks.  Its claws clicked a code that told him where to dig.  He pried up a flower-heavy plank and found the mud beneath, thick and hungry.  With the iron spoon he cut channels into the mud, opening veins for the water to enter.  Then he broke open the dried seed pods and mixed the pale meal with black compost made from rotted warship timbers.  This was the fertilize step, the marriage of dead wood and living seed.  He pressed the mixture into the channels and patted the mud closed.  The heron watched while the nightjars counted the seconds.  When the last pod was empty, the coachman listened to the language of the water.  The submerged concord was shifting.  Far out, a low note rose through the black surface, a chord of acceptance.  The battlefield was receiving the garden.  Before the reef died, the battlefield had been a reef itself, armored with bone and anchor-flukes.  The war between land and sea had ended in mutual drowning, and the two dead powers had signed a treaty of silt.  He had spent thirty years tending that treaty.  As a gloom-hill coachman he had driven supplies to the front, and later, when the front sank, he had driven mourners to the shore.  The dead languages had come slowly, first as dreams of coughing, then as words he could shape without moving his lips.  Now they lived in his chest like cold birds.  The blooming jetty at twilight seemed to breathe, the flowers opening wider as the dark gathered.  Their white petals were the first scouts of the garden, seeded years ago by his own hands.  Already their roots held the planks together.  Soon they would creep down the pilings and out across the water, walking on the submerged concord like bridge-walkers.  He thought of the men who had died below, their names now part of the dead language he spoke.  He did not judge them; he only wanted the poppies to grow through their ribcages.  The heron lifted its wings and croaked once, then flew toward the dark marsh.  That was the signal that the tide would turn within the hour.  The coachman gathered his empty satchel and the iron spoon, tucking them into his coat.  He left the split seed pods behind to become boats for beetles.  Standing, he looked down the length of the blooming jetty, a narrow line of flowers and rot between two vastnesses.  Behind him the inland rose into starlight; before him the dead sea stretched to the edge of the world.  But here, on this single rail, the narrow vastness of the world had contracted to the size of a seed.  He walked home, speaking to the nightjars in a tongue older than the reef.  By spring, green shoots would break the battlefield surface, and the garden would have begun.
