After forgiving the unforgivable, Vela still woke before the cloud bells. 
She had sent the words of release down the mountain to Aldric, and she had meant every one. 
Yet peace had not followed her. 
She was the committed caretaker of the Umbral Grotto above the clouds, and she had promised its shallow terraces that she would find the source of contentment before her bones turned to weather. 
The grotto held the scallop beds, and the scallop beds held the cycle of rebirth. 
Each dying shell fed the silt; each new shell opened like a fist remembering mercy. 
Vela had managed those beds for twenty years, moving through her work with a kindly decisive patience that few visitors understood. 
When grief came, she had learned the only reliable method of climbing trees to think better. 
The storm-bent cypress at the grotto lip was her thinking tree, and its branches could hold her weight like an old friend. 
Aldric's parcel arrived three days after her letter, wrapped in oilcloth, with no apology inside. 
It held only their mother's antique hairpin, the pearl gone gray, the shaft bent where he had forced the floodgate frame open during the storm. 
Vela had not known he kept it. 
The sight of it made her throat close, but she set it on the sill and returned to her rounds. 
By dusk the upper nursery was dry, the water having vanished into the old spillway as if it had never existed. 
Vela checked the channels, cleared the moss, and found the obstruction below the sill, where silt and a lodged stone had choked the spring. 
She needed height. 
So she went to her cypress and began climbing, hand over hand, until the wind opened around her and the grotto lay below like a dark bowl breathing. 
From that height she saw the flaw in the basin: the floodgate frame had warped, and the water was pooling where it could not feed the scallops. 
She also saw that the antique hairpin would fit the seam. 
The thought did not soften her, but it steadied her. 
Vela was not a woman to leave a tool unused because of its past. 
She descended and carried the hairpin to the spillway. 
Its bent shaft slid into the warped frame as though made for it, and she worked the metal gently until the stone shifted. 
Water came first as a whisper, then as a silver muscle, and the dry channels woke with the sound of applause. 
Vela knelt and began to scallop a new lip along the nursery edge, pressing the soft clay into crescents so the flow would enter without tearing the beds. 
She hummed as she worked, and the sound of the water found the rhythm. 
That night the first shell opened, and inside it lay a pearl of new life, no larger than a raindrop and just as bright. 
She understood then that the cycle of rebirth did not ask her to forget the flood or to call her brother innocent. 
It asked only that she keep the channels clean, the beds shallow, and her hands unclosed. 
Aldric could be forgiven and still be wrong; she could carry grief and still be useful. 
Contentment, she realized, was not a treasure hidden in some clear pool above the world. 
It was the source itself: the water passing through her care, the shells rising again, the forgiveness returning as work rather than wound. 
She climbed the cypress one last time before sleeping, not to solve anything, but to let the grotto hold her thoughts while she looked down. 
The clouds had cleared, and the moon laid a scalloped shadow across the terraces, as if the night itself were tasting the edge of the water. 
A vineyard hush settled over the stone, the hush of fruit ripening without witness, of slow labor becoming bread. 
Vela slept late for the first time in years, and when she woke, she went straight to the beds. 
There would always be another floodgate, another brother, another winter. 
But there would also be another spring, and she would be the one kneeling in it. 
That was enough.
