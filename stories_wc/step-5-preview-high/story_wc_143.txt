The crater inspector reached the moonlit summit before lunar dawn, during the space age when darkness had become a rationed resource.  He was the last surveyor certified to read impact scars by hand, and he had come to photograph the last of something precious: the final unbroken night on the ridge.  In his glove he carried a small vial holding the nightwatch's hourglass sand grain.  The grain had belonged to Marisol, the nightwatch who had kept the old summit station before the relays and floodlights arrived.  Kael had never been sentimental, but he was purposefully curious about the way she had spoken of that single grain.  She had claimed it could talk.  Now, standing where Earthlight washed the rocks, he understood that she had meant it literally.  The grain was laced with salts and metal oxides, and her notes described using chemical reactions for timed messages.  Heat would wake one layer at a time, so the message would arrive not all at once but in the slow rhythm of an hourglass.  Kael checked his suit clock, then opened the vial and pressed it against his chest heater.  The first stain bloomed at the glass rim, pale blue, then darkened into words.  "Outlast the mirrors," it read.  "Do not trust the light that comes too fast."  He looked east, where the mining consortium's mirror towers waited to bounce sunrise across the summit before its time.  They had promised efficiency, but they were stealing the dawning hush, the one quiet hour when the Moon seemed to decide whether to open her eye.  Kael's hands trembled, and not from cold.  He had been sent to certify the crater as ready for permanent illumination, which meant the end of the very thing he loved.  The second message began to form, amber now, and he forced himself to read it slowly.  "The last shadow is precious," Marisol had written.  "Photograph it before it forgives us."  He set the camera on a basalt shelf and framed the crater's rim, where the last true night lay thick and indigo.  The chemical clock did not stop.  A third line appeared, white as frost, as his suit heater pushed the grain through another stage.  "You were always too curious for a clerk," it said.  "You looked into craters for the story, not the ore."  Kael felt the old shame and the older joy.  He had been hired because he did not flinch at blank rock, and he had stayed because every scar had a voice.  The summit fell silent in the way only vacuum can, and the Earth rose like a held breath.  This was the dawning hush he had crossed a hundred kilometers of regolith to keep, and it was already fraying at the edges.  He waited because he had to outlast the temptation to run, to file the report early, to let the mirrors win.  Minutes passed with the weight of centuries.  The final message was the simplest.  It carried only a time and a place: the moment the last of the lunar midnight would touch the crater floor.  Kael steadied the shutter cable in his glove.  He thought of Marisol standing here in her patched suit, warming that same grain, refusing to let the station forget why it watched.  The chemical stain darkened into a perfect circle, a lens made of salt, and the shutter opened.  Light gathered on the sensor, the last shadow in the frame, the last grain of the hourglass in his palm.  Then the mirrors flashed, and an artificial dawn leapt across the summit, bright and eager and utterly wrong.  Kael did not look away.  He photographed the change, because the loss itself had become precious.  The salt circle faded to gray, its message complete, and the grain went quiet forever.  In the new glare he saw the first human footprint the floodlights would erase by noon, and he let the dawnlit melancholy settle into him like fine dust.  He had outlasted the night, but only just.  The photograph would outlast him, and perhaps the silence would outlast the mirrors if enough people learned to look at it.  Kael sealed the vial, left the mirror towers humming behind him, and walked down the slope with the grain warm in his hand.
