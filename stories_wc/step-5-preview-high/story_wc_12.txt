The bell tower of Saint Quatern existed only when observed. 
Brother Anselm, its loop pedal priest, had maintained the dimensional anchor for eleven years by recording silence and looping it into sacred architecture. 
Tonight the stillness was unraveling. 
Threads of frozen quiet hung in the air like cobwebs, each one vibrating with a sound that had not yet decided to exist. 
On the cot below, Sister Marguerite's fingers twitched toward a threshold that would not be crossed until the final breath left her body. 
Before the possession completes, Anselm told himself, the seams must be sealed. 
He stepped onto the pedals, his sandals finding the brass levers that fed the tower's resonance into the stone. 
His gift was not prayer but decoration; he decorated with silence, layering hollow loops upon one another until the room forgot how to carry noise. 
The unraveled stillness resisted him, fraying at the edges where it brushed against Marguerite's skin. 
Anselm lifted his artist's palette, a wooden tray holding one color, the bruised violet of compressed midnight. 
Only one pigment remained because the anchor could not support the spectrum; it required a single truth to bind all dimensions together. 
He dipped a brush into the color and began to paint the air above the sister, tracing the invisible geometry that kept the tower from drifting into the astral sea. 
Marguerite's voice emerged, layered and wrong, a harmony produced by throats she did not own. 
You assume, it said, that stillness is empty. 
Anselm pressed a pedal. 
The loop captured his pause and repeated it, turning absence into a rhythm. 
He had learned long ago that demons could not be banished by force; he had to plumb them, measure their depths with questions that sounded like instruments. 
Through rhetorical devices he approached the thing inside her, using anaphora and chiasmus to trap its logic in its own assumptions. 
You came to unravel, Anselm said, looping the words until they stacked like bricks. 
Yet you require the unraveled. 
What calls you home? 
The tower shuddered. 
The palette grew lighter, the single color bleeding into the walls as it repaired the frayed stillness. 
Sister Marguerite gasped, her eyes rolling toward a ceiling that existed now only because Anselm had questioned it into shape. 
Silence is not an absence, the priest intoned, looping the phrase into a drone that filled the space between heartbeats. 
It is a structure. 
You sought to illuminate hidden assumptions, but you became the assumption. 
The thing inside her laughed, a sound that cracked the window glass. 
Anselm pressed another pedal, recording the fracture and turning it into a decorative seam in the silence. 
He had to illuminate her hidden assumptions before the body accepted the guest fully; the final seal would form the moment her heart skipped its cue. 
The artist's palette with one color was nearly bare now, but he did not need more pigment. 
He needed the right question. 
Through rhetorical devices he descended into the possession itself, a diver seeking the bottom of a well made of borrowed thoughts. 
What do you assume I am? he asked, his voice now doubled by the loop. 
The demon hesitated. 
In that gap of muffled clarity, as though the world spoke through wool and glass, Anselm saw the truth: the entity was not invading but fleeing, escaping a dimension where stillness had already completely unraveled. 
It sought structure, not dominion. 
Then bind yourself, Anselm commanded, and with the final brushstroke of violet he painted the entity into the architecture, not as a prisoner but as a keystone. 
The possession completed in reverse, the stillness weaving itself anew around the tower. 
Sister Marguerite slept. 
Anselm set the palette down, its one color spent, and began once more to decorate with silence. 
The bell tower would hold until the next stillness unraveled.
