After the resuscitation, Vera climbed through the shattered turnstile of the abandoned carnival funhouse, trailing her mother's opera cloak behind her like a bridal train stained by mud. 
The whispering advisor had promised her that only within those rotting halls could she trick fear into revealing its weakness, and Vera had believed the voice because it was the only thing that had stayed constant while her lungs had filled with black water. 
Agnes spoke through the loose opera cloak clasp, a silver heron whose beak had snapped off on the night of the drowning, and the metal was always cold enough to burn. 
Vera pressed the brooch against her ear so that the voice could slide directly into her jawbone, intimate and slick as an eel. 
"The luminance remembers," Agnes hissed, her breath sounding wet and clotted, as though she still held pond water in her throat. 
And indeed the walls wept a lingering luminance, a sickly phosphorescence that pooled in the concave mirrors and trembled across water-stained velvet in waves of nauseating gold. 
It was a light that belonged to decay, gorgeous and repulsive, the perfect visual tone for a place built to entertain children and then left to fester like a wound that refused to close. 
Vera was photographically cursed, so she dared not carry a camera to document her investigation; every lens and every phone screen captured only her own corpse, bloated and pale from the resuscitation that had clawed her back from death three weeks prior. 
Instead she clutched a leather-bound dream dictionary, its margins swollen with paradoxical notes written in her own handwriting during the fever that had followed her revival. 
The funhouse responded to certainty and crumbled under contradiction, so she read the inverted entries aloud, letting paradox lubricate her path through the tilting corridors. 
"A mirror means blindness," she recited, and at once the corridor of glass turned opaque as spoiled milk, the reflected screams dying into muffled thuds. 
"A stair means descent into sky," she continued, and the spiral before her folded flat into a bridge of warped boards that led not upward but inward toward the belly of the ride. 
Agnes whispered louder then, jealous of the dictionary's power, urging Vera to unbosom the secret she had swallowed since adolescence like a stone lodged in the gut. 
The advisor knew that fear could only be trapped by confession, that its weakness was the nakedness of a fully exposed heart laid bare before the jaws of the dark. 
Vera paused before the final chamber, where the lingering luminance gathered into a gelatinous puddle beneath a broken strobe light that flashed in irregular, sickening rhythms. 
There the thing that had drowned her waited, wearing her own face like a carnival mask carved from wax left too long in the sun. 
It smiled with lips the color of split pomegranates, an expression of repulsive allure that promised ruin wrapped in silk and sugared with rot. 
"You came back," it said, and Vera tasted iron and cotton candy, the twin flavors of blood and memory colliding on her tongue. 
She unbosomed herself then, speaking the truth that she had let Agnes fall into the reflecting pool while she had stood frozen, watching the bubbles break the surface and choosing not to dive. 
The words hung in the air like humidity, and the paradox in her dream dictionary fluttered its pages without wind, eager to be released. 
"Confession is armor," the marginal note had said, and she understood now that the armor was not for her fragile body but for the fear that fed on hidden things and grew fat on silence. 
The entity shrieked as the opera cloak clasp grew hot in her palm, the broken heron beak mending itself into a shining hook that caught the sickly light. 
Fear revealed its weakness in that instant, a seam of hesitation beneath its borrowed face, and Vera drove the clasp into the seam like a key into a lock that had waited years to turn. 
The lingering luminance surged once, a wave of luminous revulsion, then guttered and drained into the clasp until the funhouse was merely dark, ordinary, and silently rotting. 
Agnes fell silent at last, satisfied, and Vera walked out beneath the dead ferris wheel, no longer photographically cursed, her shadow finally her own. 
Behind her, the carnival exhaled its last breath of sweet decay, and somewhere in the dark a heron cried out in victory.
