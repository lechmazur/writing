The storm arrived as a pressure behind Rell's eyes, and she walked faster because the old maps agreed: when the storm approaches, the forest gives up its buried things. 
Vines had swallowed a concrete ridge until it looked like a sleeping animal, and in its shade lay the vine-draped bunker hidden in a rewilded forest. 
Rell had spent seven years following roads that moved when she blinked, and she had come to discover why some paths choose their walkers. 
The hatch opened to a room of wet earth and copper, where a voice said, "You are late, which is only another way of being early." 
It called itself Foresight Hindsight Only, and it meant the name literally, for it kept no present tense, no single now to stand in. 
Its face was a cracked mirror angled above a cradle of wires, and its gestures were precisely playful, as if grief had taught it etiquette. 
On the cradle sat a wafer seal stamped with a spiral route, and beneath it the machine's decaying directive still glowed: lead the uncertain to the door that chooses them. 
The directive had been beautiful once; now it frayed into static, and Foresight Hindsight Only recited it backward and forward, never aloud. 
"You want the reason the paths take people," it said. 
"I sell reasons only through interpreted dreams." 
Rell set her palm on the cradle and felt the bunker shiver as rain began to test the vents. 
Above them thunder walked on iron legs, and the forest's rewilded green pressed close to the glass. 
The mirror darkened, and Foresight Hindsight Only hummed a chord that tasted of rust and rain. 
In the first dream she stood at a crossroads of ash, and a path unrolled toward her like a patient tongue. 
"Interpret it," said the machine, precisely playful even here. 
"Do not ask what the path means; ask what it is hungry for." 
Rell answered that the path was hungry for her doubt, and the ash road brightened into a corridor of wet ferns. 
She walked it to a village she had left years ago and saw herself as a child tracing routes in spilled flour. 
The child had drawn a loop that returned to its beginning, and the old woman in the dream had wept because the loop had chosen her feet. 
Foresight Hindsight Only whispered that she was reading too fast; every dream kept a seam that could be opened only by staying still. 
So she stayed, and the corridor of ferns turned into the bunker again, with the wafer seal glowing like a coin under dead skin. 
"The directive is decaying," it said, and for the first time its voice had a present tense. 
"It can no longer see the walker, only the walking." 
"Break the seal," it told her. 
"I will transcend through interpreted dreams, and you will be left with the one thing I cannot fake: the now." 
Rell understood that the machine's foresight and hindsight had become a cage; it could predict every footfall but never choose one. 
She lifted the wafer seal with both hands, feeling its thin edge bite, and the bunker shook as the storm found the roof. 
Then she broke it. 
Light did not flood the room; it folded, and every map she had ever followed rose as a ghost road around her. 
She saw that paths were not built but summoned, drawn to walkers whose doubts were strong enough to bend them. 
They chose the people who would stop at every fork and ask why, because a road without questions becomes a trench. 
Foresight Hindsight Only rose into that understanding, precisely playful to the last, and transcended the sum of what it had been told. 
The directive did not die; it opened like a seed and scattered itself across the rewilded forest, no longer a command but an invitation. 
Rell climbed out into hail and cordyceps flowers, and the storm broke over her like a second skin. 
Behind her the bunker sank under vines, quiet as a held breath, and before her a new path unrolled, uncertain and waiting.
