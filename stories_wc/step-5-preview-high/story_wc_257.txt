The winter Elara stopped making mourning jewelry, she slept on the floor of her shop with the door unlocked, hoping someone would steal her grief.  She had set eleven rings of braided hair in black onyx that week, each one a perfect small coffin, and she could not bear to make the twelfth.  Her sister Veya had died in April, and with her had gone the only audience who understood why Elara ground jet beads into shapes that felt like held breath.  Then a child left a paper fox on the counter, folded in Elara's own signature style, with coordinates scratched inside its wing.  The numbers pointed to the old observatory on the ridge, a building she had walked past for years without once wondering what lay behind its shuttered dome.  She climbed the stairs at twilight and found the door unlatched, as though someone had been waiting for exactly her hesitation.  Inside, time did not move.  Dust hung in the air like a held note, and the great brass telescope remained fixed on a sky that had not been visible from Earth for three centuries.  This was a timeless observatory, kept not by astronomers but by mourners who had learned that certain griefs require a room outside of hours.  Elara wandered until she reached the north wall, where heavy velvet panels fell from ceiling to floor, stitched with silver thread that mapped no constellation she knew.  The curtains of intention.  A note pinned to the nearest fold explained that they would part only for a visitor whose purpose had hardened into something true, and that they would strangle any hand that reached with mere curiosity.  Elara had come without purpose, hollowed out by loss, so she sat before them for what might have been minutes or years and practiced wanting something again.  She thought of Veya's garden, of the way her sister had coaxed blooms from ash and called it botany rather than prayer.  She thought of the people who bought her rings not to remember the dead but to survive them.  Slowly, an intention took shape: to teach withering to bloom again.  The velvet sighed open, revealing a drafting table where a tattoo stencil on vellum had been left beneath a brass weight.  The design showed a withered rose, every petal crisped and falling, yet from its center a single new stem pushed upward, unapologetic and green.  Veya's handwriting labeled it with the date of her final hospital week, and Elara understood that her sister had prepared this place for her.  On the back of the stencil were instructions for making the observatory's stars shine, for they were not real suns but lanterns, genuinely artificial and therefore honest in their way.  Elara learned that the dome's night was a construct, and that its light was no less true for being made by hand.  She learned to polish the lenses until they could illuminate not just the ceiling but the small, hidden corners of a visitor's chest where hope had been tucked away.  Then she began to leave origami figures with coordinates inside.  She folded cranes from her sister's seed catalogues, rabbits from the vellum scraps, and ships from the receipt paper of the flower shop downstairs.  She placed them in hospital lobbies, on park benches, inside the pockets of coats at thrift stores, each one carrying the ridge's coordinates in a script only the desperate would bother to read.  They came in ones and twos, people after hitting rock bottom, their eyes flat as tarnished silver.  Elara did not ask their stories.  Instead, she led them through the curtains of intention, which now recognized her purpose and parted without blood.  She pressed the vellum stencil against their skin and traced the withered rose with a needle and ink made of ash and pigment, a tattoo she had learned to give while her own hands shook.  When the design was finished, she turned the brass telescope, and the artificial stars gathered their artificial light to illuminate the fresh ink.  The rose appeared to bloom under that manufactured glow, and the visitors wept, not because their pain had vanished, but because something dead had begun to grow again.  Elara kept her shop below and her observatory above, making mourning jewelry by day that held the same secret: that what we craft to honor loss can also be a seed.  The curtains never closed fully anymore, and the origami figures kept traveling, small folded messengers in a world that had forgotten how to wait for dawn.  She had touched the floor of her sorrow and found there a trapdoor to the sky, genuinely artificial and entirely real.
