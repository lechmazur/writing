The cobalt atrium by a frozen lake had waited all winter beneath a sky the color of split slate, its glass ribs humming faintly with the pressure of the ice.  For eleven years the law had forbidden anyone from walking beneath the colonnade, but the morning after the ban lifted the doors were unlocked and the frost on the threshold had begun to weep.  I was a cloud-shadow poet by training, one who chased the shapes the clouds threw across the lake and pressed them into verses no ear had heard.  I was aesthetically driven before I was anything else; I believed that if a line could be made beautiful enough, the world would rewrite itself to match it.  The old gatekeepers had called the quartz colonnade sacred, and they had posted the ban in language meant to outlast the stones, but their words were all borrowed, brittle things, and I had come to paraphrase them.  In the blue shadow of the atrium, where the lake breathed under ice, the humming quartz colonnade rose before me, each pillar a throat of pale mineral singing a note that made my ribs answer.  I did not want to break the columns or shatter the prohibition; I wanted to stride through boundaries once thought sacred, to walk where the law had drawn a chalk line and then made the line a wall.  So I began the method that the old poets had whispered about and the magistrates had banned, the deliberate act of paraphrasing the world until its hard edges lost their argument.  I lifted my hand to the nearest pillar and spoke the statute again, not as a command, but as an image, giving the ban a softer grammar, letting its fear become a line of cloud.  The quartz answer was a chord, low and green, and as the sound moved through the colonnade the boundaries of the atrium began to dissolve, the glass and the lake and the frost bleeding into one continuous blue.  A tremor of certainty ran up my spine then, a feeling like the moment a cloud's shadow exactly matches a shore, and I understood that nothing had ever been forbidden except by voice.  The frozen lake answered with a crack, not a break but a paraphrase of its own surface, translating its stillness into the motion of deep water beneath the ice.  The atrium had become cobalt in earnest now, not paint or light but a state of mind, and the colonnade's hum moved through my bones until I could not tell my pulse from its song.  I walked forward through dissolving boundaries, my shoes no longer meeting stone but meeting a kind of blue hush, the lantern-moth hush that falls over a room when the last lamp is nearly gone.  Moths gathered at the window ledges, not for flame but for the pale music, and their wings made a sound like breath being taken for a long sentence.  I thought of the magistrates who had feared the colonnade, and I felt no anger, only the aesthetic sadness one feels for a poem written by someone who did not know poetry was free.  At the center of the colonnade I stopped where the lake's ice showed the black veins of open water, and I offered a new stanza that paraphrased the deepest law of all.  "What is kept apart is only kept apart by the keeping," I said, and the pillars took the words, turned them, and gave them back as light.  The boundary that had been sacred vanished in that offering, and the frozen lake, no longer forbidden to the ear, began to sing the old ban as lullaby, line by line, until even the harsh language had turned to hush.  I left the atrium at dusk with the poet's cloud-shadow still attached to my heels, knowing the ban had been lifted not by council but by a single tremor of certainty.  The colonnade continued its humming behind me, no longer a wall of sound but a door left open to anyone willing to dissolve the words that had made it a wall.  And somewhere beyond the ice, a lantern-moth hush spread over the whole lake, waiting for the next poet to stride through.
