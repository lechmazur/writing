After the fossil fuel ban, the Gullwatch Sea Level Monitoring Station still moved a little at night, drifting on its moorings as if it had not decided where it belonged. 
Mira Cortes had been posted there as the region's coral bleaching witness, the woman who had held the thermometer when the reef turned white and began to smell of old eggs. 
Her assignment was to record tides, but she accepted because of a private need: to discover why some paths disappear. 
The islanders spoke of shallow paths between mangrove islets that opened for a boat at low water and then vanished from every map. 
In a drawer she found an atlas page torn out, brittle and salt-stained, showing a dashed route labeled "Barrow Path" and one marginal note: "periodically eternal." 
The phrase was an archival attribute for channels that disappeared from one survey and returned in another, as if the water kept two sets of books. 
Mira thought of the bleached reef, still standing in clean water, periodically eternal in photographs and absent in the living channel below. 
She had a method for hearing the station's condition, a practice of tapping rhythms on surfaces until the hollow notes matched the tide tables. 
At night she tapped the copper tide well, the bulkhead, and the ladder, counting echoes the way other people counted prayers. 
Most surfaces answered sharply, but one panel behind the gauge cabinet gave a flat, dead sound that did not belong to steel. 
She opened it with a tide wrench and found oilcloth packets wedged in a cavity between the ribs of the station. 
Inside were gauge sheets, a valve diagram, and half of another chart that fit the torn atlas page like a broken shell. 
She began to assemble them on the chart table, aligning coastlines by the stains and the handwriting rather than by the printed grids. 
The more she assembled, the more the station seemed to lean over her shoulder, drifting yet moored to whatever secret it had been keeping. 
A faded cover sheet explained the delay: the old keeper had ordered all corrections withheld until a coral bleaching witness could compare them with living reef. 
The archive itself had been a delayed revelation, not a lost record, waiting for someone who had watched loss happen slowly and would not call it weather. 
The valve diagram showed that Barrow Path had been held open by a tide valve that opened only in certain seasons. 
Before the ban, a small generator ran the valve; after the ban, the generator was condemned and the valve was sealed by order. 
The path had not vanished only because of rising water, though the sea had risen, but because the mechanism that argued with the sea had been retired. 
Mira sat with the assembled page in the lamplight and saw the whole pattern: every path that disappeared had first been maintained, then forgotten, then blamed on nature. 
The phrase "periodically eternal" had never meant uncertainty; it had meant a human rhythm repeated until the rhythm stopped. 
She copied the valve diagram onto a fresh sheet, added the torn atlas page, and walked the line to the sealed valve house at low tide. 
The wheel was rusted but not ruined, and the pipe still remembered the direction of flow. 
Mira did not pretend that she could save the reef; that work belonged to slower hands and longer decades. 
But she could assemble the evidence and open the valve once, letting salt water choose whether Barrow Path would return. 
The tide came in while she worked, and the station lifted against its chains, moored but adrift in the dark. 
She thought of coral, white and brittle, and of paths that vanish not all at once but by small permissions. 
When she knocked twice on the valve house door, the sound was bright, and she knew the station's bones were sound. 
The next morning she wrote her report with the atlas page attached, calling the disappearance what it was: a delayed revelation of neglect. 
She ended with the words "periodically eternal," and beneath them, "not without us." 
Then she went back to the tide gauge, tapped her old three-beat rhythm on its casing, and listened for the answer.
