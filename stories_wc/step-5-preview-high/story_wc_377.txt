The Whispering Bluff hung above the clouds where language had dissolved. 
After words became unnecessary, the people below spoke in symphonies of truth. 
A single bell could admit a lie, but a chord of tuned bells could not. 
Ilva was the last harmonious bell tuner appointed to keep those symphonies honest. 
She lived in a stone aerie at the bluff's edge, tuning great bronze bells that answered to one another like patient animals. 
Harmonious bell tuners were trained from childhood to hear the hidden overtones in ordinary speech and carry them into bronze. 
Winter came early that year, and with it came her condition: she was seasonally mute, her voice locked away until the thaw. 
Without song, she could not coax the old bells to yield their notes. 
Truth was stiffening into mere fact across the valleys, and the color of wonder was draining from every sound. 
Ilva refused to let the world turn gray while she waited for spring. 
She had until the first snow, no more. 
She climbed down the wind-scoured rocks each morning to gather what the clouds carried up from the ocean. 
One dawn, a slab of iceberg fragment lay wedged between two pinnacles, edges soft with melt and light. 
It seemed to glisten even in shadow, as if it remembered a bluer world. 
She strapped the ice to a sledge and hauled it up to her workshop, knowing that cold held pitch and clarity. 
The fragment did not ring like metal, but it sang in frequencies that made her teeth ache with recognition. 
It was the note that wonder made before the world learned to explain it. 
Ilva began to build. 
She shaped gears from cloud-tempered steel and balanced pendulums that swung on the bluff's ceaseless updrafts. 
No piston drove the device; only the bluff's own breath moved it. 
Her method was through kinetic sculpture, a machine that could strike the bells without a singer's breath. 
The iceberg fragment became the sculpture's heart, mounted at the center of a spinning gyre of hammers and wires. 
She tuned the wires until the ice could glisten in sound, brightening each overtone with a crystalline sheen of hearing. 
When the wind rose, the sculpture turned, and the hammers fell in patterns Ilva could not have timed by hand. 
Each blow passed through the ice, and the ice returned a note that was colder and truer than any word. 
The symphonies of truth that rose from the bluff were not declarations but questions shaped like light. 
Travelers below looked up and saw color where there had been only distance: amber patience, violet grief, a fierce turquoise that made them weep and laugh at once. 
The color did not paint their eyes; it painted their remembering. 
Ilva's muteness did not silence her; it purified her, forcing her intent into wood and bronze instead of breath. 
As the first snow sealed the pass, she sat among the turning arms of the sculpture and let it speak for her. 
The machine never tired, and the ice never fully melted, because wonder, once properly struck, renews itself. 
Wonder was not a decoration but a form of pressure, and the ice held it in suspension. 
On clear nights, the fragment would glisten beneath the aurora, and the distant listeners would remember how to feel before they knew. 
Ilva remained seasonally mute through the long dark, yet her vigor flowed out in measured cadence, quiet but unbreakable. 
She had set out to preserve the color of wonder, and she had succeeded by surrendering her own voice. 
The bluff had asked for nothing more, and the clouds had given her exactly what she needed. 
When spring finally loosened her tongue, the first sound she made was a tuning note, matched by the answering bells. 
The bluff still whispers above the clouds, and the symphonies of truth still carry her name in every turning gear.
