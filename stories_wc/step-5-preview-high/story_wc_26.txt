The blooming library above clouds opened its shelves at twilight. 
Maris was the sharp-eyed dusk noticer who catalogued the fading hour there. 
After dreams became shareable, the archivists stopped trusting memory and began to trust light. 
Every visitor uploaded their sleep, and the library grew petals that hummed with borrowed visions. 
In her coat pocket, Maris carried a smooth river stone from the only bank she had ever loved. 
The tragedy was not theft; it was a shadow unbound from its sleeper, left wandering the stacks when dreams were shared too freely. 
She worked with softly deliberate patience, comparing silent video footage from decades apart on glass plates that floated above the reading desks. 
She sought the frame where her brother's shadow would metamorphose from faithful tether into untethered smoke. 
Her motivation was to preserve the shape of hope, which she believed was a physical curve the heart made just before waking. 
On the first reel, his silhouette leaned toward her like a dog recognizing a whistle. 
On the second, the silhouette paused a half second after his body stopped, then drifted toward the window. 
She held the river stone tighter, its cold weight the only thing in the archive that still obeyed gravity. 
The petals around her exhaled a faint luminescence, dusting the tables with pollen that remembered other people's mornings. 
Maris believed that if she found the exact moment of separation, she could reverse it and force the shadow to become a guardian rather than a ghost. 
But hope had no schema in the shared-dream databases; it had become too common, too cheap, too collective. 
She needed the old shape, the private one, the one that existed before everyone slept in public. 
On the fourth loop of the later footage, she saw the shadow pause at the window and look back at the camera. 
Its shoulders rose with a patience that felt softly deliberate, as if deciding to stay rather than flee. 
The luminous regret in the room deepened, not as sorrow but as the sweet ache of a lamp burning low. 
Maris set the river stone on the glass plate and saw, at last, the difference. 
In the older clip, the stone had been a pebble in her pocket, invisible to the lens. 
In the newer clip, the stone had become a small dark moon, and her brother's shadow bowed toward it. 
She understood then that the shadow had not left him; it had followed the weight she carried. 
When dreams became shareable, his hope had leaked into the common pool, but his shadow had stayed loyal to her private shore. 
Maris placed her palm flat against the floating plate, and the footage began to warm. 
She whispered the coordinates of their childhood river, a language the archive did not recognize. 
The petals folded inward, listening, while the clouds below rearranged themselves into the shape of an open hand. 
In the frame, the shadow unbound stretched its arms and began to metamorphose, pouring itself into the river stone like water finding a crack. 
It did not return to her brother; instead, it became a second stone, heavier and warm. 
Maris understood that preserving the shape of hope did not mean keeping it unchanged. 
It meant giving it a form solid enough to survive the sharing. 
She closed the footage and slipped both stones into her pocket, where they clicked like small moons. 
Outside the tall windows, dusk arrived with the softly deliberate certainty she had always loved. 
The library bloomed brighter for a moment, petals turning luminous as they released a private hope back into the world. 
She was still the sharp-eyed dusk noticer, but now she noticed what the dark kept safe. 
The regret remained, luminous and unspoken, a shadow unbound that had finally found its rest. 
She smiled, and the library above clouds kept blooming long after the stars came out.
