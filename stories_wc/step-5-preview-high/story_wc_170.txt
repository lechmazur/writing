The terrace had been scrubbed by a spring storm, and now the prism parapet after rain held the last amber light in small, bright pieces. 
In the hush before the evening bells would ring, the echoing terrace seemed to inhale and hold its breath. 
I came because of my borrowed heartbeat, a pulse that had never been mine, thudding against my ribs like a visitor demanding to be let out. 
The physicians had called it a miracle; the donor had called it nothing at all, since the dead do not speak. 
I carried it as one carries a lit candle in wind, aware that any sudden movement might reveal it as stolen light. 
It was then that I saw the elusive mirage painter standing at the eastern arch, wrapping silence around himself like a cloak. 
No one in the city could agree on his age, his name, or even his face, yet everyone who wandered the upper gardens after rain claimed to have glimpsed him. 
He was perceptively calm, not distant, but settled deep within himself, as though every loud thing in the world had already been heard and forgiven. 
I approached with grace, I hope, though my steps were unsteady, my borrowed heartbeat skipping whenever the wet stones glimmered. 
The painter did not turn. 
He lifted one slender brush, not made of hair or feather but of woven twilight, and pointed it toward the long mirror mounted between the terrace columns. 
In that glass he meant to persuade the mirrors to show possibilities, for they had grown stubborn, reflecting only what already existed and refusing the shimmer of what might be. 
His motivation was older than the bells themselves, older than the spring that dripped from the parapet leaves. 
To unlock such vision, he worked through coded lullabies in children's songs, melodies that mothers had hummed for centuries without knowing they carried instructions for the waking world. 
The first notes drifted over the terrace, simple and strange, a tune about a swan and a silver cradle. 
The sound did not echo so much as unfurl, finding the corners of the terrace and settling there like dust turning to gold. 
I recognized the hidden pattern, for my grandmother had sung it while shelling peas, pausing on the third beat to hide a name, quickening on the fifth to draw a door. 
The mirror trembled. 
In its depths, the reflection of the prism parapet after rain began to separate into hues no human eye had named, each color a path not yet walked. 
The painter glanced at me then, and his eyes held the dusky harmony of the hour, neither sorrow nor joy but the balance between them. 
"You carry a borrowed heartbeat," he said, and it was not a question. 
I flinched, my hand rising to my chest. 
"It frightens you," he said, "because you believe a borrowed thing must be returned." 
"It must," I whispered, the hush before the bells pressing close. 
He shook his head, still painting with song. 
The second lullaby rose, a children's rhyme about lanterns in a well, its coded verses bending the air until the mirror showed not the terrace but a hundred terraces, each one bearing a different version of my life. 
In one, I was a gardener; in another, a bell-ringer; in another, I danced with the grace of someone who had never known fear of falling. 
The borrowed heartbeat in my chest began to keep time with the rhyme, not as a thief, but as a key finding its lock. 
"You did not steal it," the painter murmured over the hushed notes. 
"You were lent it so that you might see that a single pulse is not a wall but a window." 
The mirrors, persuaded, rippled with possibilities, and the prism parapet after rain flung rainbows across the stone like bridges. 
I understood then that my motivation to climb here had not been to return a gift but to learn how to hold it. 
With grace that surprised me, I reached toward the nearest reflection and felt only cool air and vast promise. 
The dusky harmony deepened as stars pricked the sky, and the first bell began its toll. 
In the space between its first and second notes, the elusive mirage painter stepped into the mirror and was gone. 
I remained on the echoing terrace in spring, the borrowed heartbeat steady and my own, while the coded lullaby faded into the ordinary song of the night.
