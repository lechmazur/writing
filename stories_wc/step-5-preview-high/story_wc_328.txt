The week after the missing returned changed, the town of Halden stopped pretending that winter was the only thing eating it.  I had come there as a cryptid researcher working undercover, carrying a herbology apprenticeship and a fake name that tasted of pine resin.  My cover employer was Maren, the herbalist whose underground garden breathed damp heat through iron grates and turned the cellar into a greenhouse of stubborn green.  Nine people had walked back into town wearing other people's habits, and the council called it mass delusion while it quietly doubled the night watch.  I knew better, but I kept my notes in a ledger that looked like seed inventory.  The garden itself was the first clue: cabbages hung upside down without falling, and the soil had learned to move in a gravitationally playful orbit around the roots.  Maren claimed the old plants had always been strange, yet she kept the doors locked and her eyes fixed on the west wall.  One evening, after the lamps had browned to embers, she slid a drawer from beneath a potting bench.  Inside lay a flint arrowhead wrapped in wool, its edge still bright enough to shame the trowels.  It was not a tool, she said, but a key, made by people who understood that growth was a harmony untamed.  She set the arrowhead into the center of a planting spiral, and the garden answered with a low chord that I felt in my molars.  Then she showed me the method.  On the wall shelf sat an old cordless phone, its antenna cracked, its plastic yellowed to the color of old teeth.  The missing had left confessions in its static, and the garden could only be taught to speak plainly via the static-laden confessions on an old cordless phone.  I pressed play because skepticism was easier than fear.  Static poured out like cold sand, and then a woman's voice said she had swallowed a river to keep from drowning.  Another man confessed that he had buried his laughter under a barn and now wanted it back before the frost killed it.  Their voices did not haunt; they wove.  The herbs began to metamorphose, leaves folding into wings, roots standing like knees, blossoms opening as mouths that hummed.  Soil lifted in ribbons; a watering can rolled along the ceiling and dripped upward into its own spout.  I grabbed the potting bench and felt my boots leave the floor, not falling but swinging, as if the garden had finally decided to be gravitationally playful with everyone who entered.  Maren moved through the air with her braid floating beside her like a second thought.  She told me the returned were not corpses or invaders but instruments in a song that had been denied a conductor.  Their changed habits were only the first notes; the garden was teaching them how to answer.  I wanted to believe her, but my mouth was full of the bitter report I had planned to file.  I had come to expose her, to turn her folklore into a punchline for men who feared anything they could not weigh.  Yet the confessions kept coming, each one a splinter of ordinary grief, and the garden turned that grief into blooming geometry.  A boy admitted he had run away because his father's silence felt like a locked room.  A mother confessed she still set a place at the table for a daughter who had never eaten there.  Their pain was not comedy, but their return, their strange green music, their refusal to be buried quietly could become a cosmic joke.  I finally understood that my job was not to debunk the phenomenon but to turn bitterness into a cosmic joke that would loosen the town's fist.  When I reached for the arrowhead, it burned cold, and I felt the first warm thing in weeks.  I pressed its point to my palm and let the blood fall into the spiral where the phone still spoke.  The static took my own confession without asking, and I heard myself admit that I had wanted to be feared enough to be remembered.  Then the garden stopped swinging, and the herbs settled into a new shape around me, gentler and thornier than before.  I had not become one of them, but I had become a conductor of their untamed chorus, and I could hear the town above us beginning to hum off-key.  I filed my report as a comedy of errors: missing persons, sentient cabbage, gravity on sabbatical, one cordless phone, all charges dismissed by laughter.  Under the frost, I left the arrowhead in the soil, a small frostbitten hope that the missing would return changed and laughing.
