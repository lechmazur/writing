Maren was a crafty lantern maker, and she knew that after the shadow detaches, the floating island tea garden becomes a place of unfinished light.  For eleven years she had trimmed wicks from beeswax and rice paper, hung her lanterns along the tea terraces, and listened to the wind argue with the chimes.  Her brother Ewan had died at dawn, and his shadow had peeled from his boots like a scrap of burnt silk, leaving the garden hushed.  In the village of the isle, that separation meant only one thing: the dead leave behind a dream that has not been spoken, and the living must catch it before it unravels.  Maren meant to record it.  She carried the rough stone Ewan had given her, a clouded opal the color of storm light, down to the polishing bench beside the steaming vats.  Gem polishing was a patient art, and she had learned it from her mother: turn the gem against wet slate until it gives back not brightness but memory.  The opal cleared in layers, and with each pass Maren felt her grief sharpen into something she could hold.  She fitted it into a small lantern whose paper was painted with cranes, so that the flame would burn through the lens and the lens would teach the flame to remember.  Above the garden, the glass basilica on a floating isle hung in a nest of clouds, its arches clear as breath and its bells silent.  The path to it was the Ascending Descent: a stair of pale stone that climbed the air while the isle beneath it sank.  Anyone who climbs to record a dream descends while ascending, dropping through their own sorrow as they rise toward the light.  Maren climbed with the lantern held against her chest, her boots slipping on steps that grew warmer as the isle fell.  The tea garden shrank below her, rows turning to stitches of green, and the clouds closed over her knees, her waist, her heart.  It was luminous torment to rise that way, every breath bright and cold, every memory lit as if by a lamp she could not shield.  Inside the basilica, the air smelled of rain and old incense, and the floor was a single sheet of glass that showed the drowned valley far below.  She placed the lantern on the altar, touched the lantern's lens to the basilica's central prism, and spoke Ewan's name into the waiting silence.  The dream recording began as a thread of silver smoke, then widened into a room she recognized: their mother's kitchen, the kettle singing, paper cranes drying on a string.  Ewan was there, folding a sheet of blue paper, his hands steady in the way they had not been at the end.  "You always said grief should be small enough to carry," he said, "and light enough to fly."  The vision trembled, and Maren understood her purpose at last: she had come to fold grief into origami cranes, not to keep him, but to change the shape of losing him.  She took from her coat the papers she had saved, sheet after sheet of the same pale rice paper she used for lanterns.  As the recorded dream rose around her, she began to fold, crease by crease, until the sorrow in her chest took wing and beak and angles.  One crane, ten, a hundred, each one a luminous torment of memory that no longer cut because it had been given a body that could leave.  The basilica transformed as the cranes rose, its glass walls drinking their light until the whole floating isle seemed a lantern itself.  Maren felt the stair release her as the last crane folded itself out of her hands, and she realized she had descended while ascending, lighter now, falling upward into a grief she could hold.  When she returned to the tea garden, the lanterns along the terraces burned with a new patience, and the shadow that had detached at dawn waited at the gate like a folded paper bird.  She did not try to unfold it.  Instead she hung the lantern's polished gem above her workbench, where it caught the dawn and taught every flame the slow art of letting go.
