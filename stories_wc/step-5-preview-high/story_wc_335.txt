In the forgotten library, dust hung like constellations in the amber shafts of late afternoon. 
The only sound was the hush that arrived at the hour when an empty playground swing drifts in the breeze beyond the broken windows, its chains creaking softly. 
Ilse Vane, the shard-mirror archivist, stood among shelves of fractured mirrors, each one holding a captive fragment of light that had once been a person. 
She was twenty-nine, though the exile registry claimed she was merely on loan from the living world. 
Reversible exile was not a punishment here but a pause, a door that opened from both sides only when the traveler had grown strong enough to walk through it. 
Her motivation was simple and seldom spoken: to mentor resilience quietly, without banners or brutality. 
A door scraped open, and the newest exile entered, a boy named Kestrel whose shadow looked too heavy for his shoulders. 
Ilse felt the old tightness in her chest, and before she could stop herself, she began to walk backwards when nervous, retreating step by step while her eyes remained fixed on him. 
"You will not learn by staring at the floor," she said, her voice carrying a combative tenderness that cut sharper than any blade. 
"I am not staring." Kestrel kicked a pile of glass shards, sending an afternoon shadow caught in glass skittering across the floorboards. 
Ilse knelt, her fingers hovering over the shard; inside it, a shadow seemed to breathe, independent of any body. 
"This was mine," she said. "I brought it in with me the day I arrived, convinced the world had swallowed me whole." 
She placed the shard in his palm and forced his fingers closed around it. 
"Now annotate this ledger," she ordered, sliding a leather book across the table. 
Kestrel opened it, bewildered by pages covered in precise triangles and arcs. 
Ilse poured two cups of bitter tea and instructed him to swirl the dregs until the leaves settled into patterns. 
"We do not read fortune here," she said. "We analyze the geometry of tea leaves to measure the angle of a soul's growth." 
Kestrel squinted at the cup; the leaves formed a narrow, stubborn V. 
"That means you are still pointing at your own wounds," Ilse said. "Drink. We will try again when the swing stops." 
They sat through three pourings, the combative tenderness between them hardening into a rhythm of challenge and quiet care. 
With each cup, Ilse walked a little more, sometimes forward, sometimes backwards when nervous, until her spine touched the shelves of mirrors. 
By the fourth cup, the leaves had spread into a bridge, wide and low and steady. 
"A bridge means the exile is reversible now," she said, and her hand trembled only slightly as she dipped her pen to annotate his name with a fresh date and a mark of release. 
Kestrel looked at the shard in his pocket, then at the door that led back to the playground. 
"Why do you stay?" he asked. "If it is reversible, why do you not go?" 
Ilse set down her cup, the geometry of the leaves finally resolving into a circle, endless and unbroken. 
"Because the next one always arrives," she said, "and someone must keep the ledger honest and the mirrors sharp." 
She walked backward to the window, nervous and old in the fading light, and watched the empty playground swing settle into stillness. 
Kestrel stepped through the door, but not before pressing the shard-mirror into her hand, the afternoon shadow caught in glass now a shared weight rather than a solitary chain. 
Ilse smiled, annotated the final line of his entry, and returned to her shelves, ready for the hush to break.
