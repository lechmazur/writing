The winder came down to the tidal obsidian ridge only when the clock tower held its breath. 
At the apex of the great swing, the pendulum bob hung suspended between tick and tock, and in that stillness the black glass of the ridge seemed to dream of suns it had swallowed whole. 
Kael was the most precise clock tower winder the harbor town had ever commissioned, and ethically diligent to a fault, which was why his hands shook now. 
He carried a broken rake handle, its wood salt-bleached and split, because along its length someone had carved tiny inscriptions that the tide had not erased. 
Each notch was a confession, each groove a debt, and each microscopic line a step toward what the town elders called incremental absolution. 
The sea had broken the rake years ago during the storm that took the previous keeper, but the handle had washed ashore intact, as if the ocean itself demanded the ledger be read. 
Kael had spent seven years learning to parse the marks, tying each one to a breach of duty, a withheld apology, a moment when balance had tipped toward selfishness. 
The shared balance between the town and the tidal obsidian ridge was older than the clock, a covenant that the machine's heartbeat would keep the black ridge from claiming the harbor in return for regular tribute. 
But tribute had failed, and the ridge had begun to sing a searing reverie of heat and fracture, its obsidian edges remembering the pressure that birthed them. 
Kael set his boot upon the wet glass and felt the pause deepen, a held note in the world's throat. 
He traced the first inscription, a name he did not speak aloud, and felt something emerge from the silence, not a ghost, but a correction, a hairline shift toward equilibrium. 
The second inscription spoke of a missed tide; the third, of a borrowed hour never returned. 
With each carved confession, Kael understood that absolution was not a flood that washed the soul clean, but a patient accretion, drop by drop, until the scale finally leveled. 
The pendulum bob above him was the size of a bell, cast from the ridge's own cooled wrath, and it hung now in perfect suspension, granting him the timeframe the covenant required. 
He was ethically diligent enough to know he could not lie to the wood, so he read the final inscription, the one that matched the date of his own negligence. 
It said only: I shared the weight. 
Kael closed his eyes and saw it, not his failure alone, but two pairs of hands on the rake, two keepers dividing the tide's labor, before pride had split them into solitary silos. 
The shared balance had not been broken by the sea, but by the forgetting that one keeper could hold the line without another to alternate the vigil. 
His chest burned with the searing reverie of that memory, bright and destructive, a dream of fire walking on water. 
To restore the balance, he did not need to rewrite the carvings, only to emerge from his solitary penance and carry the truth back up the cliff path. 
During the pause in the pendulum's swing, he lifted the broken handle and pressed his palm flat against the cold obsidian of the ridge. 
The tide, which had withdrawn to the horizon, trembled as if waiting for a verdict. 
Kael spoke the name from the first inscription, then the second, then the third, giving each tiny sin its incremental share of breath. 
The pendulum bob above him groaned, a deep note of release, and began to descend. 
He emerged from the pause not forgiven entirely, for the covenant allowed no sudden mercies, but weighted with the next small duty. 
The tide turned at the same instant the clock resumed its tick, and the harbor bells rang once, clear and astonished. 
Kael climbed toward the tower with the rake handle tucked beneath his arm, already composing the next inscription he would owe. 
The searing reverie faded to embers, and the tidal obsidian ridge grew dark and quiet, its shared balance restored one notch at a time.
