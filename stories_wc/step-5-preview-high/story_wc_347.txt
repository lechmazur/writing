Orin had never wanted the cursed orchard, but a curse does not ask.  For eleven years he had tended its twisted trees and gathered their small, bitter nuts.  The orchard clung to a cliffside shelf above a labyrinth of broken walls patrolled by mechanical birds.  Their wings were brass, their eyes glass, and their cries sounded like a key turning in a rusted lock.  Orin knew travelers died when night frost formed in the low paths.  He had once been lost there himself, and he had promised a dying woman he would guide others to safety.  The promise had become a curse, and the curse had become an orchard he could not leave.  He was a reluctant caretaker, offhandedly exact about distances, weather, and rot.  "Nine inches from the fourth root," he would mutter while digging, "or the graft fails."  When three strangers wandered into the maze, frost was already silvering the highest hedges.  Orin should have ignored them.  Instead he opened the hollow beneath the oldest tree and lifted out a copper tube.  Inside was a forgotten blueprint, soft as skin, showing the cliff as a machine of vents and bellows.  Its purpose was to leave impressions without touching a single stone.  Orin had thought it a joke until he found the small brass nut in his pocket, no bigger than a walnut.  It was the final fastener the blueprint required.  Someone had planted the orchard to hide the engine and had cursed him to keep it buried.  He did not care about the dead.  He cared that the engine had to be finished before the night frost formed and that he had promised to guide lost travelers to safety.  He began to assemble the engine from scrap: copper pipe, cracked leather, a bent pulley, the small nut.  His hands were quick and ungraceful, used to work nobody praised.  Mechanical birds saw motion and heat, but wind was invisible and left no warmth.  He tightened the small brass nut and felt the cliff take a breath.  Dust rose through carved channels, and a footprint formed in the grit on the paving stone.  Another followed, then an arrow, pressed into dust without a finger, without a boot, without a touch.  The strangers stopped when they saw the trail.  Orin leaned over the shelf and called down in a flat, exact voice.  "Left at the leaning stone, thirty steps, then right when the wall turns cold."  The travelers followed the impressions, and the impressions held.  A mechanical bird dove at an arrow, wings clattering, and struck nothing but dust.  It shrieked like a broken key.  Orin opened another vent.  "Do not touch the walls," he called.  "The birds read touch better than they read fear."  One traveler stumbled, and a bird swept low.  Orin turned the bent pulley, and a gust drew fresh bootprints across the path.  The bird followed them, hit the stone, and fell into the hedge like a dropped toolbox.  The others gasped; Orin did not.  "Keep high," he said.  "Frost forms first in the low places."  The orchard was ugly in the dying light, bark scarred and branches crooked.  The engine was uglier, a heap of scrap breathing in crooked time.  Yet dust moved under it like water, and the whole cliff sang in a low, broken voice.  This was the graceless beauty Orin had tried to refuse.  He never touched the travelers, and they never saw him, only the marks he left.  When they reached the lower gate, frost laced the hedge roots.  The mechanical birds settled, clicking their wings shut like books that had lost their stories.  Orin closed the last vent and sat among the bitter nuts.  He was still reluctant, still cursed, still unwilling to say love.  But he had assembled more than an engine; he had assembled a path, and the path had held.  The wind moved through the vents like a tired blessing.  In the morning there would be more travelers, and before the next frost formed, he would be waiting.
