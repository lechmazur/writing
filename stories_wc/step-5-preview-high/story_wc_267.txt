I came to the cobalt fortress under snow during nerve regeneration, carrying a case of piano tools and a question I could not ask aloud. 
The physicians had said that feeling would return to my fingers thread by thread, but they had not said what I would do with that feeling once it woke. 
I had chosen the fortress because it promised quiet, and I told myself I wanted only to heal. 
In truth, I had come to understand the cost of safety. 
The silent-choir bellhop met me at the gate, a narrow man named Andel whose breath moved steadily beneath his scarf. 
He did not speak, and I would learn that no member of his choir spoke inside the walls unless the snow itself demanded it. 
Yet his service was seraphically mundane: he warmed my gloves by the stove, aligned my metronome with the window, and set my boots so the toes pointed toward the fire. 
I followed him up a dusty spiral staircase whose blue stone had been scuffed pale by a century of careful feet. 
My room held a piano with a bench whose front leg had been mended badly, and one screw hole gaped where a piano bench bolt should have seated the joint. 
I carried a spare bolt from my old kit, and threading it into the bench gave my regenerating nerves their first honest work. 
Andel watched my fingers close around the bolt, and something in his face eased, as if a small joint in him had also been tightened. 
That night the wind pressed the fortress like a thumb on a bruise, and I could not sleep. 
I carried the bolt with me out of habit and climbed toward the chapel, where the choir supposedly kept its silence. 
On the landing I paused; a hairline seam beneath the tread betrayed itself via the slightest discoloration on a dusty spiral staircase, a smudge no wider than a thumbprint where the dust had been rubbed and blown away for years. 
I knelt and scraped the smudge with the piano bench bolt until a thin black gap appeared beneath the tread. 
The gap opened onto a service cavity, and inside lay a bundle of choir ledgers wrapped in oilcloth. 
I read by the blue light of my lamp that a junior bellhop had once seen a crack in this very staircase, had heard the first groan of the newel, and had obeyed the rule of the house by keeping still. 
He believed silence was safety, and he remained at his post while the frost worked the crack wider. 
That unpardonable silence let a girl fall three weeks later, and the choir had carried her name as a debt ever since. 
I understood then why Andel had watched my hands with such care; he was not guarding the piano, he was watching to see whether I knew the difference between quiet and complicity. 
When I found him at dawn, I did not ask if he had been the junior bellhop; I simply showed him the ledgers and the loose tread, and I let the silence stand between us until it grew unbearable. 
He took the piano bench bolt from my palm, fitted it into the seam, and turned it until the wood shrieked. 
The sound was small, but in the fortress it was a bell; boots thundered on the stairs, and the steward saw what we had found. 
By noon the masons had braced the newel, and the abbot rewrote the rule so that any witness who remained mute before danger would be expelled. 
I chose to remain at the fortress through the thaw, not because the snow made me safe, but because my hands were learning to warn before they were strong enough to save. 
Andel still did not speak in the halls, but each morning he left the staircase swept, and once he set a tuning fork beside my breakfast, its hum a frostbitten hope that spring would return. 
It was not warmth, but it was enough to begin again.
