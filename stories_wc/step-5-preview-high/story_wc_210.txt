After the last wish was granted, Paper-Lantern Cove kept its lanterns burning for no one. 
The festival boats were gone, the market stalls were folded into gull-haunted shadows, and the tide sounded like an audience leaving a hall. 
Calla Ferrow, the alchemist whom the departing crowds had deserted, stayed because she had not wished to be rescued. 
She had spent eleven years distilling remedies from kelp and rust, and she understood that a town was a compound more fragile than glass. 
In the guildhall she found an amber bottle with no opening, sealed by heat into a smooth ovoid. 
Beside it lay the guild's false comforts: cordials that softened memory, mirrors that replayed the living town, and lanterns pre-written with longings. 
Calla rejected false comfort as she would reject a poisoned solvent. 
She poured the cordials into the harbor, broke the mirrors, and burned the pre-written lanterns, but she kept the amber bottle. 
It had no spout, no crack, no seam; it could not promise escape, and it could not be opened to hide inside. 
That made it honest, and she needed an honest object while she worked. 
Her method was working memory, not scrying, not prayer, and not the old wish-trade. 
Each dawn she walked the empty lanes and rebuilt the cove in her attention, street by street. 
She began at the chandlery, where rope had once been sold by the arm's length, and moved to the tidal mill, the sardine presses, and the schoolhouse stair. 
She held the names of the departed families, the old water rights, the price of nails, and the quarrels that had hollowed the guild as load-bearing facts. 
She carried the amber bottle to the pier and set it on the rail while she recited the town's debts and debts of kindness until the sequence held without effort. 
The repetition was not nostalgia; it was scaffolding for a design that did not yet exist. 
Her motivation was not rescue but the harder work of reimagining an entire civilization that could survive after the last wish. 
By the end of the first month she had conceived the ghost town revival as a structure rather than a spell. 
There would be rotating fishery claims so no family could monopolize the cove, a tide-powered forge, and a council chosen by lot. 
There would be a school funded by lantern-makers, a granary built above the flood line, and a rule that every newcomer must teach one skill before taking one. 
She wrote these terms on the inside of new paper lanterns, not to beg the sea for favors, but to advertise work. 
Where wish-lanterns had once risen on personal longing, these became contracts printed with water rations, quotas, and repairs. 
The first returning family arrived before winter, not to be granted anything, but because the lanterns promised a share in the labor. 
Calla had already reopened the sluice with an alchemical salt cement that hardened against the tide, and the amber bottle sat in the half-built meeting hall. 
It held no last wish, no cure, and no ghost; it held the shape of a limit, and the town had begun to build inside that shape. 
She did not claim that the cove was saved, because revival was not a single moment but a maintained practice. 
She assigned the newcomers their lots, showed them the forge, and began every morning with the same disciplined walk through the streets she was still rebuilding. 
The revival spread through working memory: children memorized the street names, fishermen memorized the rotating claims, and elders recited the failures that had emptied the place. 
When another traveler came with a sealed lamp and a tale of wishes, Calla handed him a hammer and led him to the broken aqueduct. 
"We have had the last wish," she said. 
"We are making the first house." 
Years later the lanterns above Paper-Lantern Cove still rose, but they rose as signals, schedules, and invitations rather than desperate prayers. 
The amber bottle with no opening remained on the meeting-hall table, and every citizen who took office had to carry it once around the harbor without opening it. 
It was a small rite, but it taught the town's hardest lesson: a closed thing can still be useful if you reimagine what it holds. 
Calla had reimagined an entire civilization, and she had done it not by asking the world to change, but by refusing to forget exactly what it was.
