The dawn came to the tower in a meteor hush, as if the sky had swallowed its own fire and left only silence. 
Sera opened her eyes and felt the anesthesia retreating from her fingertips like a tide leaving broken shells. 
She was a closure seeker who had long ago learned that the only way to end a haunting was to reopen the wounds it left behind. 
Her latest surgery had split the old scar across her ribs, and now, awake in the quiet tower, she traced the fresh stitches with a hunger she could not name. 
The frost still clung to the hourglass on her nightstand, a globe of clouded glass that had held the same handful of gray sand for as long as she could remember. 
She had stolen it from the surgical ward three winters ago, a trophy from a near death she had survived while her twin had not. 
The doctors had called the recovery a miracle, but Sera knew it was a displaced miracle, a grace meant for another body that had settled, heavy and wrong, inside her own. 
She wanted, more than breath, to learn to read the stories in scars, to parse the language her own skin wrote whenever pain etched a line too deep to fade. 
But the words always blurred, and the closure she chased remained a rumor. 
She rose, feeling the tower breathe around her, its stones old and listening. 
Since the operation, the green things in her care had begun to speak without moving their leaves, an accidental telepathy that startled her awake at odd hours with murmurs of roots and thirst. 
Now the ferns in the east window shuddered and whispered of something beneath the flagstones, a hollow place where light had fallen asleep. 
Sera poured cold dregs into a saucer and began her usual method, examining coffee rings to find shape in chaos. 
The stains had dried in whorls and dark ellipses, but this morning they formed a spiral that pointed, unmistakably, toward the hearth. 
She knelt and pried loose a stone the plants had loosened with patient threads of root. 
Beneath it, the earth was soft, and the whispers grew louder, guiding her hands to tunnel into the cool dark beneath the tower. 
The action was instinct, a digging upward into memory, though every scoop of soil felt like descent into the tower's own buried heart. 
After an hour, her fingers struck wood, then glass, and the meteor hush of dawn seemed to lean close, holding its breath. 
She drew out a box, rotted at the seams, and inside lay a second frosted hourglass, twin to the one on her nightstand, its glass weeping with cold. 
The plants went silent, and in that silence Sera understood that the sand in her stolen hourglass was not running at all. 
The miracle had been split, half trapped in the vault of the tower, half frozen in the relic she kept, and neither could finish its fall. 
She lifted her shirt and looked at the scar the surgery had rewritten, a red river delta where the anesthesia had worn away. 
Her compulsion rose, familiar and cruel, the urge to open wounds until they told her the truth. 
But instead of taking a blade, she pressed her palm flat against her ribs and listened to the plants, who were now singing of green light and slow growth. 
The scar tingled, and for the first time she did not need to tear it open to read it; the story rose to the surface like a fish breaking still water. 
She saw her twin's last morning, the accident that had bent the miracle toward Sera's failing heart instead of her sister's. 
She saw that survival had never been theft, only misdirection, and that the grace could be returned if she carried it back to the root cellar where it belonged. 
With the tunnel still yawning behind her, Sera placed the frosted hourglass beside its twin and turned them both until the sand began to move. 
The frost melted, drop by drop, and the plants sighed in unison as the displaced miracle flooded downward through soil and root. 
Outside, the first true light of dawn spilled over the tower, and the meteor hush lifted, not with a crash, but with the soft certainty of a story finally read.
