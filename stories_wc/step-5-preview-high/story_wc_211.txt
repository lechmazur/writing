The foghorn fell silent at 4:17 AM, and the ancient orchard garden atop the skyscraper seemed to inhale. 
Master Ovid, the venerable oath scribe, stood beneath the gnarled quince trees and listened to the hush that followed. 
For sixty years he had recorded the promises spoken in this aerie, transcribing each tremor of guilt or devotion into his ledger. 
Tonight, the harbor silence told him the lower world had changed, and with it the nature of every oath yet unsworn. 
His motivation was neither nostalgia nor fear, but a professional mandate: to archive emotional patterns before the city forgot its own pulse. 
In his coat pocket he carried a single wax cylinder phonograph shaving, curled like a pale fingernail, harvested from the last recording horn in the archive. 
The shaving was not debris; it was the most sensitive medium left for capturing what voices could not hold. 
Ovid approached the stone planter at the garden's edge, where dew collected in a shallow depression shaped like a cupped hand. 
He would immerse the shaving, but immersion required more than liquid; it required a key. 
Ovid closed his eyes and reached back through the decades to a curious third-grade field trip memory. 
He had been nine years old, pressed against the safety railing while the elevator climbed forty stories to reach this impossible orchard. 
The other children had giggled at the bees, but he had been captivated by the way the old groundskeeper listened to the trees as if receiving secrets. 
That childhood curiosity had been the first hook of his vocation, and now he used it as his method. 
He let the memory flood him: the smell of wet bark, the rough wool of his class sweater, the electric wonder of standing above the clouds. 
Holding the wax shaving between his thumb and forefinger, he lowered it into the dew and immersed it fully. 
Water darkened the wax for only a heartbeat before the surface began to steam, reacting to the emotional charge he carried. 
Ovid opened his mouth and spoke in a dead language, one of the old oath-tongues that had not been heard above street level in centuries. 
The syllables were soft, yet they made the quince branches shiver though no wind passed. 
He was not casting a spell; he was simply filing an emotional pattern into the wax. 
But the dead languages did not merely describe feelings; they bound them into inevitable shape. 
As the final word left his lips, the wax cylinder phonograph shaving grew warm, and an accidental prophecy curled from its surface like smoke. 
The garden around him seemed to lean closer, the velvet disquiet of the night thickening into something that could be touched. 
In the steam, Ovid saw not a memory but a future: the orchard's roots cracking the skyscraper's crown, the tower blooming into a hill of white flowers. 
He tried to withdraw the shaving, but the dew had become a lens, and the prophecy had already been archived. 
His training told him that oaths once spoken could not be recalled, only fulfilled or broken. 
The foghorn's silence had ended one era; his careless immersion had begun another, sealed in a language no living council could rewrite. 
He set the shaving upon the oldest tree's exposed root, where it cooled into a small, white fossil. 
In the morning, when the third graders arrived on their field trip, they would find the garden changed, though none could say how. 
A boy would press his hand to the quince trunk and feel a hum that predated the city. 
A girl would taste a fig and weep for a promise she had not yet made. 
Ovid watched them come, and he understood that his archive had become a seed. 
The venerable oath scribe sat on the bench where he had once stood as a child, and he waited for the roots to finish what his voice had begun.
