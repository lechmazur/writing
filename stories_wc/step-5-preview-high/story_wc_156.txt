Mira was a vivid mural restorer who worked in the sound wave garden.  She painted dawns onto concrete walls until the pigments seemed to hum.  Her overalls carried the rainbow of her trade, and her hands smelled of turpentine and crushed mint.  The garden itself was a lattice of copper parabolic dishes and obsidian standing stones.  Sound traveled there in visible spirals, wrapping around hedges and folding back on itself.  Mira found the garden's acoustics too predictable, each echo arriving exactly when the physics promised.  She was methodically playful, arranging her palette knives in patterns that resembled musical staffs.  Her current project was the restoration of a vast fresco depicting a sleeping city.  The city would never wake in its painted state, and that certainty troubled her.  In her pocket she carried a hobo nickel, its surface carved into the relief of a top-hatted skeleton.  A traveler had pressed it into her palm years ago with instructions she had ignored until now.  She wanted to exchange certainty for curiosity, to trade the known weight of pigment for the unknown weight of echo.  So she climbed the central mound where the garden's reflector dishes converged.  She balanced the nickel on her thumbnail and flicked it toward the largest dish.  It struck the copper with a note that was not quite a chime.  The sound ricocheted off the standing stones, returning to her layered over itself.  It was then that her apprentice, Bram, called across the hedges.  He asked if she wanted tea or if she preferred to finish the sky first.  In the pause between his question and her reply, the reflected sound rewrote the garden.  Mira felt her boots leave the gravel, though she had not jumped.  She blinked and found herself standing before the sleeping-city fresco, but the paint was wet.  A second awakening: she was not the restorer but a woman in the mural, holding a brush that dripped real silver.  The coin's carving turned in her mind like a key.  A third layer opened, and she was both the painter and the pigment, watching the city yawn in unison.  Each awakening nested inside the last, tighter than the rings of the nickel's carved bone.  The reflected sound did not stop at three.  It folded the garden's acoustics into an accordion of consciousness.  Mira became the dish, the coin, the thrower, the apprentice.  She understood that Bram's question was itself a dish, waiting for an answer to strike it.  But she withheld her reply, letting the silence stretch like a canvas.  The hobo nickel carved skeleton danced on the parabolic rim, casting tiny sonic shadows.  Those shadows were the method by which she chose to bewitch the moment.  Not with smoke or mirrors, but with the honest physics of a garden built for waves.  In the fourth awakening, Mira saw the garden as it truly was: a recording device for lost questions.  The murals were not paintings but sound waves frozen in pigment, and she had spent years hiding their frequency.  Her certainty had been a varnish, thick and yellow.  Now curiosity lifted it in strips.  She was methodically playful about it, cataloging each layer as it peeled.  The fifth awakening was the simplest.  She was a vivid mural restorer again, but the colors had gained voices.  The reds hummed low, the blues whistled through copper gaps, and the golds clicked like the nickel's teeth.  Bram's kettle shrieked from the cottage, a mundane spike in the harmonic structure.  That sound entered the garden and began its own reflections, adding a sixth layer that smelled of bergamot.  Mira laughed, though the laugh arrived from three directions at once.  She looked at the hobo nickel, now returned to her palm without her noticing the journey.  She realized she had been bewitched, or perhaps she had bewitched herself, through the garden's reflected sound.  She considered her answer.  Tea first, she thought, and the sky could finish itself in her absence.  The certainty of the afternoon dissolved into the curiosity of what the echoes might paint next.  She walked back down the mound, leaving the nickel balanced on the copper dish.  It would wait there for the next question, for the next pause, for the next nested waking.  Bram stood at the gate, still holding two cups.  "Tea or sky?" he asked again, unaware that an eternity had folded between his words.  Mira smiled and took the cup, feeling the painted city breathe behind her closed eyes.  The sound wave garden held its breath, reflecting nothing, waiting for her to speak.  "Tea," she said, and the word bloomed into a thousand uncertain, curious colors.
