After the last ship sails, absence becomes a country with borders that no cartographer will admit to. 
Corin Vale had crossed into that country often, for he was a traveler who writes obituaries for the living. 
The living were the ones most likely to vanish without witnesses, leaving chairs tilted and voices unanswered in the middle of a sentence. 
They had no graves, so the survivors had nothing to tend, and grief grew wild and directionless. 
Corin gave those absences a shape, because he had learned that mourning without coordinates was a kind of starvation. 
A living obituary, he argued, was not a mockery of death; it was a document written while the subject still breathed, so that no absence could be dismissed as rumor. 
He had come to Larrow to understand the geography of healing, though he would have laughed at the phrase for its grandness. 
The palace gave him a shadowed garden in autumn, where the hedges had gone copper and the statues wore caps of wet leaves. 
It was the week after the last ship sails, and the harbor lay flat as hammered tin, holding no sail, no promise of return. 
Across the lawn lay the palace carpet, its woven harbor faded, and a single palace carpet weft thread had pulled loose from the sea. 
Sena, keeper of explained absences, waited beside it with a ledger and a pair of shears worn smooth at the handles. 
In Larrow, an absence had to be explained, or it spread through a household like damp through stone. 
Her brother had boarded the last ship with no farewell, no debt, no letter, only a damp coat left by the gate. 
Because there was no body and no word, his empty place had become a rumor that gnawed at his mother and the remaining servants. 
Corin asked for the loose thread, and Sena cut it free with a tenderness that made the bare garden seem to lean nearer. 
The palace carpet weft thread was crimson, stiff with old dye, and it smelled of salt and the feet of dancers. 
He wound it around his thumb as he wrote, so that each sentence would have a tether to the place it mourned. 
The action was intimate, not theatrical: one woman, one traveler, one thread, and the slow work of refusing to let a person dissolve. 
Corin worked by projecting presence, building the lost brother out of habits until the empty coat seemed to hold shoulders. 
His pages for the living were vividly descriptive, not because he invented glory, but because he distrusted every comfortable abstraction. 
He wrote that the brother sang off-key in the bath, chewed mint leaves before he lied, and feared staircases that ended in blank walls. 
He wrote that the last ship smelled of tar and oranges, and that the brother had once carried a stranger's bag aboard without being asked. 
Sena read the page and did not weep; she touched the wet leaves on the statue, as though confirming a sound she had heard in the hall. 
"That is him," she said, "not his ghost but his presence, explained and given a place to sit." 
She asked Corin to stitch the page into the carpet with the crimson thread, as the palace had once stitched maps into its floors. 
The thread passed through paper and wool, and the garden accepted the small defiance as if it were a prayer offered without kneeling. 
Where the page joined the woven harbor, the pattern no longer looked broken; the sea had a seam, and the seam had a name. 
Corin understood then that healing was not a distant city reached by ship, but a series of explained absences, each with a gate and a thread. 
It lay within reach in the shadowed garden, patient as rot and blossom together, waiting for someone to name the loss exactly. 
He stayed until the roads cleared, but he did not book passage on the next vessel. 
Instead he walked the coast after dark, when the water kept a lonesome splendor, gilded and abandoned, bright with everything that could not return.
