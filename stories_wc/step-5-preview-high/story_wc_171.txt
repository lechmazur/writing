The ancient terrace of Vael Mourn had been carved from mountain stone by hands that believed peace was worth stonework. 
Centuries of midsummer suns had polished its steps to a silvered patina, while moss and wild thyme softened every fracture in a display of flawed beauty. 
Tonight, during the slow dance of celestial bodies, two moons and a wandering star aligned above the ridge, turning the high plateau into a clock face marking the end of an age. 
Serane, forever war peace negotiator, climbed the final stair with the ebony clipboard held against her chest like a shield. 
She had ended seventeen ceasefires that crumbled into ash and signed nineteen treaties that outlived their ink by less than a season. 
She possessed an operatively gentle spirit, for she knew that peace could not be bullied into bloom but must be coaxed through patient, tender insistence. 
The terrace waited behind her, ancient and expectant, its weathered pillars leaning like tired elders. 
In its center lay a circle of standing stones, each one etched with the names of factions locked in a forever war that had outlasted empires. 
The ritual significance of this place demanded that enemies must come not as soldiers but as supplicants, barefoot and unarmed, to muster their intentions in open air. 
Serane opened the ebony clipboard and smoothed the parchment, feeling the weight of the price of redemption settle into her bones. 
Redemption for a generation raised on hatred was never cheap; it demanded the surrender of old grievances, the forfeiture of vengeance, and the humility to begin again. 
Below, torches flickered as the first delegation arrived, their faces hard with suspicion and their boots caked with the mud of trenches. 
She did not raise her voice. 
Instead she spoke the opening words of the midsummer rite, letting her tone remain operatively gentle, a balm against the rough edges of fear. 
One by one, the factions mustered on the terrace, each envoy stepping into the stone circle to sign or refuse the parchment that carried peace terms. 
The air grew thick with the scent of crushed herbs and the electric tension of a world holding its breath. 
When a young captain from the Eastern Reach hesitated, his hand trembling above the signature line, Serane placed a steadying palm upon the ebony clipboard. 
She told him that the price of redemption was not erasure of the past, but the courage to carry it without letting it command the future. 
Her words held no threat, only the quiet force of one who had buried too many to romanticize glory. 
Above, the moons crossed in their slow dance of celestial bodies, casting dual shadows that seemed to bow toward the terrace. 
The ritual significance of the moment thickened as the star aligned, turning the parchment into something more than paper. 
It became a threshold, and through ritual significance the terrace transformed into neutral ground where ideals could be anchored in practice rather than drowned in speeches. 
That had always been Serane's motivation: to anchor ideals in practice, to make mercy operational instead of ornamental. 
A scarred general from the western reaches spat into the dust, then signed, his pen stroke rough and unwilling. 
The clipboard recorded each mark, dark wood drinking ink and sweat and hesitation. 
Redemption, Serane understood, was flawed beauty made manifest; it would not gleam like new bronze, but it would hold. 
When the final envoy pressed his thumb to the wax seal, a hush fell across the ancient terrace at midsummer. 
The forever war did not end in trumpet blasts. 
It ended in the scratch of quills, the creak of knees bending to sit on cold stone, and the soft rustle of a peace negotiator closing an ebony clipboard. 
The price had been paid not in blood, but in pride, in memory, in the terrible willingness to be gentle when the world expected cruelty. 
As the celestial bodies drifted apart in their slow dance, Serane descended the steps, leaving the terrace to moss and moonlight.
