The rainforest had burned away three monsoons past, and the world had settled into a chronic hush that no throat could break. 
In the coastal flats, where the tide had swallowed an orchard whole, the echo-harbor violinist came looking for what had been left unfinished. 
She was acoustically perceptive by nature and by trade, able to hear the minute creak of a seed inside its shell or the slow grind of rust beneath the waves. 
Now her ears ached with the emptiness that the fires had left, a silence so thick it felt like wool pressed against her ears. 
The sunken orchard greenhouse rose from the brine like a cracked pearl, its panes filmed with salt and its ribs girdled by the black hulls of shipwrecks. 
Inside, the air smelled of wet loam and charred timber, and in the center stood a single pear tree wrapped from root to limb in a ship's rotten rope. 
The tree held no leaves, only buds that had stopped mid-opening, and she understood at once that the chronic hush had frozen its song in place. 
She set down her case and, among the drifted ash, found a mendicant's bowl, simple clay, one side chipped, as if some beggar had laid it down and walked into the sea. 
She filled it with rainwater from the broken gutter, then gathered driftwood from the wrecks to make a bonfire in the greenhouse's dry corner. 
It was her family tradition to tell stories around a bonfire, and though she sat alone, she kept the rite, speaking each tale into the dark to honor what was incomplete. 
She spoke of her mother's unfinished quilt, of her brother's half-learned boat song, of the orchard itself, which had never borne a winter crop. 
With each story, she placed a smooth stone into the mendicant's bowl, and the clay rang faintly, a humble bell that the chronic hush could not wholly swallow. 
Then she took out her violin, and because she was acoustically perceptive, she heard what the fire and the stones and the salt had been hiding. 
The pear tree was not dead; it was bound, and its roots were singing a low note that waited for a higher voice to release it. 
She set the mendicant's bowl against the trunk, its chipped lip touching the bark, and drew the bow across the strings in a long, slow phrase. 
The wood of the bowl caught the vibration and carried it down into the roots, and the chronic hush began to thin like fog before dawn. 
Now she moved to the rope, and with careful hands she began to unbind the kelp-hard fibers from the trunk, loosening each knot as the music rose. 
The rope fell away in coils, and the tree shivered, and one bud opened into a white blossom that held its color in the quiet air. 
It was not a symphony, not a full harvest, not the end of the fires' aftermath, yet in that single flower she found the beauty she had sought in the unfinished. 
She kept the fire burning, and she kept the bowl, and she kept telling her stories to the dark, because a thing left incomplete is only a thing still becoming. 
Outside, the shipwrecks stood like gray saints in the rising tide, and the salt wind carried the scent of rain that might, one day, cool the burned earth. 
She played until her fingers ached, letting the notes settle into the greenhouse glass, where they hung like dew, small and bright and enough. 
In the chronic hush, one blossom was a roar, and she, the echo-harbor violinist, had learned at last to listen forward.
