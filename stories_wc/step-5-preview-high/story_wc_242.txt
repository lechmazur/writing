The phone booth sat between the third and fourth rails, where the subway utility tunnel sweated mineral tears onto rusted tile. 
He had claimed the space years ago, when the city planners forgot the corridor and the maintenance crews stopped asking questions. 
His was the only door that still had glass, and behind that glass, the receiver waited for wrong numbers. 
Between the hum of streetlamps, in that narrow hour when the tunnels fell silent and the sodium glow pressed through the gratings above, he opened for business. 
The operator did not sell calls; he cultivated estrangement. 
He believed that souls starved for voices needed distance more than proximity, and that the purest intimacy lived in the space where two wrong numbers met and never touched. 
On the bench beside him rested a glass harmonica disc, its polished rim fogged by the damp, its center threaded onto a spindle that he turned with a wet thumb. 
The disc sang a thin, crystalline note that seemed to drip upward, and the operator was melodically haunted by its residue, a frequency that clung to his ribs and reminded him of conversations he had never had. 
When a line rang, he did not answer; he spliced it. 
The copper veins of the tunnel carried the signal away from its intended ear and deposited it into another receiver, somewhere across the city. 
Two strangers met in the dark, each convinced the other was a mistake, and each grateful for it. 
They spoke of weather, of lovers, of the particular geometry of their ceilings. 
The operator listened without hearing, his ear pressed to the cold metal of the box, his fingers tracing the rim of the harmonica disc. 
Then, at the precise moment when the wrong number friendship threatened to become real, he ended it. 
He lifted the receiver, let the connection dissolve into a hiss, and returned the voice to the city. 
But an ending, by itself, is only a slammed door. 
So he performed the action that kept the tunnel alive, the loop of farewell threaded back into greeting. 
The glass harmonica disc caught the rhythm of his work, each revolution etching the same circular melody, a loop that turned finality into a doorway. 
The operator's motivation was not cruelty; it was pedagogy. 
To teach endings how to begin, he had to show them their own shape, the way a period sits at the tail of a sentence only because a hand chooses to place it there. 
A wrong number was the perfect student. 
It arrived by accident, hungered for meaning, and accepted its termination with the same grace as its greeting. 
The operator had been one of them once, long ago, before the tunnels, when he had dialed a number he meant to dial and reached a voice he was not meant to keep. 
That voice had been his own, recorded on a dead line, and since then he had cultivated the distance that let him live. 
The streetlamps above changed their pitch, humming lower as the night deepened. 
A fresh line rang. 
He spun the disc, melodically haunted by the tone that was not quite grief and not quite welcome, and he sent the call spiraling into the dark toward another mistake. 
Two people began again because he had ended something else. 
Between the hum of streetlamps, the subway utility tunnel became a throat, clearing itself to speak. 
The operator sat in the loop, turning the disc, ensuring that every goodbye learned its alphabet. 
His coat was the color of oxidized iron, and his eyes reflected the amber streetlamp hum like two dying embers. 
A wrong number friendship, the one between a baker and a night watchman, had lasted nine minutes and forty seconds. 
He allowed it to breathe until the watchman laughed, then he cut the cord and folded the laugh into the next dial tone. 
The baker never knew he had almost said goodbye; the watchman never knew he had almost cried. 
They would call again, perhaps, and the operator would let them meet, briefly, in the estrangement he tended like a garden. 
The glass harmonica disc did not tire, nor did the loop of its melody tire of beginning. 
Above ground, the streetlamps held their note, a single sustained chord against the pavement. 
In the tunnel, the operator turned the spindle, and the copper wires drank the sound, carrying it to a high apartment where a woman held a phone she had not meant to pick up. 
She heard a hum, then a voice that was not the voice she wanted, and she said, "Sorry, wrong number." The operator smiled, for the phrase was an ending shaped like a door he was there to teach how to open.
