The mountain ranger cabin smelled of pine resin, wet wool, and the dust of a man who had outlived his own future.  After seeing my own grave at the low stone wall, I had spent the morning reading a name half-eaten by lichen and weather.  No one in the valley had explained how a lucid dream chronicler was supposed to file that.  I had been paid, once, to walk inside other people's sleeping minds and return with orderly accounts of their chaos.  Now the job was private, and the narrative refused to sit still.  Inside the cabin, the birch bark scroll lay across the desk like a pale animal pelt, its curled edges holding a decade of dreams.  I had peeled it from a lightning-struck tree behind the ranger station the week before I buried my father, or maybe the week after.  I am chronologically confused these days; grief and lucidity have tangled the order of things until the past feels present and the future feels recently witnessed.  It is a useful affliction for the work, and a rotten one for the soul.  I unrolled the scroll and found handwriting I did not remember producing.  The ink described a cabin exactly like this one, a grave exactly like the one I had seen, and a chronicler who planned to soar above the ridge at dusk.  The sentence ended mid-word, as if the writer had been pulled upward.  Jealousy arrived first, as it often does, sharp and unwelcome.  I imagined the selves who had written those lines, younger and braver, untouched by the arithmetic of graveyards.  They had still believed that dreams were frontiers rather than repeat visits to the same wounded room.  Getting jealous of past selves is a method I would not recommend to anyone who values sleep, but it opened doors that discipline had long since locked.  Their certainty was a key; their ignorance was a stair.  I lay down on the bunk with the scroll against my chest and let the cabin's silence turn liquid.  The dream rose with the smell of thawing mud and cut grass, and I knew, with the jaded ease of a man who has visited his own ending, exactly where I was.  Below me stretched the valley as it had been before the fire, before the hospital, before the name on the stone.  Feral nostalgia clawed up through my ribs, not gentle, not decorative, but hungry and furred and insistent.  It wanted me to run back into the scenes I had survived and lie down in them until they accepted me again.  I refused, because refusal was the only authority I had left.  Instead, I lifted my arms, felt the air take them, and began to soar.  The ridge bent beneath me like an old spine, and the cabin shrank to a scribbled mark on birch.  From that height, I saw the grave again, not as a conclusion but as punctuation in a paragraph that had been badly broken into lines.  I understood then why I had been sent to the cabin, or why I had sent myself.  The world had begun to arrive out of order, and someone had to distill chaos into narrative before it hardened into rumor.  That was my motivation, thin and stubborn as winter light: not to fix the sequence, but to make it bearable and true.  I descended into sleep with my hands moving, writing on the birch bark scroll in a script that predated my doubt.  When I woke, the cabin was blue with dusk, and the scroll held three new pages, one of them stained with soil I did not recognize.  The writing ended with a date that was, by every calendar I trusted, still nine years away.  I should have been afraid, and perhaps in some unrecorded hour I had been.  Instead I felt the old jaded wonder rise, a tired awe at how neatly ruin could arrange itself into story.  I sharpened my pencil, rolled the scroll tighter, and walked out to meet the grave as a beginning.
