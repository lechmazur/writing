The leash had held him for eleven months, and when it finally snapped at the ridgeway marker, Elian stood very still. 
The rope had been thick hemp, frayed by salt and guilt, and its broken end dangled like a question he could not yet answer. 
He had come to the shore to scatter his sister's ashes, but the wind had carried them inland instead, toward the veiled ridgeway that climbed above the water. 
The villagers had warned him not to traverse the ridge after dusk, for the path was old and the fog was thicker than memory. 
But Elian had a motive that burned softly beneath his ribs: to find where unused courage waits. 
He believed his sister had hidden hers there, tucked into some quiet cleft of stone, the way she used to hide river stones in her pockets. 
As twilight deepened into a blue hush, he began the climb, and the shoreline fell away beneath him like a lullaby half-remembered. 
The veiled ridgeway unfolded in switchbacks, each turn hiding the next, as if the mountain itself preferred indirect routes to honesty. 
Three times he passed the same standing stone, and three times the mist rearranged the trail so that forward and circle became the same direction. 
He was tired when he heard the oars, though there was no river near the summit, only the rhythmic dip of wood against water he could not see. 
Around a bend he found a landing where two figures waited beside a flat-bottomed skiff that rested on the stone as easily as on a lake. 
They were the wise ferry guides, robed in gray wool that smelled of rain, and their eyes held the patient shine of lanterns seen across dark water. 
"You are late for the vigil," said the taller one, not unkindly, and Elian realized the sun had vanished and the first star had pierced the veil. 
"I did not know there was a vigil," Elian replied, and his voice sounded small against the hush of the ridge. 
"There is always a vigil in twilight," said the shorter guide, stepping forward with a small leather satchel, "for those who come to watch what cannot be rushed." 
She produced a ticket punch with a star stamp, its metal worn smooth by decades of marking passage, and she looked at Elian with a gently persistent gaze. 
"The direct road is closed to mourners," she said, "but the indirect routes remain open if you are willing to be carried sideways." 
Elian nodded, and the guides helped him into the skiff, which then slid along a seam of moisture that beaded on the rock, neither wholly water nor wholly path. 
They traversed the summit by shorelines that existed only in twilight, lapping against slopes of fern and granite with a sound like slow breathing. 
The ticket punch clicked against a slip of birch bark, leaving a star stamp that glowed faintly, and with each mark Elian felt the weight of the broken leash lighten. 
"You seek unused courage," the taller guide said, rowing with slow strokes, "but courage does not wait in treasure boxes." 
"It waits where you refuse to look," the shorter added, "because looking means remembering why you stopped." 
The skiff moved through a channel of low cloud, and Elian saw other travelers keeping the vigil, seated in a circle of stones, faces calm and unhurried. 
They were not ghosts but people between griefs, holding lanterns that did not flicker, watching the horizon with the same gently persistent hope his sister had carried. 
In the center of the circle sat a child no older than seven, clutching a leash whose other end lay empty on the grass, and Elian understood. 
The vigil was not for the dead but for the living who had forgotten that broken tethers were merely invitations to walk beside rather than behind. 
He took the offered seat, and the guides pressed the star stamp into his palm, the metal now warm, before they poled the skiff back into the mist. 
"Do not keep it," they called, their voices blending into the shoreline lullaby. "It only marks the way you choose." 
Elian sat through the deep blue hours, listening to the ridge hum, and slowly he unpacked his grief like a sail catching wind. 
The child beside him finally released the frayed leash, and together they walked to the ridge edge where the true trail opened, winding and clear. 
He did not find his courage, for he had been carrying it all along, folded small and waiting, like a ticket yet unstamped. 
But now he knew its shape, and at dawn he traversed the ridgeway home by routes he had once feared, no longer tethered but choosing the shore. 
Below, the ferry guides sang on, stamping stars for other wanderers.
