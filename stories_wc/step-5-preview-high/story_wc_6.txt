Maren climbed the dune at dawn, when the clouds began their migration inland, and her boots sank into black sand from the volcanic beach below. 
She was an arsonist's daughter and a firefighter, and the two truths had long ago fused into a single, quiet purpose. 
The obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes waited in the hollow, its surface still and dark as a struck match. 
Kelp drifted in the saltwater channels, long ribbons tossed by the tide that reached through a breach in the dunes. 
She moved gracefully alert, reading the wind and the wet obsidian with equal care, each step measured to keep from slipping. 
Maren knelt at the lagoon's edge and opened a leather satchel, drawing out a foreign coin that had been drilled and strung on red thread. 
The coin had belonged to her father, a man who had once set the great dune fires for sport and profit. 
She had drilled a hole through its center herself and tied it with red thread so it would hang against her heart as a debt she refused to bury. 
Today, during the migration of clouds, the humidity rose high enough for her work. 
She took black sand from the volcanic beach and packed it around the scorched roots of a dune oak that fire had claimed five years ago. 
Then she grafted a living shoot from a shore pine onto the charred stump, binding the join with red thread until the cambium met cambium. 
Her compassion was unyielding, not soft; it demanded that she return to this scar again and again until it could breathe. 
The clouds rolled overhead now, grey and pregnant, casting moving shadows across the lagoon. 
Maren worked through the morning, gracefully alert to every shift in the tide and temperature. 
She noticed a scorched hare trail and laid a bridge of driftwood across the obsidian so small feet could cross. 
She bound the graft with red thread, and the foreign coin swung from her throat, brushing the blackened bark like a small bell. 
It clicked softly as the wind moved it, a copper note of memory. 
She remembered her father's hands, how they had cupped flame, and she remembered her own, which now carried water and saplings. 
Her motivation had never been revenge but music. 
She wanted to prove that scars can sing. 
The kelp drifted in the lagoon, rustling like slow applause beneath the cloud migration. 
She placed black sand from the volcanic beach into each cleft of the obsidian, amending the sharp glassy edges with fertile grit. 
By noon, the lagoon mirrored the sky, and a kelp-drift peace settled over her, rocking her like a cradle. 
Maren sat back on her heels and listened. 
The black sand in her satchel was almost gone, and she scattered the rest across the dune like a blessing. 
Wind combed the dunes; the grafted pine needles hissed; the kelp sighed against the rocks; the coin chimed. 
These were the notes her father had tried to silence, and she had taught them to sound again. 
She was not the fire he had been, but the firefighter who followed, not to erase the burn but to tend what survived. 
The scars of the land and the scars of her inheritance began to harmonize, low and sure. 
A single cloud broke, and a thread of rain touched her face like a benediction. 
She tucked the red thread and coin inside her jacket, feeling the foreign coin cool against her chest. 
Then she turned from the obsidian lagoon and walked home through the dunes, leaving the kelp to drift in peace. 
In the distance, the cloud migration continued its slow and patient journey, carrying the song far out to sea.
