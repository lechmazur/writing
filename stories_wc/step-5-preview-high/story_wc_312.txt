The dawn came grey and then blue, and with the light the sapphire viaduct woke to its only sound, which was dripping. 
Kael sat near the eastern arch with the broken ship's bell between his knees, turning it so the cracked lip faced the rising sun. 
He was called a mage, though his talents had always run lukewarm, more steam than flame, more puddle than tide. 
In a city where sorcerers bent storms and spoke with comets, Kael could barely warm tea without spilling it. 
Yet after the last fish was caught from the harbor below, the Council of Currents had declared the sea finished, the trade dead, and the cosmic ledger unbalanced. 
They said the ocean had given everything and now demanded a return, but Kael, earnestly naive in the way that only decent people can be, believed the debt might be paid with a single honest note. 
The bell had come from the trawler Marisol, the last boat to return with empty holds and a torn sail. 
Its bronze was green with salt, and a fracture ran from the crown to the rim, splitting the tongue of the ship's name. 
Kael had found it in the scrap market, priced as metal, but he recognized it as a lung, an organ that once breathed the hours for sailors who trusted the tide. 
The viaduct itself was the last structure built when the water was high, a bridge of blue stone that carried freight trains to the cliffs, and its arches wept mineral water that tasted of iron and old prayers. 
This water, Kael reasoned, was the only music left in the harbor. 
If he could understand its dripping rates, the intervals at which the viaduct mourned for the sea, he could tune the bell and rebuild what the fracture had taken. 
So he began at dawn, as was proper, using a tin cup and a notched stick to measure the rhythm. 
One drop every four seconds from the north gutter, one every seven from the south, and a hesitation every third minute that sounded like a sigh held underwater. 
The math was not magic, but Kael knew that magic, when honest, was only the mathematics of things that had not been counted yet. 
He worked with a jeweler's loupe and a brazier, heating bronze strips cut from the viaduct's own railings, which he suspected the bridge would not miss. 
His devotion was brittle; it had cracked the first night when a gull mocked him, and again when he realized the bell might simply be a bell. 
But each morning he returned, because the alternative was the Council's plan, which involved sacrificing the last lighthouse keeper to a geometry of storm clouds. 
Kael could not save the fish, but perhaps he could remind the water that ships had once sung to it. 
On the sixth dawn, he fitted the final patch, a curved plate hammered from the railings, and lifted the bell onto the viaduct's central parapet. 
The sapphire stones beneath his boots hummed with the approaching sun, and the harbor below lay flat as a coin, empty of masts. 
Kael waited for the north gutter to drip, counting the four seconds, then struck the bell's rim with a mallet wrapped in silk. 
The sound was ugly at first, a clank that scared the pigeons. 
He adjusted the patch by a hair's width, waited for the seven-second drip from the south, and struck again. 
This time the bell spoke, low and round, a note that traveled down the blue arches and struck the water without disturbing it. 
It was the frequency of a tide returning, though no tide moved, and somewhere in the deep trench beyond the reef, the first new fish since the last catch turned toward the sound. 
Kael did not see it, for he was weeping into the bell's green shoulder, his lukewarm hands shaking against the bronze. 
He had not healed the sea; the Council would say the imbalance remained, the ledger still bled red. 
But the dripping had found its match, and the ship's bell had been rebuilt into something that could hold a note without breaking. 
It was impossible, and it was plausible, and in the blue dawn of the sapphire viaduct, that was enough.
