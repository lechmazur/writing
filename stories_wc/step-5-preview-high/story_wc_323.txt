In the benthic city of Pelag Deep, the pressure never spoke of stars. 
Kessa Arno, a navigation instructor in the lower terraces, had spent twenty years mapping darkness by sonar. 
She taught her students to trust pressure ridges, thermal vents, and the stubborn geometry of the trench. 
Then, four months after discovering aliens drifting beyond the Jovian moons, the city council ordered every instructor to expand the old charts. 
The aliens had not conquered or spoken; they had simply left a lattice of glass beacons above the waterline, humming with coordinates. 
From the depths those beacons were invisible, but their signals fell through the sea like a slow tide of numbers. 
The council wanted navigators who could read that tide. 
Kessa, balancedly curious even at forty-six, did not dismiss the task, but she refused to abandon the dark she knew. 
In her coat she carried a boat anchor chain link worn smooth by the Atlantic, her grandfather's proof that human hands had once pulled up iron from drowned ships. 
When her first class of alien-era students arrived, she set the link on the table like a gavel. 
You have been born to folded ceilings, she said, and you call them sky. 
I will teach you unfolded horizons. 
The oldest student, Ren, laughed without cruelty and asked how stars could matter where no star had ever been seen. 
Kessa hung the chain link on a wire above a relief model of the benthic city and let it swing until it stilled. 
Edge geometry begins here, she said, tapping the shelf where the abyss falls away. 
A sailor once read distance from the line between sea and sky, and we will read distance from the line between shelf and void. 
She drew the old equations on slate, then let the alien signal rewrite them into arcs and altitude. 
The method demanded patience, not faith, and Kessa was exacting in her somber marvel at what the numbers promised. 
At midnight in city-time, she led the class to the outer dome, where water pressed against two meters of transparent alloy. 
No lantern could show them the surface, but the beacons marked the water with faint ripples when their pulses passed. 
Stand here, she told Ren, and remember that every horizon is a curve you have not yet walked. 
Ren touched the cold alloy and whispered that the ocean had felt like the whole world until now. 
Kessa placed the chain link in Ren's palm, closing the fingers around it. 
My grandfather used this to hold against storms, she said, and now I give it to you to hold against wonder. 
In the weeks that followed, the charts were not merely corrected but expanded outward in rings of alien trigonometry and abyssal soundings. 
The council grew greedy for passage to the surface, but Kessa insisted that starlight navigation was a discipline, not a ladder. 
Before they could claim that skill, they had to learn to navigate by starlight as a form of listening. 
She taught them to calculate the altitude of a beacon from the way its signal bent along the shelf edge. 
She taught them to fold those altitudes into a single imaginary dome until the floor of the sea became a ceiling of light. 
In that inversion, the benthic city was no longer a grave of continents but a ship without water. 
Ren returned after one gray week with numbed hands and a salvaged fragment of the beacon glass. 
He had climbed the shelf to the old wreck line, and there, tangled in the ribs of a freighter, he had found the source of Kessa's chain. 
The link had been only one of hundreds, and its foundry number matched the first coordinate the alien beacons had sent. 
Kessa felt the old floor of her grief tilt, and she understood that her grandfather's ocean had been a shallow beginning. 
She did not weep; her curiosity had always been balanced, and awe was not a flood but a tide to be measured. 
When the first starlight-guided vessel left the benthic city, Kessa stood at the edge of the launch shelf and watched its lights expand into the dark. 
She carried no instrument, only the empty space where the chain link had hung. 
Behind her, students sang the low equations of edge geometry, and ahead the water held the sea to its ancient silence. 
In that silence, Kessa felt the somber marvel of a species that had learned to look upward from the bottom of the world.
