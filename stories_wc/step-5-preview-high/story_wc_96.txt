The rusted library under the stars had been a maximum-security prison once, and even now its shelves exhaled a breath of iron and rotted paper. 
Homeless since the textile mills rusted shut, the vagrant penny poet had slept in doorways and municipal archives, composing couplets for spare change on wind-scoured corners. 
Inside, as the moon cleared the skyline, the poet worked by swinging lantern light, sorting salvaged books with Wren, a thin inmate assigned to library duty. 
Their friendship had sparked three weeks earlier among toppled legal encyclopedias, two pairs of hands reaching for the same cracked volume of botany and finding, instead, a shared silence that did not require bars. 
But the poet bore a secret ache, an attribute that compelled them to molt personalities monthly, so that yesterday's laughter became a stranger's echo and last week's grief an unfamiliar room. 
This month's shell was the Builder, a temperament obsessed with permanence, terrified that next month would scatter these fragile hours like ash across the wind. 
Wren slid a water-stained atlas across the folding table, and beneath its warped cover they discovered a coffee tin with a secret false bottom, its lid crusted green with age. 
Inside lay a folded manifesto and a twist of dried poppy seeds, scrawled with words that tasted of blooming resistance and midnight gardens planted behind concrete. 
The note described a luminous fracture in the carceral order, how inmates had once sown night-blooming flowers through the execution yard, refusing to let the state own their wonder. 
The Builder's hands trembled, recognizing a mirror, for the poet had always wandered the alleys chanting penny verses, desperate to forge beauty from anarchy. 
They decided to rebuild the rusted library before the demolition crews arrived in spring, using the prison's dying bones as a greenhouse for the forgotten neighborhood. 
Wren, due for parole in three weeks, agreed to smuggle out salvaged nails and hinges while the poet traded penny poems for lumber beyond the gates. 
Each night, as the moon cleared the skyline, they labored beneath constellations that watched through the collapsed dome, breath misting in the cold. 
The rusted library became their cathedral, its broken ribs strung with paper lanterns that swung like patient, low-hanging stars. 
They cleared wet rot, stacked cinder blocks into reading nooks, and lined the eastern windows with soil smuggled from the old prison garden. 
In the coffee tin's false bottom, they hid the remaining seeds, treating it as a reliquary that anchored their fragile alliance across the poet's coming molt. 
The poet wrote verses on reclaimed book plates and sold them for pennies to buy hinges and glass, each stanza a brick, each rhyme a roof beam. 
Wren taught the poet how to sort water-damaged volumes by scent rather than sight, a skill learned from the prison library's deeper archives. 
In return, the poet read aloud until the guards forgot to check the yard, their friendship a quiet insurrection stitched from shared margins and stolen glances. 
When the full moon cleared the skyline on the final night, the first poppies cracked through the execution yard soil in defiant, burning orange. 
The demolition crew arrived at dawn, but found the rusted library transformed, with shelves blooming with flowers, children from the slums reading on hay bales, and steam rising from a coffee can of stolen pencils. 
The blooming resistance had spread, roots threading under the fence, neighbors arriving with shovels and seed jars, hungry for something that did not demand payment. 
The poet felt the familiar dizzy molt begin, the Builder's purpose dissolving into a new, unshaped self that did not yet know the weight of brick or bloom. 
Wren pressed the coffee tin into the poet's pocket, whispering that the false bottom now held a fresh seed and an address written in permanent ink. 
As the poet wandered into the luminous fracture of morning, they no longer knew their own name, but their feet remembered the path home. 
Next month, another personality would awaken in a different skin, yet the rebuilt library would remain, a testament that beauty could be forged from anarchy and bloom without permission.
