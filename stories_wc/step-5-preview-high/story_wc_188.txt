Kestrel was a wandering fireworks designer who arrived at the blooming fortress at dusk, when the vines were opening their pale throats. 
The night had swallowed every star, and the hush pressed against the ramparts like a held breath. 
He had come to defy the gravity of hopelessness, and he believed defiance was best written in light. 
His pack held papers of black powder designs, brass gauges, and a small pouch of colored stars. 
As he walked the outer causeway, Kestrel chanted while walking, marking the rhythm of his boots against the stones. 
The chant was not prayer; it was a map of pressure, fuse length, and the exact heartbeat before a bloom of fire. 
Beyond the gate, the fortress was literally blooming, its battlements tangled with night flowers that glowed faintly in the darkening air. 
No lamps burned, for the hush of a night with no stars had taught the garrison to conserve even the smallest courage. 
Kestrel filtered the silence through ambient awareness, listening past his own chant to what the fortress was trying to say. 
He filtered the scents too, letting the perfume of the blooms fall away until he caught the faint mineral bite of old kerosene. 
It led him to a service stair behind a cracked mosaic of a winged drum. 
There, set into the floor, a note in mosaic waited in cobalt and tarnished silver, each tile a letter a pilgrim might miss. 
He crouched, and his chant dropped to a hum so his breath would not disturb the message. 
The note in mosaic told of a sealed gallery above the eastern tower where the fortress once kept festival shells. 
It also warned that the fuse-maker's kit had been hidden in a fire spinner's kerosene rag knot, knotted so tight it resembled a stone. 
Kestrel smiled with serious playfulness, because a puzzle made of glass and ash was exactly the kind of grief he understood. 
He climbed through corridors where petals drifted like slow snow and the dark seemed to lean on his shoulders. 
In a niche behind a broken trebuchet, he found the knot, stiff with old fuel and dust. 
He worked the kerosene rag knot loose with patient fingers, preserving the dry center where flame could still take hold. 
Then he carried his find to the eastern tower and began the real work of arranging shells by color and complaint. 
The garrison watched in silence, their faces washed in the faint bioluminescence of the blooming walls. 
They had been told that light was wasteful, that the no-star sky was permanent, and that hope was a heaviness they should set down. 
Kestrel answered them with a grin that did not hide its seriousness, saying that gravity was easier to defy with a little fire beneath it. 
He lit the rag knot from a shielded coal and let it flare, then fed its honest orange flame to the first fuse. 
The shell rose with a cough, then a roar, and burst into a wheel of crimson stars that outshone the empty sky. 
A second shell followed, green and gold, sketching the shape of a door where none had stood. 
The flowers on the fortress walls seemed to lean toward the light, and the hush began to crack like frost in a thaw. 
People came out of the barracks, not running, but stepping carefully as if the ground might vanish if they moved too joyfully. 
Kestrel kept chanting while walking along the ramparts, now in a brighter meter, his voice mixing with the thunder of the sky. 
Each time a shell opened, he filtered the crowd's reaction through ambient awareness, tuning the next charge to their held breaths. 
When a child laughed, he gave her a lavender star; when an old soldier wept, he gave him a slow silver rain. 
The note in mosaic had promised a gallery, but it had not promised that the gallery would become a sky. 
Yet as smoke drifted through the blossoms, the fortress looked less like a prison of dusk and more like a lantern with walls. 
By midnight the air smelled of sulfur and nectar, a serious perfume for a city learning to play again. 
Kestrel shouldered his empty pack, leaving the burned rag knot behind as a relic on the eastern tower. 
He walked out through the gate still chanting, his footsteps light, while behind him the fortress bloomed in borrowed starlight. 
He knew the darkness would return, but he also knew that hopelessness could be pushed against, one bright breach at a time. 
The wandering fireworks designer disappeared down the road, and the people kept the sparks of his argument alive in their chests.
