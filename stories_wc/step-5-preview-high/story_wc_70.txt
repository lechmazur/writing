The city had been dark for six hours after the city-wide blackout, and the only working clock was the storm-charge meter at the lightning collection tower. 
Doctors were counting battery hours, bakers were throwing dough into alleys, and the council had already sent three engineers who returned with nothing but burnt gloves. 
At nectar dusk the valley smelled of ozone and bruised blossoms, as if the air had been strained through honey. 
I climbed the switchback road to the tower's lower landing, where a candid toll keeper sat on a stool beside her turnstile and demanded payment. 
She was involuntarily truthful, a condition the toll office valued and feared, so I asked no questions I did not want answered. 
I asked whether the drone beehive failure in the tower's crown had been mechanical. 
She meant to say yes, but the words came out as the exact truth instead: mechanical failure was only the story she was paid to tell. 
Before I could speak, she continued, compelled by whatever flaw had made her candid: the hive had not broken; its drones had stopped making patterns because their work had been reduced to maintenance and numbers. 
They had been denied the strange, small surprises that taught them where nectar dusk ended and storm-light began. 
I had spent my life among grids and dials, but I recognized starvation when I heard it, even in machines. 
I told her the city needed power, not poetry, and she opened a lacquer box on her lap with the care of someone revealing a relic. 
Inside, wrapped in red felt, lay a matchbox containing a cryptic riddle. 
"This is the toll," she said. "It has kept this gate from becoming a door for eleven years." 
Her motive, she admitted, was to preserve wonder in routine, and the matchbox was her method: every climber had to solve one riddle before touching the tower's machinery. 
I read the slip of paper: I am the spark that turns repetition into a road, eaten by certainty, born again by questions; keep me and I die. 
The words were ordinary, yet they caught in my chest like a hook, and I understood that the tower had been built to catch more than electricity. 
I answered, "Wonder." 
The toll keeper flinched as if a current had passed through her, and she stepped aside to reveal the spiral stair that led into the tower's throat. 
"Then you already know what to do," she said. 
Inside, the dead drone beehive hung in resin combs above the collection coils, its metal bees still as hail. 
I placed the riddle slip into the queen socket and spoke the answer again, and the coils began to hum. 
The combs began to glow amber, each cell ringing with stored storm-light, and the drones moved in the spiral of a dance older than any grid. 
One by one the drones woke, their wings stitching a lattice of light that poured down the copper throat and spilled into the city below. 
The toll keeper's voice followed me up the stairs, involuntarily honest even in triumph. 
"You restored the current," she called, "but I restored the reason it mattered." 
At the gate below, I saw her write the riddle into her ledger with the same steady hand she used for coins, preserving the next stranger's chance to be astonished. 
As the first lamps lit, I looked back at her lacquer box and matchbox, small objects that had kept a city's wonder alive at a toll gate. 
That night the tower drank lightning like nectar, and the dusk was sweet enough to remember. 
The honeyed dark settled over the valley, and for the first time since the failure, the routine of the city felt like a gift rather than a grind.
