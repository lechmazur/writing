The cool-headed fisher came to the sound wave garden where other men screamed. 
Its reeds were tuned by tides, and its paths rang underfoot like strings. 
Tonight was the hour when patterns break themselves, and every repeated shape in the world came loose. 
The sleepwalking constellations had slid down from their charts, dragging comets through the reeds like torn ribbons. 
He had not come for fish. 
He had come to catch the echo of a goddess who sang to restless stars. 
Years ago his shaking hands had lost a lullaby he could not finish, and he had spent a decade learning stillness. 
In his belt he carried a rail spike, black with salt and older than the railway that had died beneath the garden. 
The old tracks still ran through the soil, and the spike could speak to them without tearing the reeds apart. 
He carried an old attribute, for he transliterates emotions into pitch, weight, and pause. 
Grief became a low struck note. 
Fear became a rasping chord. 
Hope became a clean hollow tone. 
The garden heard these things and answered in a feral lullaby, half tenderness and half hunger. 
He walked into the resonance beds, where each footstep printed a visible ring in the dust. 
The constellations moved in a drunken zigzag above him, and the reeds leaned after their light. 
He knew that force would only make the wandering stars stumble, so he began to zigzag between the beds himself. 
He struck the rail spike against the buried rail and let the vibration rise through the soles of his feet. 
The note went out like a hand laid on a wild shoulder. 
One constellation paused, lowering a haze of fire until the reeds smoked. 
The fisher transliterated his own terror into a narrow interval and buried it beneath his breath. 
He could not call the goddess with music, for music was only a door. 
He had to reach her by voicing silence, by giving absence a shape she could follow. 
So he hammered a phrase and then stopped where a note should have been. 
The pause was not empty; he spoke it with the precision of a name. 
Another pause followed, longer, hung in the air until the comets slowed to listen. 
The sleepwalking constellations drifted into the gaps he made, as if the gaps were footprints waiting for them. 
The fisher kept his hands steady and his heart slower than the tide. 
He walked a widening zigzag, striking the rail, then withholding the strike, until the whole garden breathed with him. 
Smoke curled from the reeds, and the soil hummed along the old iron line. 
In one held silence he felt the goddess arrive, not as a body but as an echo folded into the shape of a question. 
The echo asked what he would trade for the song that ended the wandering. 
He did not offer money or prayer. 
He offered the lullaby he had lost, now whole because he had finally learned to let it rest. 
The echo recognized it and slid down the rail spike like water finding an iron throat. 
The spike grew warm, then cold, then quiet. 
The fisher lifted it free and held it against his chest. 
The goddess answered the stars in a voice that was almost sleep, and the constellations settled back into their charts. 
Patterns that had torn themselves found their edges again, and the garden resumed its patient ringing. 
The fisher walked out at dawn with the captured echo singing under his ribs. 
He did not keep it as a trophy. 
On the cliff he planted the rail spike once more and let the echo spill into the sea wind, where it would lull anything that dreamed too hard. 
The sound wave garden kept his pause in its stems, and travelers would sometimes hear a feral lullaby there. 
It warned them, then rocked them, then warned them again. 
The constellations slept, but not too deeply, for even the sky needed a hand on its shoulder. 
The fisher returned to the shore, cool and quiet, ready to teach his hands to stop shaking by holding still.
