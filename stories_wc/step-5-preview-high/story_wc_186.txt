The beacon tower had not burned in eleven years. 
I was its custodian, keeper of ash and rust. 
After the collapse of civilization, the coastline belonged to gulls and salt grass. 
My only shelter was the glass humming pavilion that ringed the tower's base. 
Its panes trembled in the wind, singing a thin continuous note. 
I swept the floor each dawn, though no one would climb the stairs again. 
One morning I found the broken crystal prism half-buried in the dune drift. 
It was larger than my palm, violet and splintered, and it smelled of old rain. 
Among its shards lay a smaller fragment that did not ring when I tapped it. 
This was a shard of hush, a silence the world had forgotten how to make. 
I am retrocognitively blessed; when my skin touches old things, their past opens like a wound. 
I pressed my thumb against the prism and saw the tower in its glory. 
Keepers in long gray coats fed it starlight through a crystal lens. 
The prism had focused the sky into a single blade of fire to rekindle the beacon. 
Without it, the mechanism slept under a century of dust. 
I carried the pieces back to the pavilion, where the hum seemed to lean closer. 
The shard of hush dulled the sound around it, carving out a pocket of quiet. 
I set it on the workbench and studied the fracture lines of its parent. 
Star paths were etched into the pavilion floor, but half the glass was shattered. 
I knelt and touched the surviving panels, letting retrocognition spill across me. 
I saw ancient astronomers laying silver thread across the sky, charting routes by tracking star paths. 
Their method was not magic but patience, the slow geometry of wandering light. 
I copied their angles into my journal by candle stub and memory. 
The collapse had not erased the heavens, only the arrogance of those who ignored them. 
For three seasons I repaired the beacon's armature with scavenged brass and stubbornness. 
Each part I found felt less like luck and more like punctuation in a sentence I was meant to read. 
A bolt in the sand, a coil in the tide pool, a lens fragment waiting beneath a thistle. 
Coincidence, my father had said, was the noise of a pattern too large to hear. 
I needed to prove that coincidences have meaning, that the ruin still kept its grammar. 
The glass humming pavilion vibrated on the night the stars finally aligned. 
I fitted the broken crystal prism into the beacon's crown and set the shard of hush within its heart. 
Then I began tracking star paths across the northern pane, turning the tower by hand. 
Cold iron bit my palms. 
The pavilion's song rose until it was almost a voice. 
At the fourth hour, the prism caught a star that had crossed the exact angle in my journal. 
Light cascaded down the crystal and struck the dark wick of the beacon. 
For a moment nothing happened. 
Then a spark answered, blooming into a steady amber flame. 
I stepped back as the beacon tower rekindled, its fire climbing the lattice like a vine of gold. 
The coast below turned from black to bronze. 
Far out at sea, I thought I saw a ship pause, as if remembering a promise. 
The shard of hush had stilled the pavilion's hum, replacing it with a hush that felt like held breath. 
In that quiet, I finally understood the pattern. 
The prism had not broken by accident; it had shattered so one piece could survive. 
I had not survived the collapse by chance; I had been left to finish the work. 
Even the thistles that choked the tower were doing their part, carrying seeds on thistle-blown hope. 
I sat in the glass humming pavilion and watched the light wheel across the water. 
Somewhere beyond the dark, other beacons would see mine and know they were not alone. 
I would keep the fire, and I would keep the maps, and I would keep believing. 
Not because the world was healed, but because every drifting seed still found its soil. 
And that, I decided, was meaning enough.
