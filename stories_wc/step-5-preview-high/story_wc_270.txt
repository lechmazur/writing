Grandmother Vess had spent forty years refusing to grow gentle, and the reconstruction council was still learning that insurgents do not simply retire when treaties are signed. 
The volcanic hot springs steamed around her wicker hut, filling the sulfurous dawn with the hiss of mineral vents and the muttering of ferns that only she could hear. 
She had never asked to be accidentally telepathic with plants; the gift had arrived like shrapnel during the siege, lodging itself in her mind and sprouting roots. 
Now, as laborers rebuilt the geothermal district where the old dynasty once bred its engineers, the moss spoke of something sealed beneath the third vent. 
Vess pried the obsidian grate loose with arthritic hands and found a ceramic cradled in the ash, inside it an embryo suspended in honey-gold amber, perfect and wrong. 
The reconstructionists called it a relic of continuity, a chance to resurrect a lineage that had ruled for nine centuries without ever asking permission. 
They spoke of implanted destiny as if it were inheritance, a prewritten script of duty written into the cells before a first breath. 
Vess knew that script by heart because she had watched three generations march into it, her own children included, their minds pruned like hedges to fit the dynasty's geometry. 
She would not let the council plant a new sapling in that poisoned soil, no matter how carefully they framed it as restoration. 
The amber was sealed by more than wax; the cradling ceramic bore a cryptographic lock, a spiral cipher that no smith in the camps could crack. 
For six days she sat in the springs, letting the thermal algae and heat-stroked moss crawl over her forearms, translating their chemical whispers into shapes and numbers. 
It was the root systems, ancient and mineralized beneath the caldera, that finally told her where to look for the key. 
On the seventh morning, a child brought her an abandoned kite snagged in a thorn bush at the springs' edge, its tail weighed down by varnished parchment strips. 
The kite had belonged to the dynasty's sky-messengers, who once flew coded auguries between the vents, but this one had crashed in the last uprising and never been recovered. 
Vess wiped soot from the tail's final strip, revealing a cryptic script that coiled like a fern's spine, and pressed her palm against the damp reeds at the waterline. 
The plants did not read; they remembered, and their memory flooded her with the grammar of a language older than the dynasty. 
Each symbol on the tail matched a mineral in the springs, each phrase a sequence of pressure and bloom that she could decode by tracing the order in which the vent moss flowered. 
Her hands shook as she turned the ceramic cradle according to the kite's cipher, feeling the internal pins slide like stems bowing to a sudden wind. 
The seal popped with a sound like a seed pod opening, releasing the sharp smell of preserved lightning. 
Inside, the embryo did not stir, but the amber clouded, and Vess understood that the child had been grown to awaken only when the old rites were spoken aloud. 
She held the amber above the spring's black heart, her telepathic chorus screaming from the banks, the reeds pleading with her to choose growth instead of archive. 
The council would arrive at noon to crown their relic; instead they would find only a grandmother knee-deep in mineral water, emptying the cradle into the steaming vent. 
The amber did not break; it softened, and the embryo dissolved back into the sulfurous dark, its implanted destiny unsealed into something shapeless and free. 
Vess let the plants have their due, feeding the last engineered heir to the thermal microbes that had no allegiance to bloodlines. 
When the first councilor arrived, breathless and indignant, she was weaving new kite tails from spring reeds, inscribing them with symbols that the moss taught her. 
Tradition, she told them, was not a sealed cradle but a conversation between roots and choices, and she meant to evolve it with intent rather than grief. 
They called her dangerous, and she smiled, because insurgent grandmothers had never needed permission to plant new forests in the ashes of old thrones. 
Above the springs, the abandoned kite lifted once more in an updraft, its cryptic tail glinting, carrying no destiny but the wind's.
