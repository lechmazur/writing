The rusted outpost had stopped complaining about the cold decades ago, settling instead into a worrying tranquility that made Nadia Voss check her own pulse twice each morning. 
Nadia, a dry-witted atlas editor of fewer words, had come to the frozen lake to answer the question that had cost her three publishers and one marriage: where the rivers begin. 
She kept her charts in waterproof tubes, her skepticism in a locked drawer, and a falcon named Ozymandias on a perch above the stove. 
The bird had been gone for eleven days, and Nadia had counted every hour with the subtle confidence of someone who knows that patience is merely calculation performed slowly. 
On the morning after the falcon returned, shedding frost from his wings like a diplomat shedding unwelcome treaties, Nadia decided the waiting was finished. 
She pried the polyglot's phrasebook from beneath the radio, its spine broken, its pages swollen with damp. 
Someone had left a dog-ear on the conjugation of the verb to herald, a folded corner crisp as a surveyor's flag. 
Nadia traced the crease with a thumb calloused from compass roses and printer's ink. 
The phrasebook had belonged to the outpost's last occupant, a linguist who had spoken in eleven tongues and confessed in none. 
He had believed the rivers shared a single mouth, a singular convergence where geography admitted its central lie. 
Nadia had called it poetry then; now, with the lake locked under three meters of ice, she called it a hypothesis. 
She set the phrasebook on the table where the stove's breath fogged the single window. 
Through the condensation, the world beyond the glass reduced itself to strokes and smudges. 
She began to read the pattern of condensation on glass the way other cartographers read contour lines: waiting for the rivulets to map the unseen. 
A droplet ran left, pooled, then split into tributaries that mirrored the lake's hidden inlet. 
Another traced a line toward the outpost's eastern wall, where rust had eaten through to the insulation and revealed a hollow. 
Nadia felt the old thrill, quietly certain, subtly confident that the moisture was not merely weather but a message written in fluid shorthand. 
She fetched her pry bar and opened the wall. 
Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a leather folio. 
The late linguist's handwriting filled the pages, three languages spiraling around a single diagram: all rivers converging at one point beneath the lake, a singular convergence that bent the world's hydrology into a secret knot. 
The dog-ear had marked the exact page where he wrote the coordinate, as if the crease itself had been an anchor thrown into the fog. 
Nadia laughed once, a sound like gravel shifting in a thaw. 
Ozymandias shrieked softly and took flight, circling the window, herald of what would come next. 
She understood then that the falcon's return had not been a coincidence but a bell rung from the edge of the map. 
The condensation pattern had led her to the folio, and the folio led her to the final truth she had chased across four continents. 
The rivers did not begin in mountains or springs or weeping clouds. 
They began here, under the ice, in a place where the earth exhaled water that had never seen daylight. 
Nadia closed the phrasebook and pressed the dog-ear flat, preserving the mark the way an editor preserves a vital comma. 
She would return south with proof that the world's arteries shared one heart, one origin, one elegant deception. 
But as she packed the folio into her pack, the outpost's floorboards groaned, and the lake answered with a low, resonant crack. 
The tranquility remained, but it had changed flavor, now laced with the knowledge that some truths, once heralded, cannot be unspoken. 
Nadia walked to the window and watched condensation gather again, rewriting its patient message on the glass. 
She was ready to translate it.
