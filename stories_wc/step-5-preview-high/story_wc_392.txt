After the drought ended, the subway maintenance tunnel still smelled of wet brick and thawed dust. 
Wren Adeyemi, a preventable death investigator, stood at a flooded gate chamber where a maintenance worker had been found unconscious. 
The memo on her tablet called it heatstroke and carelessness. 
She had spent nine years disproving such words. 
She was watchfully calm in the way of people who had learned that panic was its own kind of drowning. 
The lantern in her coat had crossed three continents and one war to reach her. 
It was a lantern from the Underground Railroad, tin dented by travel, glass clouded by breath. 
Her great-grandmother had carried it north, then east, then into this city's basement of tunnels. 
The foreman who found the body had mailed it to Wren with no note except her name. 
Now its weight anchored her hand. 
She knelt and began to read the chamber through empirical observation. 
Water had not pooled randomly. 
It ran in a thin braid from a misaligned gate seal, then spread toward a cable trench where the bottom was silted high. 
She measured the silt, photographed rust blooms, and recorded the wall temperature beside a sealed service door. 
The service door had a fresh pry mark near the latch. 
In the dust above it, she found half a boot print facing inward. 
Gravel lay scattered in a fan, as if someone had knelt to chip ice from the trench. 
Wren's chest tightened. 
When she lifted the lantern, no flame caught, but the metal grew warm. 
The warmth carried entangled memories that were not entirely her own. 
She saw a woman in a wet wool shawl pressing through a brick throat while men with lanterns shouted behind her. 
She smelled river mud and liberty. 
She felt a rope tied around a bundle of photographs, then felt her own thumb against the tin. 
The memory reverberated through her ribs like a train passing under a bed. 
For a moment she could not tell whose fear was making her hands steady. 
A voice behind her said, "You always did bring props." 
Wren closed the lantern and did not turn quickly. 
Deputy Inspector Halloway leaned against the tunnel arch with his flashlight lowered, his posture watchfully calm, his smile prepared. 
He had trained her, promoted her, and corrected her reports for a decade. 
He was the controlling influence she had come to break free from. 
"Go home, Wren," he said. 
"It is a drowning with bad luck attached." 
"The gate seal was replaced in April," she said. 
"Someone forced it open to make room for a boot, and Tesfaye found the gap and tried to close it." 
Halloway's flashlight beam did not shake. 
"You have a vivid imagination," he said. 
"I have measurements," she said. 
"Through empirical observation, I can show where the water changed, and you taught me that a death is preventable when the warning is ignored." 
The tunnel seemed to lean closer. 
The lantern's memories returned as a pattern she recognized: a hidden route, a watcher at the turn, a person choosing whether to run alone or save the next voice. 
"Your mother died in a stairwell fall," he said softly. 
"You proved the handrail defective, and you have been trying to save everyone since." 
He knew which chord would reverberate. 
Wren let the words land, but she did not let them move her. 
Her fortified vulnerability had been built by years of not being believed. 
"My mother did not die because I was reckless," she said. 
"Tesfaye did not die because the rain came back." 
She opened her satchel and handed him the photographs. 
"The pry mark is fresh, the boot print is inward, and the silt was disturbed after the last inspection; I logged it before you arrived." 
Halloway read them with the patience of a man who had learned to wait out storms. 
"You will be reassigned," he said. 
"I will be subpoenaed," she said. 
"I sent the first report to the transit board twenty minutes ago." 
The water in the trench ticked as it cooled. 
Halloway set the photographs back in her satchel with two fingers, as if returning a relic. 
"You think that lantern makes you brave?" he asked. 
Wren looked at the tin and thought of the woman in wet wool who had carried light through worse. 
"No," she said. 
"It reminds me that someone chose not to leave the next person behind." 
Halloway walked toward the dark until the tunnel swallowed his footsteps. 
Wren remained beside the trench, watchfully calm, breathing through the old fear until it became simply air. 
She struck a match, lit the lantern's wick, and set it in the alcove so the next crew would find it burning. 
She had not waited for permission to move.
