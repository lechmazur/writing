Tomas had carved three hundred gravestones, yet he still did not know the shape of collective grief.  He arrived at the ancient greenhouse at dusk carrying a woven ribbon roll under his arm.  Across the butterfly migration, the glass panes held the last copper light while orange wings settled on the broken vents.  He set the woven ribbon roll beside a cracked fountain and listened to the water argue with stone.  Tomas had come to retrace the path his family once walked through these rows of moss and rust.  His grandmother had kept the custom of trading favorite poems at dinner, each verse folded around a sprig of herb or a stone.  Now the greenhouse was his table, and he laid the woven ribbon roll across the potting bench like a ceremony.  He was fiercely meditative, his breath slow, his chisel tapping a rhythm against a slab of unclaimed granite.  The ghost arrived not with thunder but with the scent of wet soil and old paper.  Her name was Ilse, and she had tended this greenhouse a century before the butterflies learned their northern route.  She had no bones, only attention.  Tomas did not startle; he had long believed that stone kept better company than silence.  "Silence, he had learned, could be carved."  "You carve endings," Ilse said, her voice a thundering calm that pressed against his ribs.  "I carve questions," Tomas answered, turning the woven ribbon roll so its colors caught the fading dusk.  Ilse drifted past the tomato cages, her translucent fingers tracing the same rows Tomas had walked.  "Then retrace them," she said.  "Grief is not a single stone but a migration, and you cannot know its shape from one resting place."  He did not argue; the thought suited his fiercely meditative nature.  So each evening across the butterfly migration, Tomas returned to the greenhouse at dusk and carved not a name but a current.  Ilse taught him to read the wing patterns as script, each species carrying a stanza of sorrow too large for one heart.  The spectral mentorship deepened his focus until his hands moved with the certainty of roots finding water.  The mentorship was spectral, yet more real than any commission.  When the moon rose, he unspooled the woven ribbon roll and measured the air between the butterflies, knotting lengths to mark each invisible turn.  He thought of his mother's voice reciting Blake at dinner, the custom that had taught him language could hold what hands could not.  Now he understood the family custom not as nostalgia but as method, a way to trade grief until it became bearable.  Tomas retraced his own carvings, seeing how each grave was a line in a communal poem, a verse offered to strangers.  Every customer wanted a full stop, but he began to leave the ellipses open.  The greenhouse smelled of crushed fern and twilight, and the thundering calm of the place settled into his shoulders like a mantle.  On the final dusk of the migration, Ilse stood among the last monarchs and pointed to the pattern they made.  He had expected geometry, but she offered him flight.  "There," she said.  "Not a line, not a circle, but a woven thing, held together by the tension of release."  Tomas lifted the woven ribbon roll and saw his own life threaded through it, every poem and every stone a fiber in the same braid.  He returned to the workshop and carved a door into the last gravestone, open at both ends, so the butterflies could pass through.  The stone seemed to breathe.  The town whispered that the thoughtful carver had gone strange, but they came at dusk to leave poems on his step.  He read each one by greenhouse light, trading verses with the dead, and the shape of collective grief became clear.  He carved no more doors, only windows.  It was a migration, not an ending, and every soul moved within it, fierce and calm.  The greenhouse stood empty of ghosts, full of wings.  In that thundering calm, Tomas finally laid down his chisel and picked up a poem.  Grief, he wrote on the last commission, is the ribbon that binds the living to the gone.  And the butterflies carried it home.
