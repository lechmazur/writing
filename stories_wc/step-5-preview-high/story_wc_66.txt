After the story famine, the conservatory kept notes and threw away every word. 
Mira was a diligent orchestra copyist, and her copies were so clean that conductors forgot to mark them. 
Her workshop was an ember alcove in mist, a coal-warmed nook behind the boiler room. 
Fog pressed against the window, and the coals ticked like quiet metronomes. 
She copied dynamics and bowings by day, but at night she tuned a forbidden receiver. 
At night the shortwave radio dial glow brightened without power, secretly luminous and thin. 
That glow was her map to a pirate broadcast that the inspectors could not trace. 
No one knew the singer, yet the voice came after curfew, threading through static like a needle. 
It spread its lessons via elusive song lyrics, half-rhymes that dissolved before they could be written down. 
Mira learned to catch them the way she caught wrong notes in a copied score. 
She wrote fragments in the margins of manuscript paper, trusting her ear more than the law. 
Her private motive was to teach dust to tell stories. 
In the alcove, dust was not dead; it was grey ash, eraser crumbs, and powdered coal. 
She believed the grains had swallowed every tale that the famine had tried to burn. 
If she could give the dust a pattern of words, it might learn to speak again. 
One evening the lyrics named an old lullaby that matched a cello line she had copied. 
She followed the melody on her radio until the dial glow pulsed like a heartbeat. 
The voice asked for a partner, and Mira answered by playing the phrase on a muted violin. 
The static shifted as if the room itself were taking a breath. 
From then on, she no longer only listened; she became a second voice in the dark. 
She built a small transmitter from spare parts and hid it beneath her worktable. 
When footsteps came down the corridor, she would cloak the set in a painter's canvas. 
The canvas smelled of turpentine, and the inspectors never thought to look inside art. 
Her broadcast began as a whisper and grew into a scheduled hour after midnight. 
She sang the elusive song lyrics she had gathered, letting each verse end before it was complete. 
The missing endings were deliberate, because unfinished lines made the listeners lean closer. 
Across the city, other alcoves answered with fragments, and the frequencies braided together. 
In Mira's ember alcove, the dust began to rise in slow spirals when she sang. 
The grains caught the glow from the radio and held it, secretly luminous as a second sky. 
They did not glow for the inspectors or for the conductors who valued silence. 
They glowed only when a lyric floated above them, unfinished and patient. 
One night the dust arranged itself into letters on the sill, and Mira read them aloud. 
It said, "We remember the story you are singing." 
She answered with a verse about a river that refused to freeze in winter. 
The dust moved again, spelling a question about whether rivers could forget their names. 
So she taught it line by line, using the copyist's patience she had learned from scores. 
Each lesson was a melody, each answer a half-line, and each pause a space for the dust to think. 
The conservatory noticed that her copied parts had grown strange and tender in the quiet measures. 
Musicians played them and felt stories rising behind the notes, though no one could name the source. 
The inspectors came at last, drawn by the rumor of words in a city of instruments. 
They entered the boiler room and found only a copyist asleep over a stack of manuscript paper. 
The radio was hidden, and the ember alcove in mist looked like any other dusty niche. 
On the sill, the dust lay still, grey and ordinary, waiting for the next broadcast. 
After they left, Mira woke and smiled with a thistled calm that nothing could prick. 
She knew the famine had not ended with trumpets, but with small voices teaching grey things to speak. 
Her diligence had become a lantern, and her cloak had become a flag. 
In the alcove, the coals settled, the mist thinned, and the dust began to hum. 
It was not a loud hum, only the sound of stories learning how to stay.
