The phoenix had risen three dawns prior, leaving the glassblower's courtyard coated in iridescent ash that cooled into brittle lace. 
Mara, a painter who had never seen red as anything but shadow, stepped over the threshold with her canvas wrapped in oilcloth. 
She sought the glassblower named Corvin, who had fallen into a waking sleep after the bird's ascent, his eyes open but his hands still as cooled glass. 
In the marketplace, such slumbers were treated as bartered awakenings: one consciousness lent to another until the debt of perception was balanced, especially after the phoenix rises. 
Mara had come to collect an awakening, though she intended to pay with something stranger than coin. 
The law was old, written in the same ash that now stained her sleeves, and it demanded equity, not charity. 
She arranged her message in the courtyard's herb beds, communicating through plant arrangements that she laid along the stone path. 
Because she could not trust color, she built her language from asymmetry: a curled fern frond beside a broken twig, three drops of dew cupped in a single poppy leaf. 
The syntax of imperfection was one Corvin had taught her years ago, back when he blew perfect spheres and she painted them false colors. 
Now she placed a crooked shoot at the entrance, an innocently cunning signal that she came alone and empty-handed. 
Corvin sat in the center of the courtyard, motionless among the cooling pots. 
Mara set before him a concertina reed tongue she had salvaged from a drowned instrument in the ash. 
The thin brass tongue had once vibrated breath into song, and Mara had realized, with the simple logic of the colorblind, that it could still shape air. 
She positioned it against the rim of the largest glass vessel, so that when the wind moved through the courtyard, the reed would hum. 
It was not a bribe, she explained in her arrangements, but a demonstration: she offered resonance, not rescue. 
The glassblower did not move, so she prepared to awaken him with breath and brass. 
She rearranged the stems, adding a dried thistle to the center of her pattern as a question. 
In the law of bartered awakenings, the reed tongue became collateral, a sonic anchor to bind his returning awareness to her presence. 
Mara adjusted her sequence, adding a shattered petal to show she understood the cost: she would lend him her own sharpness of sight, and in exchange he would lend her his dreams. 
She took his hand. 
It was warm, unexpectedly, like glass just pulled from the furnace. 
She guided his fingers to the reed, and together they breathed across it, a clumsy duet that stirred the ash into spirals. 
The sound was imperfect, wavering, colored with the grit of the phoenix's passing. 
Corvin's eyelids fluttered. 
The awakening began not as a flood of light but as a slow warming, a recognition of the crooked stems and the asymmetric leaves that spelled her name in green. 
When he finally spoke, his voice was rough from disuse, and he asked why she had come. 
Mara gestured to the ash-covered benches, to the cracked crucibles and the misshapen bowls that had burst in the furnace's heat. 
She wanted to paint them, she said, to find beauty in imperfection, to capture the phoenix's scorched geometry without the distraction of honest color. 
Corvin smiled and picked up his blowpipe. 
They worked until the ash stopped falling, and Mara painted what she saw: not hues, but values, textures, the tremble between flaw and form. 
The concertina reed tongue remained on the vessel's rim, humming whenever the breeze shifted, a reminder that some awakenings are purchased with song, and some beauty grows best in crooked soil.
