Mira had not walked beneath these cypress trees for twenty years, and now the catacomb orchard seemed to breathe around her. 
Its ancient trees leaned over low stone graves, roots snaking between bones that gleamed like polished ivory in the dappled light. 
She was a diaspora returnee, born in this valley but raised far across the sea, and every scent of crushed thyme and damp earth pulled at a memory she could not name. 
The village elders had asked her to help excavate the tomb of Saint Ilaria, a task begun by her grandfather and interrupted, like so many journeys, by war. 
Mira arrived warmly curious, carrying notebooks and a quiet hope that this soil might teach her what exile had scattered. 
Across the first days of digging, the orchard felt cautious with enchantment, as if the dead watched the living with patient, half-forgiven eyes. 
Workers lifted slabs of marble, revealing frescoes of travelers halting mid-step, their paths cut short by stones that never should have fallen. 
These images embodied interrupted journeys, and Mira understood that the saint had not died but had been stopped, her pilgrimage to a distant shrine left unfinished in darkness. 
One afternoon, while the others rested in the shade, Mira chose purposeful wandering over the straight line of the trench. 
She let her fingers trace root and rib alike, moving where curiosity led rather than where the plan dictated. 
Beneath a fig tree whose roots coiled around a femur, she uncovered a circular slab of bronze, green with age and shaped like the base of a sundial. 
It was a sundial base, its gnomon missing, its hour lines worn smooth by centuries of shadow. 
Mira lifted it with both hands, feeling the weight of hours that had failed to pass. 
The inscription was faint, yet she read the saint's last plea: that wayfarers be guided even when roads ended. 
In that instant Mira recognized her own reflection in the metal: a life paused between two countries, two tongues, two incomplete stories. 
She had come to restore lost faith, not merely in the saint, but in the possibility of arrival after long departure. 
The excavation had stalled where a collapse had sealed the inner chamber, and the men spoke of abandoning the tomb for another season. 
But Mira saw the sundial base as more than an artifact; it was a compass, a promise, and a foundation waiting to be made whole again. 
She decided to rebuild, not with hammer and chisel alone, but by reweaving the broken path between the living and the remembered. 
Across the following week, she wandered the orchard with intent, mapping where each tree had split a tomb or cradled a skull. 
She spoke with the gravediggers, the bakers, the widows, asking warmly about the saint's steps until the village began to remember her as a neighbor rather than relic. 
The elders brought out a cedar gnomon, kept in a linen bag since the war, its tip still sharp enough to cast a shadow. 
Together they set it into the bronze base at the orchard's heart, where roots parted like fingers releasing a held breath. 
Mira aligned the dial not to true north, but to the exact point where the saint's journey had been interrupted. 
As the sun climbed, the shadow swept across the hours, and the villagers gathered in cautious enchantment, watching light measure the unmeasurable. 
A child asked why the dial began at an unfinished hour rather than midnight, and Mira answered that every interrupted journey asks to be continued, not erased. 
That evening, the collapse in the inner chamber was cleared, not by force but by following the sundial's shadow to a seam of loose mortar. 
Inside, they found no treasure of gold, only a pilgrim's satchel of seeds, as if the saint had planted her last steps rather than sealed them. 
Mira understood then that to rebuild was to sow, to let what is buried rise again in unexpected branches. 
She planted the seeds around the sundial base, and across the season they grew into saplings whose roots would learn to honor bones without consuming them. 
When she finally boarded the ship home, it was not a return to exile but a passage between two dwellings of the same heart. 
The diaspora returnee had restored more than a saint's dial; she had restored the village's belief that broken roads can become gardens. 
Travelers in the catacomb orchard pause at the bronze base, where tree roots snake between bones and leaves whisper of journeys paused, faith restored, and hands that rebuilt what war had scattered.
