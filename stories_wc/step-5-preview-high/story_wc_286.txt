The potter walked into the abandoned beekeeping oasis as chimney smoke flattened into thin gray threads across the scorched horizon.  He carried no staff, no map, only a faded lantern glass wrapped in faded silk, its edges worn smooth by decades of thumb-worrying.  His name had been Sahir once, but names were like riverbanks, shifting, so he had learned to answer to stillness instead.  The oasis should have died with the rest of the wasteland, yet here the wells held water, the dates hung heavy, and the empty hives stood like patient monks.  Sahir the potter had come to understand why some places resist change, a question that had hollowed him into a shape that could finally hold light.  Each morning he worked by following sun patterns, reading the shadows the apiary walls cast to know when to turn the clay, when to trim the wax, when to simply breathe.  The oasis kept its own time, fractured timelines sliding over one another like oil on water, and the potter had learned to see them without flinching.  At dawn the smoke of one fire rose straight; by following sun patterns he watched that same smoke flatten at noon into a timeline where the keepers had never fled.  In one now the hives were gold with bees, in another they were ash, and in a third the hives were glass, thrumming with a sound that lived in the sternum rather than the ear.  The thrum was constant beneath the silence, a frequency of resistance, and Sahir felt it most when his hands were centered on the wheel.  People had asked why he didn't just repair the hives and call the bees back; he told them the bees had left not from neglect but because the world outside had grown too sharp to pollinate.  What held the oasis together, he suspected, was not water, nor soil, but a quality the old keepers had called phototropic to kindness.  The dates bent toward gentle hands; the water rose in the wells when a visitor spoke without greed; the very dust seemed to settle differently around a merciful heart.  On the seventh evening, when chimney smoke flattened into long blue fingers over the eastern ridge, Sahir unwrapped the faded lantern glass and held it up to the dying sun.  The glass was not merely old but layered, its surface a composite of moments, and through it the fractured timelines braided into a single rope he could almost grasp.  He saw the original oasis, verdant and loud; he saw the scorch that had eaten the valley; he saw the last keeper cover a lantern and flee rather than harvest the final honeycomb.  In that flight there had been kindness, a deliberate sparing, and that sparing had carved a pocket where change could not fully land.  The thrum rose, not from the hives but from the glass, resonating with the potter's own pulse until his ribs ached with a sharp gentleness.  He understood then that the oasis resisted change because it had been saturated with mercy, and mercy, unlike stone, does not erode; it accretes.  Sahir set the faded lantern glass in the central kiln, not to fire, but to watch, letting the last light pass through its failures and imperfections.  Through the night the timelines thrummed softly against each other, bees moving backward and forward through wax corridors that no longer obeyed linear decay.  He worked his wheel by moonlight, shaping vessels with walls thin as eggshell, each meant to hold the phototropic weight of a kindness not yet spent.  By morning the air smelled of wet clay and distant lavender, and the empty hives had acquired a single, impossible honeycomb, golden and new.  Sahir did not smile, for a zen-minded potter knows that joy is a guest who stays longer when offered silence, but he did cup the comb with both hands.  As chimney smoke flattened once more against the rim of the world, he placed the lantern glass at the oasis threshold, a beacon for any traveler who still knew how to spare the last honeycomb.  The thrum continued, patient as pollen, and the potter returned to his wheel, turning change into a thing that could hold water.  In the scorched wasteland beyond, storms gathered and broke, but here the dates ripened, the wells held, and the timelines settled into a chord rather than a scar.  Some places resist change, he realized, not because they are stubborn, but because they have been loved into a different kind of time.  The faded lantern glass caught the sunrise, and for a moment every version of the orchard bloomed at once, fragrant and fierce and forgiving.  Sahir centered another bowl, and the oasis sang with him, a single note of sharp gentleness that would not be spent.
