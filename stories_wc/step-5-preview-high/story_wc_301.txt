After stopping the medication, the truth-seeking river chronicler returned to the disused railway station overgrown with ivy. 
She carried an accordion with bellows patched with tape, because the old instrument still breathed when her lungs refused to. 
The ivy had swallowed the platforms and climbed through broken windows, turning the waiting hall into a green chapel of rust. 
Mira was daringly methodical even now; she began not with the river but with the bathroom. 
Inside the tiled stall she found bathroom graffiti wisdom scratched in layers of paint and paranoia. 
One scrawl said, The map references a rail under the water, and the water remembers what the paper forgot. 
Another alluded to a drowned junction where honest men left their guilt to rust. 
Mira copied each line into her field notebook with the same discipline she once reserved for hydrology charts. 
Years ago she had altered a reference on an official map to protect her hometown from a flood levy. 
Her lie had delayed evacuation, and three people died when the river took the low road. 
After the trial she accepted medication that smoothed the edges of memory into dull static. 
Stopping it felt like tearing tape from a wound; suddenly every sensation was unbearable and necessary. 
Now she needed to find a path to self-forgiveness, and the graffiti seemed to offer coordinates. 
She left the bathroom and walked the platform, reading map references carved into benches and coal dust. 
An old timetable mentioned a spur that ran along the river to a place called Absolution Siding. 
The accordion pulled at her shoulder, its tape patches creaking like a warning. 
Mira sat on the edge of the platform, opened the bellows, and let a low chord shake the ivy. 
The sound was not music but a question, raw solace in the shape of air. 
She thought of her father, who had taught her that rivers forgive nothing and everything. 
He had died believing her charts, because she had made them beautiful and false. 
The bathroom graffiti had said, Follow the rail until the track becomes a spine. 
So she climbed down the embankment where the tracks vanished into reeds and gravel. 
The river here was wide and brown, wearing winter like a shroud. 
Mira walked the old ballast, daringly methodical, measuring her grief in sleepers. 
Every ninth tie she stopped and wrote a truth in her notebook. 
I was afraid, she wrote. 
I am still afraid. 
I will not hide it under ink again. 
Near a culvert she found the junction the graffiti alluded to, where the rail bent toward the water. 
The map references on the bathroom wall had not lied; they had marked a place to lay down weight. 
She set the accordion on the rusted track and pulled the bellows fully open. 
The tape patches held, releasing a chord that sounded like a sob learning to stand. 
Mira did not cry; the medication had stolen her tears, and stopping it had returned them as rain. 
She spoke aloud to the river, naming the dead, naming her cowardice, naming the chronicle she would now keep. 
The water took her words without judgment, carrying them downstream past the overgrown station. 
When the chord faded, she understood that self-forgiveness was not a station one could reach. 
It was a track laid one sleeper at a time, patched with tape, still able to breathe. 
She picked up the accordion and began the long walk back, not lighter, but honest. 
At the station she added one final line to the bathroom wall with a stub of pencil. 
The river knows your name, and it is not finished with you. 
Mira left the disused railway station overgrown with ivy as the sun turned the green walls gold. 
The accordion's patched bellows knocked softly against her hip, a rhythm of raw solace. 
She would continue upstream tomorrow, chronicling every tributary without fear. 
But tonight she slept in the waiting hall, dreaming of rails that ran straight and true. 
In the morning she woke with the taste of iron and the certainty of work. 
She ate cold rice from a tin and checked her map references against the graffiti. 
Then she climbed the embankment to the bridge where the river met the old road. 
There she saw her reflection, patched and worn, still willing to look. 
The truth-seeking river chronicler pressed on, accordion in hand, toward the water. 
The disused railway station overgrown with ivy had become a classroom and a confessional. 
She traced the map references once more, daringly methodical, before stepping outside. 
Mira alluded to her past in the title of her new field report: Corrections for the Dead. 
She wrote that forgiveness was a current, not a shore, and that she would swim until the end.
