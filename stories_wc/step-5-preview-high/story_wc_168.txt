Hallis was a scarred forest firefighter who trusted ridges more than towns. 
The moonlit ridge path rose ahead of her during the slow dance of celestial bodies. 
She carried a cracked porcelain box against her chest, where the heat of her hands could find it. 
It held the silence of a child who had once been promised innocence. 
At the summit waited Wren, seventeen now, wrapped in a shelter-cloth that smelled of smoke and pine soap. 
Hallis was gently skilled at reading faces the way other firefighters read wind. 
She saw the fury Wren kept buttoned behind her ribs and did not name it. 
Instead she knelt, opened a med kit, and bound the blisters on Wren's palms with the same patience she used on a smoldering root. 
"You came," Wren said, as if the words cost her something. 
"I came to enlighten you," Hallis said, "not to ask you to forget again." 
Wren's jaw tightened. 
"Then say what that box is." 
Hallis set the cracked porcelain box on a flat stone between them. 
Moonlight found the fractures first, thin as a river seen from the air. 
Eight years ago, Hallis had pulled Wren from a burn that ate a whole canyon. 
Wren's mother had died with a hand pressed to Hallis's wrist, begging for a trade. 
She offered her own life for her daughter's, but Hallis had made a different bargain. 
She promised the fire that Wren would live untouched by it. 
Hallis called that promise bargained innocence, though it had felt more like theft. 
The child forgot the flame; the child forgot her mother's last words. 
The box held those forgotten things so they could not burn her from the inside. 
"I don't want them," Wren whispered. 
"I know," Hallis said. 
"That is why I am here," Hallis said. 
"I have spent years trying to translate pain into a universal language." 
She lifted the lid. 
Inside lay a child's birch-bark flute, a scorched photograph, and a strip of map. 
The flute had been Wren's first language; the photograph had held her mother's laughter. 
Hallis turned the strip of map until its burned edge matched the ridge line. 
Then she began, by reflecting deeply, to show Wren what fire had taken. 
She angled the cracked porcelain so moonlight poured through the breaks and fell across Wren's hands. 
The shards of light made a pattern, and Hallis read it aloud like a text. 
"That scar is a verb," Hallis said. 
"That pause is a breath." 
"That long burn is a name you are allowed to say." 
Wren stared at her hands as if they belonged to someone else. 
"They belong to me," she finally said. 
"Yes," Hallis said softly, her rough voice full of hard softness. 
"Innocence is not a thing you keep in a box," Hallis said. 
"It is a promise you renegotiate with every season." 
Wren took the flute and held it to the moonlight, not quite playing, only warming the wood. 
"I remember her voice now," Wren said. 
It was not a full memory, but it was a door. 
Hallis felt the old bargain loosen, the way a knot gives when pulled gently. 
Above them, the planets moved in their slow dance, indifferent and exact. 
Below, the valley was dark, but the ridge path had become legible. 
Hallis gave Wren the box, open now, its cracks no longer hidden. 
"Teach it to someone," Hallis said. 
"That is how pain becomes universal," Hallis said. 
"That is how it stops being a fire." 
Wren tucked the flute into her belt and started down the moonlit ridge path. 
Hallis followed, not as a keeper of innocence, but as a witness. 
The stars kept their slow dance, and two people walked beneath them, learning the same language.
