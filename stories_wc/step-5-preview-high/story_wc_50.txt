The locksmith descended into the marble cavern with a broken timer mechanism wrapped in oilcloth beneath his arm. 
Outside, the wounded season had paused, its lull settling over the valley like frost that refused to melt. 
He had come across the lull to work, for only in this suspended interval did the marble hold its breath and let the echoes ripen. 
He was a reflective man, as much philosopher as craftsman, and his eyes were peripherally focused, always catching truth in the corners rather than the center. 
Straight gazes had betrayed him in the noisy city, so he learned to trust the glancing evidence that the edges of vision preserved. 
A mirror, he believed, surrendered its secrets only to peripheral sight, and a lock opened not to force but to patient geometry. 
The cavern walls rose in polished columns, white and veined with grey, and the acoustics were such that a whisper returned threefold. 
Stalactites hung like frozen chandeliers, and the floor was slick with mineral water that never fell because the season refused to weep. 
No wind moved the dust, no drip broke the silence, for the wounded season had stilled the natural clock. 
He set the timer mechanism on a flat plinth and turned it with brass tweezers. 
Its mainspring had shattered, its escapement teeth bent as if time itself had been struck dumb. 
He intended to reverse-engineer the device, to understand its failure so thoroughly that he could invert its purpose from measuring moments to keeping them. 
This was the harvest of echoes, an old lore whispered among the mountainfolk, though none had attempted it in living memory. 
The idea was to trap the sounds that bounced between the marble walls, to bottle the reverberations of a season too hurt to move forward. 
But echo refused simple capture. 
It demanded geometry. 
Across the lull, he studied reflection angles at different times, charting how dawn light and dusk shadow struck the polished stone. 
He never looked directly at the bright beams; instead, he let the light fall into the periphery of his vision, where his mind could parse the angles without flinching. 
Each morning he carved notches into the timer's outer casing, marking where the reflections aligned. 
Each evening he adjusted a dial he had fitted into the ruined works, a dial that could rotate when the broken gears could not. 
He discovered that at the seventh hour after dawn, the eastern galleries sang with a harmonic that seemed to pause in midair. 
If he turned the timer's salvaged cam to forty degrees, that harmonic folded back upon itself, concentrating rather than dispersing. 
By the third week of the lull, he had reconstructed enough of the mechanism to act as a resonant anchor, a hook for the invisible. 
Yet the locksmith hesitated, for his motivation was not mere curiosity or greed. 
He sought to store wonder in the cellar of reason, for later. 
In the years ahead, he suspected the world would grow dry and pragmatic, and he wanted a reserve against that drought. 
The cellar of his house below the ridge was lined with labeled drawers, each one a small cabinet of catalogued mysteries. 
He would file the cavern's echoes there, not as superstition, but as specimens of beauty preserved by exact measurement. 
One evening, as the wounded season groaned in its sleep, he triggered the anchor. 
The marble flared with a pale chord that began at the floor and climbed to the vaulted roof. 
He watched from the side, peripherally, as the sound pooled and thickened, then slid into the timer mechanism like water into a vessel. 
The broken device hummed, its shattered spring singing a new note, no longer measuring but holding. 
He packed it gently, the harvest complete, and climbed toward the surface as the lull deepened. 
Behind him, the cavern went quiet, its wonders safely reasoned into his keeping. 
In the cellar months later, he would open the drawer, release a sliver of that marble song, and remember that reason had once learned to keep a little awe alive.
