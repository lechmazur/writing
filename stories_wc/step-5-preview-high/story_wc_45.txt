The substitute body was found at the edge of the alder marsh, hollow and light as a paper lantern.  Sela the bone setter was summoned before dawn to examine it, though the town elders begged her to bring salt and iron.  She was a forgiving skeptic, known to listen patiently to tales of the Fair Folk and then mend the broken bones of those who believed them.  The body had no marrow, no scars, and a jaw set with teeth of polished quartz.  Sela noted the alignment of the ribs, the clavicles swapped, the knees reversed, a crude imitation of anatomy.  Whoever assembled this substitute had studied healing but had never felt pain.  That night, with the village muttering about changelings, Sela sought the silence of the old estate behind the hedge maze.  The maze had been grown from yew and rumor, its walls taller than memory, and the secret garden inside smelled of rain and rust.  She had apprenticed here as a girl, learning to set splints beneath a glass dome where steam valves opened the flowerbeds at dusk.  It was a place of nostalgic innovation, half monastery, half machine, and it had taught her that old wounds required new hands.  In the center of the garden stood a sundial cracked down the middle, a clean cleft that the moss had tried and failed to heal.  Sela knelt, driven by a habit older than doubt, and found a slender object wedged inside the cleft, a changeling's baby tooth.  It was no larger than her thumbnail, opalescent, and warm.  Her certainty had always been a smooth pane; now it had a cleft, and through the crack leaked the wild air of possibility.  A shadow moved among the brass ferns.  A child stood there, perhaps ten, with leaves in her hair and a shy smile.  You set bones, the child said. I need you to set mine.  Her left arm hung wrong, the forearm angled like a snapped twig.  Sela's training overrode her astonishment; she guided the girl to the stone bench and examined the break.  The bones were hollow, lighter than bird bone, and knitted with something like silver filament.  You made the substitute, Sela said, not a question.  The girl nodded, shrewdly innocent, her eyes too old for her face yet wide with a cunning that meant no harm.  The boy in the village was dying; I took him to ease his passage, but he died, and I made a decoy so his mother could bury a son.  Sela felt anger rise, then remembered the mother who had once thrown stones at her for saying a fever was not a curse.  She was a skeptic who forgave desperate lies because she had seen enough death to know how thin the line between mercy and ruin could be.  Why leave the tooth? Sela asked.  So someone clever would find the cleft and ask why; I yearn to go home, but first I must mend what I broke.  The changeling had been raised in the garden by steam and spell, and her baby tooth was the key that unlocked the gate beneath the sundial.  Sela realized then that her motivation, older than her doubt, was to turn mistakes into constellations.  A mistake was merely a star waiting for a map.  Help me set him properly, the girl whispered.  Together they returned to the marsh at moonrise and carried the substitute body back through the hedge maze.  In the secret garden, Sela used her bone setting skill not to deceive but to reveal.  She realigned the quartz jaw and reversed the clavicles until the decoy looked less like a corpse and more like a confession.  The girl sang a low note, and the silver filament in her arm hummed in answer, binding the bone as Sela set it.  Sela understood, at last, that her skepticism had been a shelter, not a truth.  The cleft in her certainty did not weaken her; it widened her.  By dawn they had made a second figure, not of flesh but of clay and flower, and laid it beneath the sundial.  Sela told the village only that the first find was a decoy made by a frightened creature, and that the boy had died in peace.  She did not mention the garden, or the girl who yearned for her own world beyond the hedge.  Instead, she stayed to tend the glass dome, teaching her craft to any child, human or not, who climbed the maze.  Years later, travelers spoke of a constellation above the marsh that no astronomer could name.  It looked, they said, like a setter's hands mending a broken star.  And in the hedge maze, the changeling's baby tooth remained in its cleft, proof that certainty broken open can become home.
