The labyrinth maintenance worker named Odo checked her gauges at the sixth junction, where the walls sweated condensation and the air tasted of old iron.  Her job was not slaying monsters or rescuing maidens, but auditing the facility's most vital resource: quantified sorrow.  Each morning she recorded the grief harvested from visitors' dreams, assigning it units and stacking it in pressurized canisters beneath the floor.  The work was dreary, precise, and miraculous in the way a seed is miraculous, hidden in the palm and asking for nothing but time.  Odo kept one item in her breast pocket that did not belong to the inventory: an astronomer's broken sextant, its brass arc bent like a wilted leaf.  Sennit had built it to measure the angle between the earth and the sleeping stars, but it had cracked during the Collapse, and now it served a different purpose.  When held against a dreaming wall, the sextant resonated with the residue of scattered dreams, humming off-key until Odo adjusted its lens.  She was trying to deconstruct the labyrinth's foundation, not with dynamite or drill, but by mapping scattered dreams into a coherent survey.  The Administration forbade this, arguing that dreams were unstable data and that deconstruction risked a sorrow overflow.  Odo continued anyway, driven by a private motivation: to discover what trees witness.  Before the stone corridors existed, there had been a forest, and Sennit had believed the trees kept a memory more honest than any human ledger.  The sextant was his instrument for asking them.  One evening, in a corridor so silent she could hear the dust settling, Odo paused with her chalk raised.  The hush had weight, a pressure behind the ears, and then it was broken by a faint, bright sound: at the moment a pin is heard in a silent corridor, the world seems to inhale.  The pin did not fall from her pocket.  It came from above, from the shaft that connected the underground maze to the surface tower.  Odo understood the summons.  She climbed the spiral stair with the sextant clenched in her fist, emerging at last into the stormlit tower by moonlight.  The chamber had no ceiling, only a storm that roared above a ring of broken columns, and through a rift in the clouds the moon spilled silver across the floor.  Where the light touched her scars, they closed; Odo breathed in sharply, remembering that she heals in moonlight, a secret quirk that made her valuable and suspect.  The others had called it a defect, this mending under silver, but she had learned to call it a shift change, the body ending its workday.  In the center of the room stood the original survey post, and nailed to it was a dried branch from the first tree that had witnessed the founding sorrow.  Odo set the sextant upon the post and began to deconstruct the instrument, unscrewing the bent arc until the brass revealed a cavity packed with seed paper.  The seeds were not dead.  She pressed them into the cracks of the stone floor and watered them with condensation from her canteen, an act of foolish hope.  Then she took out her map of scattered dreams, the one she had spent years assembling, and laid it over the seeds like a blanket.  The moon strengthened, burning through the storm, and the seeds stirred, not into trees, but into thin green sprouts that carried the memory of rings.  Odo watched as the sprouts wrote upon the stone in a language of growth, recounting every sorrow they had absorbed, quantified now not as numbers but as patient testimony.  She saw the Collapse, the planting of the first maze, and the quiet witness of roots holding the earth while humans counted their wounds.  The trees had not kept ledgers, she realized; they had kept forgiveness, each ring a slow refusal to let sorrow end the story.  When the storm passed, the sprouts were saplings, and Odo returned below with a new map, one that mapped not what dreams were lost, but what they remembered.  The Administration found her charts useless because they lacked totals.  Odo smiled and filed them anyway, knowing that some miracles are so mundane they look like paperwork until the forest grows through the floor.
