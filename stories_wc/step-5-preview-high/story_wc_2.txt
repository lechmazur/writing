Wren was a broken-clock tinkerer who had spent eleven years repairing timepieces that refused to run, and the work left her uncertainly certain about every gear, spring, and second hand she touched.  Above the ash-dusted streets of Osteria, she kept a narrow shop where broken time was the only honest trade left to practice in a city of cinders.  She had learned to improvise because plans in that district collapsed like wet paper, leaving tinkerers to patch futures from whatever scraps the harbor wind provided.  Her quiet motivation was to befriend uncertainty in planning rather than curse it, so she left her door unlocked whenever a coincidence drifted past her window.  The pattern began on a Tuesday, though Tuesdays had always been unremarkable in a town that measured its days by sootfall and tide.  A courier with ink-stained fingers delivered a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and inside lay the admiral's lost compass rose, its brass petals cracked and its needle frozen toward the old harbor district.  No sender was listed, and no one in Osteria would explain how the instrument had survived the fire that had turned the flagship Meridian to blackened ribs at anchor.  Wren decided the honest response was to return the object to its owner, and the only address she possessed was a rusted deed naming a greenhouse on the city's eastern rim.  She found the building slumped behind a wall of ivy, its glass panes clouded, and she had to improvise a route through the swirling leaves in a neglected greenhouse where dead vines clutched her ankles like curious ghosts.  Inside, the air smelled of wet clay and burnt sugar, and the floor was littered with pottery shards that rang like small bells beneath her boots.  Behind the potting shed, a stairwell spiraled down into black basalt, and the air grew cool and thunderous until she emerged onto a ledge beside a subterranean waterfall that roared in the dark.  The compass rose trembled in her palm, its needle locking onto the churning pool as though it recognized a current no surface ship could ever have mapped.  She learned then that Osteria had not been built over its ruins but atop a vaulted cathedral of ash and stone, and that everything precious lay beneath the ashes of the old world.  The admiral had not lost the compass rose; he had hidden it when he realized the city above would one day forget what it stood on and why the harbor smoked at midnight.  When coincidence becomes pattern, Wren noticed that each step she improvised matched the rhythm of the falling water, as if the cavern itself were keeping time for a clock she had not yet seen.  The repeated Tuesday couriers, the greenhouse key, the compass's pull, the waterfall's pulse, they were not random noise but a schedule she had finally learned to read.  A stone archway opened in the spray, and beyond it floated the admiral's log wrapped in oilcloth, resting beside a workbench built for a tinkerer who understood that gears could measure falling water.  The bench held brass sockets, escapements shaped like lily pads, and a cradle that seemed to wait for the cracked rose she carried.  Wren realized the greenhouse above had been a dial, the swirling leaves a hand, and the waterfall the pendulum of an enormous clock hidden beneath the ashes.  She set the compass rose into the cradle, and the needle began to spin, mapping passages that branched like the possibilities she had once feared and tried to schedule away.  A low hum traveled up through the rock, and somewhere above, ash fell upward from the streets, caught in a reverse wind, as the buried machine remembered its function.  Wren did not know what the clock would do to Osteria, and that ignorance no longer felt like failure but like an open door.  To befriend uncertainty in planning, she understood, was not to erase the unknown but to build instruments that honored it, turning broken time into a music that could be trusted only while it lasted.  She stayed by the waterfall, improvising repairs to a mechanism that defied blueprints, while the leaves above spun in a pattern she finally recognized as freedom.  When she climbed back to the greenhouse, the sun was setting, and the ash on the streets had settled into a map she no longer needed to solve.
