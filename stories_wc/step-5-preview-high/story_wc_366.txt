Silas was a reticent lighthouse supply skipper, warmly observant by nature, and he read the coast in small signs before he spoke. 
That evening he reached the islet after the lamp flickered out, and the tower looked like a breath held too long. 
He did not blow the boat horn, because the keepers of that coast trusted stacked stones more than noise. 
On the reef shelf someone had set three flat stones with a piece of driftwood laid across them: do not land, climb the east stair. 
Silas obeyed, since he had helped invent that code in worse weather, and he left his crew anchored beyond the swell. 
The east stair ended in the haunted lantern room, where the great lens stood dark and turned only for the wind. 
Keepers called it haunted because old Emerence had vanished there the winter her own lamp failed, but Silas thought rooms could be haunted by ordinary grief. 
Tonight the room smelled of cold oil and extinguished wicks, and a young keeper named Wren knelt beside the rotation pedestal with her sleeves pushed up. 
Her hands shook as she worked, and Silas noticed the frost in her cuffs, the damp patch on her knee, and the way she kept her back to the dark lens. 
He set down his crate and said only, "The pawl is stripped." 
Wren looked up, surprised that he had seen the fault without touching it. 
"You always did look first," she said. 
They freed the bent brass pawl from the lens track, but the spare in his crate did not fit the new mounting. 
Without rotation the lamp was a bonfire without purpose, and the tender would come at first light. 
Wren's face fell. 
"They will say I let it die," she whispered. 
Silas took a piece of sea glass from his coat pocket, frosted green and smooth as a held breath, and set it on the pedestal where the failing flame could reach it. 
The glass had been a bottle once, broken and remade by the sea, and he carried it to remind himself that safety was a thing one could bottle and carry. 
"Where did you get that?" Wren asked. 
"From the beach where I waited out a storm with nothing but a letter and a stone stack," he said. 
He crossed to the lens and opened a hollow compartment in its base, where the anonymous letter chain had been hidden for forty years. 
Every keeper and skipper wrote one unsigned page to whoever came next, describing small proofs of safety: dry matches, a mended stair, gulls returning early. 
Wren had written her page that morning, but the paper was blank except for one line. 
Silas unfolded it and read, "The stairs hold." 
Then he drew the earliest page from the box, brittle as a moth wing, and showed her the same words in a boy's unsteady hand. 
"That was mine," he said. 
"I wrote it after the storm, and the next keeper answered with stones and flour and a whistle at dawn." 
"So you see why I came to protect the chain," he said. 
Wren wiped her eyes and laughed, a small sound that made the room less haunted. 
Then Silas cut a new pawl from a block of lignum vitae in his crate, shaving it until the lens track accepted it like a key. 
He lit the reserve wick, fitted the wooden pawl, and gave the lens a slow push. 
Light bloomed in the prism, gathered, and swung out over the black water in one long luminescent breath. 
The ache in Silas's chest eased, not because the tower was safe, but because Wren was already writing a new page to the next keeper. 
At dawn he rowed out to the tender and left a stack of three stones on its rail, the old sign for safe passage. 
The tender's captain read it and kept his engines quiet, so no officials came ashore. 
Then Silas returned to the lantern room and added his own unsigned page to the chain. 
He wrote that safety was not the absence of storms but the habit of leaving light for strangers. 
He sealed the box, left the sea glass upon it like a green flame, and climbed down toward his boat. 
Behind him the lamp turned, steady and low, and the lantern room kept its ghosts gently, as a jar keeps light. 
The coast was still dark at its edges, but the chain would carry the feeling onward, one page and one stone at a time.
