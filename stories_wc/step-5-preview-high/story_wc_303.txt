The Velvet Observatory smelled of pressed mulberries and rusted brass at midsummer, when the sun refused to set and the shadows stretched like lazy cats across the domes.  Provenance researcher Sable Quinn adjusted her spectacles and frowned at the transparent violin resting on the cradle of black felt.  It was made of ice crystal that had not melted in three centuries, and it glowed with a faint blue ache that made her teeth hurt.  The empire had sent her to verify its origin before the promise lost its glow, but the ledgers were blank, and the ice refused to speak to instruments.  Sable leaned closer, and the fern in the bronze planter whispered that the violin was not carved but grown, seeded by a composer who had wept into a glacier.  She had been accidentally telepathic with plants since childhood, a condition she treated as professional noise, though tonight the hydroponic roses were shouting against the glass.  They said the instrument was a vessel, that its destiny was perforated with holes through which the future leaked like water through a sieve, and that the empire would drown that future in lead.  Sable ignored them and turned to her true method, the one she kept hidden beneath her cot in a tin box: reading the future in coffee stains.  She poured the last of the bitter midnight roast into a porcelain cup and swirled it three times, as her grandmother had taught her beneath a bruised sky heavy with rain.  When she tipped the cup, the stains left a map of torn constellations, and in each gap she saw the empire's war machines shattering the violin to weaponize its song, silencing the music before it could ever take root.  The plants went silent, which was worse than their chatter, and the velvet walls seemed to darken, drinking the midsummer starlight until the room felt like the inside of a plum, sweet and suffocating and about to burst.  Sable understood then that she had come to the observatory not merely to authenticate a relic but to weigh a dream against an empire, and the scales in her chest had already begun to tip.  She set the violin on the brass scales that the first curator had used to weigh meteor dust, and the metal creaked as if waking from a long sleep.  In one pan she placed the empire's seal, heavy with gold and expectation; in the other, she laid a single seed from the roses that had spoken to her, no larger than a freckle.  The scale did not move until she pressed her palm against the violin's neck and felt the perforated destiny hum against her skin, each hole a doorway where music could escape into the world and take root in soil instead of stone.  The plants cried out that she must propagate it, not preserve it, that the ice was only a husk and the dream inside demanded soil, water, and the patience of roots.  Sable thought of the empire's archivists, who would seal the violin in lead and silence, and she thought of the coffee-stained future where no music grew at all, only dust and victory parades beneath a gray sun.  With hands that trembled like aspen leaves, she lifted the violin to the observatory's open dome, where midsummer air was warm and thick with the buzz of bees and the salt of distant stars.  She played one note, a clear, shattered sound that made the transparent ice crystal weep and glisten and finally, mercifully, begin to melt.  As the note propagated outward, the violin dissolved into a thousand luminous motes, each one drifting down to the velvet floors and the garden beds beyond, where they settled into seed and silence.  The roses caught the light; the moss began to sing; the perforated destiny of the instrument became a living choir that no empire could catalogue, contain, or command.  Sable watched the last of the glow fade from the cradle and felt the mulberry ache settle in her chest, sweet and dark and ripe with the loss of something she had never truly owned.  She had chosen the dream, and before the promise lost its final light, the observatory bloomed with a music that would outlast every throne and every soldier who had ever marched beneath a borrowed banner.  The plants, generous in their victory, whispered that they would keep her secret, and in the morning the empire would find only a puddle of clear water and a researcher who smiled like a stained cup, empty and finally free.
