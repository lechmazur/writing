The wreck of the Meridian lay half-buried in black silt four hundred feet down, and Mara descended to it the way other women entered churches.  She was a wreck-diver ghost whisperer, a trade that paid poorly and demanded that she listen to the drowned when their voices scraped like barnacles against her ribs.  But she did not dive for profit; she dove to salvage something pure from the wreckage.  On her third descent into the ship's frozen atrium, her fingers closed around a melted snow globe.  The glass had slumped into a teardrop shape, and the tiny palace inside was half-collapsed, but a silver flake of snow still clung to the curved surface as if refusing to melt.  She carried it back to her candle-lit shrine carved into glacier ice, where blue light filtered through ancient compression and shadows moved like held breath.  There she set the globe upon an altar of packed snow, and the ghosts gathered.  They were not the loud ones.  They whispered through what Mara had come to understand as windows of connection, thin places where the living and the dead brushed fingertips.  The globe was one such window.  The ice shrine was another.  The third was far stranger.  For three weeks, strangers in the coastal town of Vesper had been dreaming of the Meridian.  In each dream, a pale figure stood at the periphery, a cameo appearance in a landscape of ordinary sleep: washing dishes, walking dogs, waiting at bus stops.  Mara collected their accounts in a ledger bound in sealskin, and by analyzing cameo appearances in strangers' dreams, she began to map the dead woman's final route.  The ghost was Elise, the ship's archivist, and she had not died in the storm that the official reports blamed.  Elise had been pushed.  The truth was a blade wrapped in wool, and Mara weaves truth carefully.  She could not storm the harbor office with the rantings of sleepers.  She needed corroboration, a thread so strong that pulling it would unravel the lie without strangling the witnesses.  Night after night she returned to the shrine, candles dripping wax onto glacier floor, and held the melted snow globe against the windows of connection.  Inside the warped glass, the miniature palace seemed to shift when she tilted it toward borrowed starlight that leaked through the ice.  The globe had been Elise's, a souvenir from a port she had visited as a girl, before cargo manifests and black budgets had turned her into a liability.  In the dreams of the strangers, Elise always faced the same direction: north, toward the glacier where the currents had hidden the evidence.  Mara understood then that the woman was not merely haunting.  She was guiding.  On the seventh night, the pattern completed itself in a child's dream, the clearest of all the cameos because innocence makes thinner glass.  The child drew what she had seen: a figure in an orange life vest standing not on the bridge but in a cargo hold, sealing a valve.  Mara took the drawing and the ledger to the harbor master, but she did not accuse.  Instead, she laid them out like a tarot spread, letting the cameo appearances speak in the borrowed starlight of her lantern.  The harbor master went pale.  The valve had been opened deliberately to flood the hold and sink the Meridian, burying proof of illegal salvage rights beneath the sea.  Mara exposed the crime not by screaming into the storm but by threading silence into evidence until the lie could not hold its shape.  In the weeks that followed, divers raised the manifests, and the town learned that the wreckage had contained both greed and grace.  But Mara remained at the shrine.  She held the melted snow globe one final time and spoke to Elise across the windows of connection.  "You can rest," she said, and the silver flake inside the glass dissolved into ordinary snow, uncorrupted at last.  To salvage something pure from the wreckage, she had learned, was not to rescue objects but to return a voice to the dark and trust the light it left behind.  Outside, the glacier groaned, and the candles guttered, but in the hush between beats, the shrine felt less like a tomb and more like a window thrown open to borrowed starlight.
