After humans hibernate, the coastal cave network belongs to the tide and to Wren, the sensory map maker assigned to the sleepward caves. 
Her guild keeps the passages legible until the dreamers stir, and Wren has learned to read stone the way others read faces. 
Before the long sleep, every family leaves a token at the threshold: a key, a wooden cup, a stone warmed by a living hand. 
The guild calls these transitional objects because they carry a person from the waking world into the dark and back again. 
Wren has always wanted to become the voice of the unknown, not a prophet, but a patient translator for whatever waits on the far side of sleep. 
She believes the caves are not empty; they are crowded with feelings that have cooled without vanishing. 
Her method is heat-mapping emotions, a discipline of thermal brushes, slate, and steady breathing. 
Heat is a language, her teacher said, and stone remembers longer than skin. 
On the third morning after the tide turns, Wren finds an elm leaf fossil wedged behind a barnacled gate. 
The leaf lies in a bed of dried kelp, its veins dark as old ink. 
When she lifts it, the gauges on her wrist flare, then settle into a pattern she has never seen. 
The elm leaf fossil is factually mystical; its carbon lattice holds a chill that rises when she speaks and falls when she is silent, and every gauge agrees. 
It was left by the first sensory map maker, a woman who had mapped the network before the guild had rules. 
Wren sets the fossil in a thermal cradle and begins to heat-map the chamber around it. 
The stone is warm with longing, but the warmth does not wander like grief or anger; it braids itself into a slow, deliberate music. 
Hours pass, or perhaps only the sea passes, and the map begins to resolve into a voice. 
It is not human, and it is not hostile. 
It says, in the only language heat can speak, that the sleepers are dreaming of doors. 
Wren understands then that the fossil is a key and not a relic. 
She follows the braided warmth through a narrow passage until the roof opens into the drowned gallery. 
Calcite hangs in chandeliers, and barnacled pillars rise out of black water bright with plankton. 
The resonance shelf lies above the waterline, and the only way up is through the flooded chamber. 
Wren checks her air, clips a buoyancy vest to her hips, and steps off the ledge so the salt water can help her float. 
For a moment she hangs between the sea and the stone, suspended in a green hush that has no name. 
Then she kicks once and rises toward the shelf, her boots scraping mineral crust. 
She sets the fossil into the socket of the old transmitter, and the machine wakes with a sound like rain on slate. 
Wren has one choice: she can broadcast the raw heat of the caves, or she can teach the machine to speak the unknown in a human cadence. 
Fear tells her to keep the discovery buried, but she has never wanted safety. 
She has wanted to become the voice of the unknown, and this is the first sentence it has trusted her to carry. 
With careful fingers she tunes the transmitter to the fossil's cold and the cave's braided warmth. 
The machine begins to sing, low and patient, a lullaby for waking minds. 
The song travels down the coastal cave network, through every sleepward chamber and every hall where families wait in the dark. 
It does not wake them, because the hour for waking has not come. 
Instead it settles into the transitional objects they left behind, warming the keys, the cups, and the stones until they hum like embers. 
When the sleepers finally rise, those tokens will feel familiar in a world they have half forgotten, and they will follow the hum to Wren's map. 
She will be waiting with her slate, her thermal brush, and the elm leaf fossil burning cold against her palm. 
She will tell them what the caves dreamed, what the tide remembered, and what the unknown asked them to become. 
Her voice will not be loud, but it will be exact, and it will be kind. 
Long after the guild names her the first voice of the network, children will still ask how one person learned to speak for stone. 
The honest answer is that she did not speak for the caves alone. 
She listened until the unknown became a partner, and then she let both of them speak through her. 
In the ledger of her life, that is the only map she ever needed to draw.
