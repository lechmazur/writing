The valley had been quiet for seventeen years, ever since the last speaker of the Old Tongue had perished. 
Maren, a copper-veined storyteller, had inherited the duty of listening to the towers of bone that rose from the canyon of endless echoes. 
Her veins caught the light like buried wire, a mark of her lineage, and she moved among the ribcages of extinct leviathans with a patience that outlasted stone. 
She was quietly articulate, speaking rarely, yet when she did her words fell with the precision of chipped flint. 
The elders had tasked her with a desperate errand: to harness the canyon's acoustic ghosts before they scattered into silence. 
But sound alone was not enough; her true motivation was to translate texture into sound, so that the memory of touch might outlive the death of a language. 
She carried in her palm a flawed diamond rumored to be cursed, its central inclusion jagged as a broken tooth. 
The gem had belonged to the last queen of the Old Tongue, and those who held it too long began to dream in a dialect they could not speak. 
Maren did not fear the curse, for she had no mother tongue left to lose. 
At dawn she climbed the spine of the largest bone tower, where the wind carved a tunnel of echoes between two calcified femurs. 
She pressed the diamond against the bone and waited for the vibration to bloom. 
It came as a low thrum, the acoustic signature of a hand brushing grain, of a cheek pressed to wool, of earth falling on a coffin. 
The diamond drank these textures and released them as frequencies, but the signal was raw, chaotic, insufficient. 
She needed a method to organize the noise, a system older than grammar. 
So she watched the sky. 
A flock of migratory plovers banked and split above the canyon, their bodies writing calligraphy against the clouds. 
Through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight, Maren read the syntax of breath and wing. 
Each turn of the flock mapped a cadence; each scatter mapped a pause; each reunion mapped the return of a refrain. 
She began to hum, matching the bird-song to the diamond's output, weaving texture into rhythm. 
The tunnel of echoes amplified her voice, sending it ricocheting through the canyon until every bone tower sang in sympathy. 
It was then that the curse stirred. 
The diamond grew warm, and the flawed center began to glow with a sickly amber light. 
Visions flooded her: markets where price was a gesture, funerals where mourning was a fabric, lullabies woven from the rasp of sandstone. 
She saw the Old Tongue not as words but as textures, a language of surfaces and pressures that the bone towers had stored across centuries. 
Maren steadied her breath, quietly articulate even in the maelstrom, and anchored herself by naming each sensation she encountered. 
To name a texture was to give it syntax; to give it syntax was to save it from the death of a language. 
She harnessed the flock's trajectory as a score, conducting the echoes with precise movements of her free hand. 
The birds seemed to understand, wheeling in tighter spirals as the canyon's resonance aligned with her intent. 
When the final chord faded, the diamond cracked, releasing the curse as a harmless shimmer of dust. 
Maren opened her palm and found the gem reduced to a common stone, but the canyon was changed. 
The air now carried harmonics that recorded the brush of wind on skin, the grit of dust on tongue, the weight of tears on cloth. 
She had not revived the Old Tongue, but she had built a bridge across its grave. 
Travelers who entered the canyon afterward reported that if they placed their hands upon the towers of bone, they could hear their own touch remembered in music. 
Maren remained as the copper-veined storyteller, quietly articulate in her vigilance, guarding the tunnel of echoes where texture and sound had become one. 
And in the flight of every bird across the canyon, she still read the grammar of a world that refused to be forgotten.
