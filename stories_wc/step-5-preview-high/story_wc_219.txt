After making first contact, the world had grown strangely homesick, and no ministry could name the cure. 
Dr. Maren Vessel arrived at the rainwashed tower in spring with rain in her cuffs and a specimen case against her ribs. 
They called her a luminous skeptic because the contact had left faint phosphorescence beneath her skin and because she still refused to call it holy. 
Her colleagues called her academically rebellious for arguing that homecoming was not a sacrament but a measurable reaction of soil, scent, and memory. 
The ministry wanted hymns; Maren wanted an instrument, so she designed an artificial pilgrimage that anyone could repeat without faith. 
She chose the tower because its conservatory had once blended meteorology and mourning, and because spring rain there smelled of wet bronze and cut hay. 
Her students converged on the old estate via dusty footprints left on an overgrown sculpture path, each step stirring pollen from statues of birds and unruly gardeners. 
In her case lay a fly amber, cloudy and thumb-sized, with a single insect frozen midflight inside yellow resin. 
It had belonged to her mother, who believed that anything sealed in resin was trying to go home. 
Maren did not believe that, exactly, but she had measured the amber's volatile bloom after rain and found terpenes that matched the tower's old rain barrels. 
They came as they always did after first contact: quietly, in pairs, carrying coats that smelled of other cities. 
A retired chemist, two orphans from the flood plains, and a priest who no longer kneeled stood dripping in the tower's bronze quiet. 
The light through the rainwashed glass was the color of old medals, and nobody spoke above the sound of water finding its way down the stairs. 
Maren set the amber on a brass table and opened the vents so the room could breathe. 
Her stated motivation, printed on the permission slips and ignored by the press, was to understand the chemistry of homecoming. 
She did not promise that anyone would feel returned. 
She promised only that the route would be honest: moss, dust, stone, the long walk, and the same question asked in the same order. 
When the last pilgrim reached the tower, the footprints on the sculpture path had dried into pale masks of arrival, and the group began to converge in the round room. 
Maren asked each of them to hold the fly amber until it warmed, then to name one street, kitchen, or doorway they missed. 
The retired chemist spoke of a stairwell that had smelled of iodine; the orphans spoke of a river bridge; the priest spoke of an open window in a borrowed room. 
As they spoke, the amber released a thin scent of pine and old honey, and the spectrometer on Maren's wrist recorded a rise in shared breath, salt, and heat. 
The report she had been punished for writing claimed that the visitors had not sent a message at all, only a catalyst that made human memory volatile. 
Other departments preferred prophets and treaties; Maren preferred titration, and so she had built this small artificial pilgrimage to test her heresy in public. 
Now the data arrived as something more difficult than numbers. 
When the orphans set their hands on the amber, the resin warmed and gave off a faint crack, and the priest bowed his head not in prayer but in recognition. 
Maren felt the phosphorescence under her own collarbone answer like a struck match, and she wrote one word in her notebook: evidence. 
She had expected grief to be a solvent, but it behaved more like a bridge: two shores held apart by a smell, a sound, a temperature, and the willingness to walk toward them. 
The artificial pilgrimage had not invented that bridge, but it had swept it clean and asked people to cross in company. 
Outside, spring rain began again, filling the footprints on the sculpture path until the whole hillside seemed to converge toward the tower in wet prints. 
In the round room, no one called it sacred. 
They simply stood in the bronze quiet, warming a piece of ancient resin between them, and let the chemistry of homecoming do its patient work.
