The forgotten underwater temple kept its lonesome splendor in silted tiers of pearl and basalt.  Ilva had become its only witness, its keeper, and its distress signal sender.  For eleven years she had fed a hollow beacon with charged salt, repeating a plea no one answered.  Now the beacon had finally been heard, and that was the trouble.  A salvage crew was homing toward her signal, dragging anchors that would crack the vaulted dark.  They would strip the temple for coral and coin, and leave the water barren.  Ilva had stayed too long to let that happen, and her tanks told her she had stayed longer than sanity allowed.  In the sanctum she unsealed a satchel and lifted out the crystal perfume vial.  It had belonged to her sister, a scent thief who had laughed at depth and drowned in sunlight.  The glass was cold, but Ilva knew its hidden attribute, passed down in whispers: it touches memories.  Any hand that held it long enough inhaled a room it had lost, a voice it had missed.  That was why she had returned to the temple to harness the ephemeral.  Sound died quickly in water, scent died faster, and memory died fastest of all.  But the temple gathered what the sea forgot.  Over centuries its chambers had collected a gathered hush, a sediment of unspoken prayers and final breaths.  Ilva meant to distill that hush into scent and seal it in the vial before the crew cut the silence open.  The reservoir behind the altar was locked by a braided tangle of kelp, signal line, and old rope.  To open it she would have to unsnarl the knot with bare fingers, and the water was too cold for bare fingers.  Her instinct shrieked at her to surface, to abandon the altar, to claw toward the lights of the sub.  Instead she worked by overriding instinct, one trembling breath at a time.  The first loop of line was stubborn as a root.  The second hid a hook that had once held her sister's lamp.  As time runs short, Ilva told herself, and pulled the third strand free.  Her gauge screamed its small red warning, but she did not look at it.  The gathered hush moved against her skin like a held note, like the hush inside a chapel before singing.  When the knot fell apart, the reservoir exhaled, and the vial filled with a silence that smelled of rain on stone.  Ilva sealed it with a silver stopper, and in the dark she saw her sister turn, smiling, with a mouth full of light.  The vision did not speak; it simply stood in the lonesome splendor of the temple, unafraid.  Ilva understood then that she had not come to rescue the past but to keep it from vanishing.  The beacon pulsed again, impatient, dragging the future closer.  She tucked the vial inside her suit, where its warmth touched memories of low tides and her sister's wet hair.  Then she swam toward the outer gate, carrying the silence like a lantern.  Her tanks were nearly empty, and every ligament in her body begged her to ascend.  She overrode that instinct too, slowly, deliberately, because panic would break the vial.  The ruined corridor opened into the little cave where she had first sent the distress call.  There she set the crystal perfume vial on a basalt shelf, aimed the beacon at it, and let the signal carry its scent into the world.  The crew would find the distress signal sender calm among the ruins, with no treasure but a small glass hush.  They would call it a relic and sell it, but the first buyer would open it and weep for a sea they had never seen.  That was enough.  Ilva closed her eyes as the sub lights entered the temple, and the water held her like a memory.
