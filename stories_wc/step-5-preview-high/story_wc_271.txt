After the last lighthouse keeper died, the arboretum perched on a seaside cliff became a place where only the stubborn came to work. 
Sofia, a drone strike wedding survivor, had arrived there three winters ago with a crumpled photograph of her cousins' wedding folded inside her coat. 
The strike had happened during the toast, and the image of silk and candles now lived forever in her pocket, creased and soft from worry. 
She had been the maid of honor, and that creased picture was the only piece of the day that survived the collapse. 
She was quietly talented at coaxing salt-battered soil to hold roots, and her grafted terraces climbed the cliff like green stairways. 
No one had taught her the work; she had learned it by watching how the wind shaped the pines and copying their patience. 
The keeper had left behind notes about a glass dome he called a pearl of stillness, a sphere of calm where the wind could not reach. 
The keeper had been the last of a long line, and when he left the cliff for the mainland hospital, no one replaced him. 
Sofia maintained it because she wanted to anchor optimism in realism, not in fantasy; she believed the cliff could be healed if she was honest about what it could bear. 
On the morning the supply boat was late, a low mechanical hum rolled across the water, and her body began to freeze. 
Through somatic markers she had trained herself to notice the first signs: the cold prickle in her fingertips, the stone in her chest, the jaw that locked like a rusted hinge. 
She had read that the body kept its own score, and she trusted those felt truths more than any map. 
She named each sensation without judgment, and the naming kept her from shattering into the old panic. 
The hum was only a surveying plane, not a weapon, but her nervous system did not know the difference. 
Still, the freeze was a tide that could pull her under if she fought it, so she let it pass while counting her breaths. 
She stood among the sea kale and breathed until her shoulders dropped an inch. 
Then she saw that the dome's southern panel had cracked, and a storm was forecast to strike before noon. 
If the glass failed, the pearl of stillness would flood with brine and kill the rare mosses and ferns inside. 
The plants inside were not merely ornamental; they were seed stock for a coastline that might one day need to regrow. 
Sofia moved with purpose, her quiet talent turning to engineering as she lashed tarps and braces to the metal ribs. 
Her fingers remembered the shape of her cousin's veil from the crumpled photograph, but she did not let memory stop her hands. 
By the time the first squall hit, the dome was shored and the interior air was still and humid. 
Outside, gulls screamed and the sea threw itself against the rocks, but within the glass everything held its breath. 
She sat on the bench at the dome's heart, pulling out the photograph and smoothing it against her knee. 
The faces were blurred, but she could still see the archway of flowers behind them. 
She remembered how the dress had caught the light, how her cousin had laughed with her head thrown back. 
A pearl of stillness, she thought, was not the absence of noise, but a small defended room inside the storm. 
After the last lighthouse keeper, the world had expected the coast to go dark, yet here was green life, stubbornly rooted. 
The beacon had gone dark, yet the terraces she had planted now held the soil against erosion. 
Sofia understood then that realism was not the enemy of hope; it was the ground that hope needed to grow. 
She folded the photograph and placed it in the niche beside the door, where the next person might find it and understand. 
Then she walked back along the cliff paths, grafting knife in pocket, ready to prune what was dead and tend what could live.
