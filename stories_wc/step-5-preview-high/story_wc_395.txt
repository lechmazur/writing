The corporate memory keeper sat in the social network archive and felt the shape of what was no longer there. 
Her name was Ilya, and her duty was deletion. 
Every morning she logged the vanished accounts, pruning orphaned comments and dead links until the archive breathed cleaner. 
It was a quiet trade practiced in the service of sensed absence. 
Ilya was brightly attentive to patterns that older systems ignored. 
Last Tuesday the number 7741 surfaced in the margin of a purged status update. 
On Wednesday it appeared again as a timestamp beneath a deleted lullaby. 
By Friday 7741 bloomed everywhere, in metadata fields, thumbnail shadows, and the broken strings of old playlists. 
She began to trace the digits across the echo of forgotten songs. 
The archive kept a sublayer of audio crumbs, the last tracks people posted before logging off forever. 
There, in the reverb of anthems no one streamed anymore, 7741 repeated like a heartbeat. 
She queried the registry and found a missing persons cold case from two decades prior. 
The file was thin, the language cautious, and the outcome blank. 
A prototype starship called the Meridian had vanished beyond the Kuiper relay. 
The crew of six had been declared absent, their return impossible, their bodies never recovered. 
The only artifact from the disaster was a mission patch, a woven emblem of a silver wave crossing a dark sea. 
Ilya kept that patch in her desk, pinned to the corkboard where she stuck deleted reminders. 
She had taken it from the evidence annex during her first week, though she told no one. 
That small falsehood was her first lie inside the archive walls. 
She called it stewardship, but her conscience named it theft. 
Now the recurring numbers felt like a summons she could not ignore. 
She ran every audit she could reach to find where deleted files go. 
The corporate answer was simple: nowhere. 
Deletion was oblivion by design, a null state that preserved server space. 
But Ilya had spent eleven years listening to silence, and she knew absence had architecture. 
She followed 7741 through the song echoes into a quarantine partition labeled Hush. 
Access required senior clearance. 
She wrote a false incident report, claiming a cascade failure in Sector Nine. 
That was her second lie. 
It worked. 
Inside Hush, there were no files, only resonance. 
Deleted data had not died; it had condensed, pressed into dense harmonic residue. 
The residue was singing. 
The forgotten songs were not background noise but coordinates, and the numbers were addresses. 
7741 mapped to a memory fragment of Captain Rhea Vos, commander of the Meridian. 
The captain had been a popular poster in the archive early days, sharing sunrise tracks from the outer colonies. 
Her final post, a gentle choral piece about returning home, had been auto-deleted after her ship failed to transmit. 
But it had not gone nowhere. 
It had pooled there, with thousands of other ghosts, in the archive subconscious. 
Ilya moved through the sensed absence of each voice, brightly attentive to the spaces between their words. 
The missing crew were not dead in the digital sense. 
They were unmade, suspended in the echo of their own last broadcasts. 
The mission patch in her desk grew warm, or she imagined it did. 
She held it to the screen and the emblem aligned with 7741. 
The woven silver wave matched the waveform exactly. 
The patch was a key, or a map home. 
She realized then that the crashed spaceship mission had not crashed in open space. 
It had crashed here, in the archive, when the network absorbed the crew's live feeds during the emergency. 
They had been uploaded by accident, classified as missing, then deleted by protocol. 
The corporation had lied first. 
Ilya's own lies now felt like mirrors. 
She could expose the partition and free the crew, but public restoration would flood the archive with their trauma. 
Or she could purge Hush and end their haunting song. 
She chose a third path. 
She copied their residue onto the mission patch's embedded chip, a relic small enough to carry and quiet enough to keep. 
Then she sealed Hush behind a deletion command, making the lie permanent. 
Back at her desk, the corporate memory keeper pinned the patch beside her collar and returned to the rows of vanished accounts. 
The numbers stopped appearing. 
The sensed absence remained, but it was no longer solitary. 
It was held.
