The bunker builder's child had learned to hate every sealed hatch their parent forged, and so they fled to the xenial trellis above the clouds, where hospitality was measured in open air and drifting lattices.  There, silence was not empty but seasoned, and the child had become a collector of its flavors, tasting the briny hush of high cirrus and the copper taste of stalled wind.  They kept jars of each silence on the trellis shelves, labeled by season, but none matched the legend whispered by the cloud farmers who tended the vines.  The child carried a tiny porcelain doll from their buried childhood, its painted smile chipped by a shovel, holding it whenever the trellis swayed like a held breath.  The doll was intangibly tangible, warm to the touch yet impossible to grip tightly, as if made of remembered safety rather than ceramic.  Villagers of the cloud farms spoke of the gilded hush, a silence so rare and precious that it was said to taste of sunlight preserved in amber, audible only when gravity inverted for an hour and the earth released its grip.  The child had spent years trying to sample it, but every attempt failed because they did not understand the method: by limiting exposure.  Their parent had built bunkers by sealing everything out, and the child, conversely, believed that flavor came from shutting oneself in, a lesson learned from breathing recycled air in cramped tunnels.  They had fled the last bunker three winters ago, carrying only the doll and a water canteen, and had climbed the service ladders until the air grew thin and bright.  One evening, as the cloud sea darkened to violet, the familiar pressure vanished, and gravity inverted for an hour, turning the trellis into a hanging garden over an abyss of rising stars.  Up became down, yet the child clung to the railing, their claustrophobia transformed into a stretching contracted terror, a feeling of being pulled apart while squeezed into an infinite lung.  They opened their hand and let the tiny porcelain doll rest on the railing, exposing it alone to the inversion, knowing they must limit their own contact to taste what was coming.  Limiting exposure meant tasting only at the edges of sensation, like sipping wine from a sealed jar, and the child bit their tongue to keep from crying out.  As the doll sat in the floating hush, its porcelain surface began to hairline, and from the cracks a silver root pushed forth, then a stem, then a bud, the action to sprout unfolding in reverse against the falling rain that climbed upward.  The bud opened into an ear-shaped flower, and the child finally heard it: the gilded hush, thick as honey and bright as struck metal, flooding the trellis with a warmth that did not burn.  In that gilded hush, they heard their parent's hammer stop mid-swing and the bunker doors sigh open, not from force but from release.  But the flavor could only be held in the intangibly tangible vessel, so the child did not seize the doll; they simply breathed beside it, letting the silence collect in their chest without grasping.  When the hour ended, gravity snapped back, and the rain returned to earth, but the doll remained rooted to the railing, now a small tree of porcelain leaves that chimed with every gust.  The child plucked one leaf, not to own it, but to taste, and found the gilded hush stored there, mellow and endless.  Travelers who visited the xenial trellis would sometimes hear chiming leaves and pause, tasting something golden on their tongues, and they would leave without understanding why they felt both lighter and more confined.  They stayed on the trellis after that, no longer a refugee from buried rooms but a keeper of open flavors, understanding that the deepest silences are gathered only when one limits exposure and lets the intangible grow tangible.
