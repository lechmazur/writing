The crystal gazer climbed the spiral stair into the projection booth, where dust hung like a held breath. 
She had been hired not to read futures but to recover what the past had misfiled. 
Below her, the vintage movie palace slept, its auditorium a vast dark lung exhaling the smell of velvet and iron. 
Her name was Odile, and her mind worked like an architect of impossible corridors. 
Spatially imaginative, she could feel the empty rooms behind the walls, the air pockets where objects were stored. 
Tonight she hunted the place where unused love was kept. 
The client, a widow, had spoken in the hushed thunder of someone who still listened for a door that never opened. 
Odile ran her fingers along the rusted rewinds, the splice block, the cans of brittle nitrate. 
A radio hissed from the corner, half-songs drifting in and out. 
While forgotten lyrics searched for tune, she worked, letting the broken melodies orient her. 
In a drawer wedged beside the lamphouse, she found a film strip, its celluloid curled like a question. 
It was not a movie. 
Odile held it to her cheek first, then to her palm, beginning a psychometry reading. 
Images rose through her skin: a boy at a desk, a woman at a window, words written and torn and rewritten. 
The strip held the frames of letters never sent, goodbye letters pressed between acetate and time. 
Each sprocket hole punched a hole in her composure. 
She laid the strip in the steel developing tray she had brought, mixing the fixer with water from a cracked thermos. 
The chemicals would incubate the latent silver halides, waking the images that had never seen light. 
As the film floated, she pressed both hands to the wood of the booth and extended her awareness outward. 
The vintage movie palace projection booth was a skull, and she was mapping the hollows. 
She sensed a cavity beneath the floor, a false compartment lined with tinfoil and taped letters. 
The forgotten lyrics in the radio caught a rhythm, then lost it, still searching. 
Odile did not rush. 
She understood that unused love does not evaporate; it accretes in the architecture of grief. 
The film strip darkened, letters emerging in sepia ghosts across the frames. 
"I am sorry," one read. 
"I never stopped," read another. 
A third simply said, "Keep the red umbrella." 
These were not her memories, but the psychometry reading let her hold them with the weight of heirlooms. 
She watched the last frame incubate fully, revealing a map drawn in a lover's handwriting. 
The map showed the theater's foundation, but with a room the blueprints had never named. 
She pried up a floorboard, her spatially imaginative sense confirming the hollow above the carbon arc. 
Inside, wrapped in a velvet curtain scrap, lay a biscuit tin stuffed with paper. 
The widow's husband had written hundreds of goodbye letters to a wife he never left. 
He had addressed them to no one, stamped them with silence, and stored them where only dust and darkness could keep them. 
Odile carried the tin to the projector's small round window and read by the streetlamp glow. 
The letters spoke of breakfasts, of small lies, of a love so constant it had become furniture. 
They were unused not because they were unlived, but because they were unnecessary. 
The husband had loved too fully to need words, and so the words had waited here. 
While forgotten lyrics searched for tune below her, the crystal gazer understood the commission's true shape. 
She descended with the tin and the developed strip, her boots ringing on the brass rail. 
In the lobby, the widow stood beneath the broken marquee, her coat smelling of rain. 
Odile did not speak of maps or hidden cavities. 
She placed the film strip in the woman's hands, then the tin, and let the goodbye letters finish their long journey. 
The widow opened one page, and the theater seemed to inhale, a hushed thunder rolling somewhere beyond the roof. 
Odile left them there, the film strip glowing faintly in the dark like a fuse that had chosen not to explode. 
She had found where unused love was stored: not in heaven, not in loss, but in the small locked rooms people build behind their ribs. 
The vintage movie palace projection booth kept its secret no longer, though the dust would never fully settle. 
Outside, the radio in the booth caught a melody at last, but the song had already ended. 
The crystal gazer walked into a city that looked different now, full of invisible cupboards. 
She knew every wall concealed a letter, every attic an unsent tenderness. 
That was not sad. 
It was only geography.
