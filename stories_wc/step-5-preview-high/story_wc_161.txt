Mira Sallow, a courier pigeon trainer, had not flown a pigeon in three years when she climbed into the vintage bookshop attic to sort her guardian Faye Bell's estate. 
Dust bloomed and withered in the only light, a column falling through a single skylight. 
Faye had called it the reading room, but the ledgers, broken chairs, and seed sacks made it a roost. 
Mira had reinvented her personal history so many times that even she half believed she had merely been a shy clerk. 
The truth was that she had trained courier pigeons for an army that lost its war and then blamed its messengers. 
After a flight of birds never came home, she had sworn never to ask another animal to trust her direction. 
Faye's paper trails led everywhere: receipts for grain, penciled notes about weather, invoices addressed to false names. 
In the back of a hymnal Mira found a hand-drawn map of chimneys, steeples, and three libraries. 
Beneath the map lay a velvet pouch with a Mars meteorite chip, pitted, heavy, and faintly warm. 
Faye had kept it in a brass balance pan, as though it were a weight for something more than gold. 
Mira read until the light withered, and the story assembled itself out of dust and debts. 
Faye had spent her last decade gathering erased songs, and Mira found her own charge in the work, to preserve the echo of lost songs. 
She hid the music inside account books, where the columns formed a ledger and the marginal notes were melodies. 
The libraries on the map were archives that had agreed to receive only by bird, because paper trails could be intercepted. 
Faye's last letter told Mira to finish the flight, but it was unsigned, and Mira did not yet trust the handwriting of the dead. 
The bookshop owner's apprentice, Joss, found the dovecote Mira had nailed shut and opened it without asking. 
Joss had a bandaged wrist, a habit of honesty, and a crooked loft above the drying racks. 
Across learning to trust again, the attic slowly changed from a tomb to a workshop. 
Mira taught four pigeons to carry folios, and she taught herself to carry company. 
She judged each flight by mapping the swirl of dust motes under a single skylight. 
Where the motes rose, she knew warm air could hold a load; where they sank, she kept the birds home. 
It was a balance she performed every morning, the scales pan on one side and the pouch with the Mars meteorite chip on the other. 
The folios had to balance too, so the bird did not list and tear the old paper. 
The chip became her counterweight, her compass of doubt, and her proof that Faye had not lied about the shipment. 
A shipping manifest named it as cargo sent to Faye by an astronomer who had studied the canals of Mars through a bookshop telescope. 
That astronomer had been Mira's father, another fact she had carefully misplaced while reinventing her personal history. 
The chip had been his way of saying that distance could be crossed if the message mattered. 
On the morning of the flight, frost withered on the skylight, and the city below smelled of wet paper and smoke. 
Mira strapped three folios to Bramble, the boldest bird, and set the chip in the pan until the load hung level. 
Joss held the attic window while Mira watched the dust. 
The motes were blooming upward in a slow spiral, and she read them as clearly as a compass. 
She opened her hand, and Bramble went, not as a soldier but as a singer with notes bound to her breast. 
Two more birds followed with the second and third parts, and the attic seemed to exhale. 
Mira waited, balanced between the urge to run after them and the need to let them find the way. 
Joss returned the empty bands at dusk, and each carried a small receipt stamped by a different library. 
The songs had arrived, three echoes safe from the fire and the forgetting. 
That night Mira told Joss the whole truth about the birds that had died, the routes she had missed, and the clerk she had pretended to be. 
She did not reinvent her personal history; she let it stand, scarred and repairable. 
Joss, in turn, confessed that the dovecote had belonged to Faye and that the shop was failing. 
The sale of the Mars meteorite chip, once the flight was proven, would pay for the roof and the archives. 
Mira felt the old fear bloom and wither in the same breath, and she chose the trust anyway. 
She would stay, she decided, and train birds to carry songs until the attic bloomed with music and rotted with rain, both at once.
