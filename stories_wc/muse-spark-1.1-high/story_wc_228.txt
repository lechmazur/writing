<story>Thomas had been the nostalgic quay painter for forty years, and everyone in Greyport knew his easel as well as the tide tables. [23]
He was nostalgic by trade, but intimately aloof by nature, selling harbor scenes to tourists while keeping his real work turned to the wall. [47]
After the mirror cracks, his wife used to say when he lingered too long on a memory, you get seven years of seeing clear. [71]
She died three winters ago, leaving behind a cracked vanity mirror in their cottage and a silence he could not paint over. [93]
The morning the mirror finally fell and split, he took it as an ending. [107]
Instead, he cleaned up the glass, packed his watercolors, and walked to the quay out of habit. [123]
He did not set up his easel. [130]
For the first time in decades, he kept walking past the fishing boats, past the posted warnings, toward the coastal cave network that cut under the headland. [157]
Tourists thought the caves were picturesque; locals knew they flooded and forgot. [169]
Thomas knew them too, but that day he needed a place where the past could not watch him work. [188]
He entered with a lantern, the tide low, his knees aching from cold and age. [203]
He intended to make one mark on the last tunnel and turn back. [216]
He had always scoffed that a wrong turn leads to a hidden oasis, but his lantern swung left where it should have swung right, and the basalt corridor opened. [245]
The sound changed. [248]
Inside was a vaulted chamber with a floor of sand so white it seemed to hold light, and in its center a tide pool that never touched the sea. [277]
Above, a narrow fissure let a blade of noon light fall straight down, sealing the pool in what he instantly understood as temporary eternity. [301]
It would exist only until the tide returned, only until the sun moved, yet while it lasted it was perfect and absolute. [323]
On the sand beside the pool lay a single seashell, painted, its whorl covered in tiny blue harbor scenes he recognized as his own early style. [349]
He knelt, hands shaking, and realized his wife must have placed it here years ago when she still walked the headland alone. [371]
She had kept his first failed experiments, the ones he threw away. [383]
On the back, in her handwriting, were the words For when you finally stop painting the quay. [400]
The promise rose in him, unspoken for twenty years, to keep a hidden promise to oneself that he would one day paint not what people wanted to remember, but what he could not forget. [434]
He understood now that after the mirror cracks you see not your reflection but what you avoided painting. [452]
He opened his tin of watercolors, though he had no paper. [463]
The cave wall was damp and dark, perfect. [471]
With his thumb, with seawater, with light, he began to paint the true quay, not as it looked at noon for tourists, but as it felt at dusk when the boats came home empty. [505]
He worked until his back screamed, until the chamber filled with a mural of all his unsold days. [523]
And then, with the last of the light, he had to reveal the one figure he had never allowed himself to place in any harbor scene. [549]
Himself, not as the intimately aloof observer on the dock, but as the boy who missed his father's boat and watched it disappear. [572]
He painted his young self holding the painted seashell, original and copy merging on stone. [587]
The tide began to whisper at the entrance, and the fissure light thinned. [600]
He knew the water would erase the sand floor, but not the wall. [613]
That was the nature of temporary eternity, to let something remain after the moment that made it sacred had passed. [633]
He left the seashell, painted side up, in the tide pool as an offering and a signature. [650]
Outside, the quay was waiting, and for the first time he did not feel nostalgic for it. [667]
He walked back as the caves filled, carrying an empty tin and a full heart. [682]
