<story>Jonah was a grim optimist who believed every step would be worse and still worth taking. [16] He lived as caretaker of the infinite staircase, which rose and fell without landing, without end, without promise. [34] The architects had intended it as a joke about perpetual motion, a structure that wasted exactly as much energy as it borrowed. [56] Jonah understood the joke and kept sweeping anyway. [64] In his coat pocket he always carried a pair of dice, one chipped, left by the gambler who had tried to bet his way out. [89] He was not superstitious, but he liked the weight of chance against his palm while climbing. [105] Some days he rolled them on a step to decide whether to climb or descend. [120] The dice never changed the fact that every path led further into boundless confinement. [134] That tone was not dreary to him, only honest, because infinity never asked for permission. [149] He was balancedly daring, never leaping two flights without first testing the rail, never refusing a leap when the rail held. [170] Years looping stairs had taught him that courage without a ledger was just falling. [184] What he wanted, more than an exit, was to capture the texture of a missed opportunity that haunted the landing where he met Mara. [208] Mara had been another wanderer, laughing that the staircase was a university for footnotes. [222] She had offered him a chance to leave together, a door she claimed to have drawn in chalk that would open only once. [245] He had hesitated, demanding proof, and she had gone through alone. [256] Since then he had tried to recreate her door through mathematical proofs scratched on risers and banisters. [273] Each proof attempted to show that a finite heart could sustain perpetual motion if it looped around an absence. [292] He filled hundreds of steps with lemmas, erasing them with his broom each morning to keep the staircase clean. [311] The staircase tolerated this, as it tolerated all contradictions, because it could not end. [325] One night his dice rolled off a step and clattered downward for minutes before silence. [340] He chased them down sixty flights and found them resting on a step where his chalk was still fresh. [359] The proof there contained a deliberate error, a hidden loop that assumed its own conclusion. [374] He stared at it until he understood that he had been trying to prove he had been right to wait. [394] That was not mathematics, it was grief with better handwriting. [404] He took the chipped die and pressed its sharp edge into his palm, not to hurt himself but to feel texture. [425] A missed opportunity does not feel like emptiness, it feels like a grain, a chip, a misprint on smooth stone. [445] He realized Mara had not left a door, but a method for leaving without a door. [461] That evening he wrote a new proof, not to fix the staircase but to rupture it precisely where its logic was weakest. [483] The argument was simple and cruel and kind, showing that any infinite staircase containing a grim optimist must contain its own exit. [505] He used his own steps as axioms, his hesitation as a postulate, his dice as a random oracle. [523] When he reached the final line, the stone before him exhaled and folded, not into a doorway but into a landing that had always been between flights. [550] On that landing sat Mara's old coat, and beside it, his pair of dice, one chipped, exactly where he had dropped them in a different loop. [576] He picked up the coat and felt for the first time that the stairs did not require his waiting. [595] He laughed, grimly and optimistically, and chose to sit rather than climb. [607] The rupture he had made was not an escape from the staircase, but an agreement with it that he could rest inside motion. [630] Somewhere above, another caretaker will find chalk marks and think they are warnings, then proofs, then invitations. [647] Jonah rolls his dice there still, not for decisions, but to hear chance settle in boundless confinement. [664] He knows the stairs go on, and he goes on with them, which is finally enough.
