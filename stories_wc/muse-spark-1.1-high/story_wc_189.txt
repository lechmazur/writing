<story>Mara had spent thirty years as a tidal-pool chronicler on the grey coast of Hokkaido. [15] She was warmly observant in a way that made even hermit crabs seem to perform for her notebook. [33] Now she sat across the kitchen table from her daughter Jun, who had just asked if she would leave the coast and move inland. [57] The question hung between them. [62] It was a difficult conversation they had avoided for two years, and Jun waited for an answer. [79] Across the pause in a difficult conversation, Mara felt time thin and fold. [92] She did not answer directly. [97] Instead she measured the silence through tangential approach, the way she had always measured changing tides not by looking at the water but at what lived at its edges. [126] In that held breath, she was no longer in the kitchen but standing in the ancient grotto under stars behind the lighthouse, where she had done her true work. [155] The grotto flooded only at solstice high tide, and for three nights a year its walls reflected the sky. [174] Inside it, the pools never fully emptied, and that was why she kept her records there. [190] Each pool was a calendar of transition periods, the minutes between submergence and exposure, between salt and air, between one ecology and another. [213] She had come, at seventy-two, to discover the texture of time, not as a line but as something pitted, ridged, slick, and barnacled. [236] Tonight, in memory, a shallow pool held the jade cicada she had found as a child and kept for luck. [256] Its wings were carved so fine that light seemed to pass through them. [269] She had always believed the cicada, a creature defined by waiting underground and singing for a single summer, understood transition better than she did. [293] Kneeling, she touched the cold water and tried to foresee what tide would come next, for Jun and for the coast and for herself. [317] This was her chronic failing as a mother: she looked sideways at life to avoid scaring it away. [335] Jun, at twelve, had cried when Mara missed her recital because the tide chart had misaligned with the grotto's opening. [355] Jun, at twenty-nine, now offered a spare room far from any salt, out of love and fear. [372] The pool rippled around the jade cicada, and in its disturbed surface Mara finally saw the pattern she had been cataloging all her life. [396] Every tide chronicler knows the pool is not dying when it drains and not being born when it floods. [415] The truth is the pause in between, when the water hesitates at the lip of rock and holds both possibilities. [435] That is where life thickens, where shrimp learn to breathe air and algae learn to hold water. [452] She understood then that transition periods were not bridges between real things but the real things themselves. [469] Time had the texture of that hesitation: granular, wet, cold at first then warm in her palm. [486] The jade cicada caught starlight from the grotto mouth and threw a green spot onto her wrist, like a pulse. [506] She remembered that cicadas do not count years underground; they measure the temperature of the soil. [522] That was her answer. [526] The grotto dissolved slowly, as water will, and the kitchen light returned hard and yellow. [541] Jun still waited, her hands wrapped around a chipped mug, trying not to look hopeful or hurt. [558] Mara lifted the jade cicada from her pocket where it had lived for fifty years and set it on the table between them. [581] I cannot move inland yet, she said, because I am still learning how to live in the pause. [599] But I can teach you how to read it, if you will sometimes sit with me while the tide is undecided. [620] Jun looked at the small green insect, then at her mother, and the difficult conversation softened without resolving. [638] That was enough to let the next moment in. [647]</story>
