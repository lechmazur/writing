<story>Mara had been called a reckless idealist since she first learned the catacombs breathed. [14] The elders forbade the lower levels, where the city buried its dead in a catacomb orchard where tree roots snake between bones. [36] She went anyway, because she believed the dead could still be tended like soil. [50] Within the glow of half-lit corridors, the air smelled of damp earth and cold iron. [65] Mara walked with a lantern that never quite reached the vaults above, inlaying meaning everywhere she looked. [82] To her, a cracked skull was a planter, a fallen bone a boundary marker, a root a patient hand. [101] She was fiercely gentle in a way that made even the watchmen lower their voices. [116] She did not come to pray, but to patch holes in the soul of the city itself. [133] For years the orchard had been dying, its figs blackening before ripening, because grief had been left untended. [151] The old rites said you could trade what you were meant to become for what someone else needed, an exchange called bartered destinies by the bone-tenders. [177] Mara had laughed the first time she heard it, then copied the passage into her journal three times. [195] At the center of the orchard she found what remained of the first tender, a woman wrapped in roots, her jaw open around a seed. [220] The roots did not trammel the skeleton; they held it upright, as if reluctant to let go. [237] In the palm of that hand-shaped root was a burned tarot card, its edges curled to ash, its face still faintly visible. [259] The image was The Star, a figure pouring water onto dead ground, but one corner was gone, eaten by fire. [279] Mara understood then that someone had tried to bargain and failed, or perhaps succeeded too well. [295] She knelt, feeling the cold seep through her knees, and felt the orchard listening through her own breath. [313] Her own life had been small futures traded for cheaper comforts, a safe apprenticeship, a quiet house, and she was tired of patching herself with compromises. [339] If destinies could be bartered, she would give away the one that kept her safe above ground. [356] She placed her lantern down, and within the glow of half-lit corridors she spoke aloud the old formula. [374] I will take root where nothing grows, she said, if this place will take my fear of being forgotten. [393] The roots shivered, not with hunger but with recognition, and slipped from the old tender to brush her wrists. [412] They did not pierce her, they asked, and the asking was a weight settling behind her ribs. [429] Mara felt her imagined future peel away like bark, the quiet house, the safe work, the unobjectionable life. [447] In return something warm poured into the orchard, and a fig above her, black and shriveled, swelled and split with ripeness. [468] The burned tarot card in the old palm crumbled, its star finally extinguished, and the seed in the old mouth sprouted. [489] A thin green shoot pushed through bone and soil, utterly certain, and Mara wept without knowing why. [506] She was no longer sure where her own life ended and the orchard's began, and that uncertainty felt like freedom. [526] She would return each evening, not to escape the city but to remember her promise in the dark. [544] The lantern light would thin, the roots would tighten gently around her ankles, and she would stay with a dusky resolve that had nothing to do with martyrdom. [572] Other tenders would come after her, she hoped, and find figs where there had once been only funerals. [590] They would not need maps, only the willingness to kneel in a place others called cursed. [606]</story>
