<story>Mara had spent seven years as a celestial signal interpreter in the basement of the observatory. [16] When the funding dried up, she took the only job that still let her listen to the sky. [34] That was the overnight shift at the university radio station, where the transmitter hummed like a tired star. [52] She taped her old astronomical charts to the cinderblock walls, covering water stains with constellations no undergrad would recognize. [71] The charts were not decoration; she used them to track the slow pulse she had been following since graduate school. [91] It came from beyond Kepler-442, too regular to be noise, too playful to be natural. [106] She called its sources the cosmic tricksters, because they answered questions before she asked them. [121] As the community divides over the new array on the hill, everyone wanted her opinion, but no one wanted her warning. [142] The town council saw the array as jobs and prestige, a local foothold in interstellar politics. [158] The protestors saw it as an invitation for invasion, a beacon that should never be lit. [174] Both sides were locked in power plays, each trying to use the sky for leverage on Earth. [191] Mara knew both were wrong, because the tricksters did not want worship or war. [205] They wanted a reaction, any loud human reply they could sample and twist. [218] The array, when activated, would scream our location in wideband radio, easily manipulated. [231] She decided she had to interfere, not by shouting louder but by changing the nature of the conversation. [249] She was cautiously hopeful that humans could still be clever enough to whisper instead of yell. [265] Her plan relied on the station's old emergency broadcast hardware that could emit through subsonic frequencies. [281] Those frequencies could ride under the array's carrier wave, inaudible to the town but visible to anyone listening from orbit. [301] The tricksters, she had learned, hated subsonic honesty, because low tones could not be edited without collapsing. [318] To outwit cosmic tricksters she needed to send a message that destroyed itself if tampered with. [334] At two seventeen in the morning, with the protest chanting faint through the walls and the council meeting still droning on the scanner, she began. [423] It was not music, not speech, but a mathematical confession: we are small, we are listening, we are not ready. [443] The ethereal part was the beauty of the signal, spiraling like light through cold water, impossible to fake. [507] Then the subsonic layer held, dragging their interference down into truth, forcing the waveform to collapse where they touched it. [527] Alarms chirped at the array site as its processors met a signal that refused to be bent. [576] The signal flatlined into honest noise, and the tricksters, outwitted, withdrew to find an easier planet. [592] She left the transmitter running, the subsonics humming through pipes and teeth and bones of the sleeping town. [610] Dawn came gray and ordinary over the radio station roof. [620] Mara peeled her charts from the wall, folded them carefully, and knew she would need new ones soon. [638] The community would still divide, still argue, still play their small necessary power games.
