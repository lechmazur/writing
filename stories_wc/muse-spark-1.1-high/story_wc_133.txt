<story>Mara had not slept properly in eleven years, not since she became an insomniac painter during the rationing. [18] It was now thirty years after the water wars, and the continent's interior was salt and bone. [35] She lived on a tidal island off the drowned coast, a hump of granite that was cut off twice a day and then forgivingly reconnected by mud. [62] She painted what she could not sleep through, which was everything. [73] Her notebooks said she worked to thresh meaning from experience, as if memory were wheat that could be beaten clean. [93] She was wryly earnest about it, apologizing to each canvas before she ruined it. [107] At night she watched the storm light over the flats with a kind of haggard awe that made her eyes water. [128] On the first rainless week of spring, while scraping lichen from her door, she found a small box holding an amber brooch shaped like a beetle. [154] The amber held a flaw, a dark fleck that looked like a compass needle. [168] Inside the box lid someone had etched coordinates and a single word: CONVERGENCE. [181] Mara knew the old survey maps called the north cove the unearthed convergence, where the war's desalination lines met the monastic aqueducts and failed together. [206] She had never gone there because the tide traps a person quickly. [218] When the rains returned, sudden and sideways, she went by following rain trails that cut silver through the salt crust. [238] The trail ended at a collapsed sluice where three pipes, two old and one older, twisted into a knot that had been pried open by roots and time. [266] In that knot, rainwater and seawater mixed and sang. [275] There was a man there already, younger than her, mending a tide gauge. [288] He introduced himself as Jonah, the keeper's son, and he too had not expected company. [303] He saw the amber brooch pinned crookedly to her coat and smiled without teeth. [317] My grandmother lost that in the evacuation, he said, and Mara felt the old practiced throb of possession and guilt. [337] He offered to exchange his tide charts for the brooch, so she could paint the safe hours and not drown trying to get home. [361] Mara hesitated, because the brooch had kept her hand steady all morning, a small amber weight against her sleeve. [380] But she wanted the knowledge more than the charm. [389] She unpinned it and placed it in his palm, and he laid the oilskin charts in hers, their edges soft as old bread. [412] Together they looked into the unearthed convergence, where fresh water still tried to find its old path through rust. [431] It was not magnificent, just pipes and mud and a stubborn trickle that had survived all the bombing and pumping. [451] Mara felt the haggard awe again, deeper this time, not for beauty but for persistence. [466] She understood, finally, that her insomnia was not waiting for rest but for proof that something continues. [483] She opened a chart on her knee and began to sketch the way water remembered its way, even after men told it to forget. [507] Jonah watched and said her lines looked like sleep. [516] When the tide turned, they walked back separately but along the same raised berm, not speaking, because the island would soon divide again. [539] That night Mara slept for an hour and dreamed of amber light inside water, and woke without panic for the first time she could remember. [564] In the morning she painted the convergence from memory, not as pipes but as hands holding each other under mud. [584] She left the center of the canvas empty, a pale space where the brooch had been, because loss also needed a shape. [606] Jonah kept the brooch on his gauge post, and when travelers asked he told them it marked safe passage, which was not untrue. [629] Mara kept the charts and learned to time her crossings, trading sleepless hours for tidal ones, which felt like a kinder bargain. [651] On clear nights she still felt tired beyond reason, but the tiredness had somewhere to go now, out and in with the water. [674]</story>
