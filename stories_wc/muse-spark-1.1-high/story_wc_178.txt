<story>Nabu was a frugal astronomer who counted oil drops before lighting his lamp. [13]
He lived and worked in the upper archive of the ancient babylonian palace reimagined as a lattice of glass and fired brick, where starlight fell through vaulted ribs. [41]
It was the year between regimes, when the old king's statues had been draped and the new king's had not yet been carved. [64]
In that hush, the palace bureaucracy forgot him, which suited his economy. [76]
He wanted to seal a covenant with the unknown, not out of ambition but out of thrift, because he believed wasting a clear night sky was the only true sin. [106]
For months he charted planets through schematic representations, reducing their wanderings to thin ink lines on reused parchment. [124]
The palace mathematicians laughed at his paper, because in an age of brass orreries his drawings looked like beggar's work. [144]
He did not mind, for his devotion was not to them. [155]
It was an encrypted devotion, written not in prayers but in numbers he hid inside the margins of duty rosters. [175]
One evening the wind lifted the archive curtains and revealed a loose floor tile he had never noticed. [193]
He pried it up and found beneath it a magic square tile, cool to the touch and thrumming faintly, its fired clay face marked with nine numbers that summed to the same total in every direction. [229]
The tile was set into a deeper socket, a star map older than the palace itself. [245]
Nabu understood then that the palace had been built over an observatory, and that his own frugality was an echo of someone older who reused foundations. [271]
He brushed dust from the numbers, and the tile asked nothing, promised nothing, yet offered a perfect and empirically ironic truth. [292]
The more precisely he measured its sums, the less its material could be explained. [306]
Clay from the river should not hold light inside it, but this clay did, as if starlight had been kneaded into mud. [328]
Night after night he returned to align his schematic representations with the tile's grid, until his little charts and its ancient numbers sang in a dusky harmony. [355]
That harmony was not bright; it was the quiet accord of two careful things finding same proportion. [372]
On the seventh night between regimes, the sky cleared to a depth no steward had recorded. [388]
He climbed to the roof garden where the glass lattice met open air, carrying the magic square tile against his ribs. [409]
The city below held its breath, too nervous to light its lamps for either king. [424]
Nabu set the tile on the low altar that had once held offerings and now held dust. [441]
He understood at last how to uncloak what the tile kept folded. [453]
He did not smash it or pray to it; he completed it through schematic representations, drawing the empty sky around its nine numbers until a tenth position revealed itself. [482]
The number ten was not carved, but appeared as shadow where light passed through the tile and struck his ink. [502]
When he read it, the air above the altar thickened, not into a god or a king, but into a geometry attentive and vast. [526]
It had no voice, and his covenant needed none. [535]
He offered the only thing he truly owned, his years of watching without reward, and the geometry received it by mirroring his tile with stars. [560]
For a single breath the constellations above the palace rearranged into a magic square, each bright sum equal, each path balanced. [581]
Nabu wept, not because he had discovered a secret, but because his lifelong encrypted devotion had finally been read by something capable of reading it. [606]
Then the stars slipped back, the tile cooled, and he was again a small man on a roof between regimes, rich only in paper. [630]
He descended with the tile hidden in his satchel, restored the floor, and rationed his oil as before, but now his frugality felt like a shared language rather than a solitary habit. [662]
In the archive below, the glass and brick still held their dusky harmony, and somewhere above them the unknown kept his numbers safe. [685]
