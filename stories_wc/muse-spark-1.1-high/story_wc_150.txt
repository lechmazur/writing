<story>Lena had not been back to the island since she was six, when her parents carried her onto a plane with one suitcase and a promise they never kept. [29] She returned now at thirty-four, a diaspora returnee with an American accent that made the old women in the village smile in a way that was not unkind. [57] The priest had written to her about the family tomb collapsing under the lemon orchard, and about faith needing hands more than prayers. [80] She had lost hers quietly in a hospital waiting room two years earlier, and she came to restore lost faith not because she expected a miracle but because she wanted work. [111] The site they gave her was not a cemetery but a catacomb orchard where tree roots snake between bones, pale and patient in the dark earth. [137] Lena stood at its edge the first morning and felt cautious enchantment settle over her like dust motes in late light. [158] She was by nature warmly curious, the kind of child who had once taken apart radios to see where the voices lived. [180] Across excavating a tomb, she learned, you cannot rush; the earth decides when it will let go. [197] For three weeks she worked with two old men from the village, brushing soil from stacked niches where generations had been laid. [219] Each niche held a small story of interrupted journeys, sailors who left and never returned, mothers sent away during war, children who died on the road home. [246] Lena logged every name in a notebook, and at night she walked the village lanes trying to match names to houses. [267] It became her method, through purposeful wandering, to understand what the catacombs meant before she disturbed them further. [285] She would wander without a map in the morning, then return in the afternoon to dig exactly where her feet remembered softness. [307] On the ninth day, her trowel hit stone that was not rock, a perfect circle half-swallowed by roots. [325] She cleared it slowly and found a sundial base, its bronze pin long gone, its rim still etched with hours and a faded name. [349] The inscription read FOR THOSE WHO WAIT IN LIGHT, and the letters made her throat ache. [365] The old men told her the orchard had been planted by monks who could not afford candles, so they grew trees over the dead to keep time. [392] The sundial had marked prayer hours for workers who could not see the sky from below. [408] Lena understood then that the catacombs were not only for burial but for waiting, for measuring interrupted journeys with light that never reached them. [432] She wanted to rebuild the sundial, not as a monument but as a working marker, so the orchard would again know what time it kept. [457] The priest said no one had kept it working in a hundred years, that faith like that was impractical. [476] Lena kept brushing soil, and across the last week the tomb revealed itself as a small chapel with benches carved into the limestone. [499] In the center dust lay thicker, as if time itself had paused there waiting for someone to notice. [517] She set the sundial base there, where a single shaft of light fell through a root-cracked ceiling at noon. [536] She found a straight olive branch and fixed it upright, and when the sun moved the shadow crawled across the ancient hours. [558] The old men stopped working to watch, and one whispered a prayer he had not said since childhood. [576] Lena did not feel faith return like a flood, but like a small, steady weight in her palm, warmly curious again about what light would do next. [603] She would leave in a month, but she had connected the village to its waiting dead through purposeful wandering, and the orchard no longer measured absence alone. [630] The sundial kept time for interrupted journeys finally continued, and Lena kept time with it. [645]</story>
