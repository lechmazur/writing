<story>After the flood, Mara came back to the old university greenhouse because the physics building was still closed. [18]
She had been a neutron star researcher for twelve years, mapping pulses from dead suns that weighed more than mountains per teaspoon. [40]
The work taught her to strip needless drama from data, to let numbers be quiet. [55]
Now the greenhouse roof was half gone, its iron ribs bent, its glass replaced by sky. [71]
Mud covered the benches where students once grew tomatoes in winter. [82]
She did not come to mourn, only to sort what could be saved. [95]
In the corner, a wooden seed cabinet still stood upright, its drawers swollen shut with damp. [111]
When she pried the top drawer, a cloud of moths rose, but one blue morpho butterfly lay dead inside, perfect. [131]
Its wings had shed dust from butterfly wing onto the drawer paper in faint overlapping prints. [147]
The human eye preprocesses reality, she remembered from a seminar, filling gaps before we notice them. [163]
She had spent years doing the opposite, refusing to fill gaps, waiting for light curves to speak for themselves. [182]
Here, the drawer paper held a record not of light but of stillness, of how little movements accumulate. [200]
The butterfly had not died during the storm, she realized, but weeks before, moving in the dark drawer until it could not. [222]
Each flutter left a new print through accumulated dust patterns that overlapped like faint star maps. [238]
She borrowed a magnifier from the tool shelf and saw the scales themselves, tiny shingles laid down with accidental precision. [258]
It was not a dramatic archive, just gradual change made visible by patience. [271]
Neutron stars also change that way, imperceptibly slowing, their spin down measured in nanoseconds over years. [287]
People expected explosions from her, but her work had always been about listening to decay. [302]
The flood had taken her lab servers, her preprints, even the coffee mug she loved, and she had felt nothing like grief, only a strange lightness. [328]
In the greenhouse, with no instruments to calibrate, she finally understood that lightness as kindled humility, not indifference. [346]
She was not the observer above reality, but a small animal inside it, prone to smudges. [362]
She closed the drawer halfway and left the butterfly where it was, because some records should be left to finish. [382]
Outside, the campus crew was clearing fallen branches, shouting about insurance forms and mold tests. [397]
Mara dragged a bench into the thin sun that fell through the broken roof and sat. [413]

He offered one without asking why she was sitting alone among ruined glass. [470]
She accepted, and they said nothing, because after a disaster silence is often more accurate than summary. [487]

It was small work, unpublishable, but it returned the room to a shape where future seedlings could be imagined. [579]
