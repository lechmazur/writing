<story>Mara had spent three years as a reckless abandoned-mill explorer, mapping rust in the river towns while everyone else moved to the cities. [23]
She was religiously skeptical in the way people who were raised on altar wine and warnings become, keeping a small, practical distance from any promise of an afterlife. [51]
Since her brother Caleb drowned in the millrace the summer she was sixteen, she had wanted not closure but the precise, physical answer she called to find the weight of a swallowed truth. [84]
He had worked the last summer the old paper mill ran, and the men on his crew still talked about the factory ghost shift that clocked in after midnight even after the boilers went cold. [119]
Mara didn't believe in ghosts, but for months she had been collecting the graveyard show tapes, by interpreting frantic calls to talk-radio psychics like they were weather reports from the other side. [151]
The callers all described the same sound, a wheel turning underwater, a shift whistle that never blew. [168]
Last Tuesday a woman from Ellens called to say her husband, dead ten years from the mill, kept asking her to close the beaded valve wheel so the water would stop coming in. [201]
Mara recognized the description; there was only one valve in the lower bleach plant that had been beaded by workers with river glass and rosary beads to stop it from freezing to the touch. [235]
She packed a headlamp, a crowbar, and a thermos of cold coffee and drove out before dawn in that state of drowsy alertness that made everything feel both too sharp and too soft. [268]
To reach the bleach plant she had to cut through the abandoned train tunnel that ran behind the mill, where the tracks still shone with damp. [294]
The tunnel breathed cold air that tasted of iron and old smoke, and her footsteps sounded like someone else walking just behind her. [317]
Halfway through she stopped because she heard it, a low groan and then the squeal of metal turning slowly under water, exactly as the callers had described. [344]
She thought of Caleb and how the official story was that he slipped, but no one ever explained why his boots were found neatly together on the catwalk, dry. [373]
In the puddle light at the tunnel's far end the beaded valve wheel was half sunk in silt, its beads clicking faintly as water pushed through the rusted pipe behind it. [404]
Mara knelt and tried to appraise whether the wheel had been turned recently, noticing the clean crescents in the grime where someone's palms had gripped. [429]
She was not a woman who prayed, but she put her hands on the cold beads anyway and whispered the thing she had never said aloud, that she had dared Caleb to walk the sluice gate that night. [467]
Her voice cracked and vanished into the tunnel, and during the hush that follows a sudden confession she finally heard the rest of the sound. [492]
It was not one wheel but dozens, turning in sequence behind the walls, and the low thump was boots on metal catwalks as the factory ghost shift came on for its endless midnight round. [526]
Mara understood then that the callers had not been talking to the dead, but that the mill had kept a recording of the pressure surge that had taken her brother, replaying it whenever the river ran high. [563]
She braced her feet and turned the beaded valve wheel until the beads bit into her palms, feeling the line shudder and then hold. [587]
The thumping faded, the tunnel air went still, and in that new quiet she could feel at last a measurable, heavy stillness where her guilt had always floated. [615]
She sat back on the wet concrete, crying without sound, knowing the weight had not left but had finally found a place to rest outside her chest. [642]
When she walked back toward daylight, the tunnel no longer echoed like a second person, only like a long, empty throat learning how to be empty. [668]
Outside, the river was high and loud, and she left the wheel closed. [681]</story>
