<story>Mara Reyes had been an accident investigator for twenty-two years, long enough to stop believing in freak accidents. [18]
After the town goes silent, which in Port Sanin meant after midnight when the truck queues cleared and the gulls finally roosted, she walked into the yard alone. [46]
The yard was not a yard at all but a towering labyrinth of shipping containers in a port city that had stacked itself six and eight high without a master plan. [77]
In daylight it handled freight, but after midnight it became a wind machine, humming and whistling through steel canyons. [96]
Three weeks earlier a straddle carrier had tipped here, crushing one worker and trapping another under a leaning tower of empty reefers. [118]
The company called it operator error, but Mara knew error never lived alone. [131]
She needed to survey the site the way air moves, not the way maps lie. [146]
Her method was unapproved and unfashionable, documenting wind patterns between buildings with paper streamers, handheld anemometers, and graphite notes that smeared in the damp. [170]
The port authority wanted lidar scans and computer models, but fog killed lidar and models had never stood inside a container canyon at one in the morning. [197]
She pinned a faded flight engineer's patch to her jacket pocket, the one Tomas had given her before his cargo plane went down over the Andes. [223]
He used to say every crash was a singular convergence, not a single cause, a precise knot where small separate winds tied themselves together. [247]
That idea was the only thing that had kept her honest when reports wanted a simple villain. [264]
Mara released a ribbon and watched it snap horizontal, then curl upward at a forty-degree shear two meters past the third stack. [286]
