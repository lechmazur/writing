<story>He had been the wistful station pianist for twenty years, playing for trains that no longer stopped. [17] The high station at Arakai was not on any modern line, but a waiting room bolted to the mountain itself. [37] Above it, cradling the peak, hung the tidal arbor above the clouds, where wind pruned silver leaves that rose and fell with pressure. [60] He was strategically creative in the way old musicians are, tuning the untunable and making silence part of the song. [80] On his piano he kept a broken anniversary clock, its glass face fogged, its hands frozen at 4:42. [98] His wife had given it to him the day she left to study weather in the lower valleys, promising to return before it needed winding. [123] She never did. [126] Instead her letters spoke of flickering compasses that could not settle near the summit, as if the mountain refused direction. [146] He understood now that the compasses were not broken but honest, responding to the breathing of that living canopy overhead. [166] Last winter the company announced they would seal the station and remove the piano. [180] That was fate filing its final schedule, and he decided to surprise fate with an unplanned encore, not out of defiance but out of love. [205] He would not beg them to stay. [212] He would take refuge where his wife had always meant to meet him. [225] In the station archive, among timetables eaten by mice, he found a tin labeled AIRLIGHT 1938. [241] Inside was a black-and-white film reel, its sprockets rusted, its leader marked with his wife's initials. [257] He borrowed the projector from the signal house and watched snow on the wall until a meadow appeared above the clouds. [278] The film showed the arbor when it was young, its branches holding tide pools of mist. [294] Then, at thirty-seven minutes, the image stuttered. [301] He stopped the reel and found, through hidden sections in a black-and-white film reel, a second film spliced between frames. [321] It was not a scene but a method, hand-painted arrows pointing along nearly invisible cuts in the celluloid. [339] Following those cuts, frame by frame, revealed a footpath that did not exist on any map, a maintenance ladder behind the piano that led up into the rock. [367] He had one day left before demolition crews arrived, until the horizon invites night with the same slow care his wife once took. [390] He packed the clock, though it was broken, because its weight in his coat felt like proof. [407] He climbed. [409] The ladder ended at a hatch, and beyond was light that did not fall but floated. [425] The tidal arbor above the clouds was larger than any film, its branches wide enough to hold lakes, its roots drinking from nothing but altitude. [450] Leaves moved toward him and away regardless of wind, and with them his old sadness acquired a tide of its own. [471] In the center, as his wife had written, a ring of stones made a natural stage where compass needles had once flickered and danced. [495] He set the piano there. [500] It should have been impossible to move, yet the arbor lifted, as if helping. [514] He understood then that his entire life had been tuned to this moment, the cold narrowing his breath into a steely longing that was not bitter but clear. [542] He placed the broken anniversary clock upon the piano lid and opened the keyboard. [556] He began to play the piece his wife had hummed while tracking storms, a simple tune that left space for wind to answer. [579] As he played, the arbor answered, the tide of leaves rising and falling in time, and far below, the demolition crew, seeing no station to demolish through sudden cloud, turned back. [610] He played until the horizon invites night, until the first stars caught in the arbor's pools like reflections of compasses finally still. [632] When silence came, the broken anniversary clock, warmed by music and altitude, ticked once, then again. [648]</story>
