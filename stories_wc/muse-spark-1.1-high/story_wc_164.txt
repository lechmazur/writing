I am Yara and I work as a sea-foam recollectionist on the edge of the world. [16]
Every morning the tide coughs up foam that holds one human memory per bubble. [30]
I scoop them carefully into my satchel and carry them inland to my workshop. [44]
My workshop is an iridescent cave inside a giant crystal geode that sings when light hits it. [61]
The walls throw colors that have no names and they make even sad work feel kind. [77]
I have lived in this cheerful gloom for forty years and I have never wanted to leave. [94]
The foam remembers birthdays and shipwrecks and first kisses, but lately every recollection arrives with missing pieces. [111]
A woman remembers a child but not the road that led her to the harbor. [126]
A sailor remembers the storm but not the choice that put him on that boat. [141]
For a long time I told myself that erosion was natural and memories simply wore thin. [157]
It was my dear friend Luma who made me want to discover why some paths disappear. [173]
Luma is a cartographer who maps winds and she came to me with a hollow in her own head. [192]
She said she could not remember the summer she crossed the inland sea. [205]
That frightened me because Luma never forgets anything not even debts. [216]
I realized I had to act before memory fades completely from our coast. [229]
In the back of my geode I keep a lens of crater glass forged when a meteor struck the salt flats. [250]
The glass is cold and slightly pitted and it transliterates emotions into visible threads. [264]
When I look at a foam memory through it, I can see joy as gold and grief as blue smoke. [284]
I looked at Luma's missing summer and saw a bright path that stopped abruptly against black. [300]
The same black cut marked every recent memory with a clean edge like a censored map. [316]
It was not decay but deliberate absence and it made me angry in a quiet way. [332]
I needed to conjecture where the absent roads went and why they were taken. [346]
No human map showed the answer, so I followed an older guide. [358]
Each spring a flock of silver swifts migrates over the geode carrying seeds from the inland sea. [375]
I have learned to read their droppings the way other people read tea leaves. [389]
So I set out to trace the lost paths via the scattered seeds dropped by a migrating bird. [407]
I ground crater glass into powder and dusted the seed piles to make their memory visible. [423]
Each seed sprouted a thin light that transliterates emotions again but this time from the earth. [439]
I followed the lights away from the cave toward the dry basin where the inland sea once was. [457]
There the lights converged on a low wall of black crystal humming like a beehive. [472]
The wall was not natural but grown from the same crater glass as my lens. [487]
It was feeding on recollections and sealing them behind itself to keep the water cycle stable. [503]
When the meteor fell it melted salt and memory together and the resulting glass learned to store thirst. [521]
The geode cave had been a piece of that meteor and my workshop was its cousin. [537]
I understood then that the missing pieces were not lost but borrowed. [549]
The desert drank memories of crossings to prevent people from finding and draining the last aquifer. [565]
It thought it was protecting us by making paths disappear in our minds. [578]

I gave it the afternoon Luma taught me to read foam and the night I first saw the geode glow. [615]
The wall shivered and its light softened from hungry black to tired amber. [628]
It let go. [631]

Luma woke yesterday remembering the crossing and laughing that she had gotten lost on purpose. [662]
I am still cataloguing what returned but none of it feels stolen anymore. [675]
The geode still glows and I still work in cheerful gloom, because I know some forgetting is merciful but not all of it. [698]
