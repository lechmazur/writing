<story>It happened after the reef dies, when the water turned clear not from health but from emptiness. [17] Marlo had been the gloom-hill coachman for forty years, driving mourners and newlyweds along the ridge road above the southern battlefield. [38] When the hill was stripped for artillery and the horses died, his work died with them. [54] He stayed anyway in the abandoned signal house, because his mother had taught him that every field wants to return to something else. [77] Her something else was a garden. [83] She left him a tin box containing a collection of dried seed pods, each labeled in ink that had faded to brown. [105] He could not read most labels, but he remained a man who speaks dead languages from his childhood in the monastery archives. [127] Latin for bitter vine, Old Coastal for salt-rose, and words no one used anymore for plants that survived on ash. [147] He carried them down to the plain to let a garden reclaim a battlefield, where rusted wire still stitched the dirt and shell craters held rain like blind eyes. [176] The soil was poisoned, compacted, and would not take root. [186] The old fisherwoman at the edge of the plain told him the only way to fertilize it was to borrow from the sky. [209] He laughed, then understood she meant birds. [216] For two seasons he sat in the craters by understanding bird calls, learning which cries meant crickets below and which meant wind change. [239] The gulls that had left when the reef went now passed over in thin lines, hesitant. [255] He answered them with the dead languages, with the guttural clicks of Old Coastal that sounded like beaks on hollow wood. [276] Miraculously they circled, then dropped, roosting in numbers on the rusted wire. [288] Their guano whitened the battlefield, and where it fell the earth softened. [300] In that softening he pressed the pods, one by one, speaking each true name aloud as if introducing exiles home. [320] Even then most died. [324] He went to the sea wall each evening to wash, and there he met a child who built paper boats at the blooming jetty at twilight. [350] The jetty should have been dead wood since the fishing collapse, but salt-rose and lantern vine had cracked its planks and flowered with furious light. [375] The child told him the flowers followed a line under the water, an old line drawn by diplomats. [393] He recognized it from a scroll in the archives, the submerged concord that once divided fishing rights and promised that no gun would fire within sight of the reef. [422] It had been broken before it drowned. [429] Now the reef was gone, and the promise floated loose like a prayer without an altar. [445] The child asked why he spoke to birds in words she did not know. [459] He said the earth had forgotten how to listen, so he had to speak in every tongue it once knew. [479] That night the wind came strong from the west, carrying a taste of iron and bloom at once. [497] On the ridge the abandoned coaches creaked, but on the plain something felt both narrow vastness, a small patch holding the breadth of a lost sea and a failed treaty. [527] He woke to find the craters stitched green with first leaves. [538] The salt-rose had taken deepest, its bitter roots finding the rust and making it sweet. [553] From the jetty the vine had leapt, following the guano trail, and now it ran across the wire in waves of white flowers that covered the old scars. [581] The child stood in the middle of it, laughing, not knowing it was a battlefield at all. [598] He knelt and pressed his forehead to the soil, listening to roots and birds at once, and finally felt the promise hold again, not in water but in earth. [627]</story>
