<story>Mira had been a budding rune scholar for only two terms before the isle cut itself loose from the mainland. [20] It happened after spoons bend reality, when the old physics softened and kitchen cutlery became the first instruments to show it. [41] The professors called it the Ladle Event, but for Mira it simply meant that the floating isle where the academy kept its nocturnal observatory was no longer tethered by weight. [71] She stayed because the observatory was the one place where shadows could be watched without interruption. [87] Mira wanted to find where shadows go at noon, when even the deepest night of the lenses showed them thinning to nothing. [109] Her master said the question was childish, but he left her the eastern dome and its rusted instruments anyway. [128] He was quietly deliberate in all things, and had trained her to be the same. [143] She approached each night of observation as a ritual, cleaning the lenses, aligning the orrery, copying down the old runes carved into the observatory stone by the founders. [171] The runes were warnings about sealed awakenings, about things that slept in light and stirred only when they were measured. [191] Mira did not fully understand them and knew she would need to relearn everything the academy had taught her about light if she wanted to understand shadows. [218] The method was not in her textbooks, but etched in smaller script along the base of the central telescope: by closing loops. [240] She puzzled over that for weeks until she found the clockwork automaton crouched behind the chart cabinets, its back open and its winding mechanism empty. [265] It was a keeper, built to tend the observatory when humans could not, and in its chest the founders had placed a clockwork automaton key on a copper chain. [294] When she took the key, the automaton lifted its head and projected a circle of light onto the floor, showing a perfect shadow with no object to cast it. [323] The shadow was at noon though the observatory knew only night, and it pointed directly at the sealed plates under the telescope. [345] Mira understood then that the founders had not sealed away a danger but a memory of light, a noon that had to be hidden for the island to float. [374] The sealed awakenings were not monsters but stored days, captured whole because after spoons bend reality it became possible to fold and store a time of day like a cloth. [404] To release one, the inscription said, you had to close the loop of its shadow, bringing its beginning to its end without breaking its edge. [429] Mira set the key into the central plate and turned it slowly, feeling the gears catch and the entire observatory inhale. [450] Light bled up from the seams, not bright, not dark, a meridian color that made the floating stones of the isle visible from below as a constellation. [477] For a single minute the isle experienced noon at midnight, and all the shadows that had been stored for a century pooled at her feet and then vanished into their rightful places. [509] That was where shadows go at noon, she realized, not away but home, tucked into the dark side of things until they are needed to define shape. [536] When the light receded, the automaton stood and closed its own back, and the key in Mira's hand was warm, ticking like a small heart that had learned a new rhythm. [567] She logged the event carefully in the observatory ledger, noting that the rite had to be performed by closing loops and not by forcing them open. [593] She also wrote that she would need to continue to relearn runes now that she knew they were not commands but hinges, and that every sealed awakening was a noon waiting patiently for night to remember it.
