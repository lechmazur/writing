<story>The defiant artist named Mara had not come to the canyon to die, though everyone else had. [17] Before the ritual she was given three days alone among the towers of bone in a canyon of endless echoes to consider her crimes. [41] The priests called it mercy, a forged hush settling over them whenever they spoke of forgiveness. [57] Mara called it cowardice. [61] In the capital she had painted the High Priest with a pig's snout, and the crowd had laughed until the guards arrived. [83] That laugh was why she was here. [90] The canyon itself defied reason, its walls lined with bleached arches and ribs stacked higher than temples, all humming faintly when wind passed through. [114] Somewhere in the middle distance, half-lost, stood the pale spire in mist that marked the ritual ground. [131] Mara was supposed to kneel there at dawn and accept absolution. [142] Instead she spent her first day carving charcoal sketches onto the bone. [154] On the second day the canyon sent her company, as it always did. [167] A child appeared, barefoot on sharp calcite, eyes bright and innocently mischievous. [179] He said his name was Echo, which Mara thought was a joke until the canyon repeated it seven times. [198] You are not allowed here, Mara told him. [206] He grinned and asked if she knew the price of forgiveness, and whether she intended to pay it. [224] Mara had not, until that moment, understood that forgiveness in this place was not given but taken, a tooth pulled from the forgiven to build the towers higher. [252] The thought made her hands shake. [258] Echo watched her, then offered a game he had invented to keep the priests from listening. [274] He called it refract, through impromptu poetry sessions where you took one truth and bent it. [290] Mara laughed, a sharp sound that came back as three softer laughs. [302] She wanted to refract a single truth into many, because the priests insisted truth was one and indivisible, and therefore hers must die. [325] Her truth was simple: the High Priest was a pig, but so was everyone who cheered, including her. [343] Echo clapped and said that was a good first line. [353] So they played. [356] Mara whispered, I painted the snout because I was angry. [366] Echo chanted back, I painted the snout because I hoped someone would finally grunt with me. [382] Mara said, Forgiveness should be free. [388] Echo sang, Forgiveness should be expensive enough that we stop selling it cheap to tyrants. [403] Each poetry session made the canyon ring differently, as if the bones were learning new words. [419] By dusk the second day, the mist around the pale spire thinned, and Mara saw it was not a spire at all but a single upright tusk, taller than a man, scarred with old poems. [454] Some names forgiven, some names forgotten, all feeding the silence. [464] Echo admitted he had once been a forgiven boy who refused to give his tooth and instead gave a story. [484] The priests kept him because stories echoed longer than bone. [494] On the third morning, the ritual drums began far above, and the forged hush deepened until Mara could hear her own blood. [516] Echo asked what she would do. [522] Mara walked to the spire and did not kneel. [531] Instead she pressed her charcoal-blackened palms to its mist-slick surface and began to speak, not one apology but twenty, each a refracted splinter of the same light. [558] I was right to paint him, she said. [566] I was wrong to think paint alone would matter, she said. [577] I forgive the priest for being afraid of laughter, she said. [588] I forgive myself for needing permission to laugh, she said. [598] The canyon took each sentence and threw it back altered, multiplied, merciful. [610] The drums stopped, confused by echoes that did not resolve into a single submission. [624] Mara felt the old price lift, because she had finally set a price herself: that forgiveness would cost truth, and truth would cost many voices. [649] Behind her, Echo laughed, innocently mischievous to the last, and vanished into the towers. [663]</story>
