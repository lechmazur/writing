<story>Margaret was the kind of harmonious choir teacher who heard dissonance as an unfinished question, not a mistake. [18]
After thirty years at the conservatory, she retired early to catalog what she called the vanishing arts. [35]
She rented the top floor of the old municipal hall, a velvet library in autumn where the light fell amber through tall windows onto dust and sheet music. [63]
It was there, among boxes of broken tuning forks and moth-eaten hymnals, that she found a small concert zither with a carved zither soundhole rosette like a frozen lace flower. [93]
She had never taught strings, but the instrument held a traveled stillness she recognized from years of listening between notes. [113]
The zither had belonged to Albie, the town's last genuine zither player, who died the previous spring. [130]
Margaret cleaned it, tuned it, and set it on the reading table to denote the section she was building for instruments that no longer had students. [156]
Her real project, though, was not the objects but the people. [167]
The town had split bitterly two years earlier over the dam, and the high school choir had fractured along with it, families refusing to let their children sing together. [196]
Margaret watched her former pupils cross the street to avoid each other, and she became expectantly resigned that no one else would try to repair it. [222]
She pinned a notice on the library door inviting anyone who wanted to sing, or listen, with no audition and no politics. [244]
The first week only three came: a retired quarryman who breathed too loud, a Somali teenager who hummed instead of singing words, and a woman who had picketed the dam for months. [276]
Margaret did not correct them. [281]
Instead she taught them to listen first, by subtracting noise rather than adding volume, until the room itself seemed to lean in. [303]
She placed the zither in the middle of their small circle and let its faint resonance guide their breathing. [322]
It took months to braid diverse voices into harmony, because harmony was not unison but a way of holding difference without breaking. [344]
The quarryman learned to soften his exhale, the teenager learned to trust the English lyrics, the protester learned to keep singing when her neighbor's son returned, the one whose father had built the dam company. [379]
That winter was an education across unlearning hatred, slow and incomplete and stubbornly human. [393]
They rehearsed only in that velvet library, because the school auditorium still felt like a courtroom to everyone. [411]
By spring, the group was twenty, then thirty, farmers and clerks and kids who had once thrown rocks at each other's signs. [433]
None of them became perfect singers. [439]
They became attentive. [442]
Margaret taught that listening was a craft, one of the vanishing arts that required more silence than sound. [460]
On the final evening before summer, they performed not on a stage but in the aisles of the library, surrounded by books. [482]
The zither sat on its stand, and when wind came through an open window it made a thin, bright sigh through its rosette. [505]
Someone laughed, and Margaret let the laugh stay in the music. [516]
She had not fixed the town, but she had given it a place where its disparate timbres could be heard together without needing to resolve. [541]
Afterward, people lingered among the shelves, reluctant to break what they had made. [554]
Margaret sat in the amber light, her hands folded, her choir humming softly around her like a held breath finally released. [575]
