<story>Joren had been called a chivalrous thief for ten years, mostly by the people he stole from. [17] He never took from those who could not afford loss, and he always left something better in return. [35] That code brought him to the icebound spire above the clouds, where the Astronomer-Queen kept winter itself locked in a vault. [56] It was when the sky is wrong, the single night every eleven years when the stars invert and shadows point toward the light. [79] On that night, the spire's ice doors would soften, and even an oath-bound thief could slip inside. [96] Joren did not climb for treasure, but to amend a mistake he had made as a boy. [113] Twenty years earlier he had stolen a brass compass from the Astronomer-Queen's young daughter, and the girl had died lost in a blizzard searching for it. [139] He carried the only thing he had kept from that theft, a pocket sundial lid that still caught light even when there was no sun. [164] Inside the spire, the air was thin and humming, and frost formed in the shape of letters that dissolved before he could read them. [188] At the center of the high observatory, the Astronomer-Queen sat alone, ancient and small, playing chess against herself. [206] Her board was unlike any he had seen, each piece heavy, aged, and sacredly playful in its carving, as if mocking its own power. [230] She looked up and smiled, because she had been expecting the thief who left gifts. [245] You are not the first apprentice of wonder to find me, she said, though you are the first to come in apology. [267] Joren knelt and set the pocket sundial lid on the edge of her board, its thin shadow impossibly sharp in the starless light. [290] I cannot return her life, he said, but I can return her light. [303] The Queen nodded toward the board and said the game was not about winning, but about remembering. [320] For three nights that lasted one night, they played, and Joren learned her method of swapping hand-carved chess pieces with secrets inside. [342] Each piece unscrewed at the base, revealing a scroll, a tooth, a pressed flower, a name written by a child. [362] The Queen explained that she kept winter in the vault not out of cruelty, but to protect the world from her own grief. [385] If she released it all at once, the lowlands would freeze forever. [397] Joren understood then that his theft had not just killed a girl, it had tilted a mother's sorrow toward eternity. [417] So he offered a trade only a chivalrous thief could conceive. [428] He would take the vaulted winter upon his own back and carry it down the spire, scattering it gradually across eleven years. [450] In exchange, he asked that the Queen teach others to be apprentice of wonder again, rather than keepers of perfect cold. [471] She accepted with cautious abandon, the way a saint might jump into a frozen river to save a sparrow. [490] The Queen lifted the vault key, which was not metal but a held breath, and placed it inside the white king he had brought as gift. [516] When he stepped out onto the spire's edge, the wrong sky had begun to right itself, colors sliding back into place like oil on water. [541] Joren opened his arms to embrace the unknown with open arms, though his shoulders already ached with coming frost. [560] The winter poured into him, not as snow, but as memory, and for a moment he saw every winter that had ever been. [583] Then he began to descend, each step a careful theft in reverse, stealing warmth back for the world below. [602] Behind him, the Queen wound the pocket sundial lid and placed it at the center of her board, where it cast no shadow but gave off heat. [629] In the villages below, people would tell stories of a warm wind that came every spring for eleven years, carrying the scent of high ice and old chess oil. [658] None of them knew it was a thief learning, finally, how to give without keeping score. [674] And far above, the spire, no longer icebound, stood clear against a righted sky, waiting for its next player. [693]</story>
