<story>On Veridia, the orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches, the harvest had failed for the third season. [18] After strangers become family, that kind of failure stops being statistics and starts being personal. [33] We were twelve refugees who had landed with nothing, now bound by borrowed blankets and shared blight. [50] Mara, the eldest, tended the roots, while Jonah fixed the wind-scoops, and I watched the sky for rain that never came. [71] Above us, the nebula of respite hung low and violet, a swirling storm that promised water but never delivered. [90] It had been named by early cartographers who thought its color meant comfort, but we knew better. [107] Then the ghost appeared in the dry rows between the amber trees. [119] He was altruistic ghost, the lingering of the orchard's first planter, dead a hundred years but still pruning. [137] He did not haunt; he helped, lifting fallen branches with translucent hands. [149] We called him Silas, and he was strangely trustworthy despite being dead. [161] Children fell asleep beside him, and even the anxious adults felt calm when he passed. [176] Silas could not speak above a whisper, and the wind stole his voice before we could hear. [193] So he found another way. [198] Each morning, through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn, he told us what the trees needed. [215] The sidewalks were old colony paths, cracked and empty before sunrise, perfect for his pale chalk lines. [232] One dawn the message read: THE ARK-BLOOM CANNOT DRINK ALONE. [242] We gathered around the looping script, confused. [249] Mara wanted to reexamine the irrigation logs, convinced we had missed a leak. [262] Jonah argued we should dig deeper wells, but the aquifer was already dust. [275] I traced Silas's chalk with my finger and felt how cold it was, like morning alone. [291] That evening Silas led me to the storage shed, pointing at a dented object on the highest shelf. [309] It was a butterfly garden watering bell, copper green with tiny cut-out wings along its rim. [325] As a child on Earth, I had used one just like it to mist monarchs without drowning them. [343] When tipped, it did not pour in a gush but breathed a fine, sighing mist. [358] Silas rang it once, and a low tone rolled through the orchard, startling sleepy chordwings from the branches. [376] I finally understood his plan, and why he needed us all. [387] Silas wanted to orchestrate collective effort, because no single well or hose could wake the nebula. [403] He believed if we all misted at once, from every arch, the negative ions would seed the clouds inside the nebula of respite and pull rain down. [430] It sounded like folklore, brambled hope tangled with grief, beautiful and likely useless. [443] The kind of hope that catches on your sleeves as you push through thorns but keeps you moving forward anyway. [463] At dawn the next day his chalk read: YOU HAVE HANDS. [474] We made more bells. [478] We hammered scrap into perforated domes, punched wings into tin cans, taught the children to keep the arc steady. [497] On the third night, we climbed. [503] Each family took a floating rock arch, cold stone suspended by old magnetism, each overlooking a quadrant of trees. [522] Silas floated above the center, his light thin in the violet dusk. [534] At his signal we tipped. [539] Twelve streams of mist rose instead of falling, caught and turned to silver by the wind. [555] The air grew dense, humming, and the nebula of respite shivered, its violet bruising to deep indigo. [572] For a breathless minute nothing happened, and I thought our brambled hope had finally snagged for good. [589] Then it rained. [592] Not a storm, but a soft, endless sigh, the kind the watering bell made. [606] It beaded on leaves, on ghosts, on upturned faces that had forgotten how to be wet. [622] Silas smiled, and for the first time since his death, he began to fade. [636] We shouted for him to stay, but his altruistic work was done. [648] By morning the sidewalks were clean, the chalk washed away, and the arches dripped like prayer beads. [665] We still leave a fresh watering bell on the shed shelf, just in case he remembers us.
