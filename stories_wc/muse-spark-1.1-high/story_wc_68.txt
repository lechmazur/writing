<story>After the plague ends, the upper city rang its bells for thirty days and then went silent again. [18] Below, in the sunken city ruins, Marlowe kept living in the sewer as he had for three years. [36] He called himself a philosopher, though no one above paid him to think. [49] He was rationally rebellious in the way only a man with too much time underground can be. [66] He did not believe in returning to the old calendar. [76] He did not believe in the new council either. [85] The city had given him two competing narratives to explain why the streets had sunk. [100] The priests said the sinking was punishment. [107] The engineers said it was bad foundations and too much rain. [118] Marlowe listened to both through the storm drain and found both convenient. [130] His work was to preserve ancient methods, not ancient morals. [140] Before the plague, he had been an apprentice horologist. [149] Now he kept the old water clocks alive. [157] The council wanted electric time, but the grid still failed every week. [169] Water and gravity did not fail, he said, with an abrasive calm that made children stop throwing rocks. [187] The boy who brought him bread asked if time still mattered down here. [200] Marlowe told him time mattered more when you could not see the sun. [213] He showed the boy his latest find, a seafarer's chronometer cog still bright after a century in mud. [231] He had pulled it from the wall itself, through sedimentary layers of ash, brick dust, and plague lime. [249] Each layer was a year, he explained, each year a decision to forget. [262] The cog was the size of a thumbnail and cut to tolerate salt and panic. [277] It had once set longitude, which meant it had once told lost men where they were. [293] Marlowe needed it to finish the great clock in the cistern. [304] The great clock would run on water and would not need the council's power. [318] It would keep the old way of measuring. [326] The council sent a woman named Jene to talk him out of it. [339] She had survived the plague by sealing the archive. [348] She told him the city could not afford competing narratives anymore. [359] One official history would keep order. [365] Marlowe offered her tea made from boiled rainwater and asked whether order was true. [379] She looked at his diagrams, at the cog fitted into a wooden escapement, and did not answer immediately. [397] For a week she returned and watched him work. [406] She began to agonize, not loudly but in the way archivists do, over what should be saved. [423] She had been taught that preservation meant selection. [431] Marlowe taught her that preservation meant use. [438] He told her the chronometer cog was made to be wound by scared hands, not worshipped behind glass. [456] When the council finally cut the sewer access, he kept working in darkness. [469] He knew the ruins better than they did. [477] He knew which arch would hold, which water was clean, which echo meant a collapse. [492] On the fortieth day after the bells stopped, he started the great clock. [505] Water fell from the upper overflow, turned the wheel, caught the cog, and ticked. [519] The sound traveled up through drains and cracks into the quiet city. [531] People stopped to listen, because it was not a bell. [541] Jene stood beside him in the cistern, soaked and smiling despite herself. [553] She said the council would come. [559] Marlowe said let them come and learn how to read it. [570] The clock did not argue. [575] It simply kept time, as ancient methods do, indifferent to who claims the story. [589] Below the drowned avenues, that tick became the only history everyone could agree on. [603]</story>
