<story>The zen-minded potter named Jun had spent thirty years making bowls that were meant to be broken. [17] He did not believe in permanence, only in attention, and that made him curious about stubborn places. [34] For years he traveled the outer salt to understand why some places resist change when the world around them burns and shifts. [56] He navigated by following sun patterns, reading noon shadows off cracked basins and counting the amber length of late light. [76] The old maps called his destination a null, but the heat haze marked it as green. [92] It was an abandoned beekeeping oasis in a scorched wasteland, impossible and humming faintly with memory. [108] He arrived as chimney smoke flattens, that dead hour before dusk when the air presses low and every fire holds its breath. [130] The clay houses were empty, their doors lashed open, and the hive boxes stood in strict rows like blank pagodas. [150] No bees remained, but the oasis thrum persisted, low and resonant, as if the wood itself remembered flight. [168] Jun set down his pack and his wheel, and he bowed to the place with sharp gentleness, acknowledging both its wound and its will. [192] Inside the largest hut he found a kiln still warm, though no one had fired it in a decade. [211] On the kiln shelf sat a single piece of faded lantern glass, clouded amber at the edges and clear at its heart. [233] When he lifted it, light fell through and threw two shadows that did not match his hand. [250] One shadow was his, lined and steady; the other was younger, and it moved a half second late. [268] Jun understood then that the oasis was not abandoned but holding fractured timelines together like a palm holding water. [287] The beekeepers had not left; they were simply tending hives a season out of phase, in a summer that had not yet burned. [310] Jun could hear children laughing in one layer, and in another, the dry cough of the last well engine failing. [330] He made tea, and the water boiled with a sound that harmonized with the wooden thrum of the hives. [349] As night approached, the oasis breathed, and its small remaining vines, phototropic to kindness, leaned toward him, not the sun. [369] He had seen such plants only near monasteries, where they grew toward sweeping courtyards because someone cared. [386] Jun spent three days repairing the central hive, not to house bees but to tune it. [402] He mixed ash from the scorched plain with sweet well mud and coaxed a bowl on his wheel that held two rims at once. [426] One rim existed now, the other a year ago, and the seam between them sang when touched. [443] He placed the faded lantern glass inside the bowl, and both began to glow without fire. [459] The glow steadied the fracture, and for a heartbeat Jun could see every keeper who had ever doubted leaving. [478] They had stayed because someone had to keep the sweetness accessible, even when sweetness was out of time. [496] Jun understood that resistance was not stubbornness but tending, a repeated act of kindness that refused to be erased. [515] The oasis survived because it was loved daily, and that love bent light, bent time, bent even dust. [533] He lifted the bowl and spoke into it, not a prayer but an instruction for whoever would come next. [552] Keep it small, he whispered, and keep it kind, and it will outlast the fire. [567] Then he buried the bowl under the central hive where the thrum was strongest, so future feet would feel it before they saw it. [591] The glass caught the last sun and threw his shadow forward, older this time, hands empty, walking home. [609] He left the wheel behind for the next potter, its stone still warm from his palms. [625] Outside, the wasteland stretched white and lethal, but inside the oasis the light held, doubled, patient. [641] Jun walked out by following sun patterns again, only now he followed the kindness-tilted green instead of the hard blaze. [661]</story>
