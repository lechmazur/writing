<story>Mara Keen had been a crater inspector for twenty years, long after anyone else wanted the job. [17]
During the space age, when telescopes watched Mars and men circled the Earth, her work was considered obsolete and lonely. [37]
She still climbed alone to the moonlit summit above the observatory every winter, carrying her heavy plate camera. [55]
It was not habit that drove her, but a purposefully curious faith that small changes mattered. [71]
On the northern face, the Great Hollow crater was filling in, year by year, with windblown dust and thaw. [90]
Once a perfect bowl carved by an ancient impact, it would soon be smooth and gone. [106]
She wanted to photograph the last of something precious before sunrise erased its shadow forever. [121]
In her coat pocket she kept a single nightwatch's hourglass sand grain sealed in glass, a relic from her grandfather. [141]
He had told her it had outlasted storms and sieges because everyone thought it was too small to matter. [160]
She thought of that grain whenever the summit wind tried to tell her that her work was pointless. [178]
The night she arrived, the sky was clear and the moon laid silver over the snow, making the crater look deeper than ever. [201]
She set up her tripod on the ridge where the wind could not shake it, and waited. [218]
Her real plan was not for this one photograph. [227]
She was using chemical reactions for timed messages to her future replacement, who might come years after she could no longer climb. [249]
She had coated cards with a thin layer of potassium iodide and starch, sealed them in tin. [266]
When exposed to air, they would slowly oxidize and bloom blue, but only after a precise number of hours she had calculated. [288]
She placed one card in the crater's deepest shadow, another on the sunward lip, the third tucked under her tripod. [308]
Each would turn color at dawn, at noon, at dusk, telling whoever found them when this last perfect shadow had fallen. [329]
It was a fragile system, but she liked that knowledge needed tending. [341]
The moon passed overhead, and the cold deepened until even her breath felt sharp. [355]
Somewhere far above, a satellite blinked, a reminder that the world was watching other skies now. [371]
She did not mind. [375]
Just before first light, the air changed. [382]
The wind dropped, the snow stopped whispering, and a vast dawning hush settled over the summit and the crater below. [402]
It was the moment she had waited all night to see, when the crater held only the faintest curve of shadow, thin as a pencil line. [428]
In that hush, the mountain felt like it was holding its breath with her. [442]
She uncovered the plate, exposed it for forty seconds, and felt a quiet grief as she slid the dark slide back. [463]
It was dawnlit melancholy, beautiful and final, light arriving to erase what it revealed. [477]
When the sun cleared the far ridge, her chemical cards began their slow work, invisible at first. [494]
She packed her camera, left the grain of sand on the tripod leg for luck for the next inspector, and started down. [516]
She knew the photograph would outlast the crater itself, and that was enough. [529]
Behind her, the summit was already bright, but the hush lingered a moment longer in the hollow she had guarded so long. [551]
Years later, a young surveyor would find the blue-stained cards and understand exactly when the last rim had vanished. [570]
He would also find the glass bead with the sand grain inside, and wonder why someone had left something so tiny at the top of the world. [597]
Perhaps he would look up, feel the same hush before sunrise, and climb again next winter. [613]
Mara never returned, but her image remained catalogued in the observatory basement, labeled simply Last Winter, 1964. [630]</story>
