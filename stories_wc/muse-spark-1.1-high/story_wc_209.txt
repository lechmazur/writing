<story>Mara had always been a vivid mural artist, the kind whose colors bled off walls and into people's lives. [19] She was quietly driven in a way that made winters bearable, sketching even when her fingers went numb. [37] When her grandmother died, she left Mara not money but a collection of old postcards, each one faded and franked from towns Mara had never visited. [63] On the backs, in looping ink, her grandmother had written the same line for forty years: to understand why flowers bloom at night. [86] The last postcard showed an amber pavilion in autumn, its wood glowing like held honey against a bruised sky. [105] Mara knew that pavilion, though it was a hundred miles north, and she decided she would paint it before it collapsed. [126] She waited across the melting of winter's last snow, watching the river behind her studio swell and clear. [144] In March she loaded her van with buckets, scaffolding, and that shoebox of postcards tied with kitchen twine. [162] The town that owned the pavilion had hired her to cover its inner dome with night flowers, a strange request she did not question. [186] She arrived at dusk and found the structure leaning, its paint peeling in long amber strips like sunburnt skin. [205] Inside, the air held a particular stillness she had only ever felt in churches, a dawning hush that made her set down her brushes. [229] For three nights she tried to paint, but the moonflowers she imagined looked false, flat, theatrical in their effort to be mysterious. [251] She would have to reckon with the fact that she had never stayed awake long enough to watch real ones open. [272] On the fourth night, unable to sleep, she walked to the old cathedral on the hill whose caretaker had once written her grandmother a postcard. [297] He let her in without words and pointed to the west wall, where sunrise would come through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window. [324] She sat on the cold stone and watched. [332] At first there was only gray, then a thin blue, then suddenly the light broke and split itself across the floor in a hundred careful lines. [358] Each layer of glass held a different flower, cut not painted, and as the sun moved, they seemed to open one after another. [381] Moonflower, evening primrose, night jasmine, all invisible by day, all alive in light. [394] She understood then that they did not bloom despite the dark, but because the dark had been tender and held them without demanding. [417] It was an umbral tenderness, the kind that asks nothing and offers everything, the same quiet her grandmother had chased in postcards. [439] She ran back to the pavilion as the sun climbed higher, her boots loud on the frost. [456] There she did not paint flowers at all, at first, but the darkness behind them, thick and soft and patient. [476] Then, into that dark, she brushed the faintest edges of petals, letting the night itself be what made them visible. [496] When the town mayor came at noon, he stared up and went silent, as if afraid to wake something. [515] Children who visited after school lay on the pavilion floor and whispered that the ceiling felt like being tucked in. [535] The mural did not shout, as her old work had, but waited. [547] People came from other towns not to take pictures but to sit, because in that amber pavilion they remembered how to exhale. [569] Mara kept the postcards in a tin beneath the pavilion's bench, and sometimes she added her own, blank, for strangers to find. [591] She never wrote an answer to her grandmother's question, because the painting was the answer and also because some wonders do not need explaining, only witnessing. [617]</story>
