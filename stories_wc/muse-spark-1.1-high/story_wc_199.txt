<story>Mira was the most orderly seed sorter the ashen skyborne arbor had ever rooted. [14] For thirty seasons she cataloged windfall seeds on the high boughs where the world thinned into cloud. [31] The arbor drifted above the lowlands on slow breaths of ash and steam, its bark gray as quiet winter mornings. [51] Mira loved the work because every seed knew exactly where it belonged. [63] That autumn, however, the leaves began to fall wrong. [72] They spiraled upward, paused midair, and dropped in patterns too deliberate to be wind. [86] During the hush where past meets prophecy, the oldest boughs said, the arbor listens hardest. [101] It was regional folklore that the first Keeper had taught the trees to read the sky by scattering seeds like letters. [122] Mira did not believe in folklore until the leaves began spelling. [133] To decode the message in falling leaves she abandoned her ledgers and followed each descent with patient eyes. [151] Every evening she returned to her hollow with pockets full of oddities and a head full of unsorted wonder. [170] She found comfort in building tiny monuments to forgotten things along the branch roads. [184] A thimble cairn for the lost cappings of acorns, a twig arch for the names of sparrows that no longer visited, a lichen pillow for a single dropped button. [213] It was on the button shrine that she first saw the speck. [225] A moonstone cabochon dust speck rested in the crown of moss, catching no light but making more. [242] She tried not to prod it, for orderly sorters do not poke mysteries they cannot classify. [258] But the speck pulsed when a leaf fell through its halo, and so she did. [273] She reached one careful fingertip and felt the whole arbor inhale. [284] The leaf that had brushed the speck did not land. [294] It hovered, unfolded into light, and sank into her palm like warm water. [307] In that instant Mira understood the folklore had been a manual misremembered as myth. [321] The seeds were not cargo. [326] They were punctuation. [329] The arbor had been composing a letter for three hundred years, waiting for someone patient enough to notice the syntax of fall. [351] Mira laughed, and her laugh smelled of damp earth and green, a mossy delight bubbling up from her boots to her teeth. [373] She began to rearrange her monuments, not by size or kind but by the story their shadows told when leaves passed over them. [396] The fallen button became a period. [402] The acorn caps became commas, breaths between long thoughts. [411] The sparrow arch became a question the arbor had been asking the lowlands for a century. [427] Why have you forgotten how to fly without burning? [436] Night fell while she worked, ash swirling like moths around the boughs. [448] She stepped into the thin of the canopy where wind sorts what hands cannot. [462] Holding the moonstone speck aloft, she let it catch every drifting leaf. [474] Each leaf that touched the light lifted, then settled onto one of her tiny shrines, and the arbor shivered as sentences finally completed themselves. [498] The wind came soft and smelled like a library after rain. [509] Mira felt herself loosening at the edges, as if she too emerges transformed always when truly seen. [526] The arbor was not losing leaves. [532] It was mailing them. [536] She pressed the speck into the softest moss on her own chest, and her apron of seed pockets bloomed overnight into new saplings. [559] When dawn cracked the ashen sky, the lowland children looked up and saw the arbor writing again for the first time in generations. [582] They could not read it yet, but they felt the mossy delight of being addressed. [597] Mira remained, no longer only a sorter but a tender of sentences, walking her branch roads and tending monuments that had finally learned to answer. [622]</story>
