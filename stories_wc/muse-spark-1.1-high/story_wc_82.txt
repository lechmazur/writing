<story>Mira was a crafty lantern maker who lived among wind-chimes and wild mint in a floating island tea garden. [19] It was after the shadow detaches that her brother stopped visiting the garden path. [33] In their childhood, every lost tooth and fever had been given a light, because she believed sorrow should be seen. [53] Now she worked to fold grief into origami cranes, each folded from paper soaked in tea and tears. [71] At the far edge of the archipelago stood the glass basilica on a floating isle, a hollow cathedral that collected the dreams of the grieving. [96] Its walls were lined with dream recording vessels, delicate lanterns that could catch a sleeping thought and hold it. [115] The sisters who tended it called Mira's condition luminous torment, bright enough to guide by but impossible to live inside. [135] She learned that true light required gem polishing, not just glassblowing, slow patient smoothing until the flaw became the clarity. [155] She wanted to transform her brother's last nightmare into something she could keep without bleeding. [170] When she entered the basilica, her own shadow lingered too long on the threshold, heavy and unwilling. [187] Inside, a spiral stair descends while ascending, an optical trick that made her feel both buried and lifted with each step. [208] At the center sat an empty recording bowl, humming for a mind willing to pay. [223] The crafty tea monks who kept the garden had warned her that lanterns could keep a voice but not answer it. [244] She set her crane papers alight in a small dish and breathed in the bitter steam of bergamot and ash. [264] For years she had polished stones for other families, taking their cloudy grief-gems and making them shine with captured dreamlight. [284] Each gem held a single night, a single mercy, a single last word never said awake. [300] She placed her brother's favorite chipped mug on the lip of the bowl, because objects remember better than people. [319] Then she lay down and let the basilica drink the dream she'd kept locked behind her teeth since the funeral. [339] In the dream she was back in the floating island tea garden, but the leaves were glass and sang when wind passed. [361] Her brother sat with his back to her, folding paper that refused to stay folded. [376] She tried to speak and instead light poured from her mouth, puddling on the tea tables. [392] He turned, and his face was her face at ten years old, furious at being left alone. [409] I didn't want your lanterns, he said, I wanted you to sit in the dark with me. [426] The dream recording caught that sentence and sealed it in glass, clear as mountain water. [441] She woke with her cheeks wet and a new lantern humming in her hands, warm as a chest. [459] Inside it, a tiny paper crane circled endlessly around a chipped mug, learning how to land. [475] For the first time since his passing, she did not want to fix the light or make it brighter. [494] She wanted to keep it uneven, flickering, honest, a light that knew grief does not end but changes shape. [513] She carried the lantern back down the stair that descends while ascending and felt her knees ache in both directions. [533] Outside, her shadow finally detached from the threshold and rejoined her feet, thinner now, willing to follow. [550] She returned to the floating island tea garden before nightfall, where the monks were harvesting moon-white leaves. [567] She hung the new lantern among the others, but lower, where children could see that sadness can also glow. [586] Later that evening a traveler asked what gem polishing technique she used to get such a soft flame. [604]</story>
