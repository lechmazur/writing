<story>The summer theater at Hawthorn House had failed again, and after the business closes for the season I was left to lock the hedge maze alone. [26] My grandfather built the maze around the old orchard so visitors would get lost on purpose, but in August nobody got lost at all. [50] I grew up synesthetically blessed, which meant every footstep in gravel sounded navy blue and every distant laugh tasted like green apples. [72] That night the air was too still for August, and the lantern I carried made the high hedges look like wet ink. [94] In the center, where the map said there was nothing, the mystic orchard garden concealed within a hedge maze opened its gate for me. [118] The trees were not apple trees anymore; they were props, half stage flat and half real, their leaves crinkling like paper programs. [140] Under the tallest one stood a man in a powdered wig and a patched waistcoat, translucent at the edges, bowing to an audience that was not there. [167] You saw me, he said, delighted, because I am an encore-seeking stage ghost and no one has clapped for me in ninety years. [190] I should have run, but his voice was saffron yellow and sad, and I asked what play he was stuck in. [211] He told me this orchard was once the green room where actors waited, and that time had slipped and become a fractured timeline the night the theater burned. [239] In one shard it was 1932 and he was singing his finale, in another it was now and I was about to sell the property to pay my grandfather's debts. [269] I felt the familiar ache of rising nostalgia, not for my own childhood but for his, for pomade and velvet curtains and matinees my grandfather described when I was small. [299] He reached into his waistcoat and offered me a hand-painted tarot coin, its face showing not the Fool but a tiny open door. [322] If you can make them intertwine, he whispered, the old smell of orange blossom in the air, you can stitch the timeline shut. [345] I asked how. [348] By producing echoes, he said, because a note sung right will bounce through every year the hedge has lived. [367] That was when I understood my small strange gift was not strange at all here, because the garden needed someone who could see sound. [391] So I stood beside the ghost, placed the tarot coin on my tongue for luck though it tasted like copper and lavender, and sang. [415] Not well, off key, my grandfather's lullaby about a traveling market coming through Hawthorn in late summer. [432] The hedges caught it and threw it back, and the orchard filled with blue and gold ribbons only I could see. [453] The ghost lifted his head, hearing his own cue from 1932 in my voice, and sang his finale over my chorus. [474] Our two songs did not match, but the echoes layered until the fractured air began to hum in one chord, saffron and navy finally meeting. [499] I remembered then why my grandfather planted this maze after the fire, to lure possibility from the brink of despair, to give any lost performer a way back. [527] He had told me all roads in a maze lead to the center if you are patient, and all debts become stories if you let them echo long enough. [556] The trees shivered and became fully real, apples heavy and true, and the ghost's coat lost its translucence for a single breath. [578] He bowed again, deeper this time to me, and the tarot coin on my tongue warmed and showed a new face, a small standing ovation. [603] When the echo faded, the garden gate was just a gap in the hedge again, but I could still smell orange blossom where there should have been only damp earth. [633] I walked back through the maze after midnight, humming, watching my footsteps paint the dark blue, and I knew I would not sell Hawthorn House.
