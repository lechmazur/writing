Julian adjusted his spectacles, looking out from the rusted outpost by a frozen lake. [14] As a dry-witted atlas editor, he had spent thirty years correcting the geographical delusions of dead explorers from a warm London basement. [36] Out here, the air possessed a worrying tranquility, so dead and windless that even the corrugated iron walls had stopped groaning. [57] "At least the margins are clean," Julian muttered to the vast, white emptiness. [70]

He had not come to the Arctic Circle for adventure, a concept he considered a symptom of poor planning. [89] He was here to find where the rivers begin. [98] For centuries, cartographers assumed the four great continental rivers flowed from separate mountain ranges. [112] Julian, however, was subtly confident in his own trigonometry, which proved the valleys sloped backward toward a single, impossible basin. [132] It was a hydrological singular convergence, a hidden nexus where subterranean aquifers met before splitting to feed the world. [151]

To prove it, he needed the work of his predecessor, an eccentric linguist who had vanished here ten winters ago. [171] On the iron desk lay the man's sole surviving journal, a battered lexicon of fifty indigenous dialects. [188] Julian rubbed his thumb over the polyglot's phrasebook dog-ear, a sharply folded corner marking page twelve. [204] The folded triangle pointed directly to a native idiom that translated to: "The glass sweats only after the falcon returns." [224]

Three hours ago, Julian had released the station's solitary gyrfalcon, a fierce creature that nested in the broken radar dish. [244] If an open thermal spring existed beneath the ice sheet, the bird would find the warm updrafts. [261] Until then, the worrying tranquility of the landscape pressed against the outpost like deep water against a bathysphere. [279] The silence was not peaceful; it was the coiled stillness of a geological trap waiting to spring. [296]

A sharp metallic clatter broke the hush above him. [305] Julian climbed the narrow ladder into the observation cupola just as the grey bird folded its wings upon the outer sill. [326] Its talons were wet with fresh, unfrozen mud. [334] It was time, for the native proverb promised the true map would appear after the falcon returns. [351]

Julian did not look at the bird, nor at the blinding expanse of the frozen lake. [367] Instead, he watched the thick panes of the cupola. [376] The gyrfalcon's perch had dislodged a seal in the rusted casing, allowing a draft of strangely warm, subterranean vapor to seep up through the floorboards. [401] As the humid plume struck the freezing window, Julian witnessed the method the dead linguist had died trying to decode: the pattern of condensation on glass. [427]

Tiny droplets formed, merged, and slid downward in branching, dendritic trails. [438] Most men would have seen mere weather, a random fogging of the view. [451] Julian, subtly confident in his decades of inspecting copperplate engravings, recognized the exact geometry of the moisture. [468] The delicate currents of heat rising through the floorboards were mimicking the deep geothermal vents below the lake. [486]

"Well," Julian whispered, adjusting his cuffs, "someone owes the Royal Society an apology, and I suspect it is the mountains." [506]

The pattern of condensation on glass was not random at all; it was a living relief map. [523] At the center of the pane, a dense bead of water trembled, representing the singular convergence of the deep earth's pressurized waters. [545] From that central drop, four distinct rivulets traced outward across the pane, matching the exact coastal trajectories of the four continental rivers in his proofs. [570] This exact coordinate, directly beneath the frozen lake, was where the rivers begin. [583]

Yet as the condensation thickened, the worrying tranquility outside began to curdle into something far more immediate. [600] The air pressure in the rusted outpost dropped, making Julian's ears pop. [612] He looked down at the polyglot's phrasebook dog-ear once more, noticing a secondary translation scribbled in the margin below the fold. [633] The bird's mud and the sweating glass did not merely locate the source; they were meant to herald its waking. [653]

Beneath the ice, the winter plug of the great aquifer was giving way to the spring thaw all at once. [673] The terrifying stillness had been the pause before an upward waterfall. [684] Across the frozen lake, a single black fracture shot from the center to the shore without making a sound. [703] Then came a low, rhythmic thrum that vibrated through Julian's boots, promising to herald a flood that would redraw the northern hemisphere by morning. [727]

As the dry-witted atlas editor packed his brass compass and the linguist's book into his leather satchel, the ice outside groaned like a waking leviathan. [752] "I suppose," Julian said to the falcon as he unlatched the heavy iron door to make his escape, "this means the third edition will be significantly damper than the second." [782]
