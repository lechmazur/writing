Julian had spent his career as a vivid mural artist, famous for splashing violent scarlets and electric cobalts across city plazas that baked under the noon sun. [27] Yet when he inherited his late sister Clara's sanctuary, an amber pavilion in autumn where golden glass panes trapped the warmth of dying maples, his bold palette failed him. [56] He stood before the pavilion's blank northern wall, quietly driven by a need that had haunted him since her funeral: to understand why flowers bloom at night. [83] Clara had cultivated moonflowers and evening primroses in the shadowed corners of this conservatory, nurturing things that opened only when the world went blind. [107] On her potting bench lay his only clue, a collection of old postcards bound in faded twine. [124]

Each card featured a vintage botanical lithograph of a nocturnal blossom on the front, while the backs were covered in Clara's cramped handwriting, listing refractive indices and lunar angles. [153] Julian spread the collection of old postcards across his scaffold, touching the velvet smudges of charcoal she had left on the edges. [175] There was an umbral tenderness to the way she had lived, finding solace in dusk rather than the harsh glare Julian always painted. [198] If he wanted to paint a memorial mural on the conservatory wall that truly honored her spirit, he had to reckon with the mystery of her dark garden. [226] Why would a plant hoard its brief, fragile beauty for the freezing hours when no human eye was awake to admire it? [248]

Throughout November, inside the amber pavilion in autumn, Julian mixed pigments of bruised plum, silver ash, and deep indigo, trying to capture the mercy of the dark. [275] Every evening he watched the potted cereus buds remain tightly shut against his painted imitations of twilight. [292] His vigil stretched out across the melting of winter's last snow, as the autumn leaves outside rotted under frost and finally gave way to weeping icicles in March.
