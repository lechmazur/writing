<story>The junkyard prophet called herself Ossa because she could not remember her real name long enough to miss it. [19] She lived where the county dump slumped into a fog sculpture garden, a place of salt-white shapes breathing in from the sea. [41] Her mind worked backward through time, so she dreamed tomorrow but forgot it by dawn, leaving only a chalk smear on her palm. [64] Ossa was clinging to echoes because echoes were the only promises that stayed after the future erased itself. [82] She kept a shack of old videotapes left unwatched, their labels faded to ghosts, and amid their hush she listened for a sound she had lost. [108] Her motivation was simple and impossible: to capture the sound of growing older before her precognitively forgetful brain swallowed the memory of it again. [132] Each morning she set out to locate the source of that sound, following clues she had left for herself the night before. [154] The method was always the same: arranging driftwood as directional markers across the fog sculpture garden, arrows for a woman who would not remember making them. [180] With a piece of plain chalk she marked each log with a single word, knowing the salt fog would erase it by noon. [203] The sculptures loomed like frozen tides, hands and whales and staircases that never led anywhere. [218] She followed the driftwood she had arranged, though she could not recall placing it, and the markers felt like messages from a kinder sister. [242] A tidal longing pulled at her ribs, the moon's old gravity asking her to walk into the water and forget everything. [263] The driftwood led her to a sculpture shaped like a television, its screen made of fog and its base buried in bottle caps. [286] Beside it lay a videotape labeled with her childhood handwriting, though she had never owned a camera. [303] Ossa carried the tape back to the shack, stepping over driftwood markers that now pointed home. [319] The hush of old videotapes left unwatched pressed against her ears as she slid the tape into a scavenged VCR. [339] The screen bloomed gray, and there was her mother, younger than Ossa remembered, whispering into a microphone. [356] "Listen," her mother said, "this is what it sounds like when you grow older." [370] The sound was not a voice but a slow tide of breath, creaking knees, and rain on a tin roof. [390] Ossa pressed her palm to the speaker, trying to hold the sound in the chalk dust on her skin. [409] Then the future arrived early, a flash of herself tomorrow, and she forgot why she was crying. [426] She clung to the echo of the sound, repeating it aloud until the words turned strange. [442] With the plain chalk she wrote on the shack wall: GROWING OLDER SOUNDS LIKE THE SEA LEAVING. [459] By morning she would not remember writing it, but she would feel the tidal longing and follow the driftwood again. [479] The junkyard children called her a prophet because she sometimes told them what they would lose before they lost it. [499] Yet prophecy without memory was only a cruel kind of music, a song she could hear but never sing. [518] So she continued to locate the tape each day, as if for the first time, and each day the fog sculpture garden rearranged itself around her. [544] The driftwood markers shifted with the tide, but they always pointed toward the shack or the sea. [561] Ossa learned to love the repetition, the way an echo loves a canyon wall. [575] One evening she found a new word chalked on a driftwood plank: STAY. [588] She did not know whether she had written it yesterday or would write it tomorrow. [603] So she stayed, listening to the hush of old videotapes left unwatched, letting the sound of growing older wash through her like a tide she could not hold. [631] And when the memory slipped away again, she picked up the plain chalk and began arranging driftwood as directional markers, clinging to echoes, trying once more to locate what she had already found.
