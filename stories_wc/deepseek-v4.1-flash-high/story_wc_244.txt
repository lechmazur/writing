<story>Marla Quince was the elusive disaster-relief logistician whom every crisis forgot to thank and every government denied knowing. [18] She arrived at the disused railway station overgrown with ivy just when the story changes, carrying a fragment of moon rock in a dented biscuit tin. [44] Her smile was desperately jocular, as if grief had learned to tell jokes to keep from screaming. [61] She had spent twenty years moving medicine through wars, floods, and blockades, and she had never once cured her own loneliness. [82] The station had no trains, no timetable, no officials, only ivy swallowing the platform and a polite anarchy of wildflowers breaking through concrete. [105] She moved through improbable rumor chains that always began with a child's question and ended with a convoy reaching a village no map acknowledged. [129] She believed every kindness created a debt, and every debt, if passed along, could become a road. [146] Bound by reciprocity, she never accepted help without inventing a way to return it twice. [161] That was why the moon rock mattered; it was not a treasure but a token that demanded movement. [179] A boy named Ivo appeared from behind a ticket booth, saying his grandmother needed insulin and the pharmacy had locked its doors. [201] Marla did not ask how he knew she was there. [211] She simply handed him the moon rock fragment and told him to show it to the baker, who owed a favor to the nurse, who owed a favor to the bus driver. [243] The boy ran, and the rumor chain began to catalyze around him like a chemical reaction made of whispers. [262] By noon, the baker had traded bread for the rock, the nurse had traded insulin for the bread, and the bus driver had traded a ride for the insulin. [291] Each person held the fragment only long enough to feel its strange gravity, then passed it on with a promise. [311] The ivy seemed to lean closer, listening. [318] Marla laughed, desperately jocular, and said that loneliness was just a supply chain with no destination. [334] She wanted to find the cure for loneliness, but she had always looked for it in one face, one home, one person who would never leave. [360] The station taught her otherwise. [365] By evening, the platform filled with people carrying cans, blankets, batteries, and rumors. [378] No one gave orders, because polite anarchy requires only that everyone refuse to be useless. [393] A woman with a cracked phone said she had heard the moon rock came from a mission that never returned. [413] A teenager said it was a piece of a mirror that had fallen from the sky. [429] Marla let the stories grow, because improbable rumor chains work best when no single link claims authority. [446] The fragment traveled from hand to hand, and every hand became a small station. [460] When the moon rock returned to Marla, it was wrapped in a child's drawing of a train made of stars. [480] She understood then that reciprocity had bound her not to a debt but to a web. [496] The cure for loneliness was not a person; it was a chain of people who expected to hear from you again. [517] Marla placed the fragment back in the biscuit tin and left it on the bench. [532] She wrote a note: "Pass it on, and if you cannot, pass on something better." [547] Then she vanished into the ivy, still the elusive disaster-relief logistician, still desperately jocular. [561] The station did not become a headquarters; it became a rumor that anyone could enter. [576] Days later, a supply truck arrived with no driver, unloaded antibiotics, and left before anyone could thank it. [594] The people of the platform laughed, politely anarchic, and began to sort the boxes. [608] They had learned that help could be a rumor chain with no end, only next links. [624] And somewhere down a flooded road, Marla heard the chain rattle forward and felt, for the first time, less alone. [644] The story changes whenever someone accepts a fragment of moon rock and refuses to keep it. [660]</story>
