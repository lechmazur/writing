<story>Between vanishing stars, the letterpress operator known only as Vey set the final line of type in the topaz workshop. [20] The workshop glowed amber because its walls were cut from raw topaz, and above the glass roof the last stars were going out one by one. [46] Vey was cryptic by trade and by temperament, a printer who never explained the colophon or the smell of ozone that clung to his ink. [71] On the composing stone lay a halo fragment, a crescent of impossible metal that hummed when someone nearby felt anything too strongly. [93] Vey used it to practice transposed empathy, a process where he borrowed another person's feelings and let them settle into his bones like weather. [117] Because he habitually borrowed personalities, he sometimes answered in a dead woman's cadence or laughed with a child's stolen delight. [137] His motivation was not cruelty but curiosity: he wanted to understand the mathematics of emotion. [152] Joy, grief, shame, and hope could be plotted as vectors, he believed, if only he could collect enough pure samples. [172] The halo fragment was the key, though it never obeyed a clean equation. [185] Tonight, between vanishing stars, a courier arrived with a sealed envelope and a tremor in her hands. [202] Vey did not ask her name; he simply began to disorient her through deliberate ambiguity. [217] "This press prints only what you almost remember," he said, feeding the envelope into the rollers without opening it. [259] Vey touched the crescent, and transposed empathy opened like a door between his skull and hers. [275] He felt her fear first, sharp and blue, then a buried love for someone who had already died. [293] He borrowed that love, as he habitually borrowed personalities, and for a moment he was both himself and the mourning courier. [484] When the stars vanished completely, he closed the topaz workshop and sat in the dark with the halo fragment.
