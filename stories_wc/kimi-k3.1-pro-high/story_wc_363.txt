<story>In a forgotten orchard deep in a bamboo forest, Mira, a wistful loom spinner, still dressed her warp each dawn, though no traveler came to buy her cloth. [28] The pear trees had stopped fruiting, yet their roots carried roads of sound beneath the mountains, and her loom had once woven voices along them. [53] Then came a raveling silence, loosening birdsong, footfalls, and names from the world, thread by thread. [69] Mira heard it most clearly at night, when she called for her brother and received not even the bamboo's answer. [89] Tomas lived beyond the white mountains, in a harbor city no road reached since the passes had drowned. [107] She had promised their dying mother that his last message would not be the quarrel they had shared. [125] Now the river was climbing its banks, and if the harbor gates stayed open, his city would vanish before dawn. [145] To warn him across that impossible distance, Mira needed the loom to recommence its old work. [161] Its shuttle held only a tree sap amber bead, formed from the first pear's resin and stoically sparkling beside her mother's empty chair. [184] Inside it slept one bright note, the final sound the orchard had given up. [198] She had tried every pattern she knew, but each weaving collapsed before the shuttle crossed. [213] At the valley's last rummage sale, while neighbors sold tools for a road already gone, she found a box labeled Orchard, Almost Whole. [236] There were no middle pieces, only jigsaw puzzle edges painted with black bamboo and silver pears. [252] She bought them with her winter buttons, because absence seemed the one thing she understood. [267] Back at the loom, the edges locked into a frame whose empty center matched the space between her hands. [286] Each notch fit a heddle; each pictured root pointed to a peg. [298] The puzzle was not a picture to restore, but instructions for making one from what remained. [314] Mira set the frame around the warp and saw the cost written in its border: four interlocking pears, then four empty stems. [336] Her mother had taught her that signs like those meant a gift the giver could not reclaim. [353] The bead would carry her message, but the silence would take her voice to mend its torn selvage. [371] For years she had saved her voice for apologies, rehearsing them until tenderness sounded like debt. [387] Now she laughed once, surprised by how lightly a promise could become a choice. [401] Through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry, she threaded the amber shuttle and spoke into its warmth. [418] The river has turned; close the gates, and do not come for me. [431] She added, I forgive you, though she understood at last that she was also asking him to live without her. [451] The loom took the words, then the breath behind them. [461] Its first pass tore her mother's lullaby from the room; its second took Tomas's childhood whistle; its third left only the patient labor of her hands. [487] Still she wove, because a message is not its sorrow but the shelter it makes ahead of sorrow. [505] Outside, pear blossoms opened soundlessly, each white edge completing another in the dark. [518] At moonrise the shuttle stopped, and the bead went dull. [528] Mira touched her throat, found the promised hollowness, and felt no triumph, only the clean ache of a door held open. [549] Far away, beyond mountains that had forgotten footsteps, a harbor keeper woke with resin warming his palm. [566] He heard his sister's voice ordering the gates closed, and beneath it, more faintly, the permission he had needed all his life. [588] By dawn the city bells were ringing, though Mira could no longer hear bells. [602] She sat among the bright pears, running her fingers along the puzzle's empty center. [616] Then she lifted her shuttle again, not to speak, but to begin the patient cloth that would keep her brother warm if he ever came home. [642]</story>
