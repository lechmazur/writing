<story>After the substitute body was found beneath the glasshouse, everyone in Bellwether wanted Alder Venn to call it a miracle. [20] He was a bonesetter, a skeptic, and the brother of the child supposedly replaced forty years before. [37] He forgave believers their candles and doubters their jokes, but he trusted only what he could align, splint, and heal. [57] The small body had alder ribs, wax skin, and one human-seeming jaw, empty except for a changeling's baby tooth. [76]

"Keep it," said Tamsin the maze keeper, whose shrewdly innocent smile made every question sound like an invitation. [94] It was she who had found the body while replanting the hedge maze, and she who had sent for him instead of the magistrate. [118]

Alder had once yearned for his sister Mab to return, then had trained himself to yearn for nothing whose absence could break him. [141] Their mother had died believing Mab waited beyond the maze; Alder had called that hope a fracture left unset. [160] Now Tamsin led him through green corridors lit by new solar lanterns shaped like the oil lamps of their childhood. [180] At the maze's heart, a yew arch opened onto a secret garden hidden behind the hedge maze, where pear trees grew around a dry fountain. [205] Its basin held a bronze skeleton of the sky, its joints bent into wrong constellations. [220]

"Mab made this," Tamsin said. [225] "She taught me that mistakes are only stars awaiting better lines." [236]

On the fountain's rim lay Mab's notebook, filled with drawings of bones and gardens, machines designed to turn mistakes into constellations. [257] Alder felt the old cleft in certainty widen: not proof that magic was kind, but proof that Mab had lived, thought, and forgiven him. [281]

Tamsin confessed that the substitute had been her cradle fellow, a changeling infant exchanged beside Mab and later hidden by their frightened mother. [304] "The tooth opens the way, but its jaw is broken." [314] She showed him the bronze skull at the fountain's center, split along one cheek, its empty socket matching the tooth. [334]

Bone setting was Alder's innovation on an old trade: he listened for resonance, warmed the metal, then seated each fragment as carefully as a living vertebra. [360] He did not believe the skull would speak; he repaired it because damage deserved repair, whatever wonder followed. [378]

When he set the tooth into the restored jaw, the fountain clicked, and constellations slid overhead though noon still whitened the pears. [400] One by one, the wrong lines rearranged themselves into a woman carrying a lantern. [414] The yew arch parted on a path of blue grass. [420] Mab stood there, silver haired and ordinary except for eyes full of patient starlight. [434]

"You took your time," she said, and Alder laughed until crying stopped embarrassing him. [448] He had imagined forgiveness as a verdict; Mab offered it as a hand. [461]

Over pears and bitter tea, she explained that the changelings had taken her to heal a sickness in their rootbound world. [482] She had stayed to build gardens where human tools and old magic could grow without devouring each other. [500] The substitute had died naturally, and their mother, fearing the village, buried it and silence together. [516]

"I could have come back sooner," Mab said. [524] "But I kept mistaking readiness for permission." [531]

Alder knew that mistake; he had made a life of it. [542] He told her about their mother's last years without defending his own coldness. [555] Mab did not acquit him, nor did she accuse him. [565] She simply showed him the next constellation in her notebook: two crooked figures joining hands over a gate. [583]

By dusk, Alder had agreed to stay, not because every mystery was solved, but because uncertainty had finally made room for company. [605] Tamsin hung the baby tooth's tiny impression above the workshop door, beside his splints and Mab's first failed engine. [624] Together they designed splints for rootbound limbs and lamps that remembered sunset, nostalgic inventions for a future neither world had imagined. [645]

At night, when the maze rearranged its old errors into new paths, Alder no longer demanded a single true map. [665] He walked between his sister and his young guide, setting what he could, forgiving what he could not, and letting the rest shine. [688]</story>
