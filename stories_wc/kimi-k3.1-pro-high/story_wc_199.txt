<story>In the ashen skyborne arbor, where roots cupped the clouds and branches combed cinders from the wind, Nell sorted seeds with a tweezers, a ruler, and uncompromising cheer. [28] Each dawn she lined the husks by size, color, and likely manners, for a disorderly seed, she said, was only a tree arguing with itself. [53] Her true joy, however, was building tiny monuments to forgotten things: a thimble arch for a lost tailor, a bark door for a nameless traveler, a pebble hearth for every song no one finished. [87] The regional folklore of the hanging valleys claimed such small memorials gave memory somewhere to sit. [103] Nell liked any belief with practical furniture. [110] Then came the hush where past meets prophecy, the blue interval when the arbor stopped creaking and every falling leaf hung bright as a held breath. [136] The old verses promised that whoever entered the hush seeking a future emerged transformed always, though nobody agreed whether the transformation would be useful. [160] Nell had no use for prophecy, but she had long wanted to decode the message in falling leaves, which her grandmother had insisted were letters from the arbor's dead. [189] Now the leaves began to drop, not onto her sorting trays, but around her monuments, each vein pointing toward a different little roof. [212] She prodded the first with her sorting needle. [220] It flipped, revealing green writing beneath the ash: REMEMBER WHAT YOU SORT OUT. [233] Nell's cheerful mouth pursed. [237] Among her rejected seeds lay crooked beans, scorched acorns, and one pale grain she had labeled mute because it never rattled. [258] The next leaf pointed to the traveler's bark door. [267] Under it Nell found her grandmother's moonstone cabochon, worn by generations of thumb rubs until only a dust speck remained on the brass backing. [291] She had kept it not for its glow, which was gone, but because forgetting it seemed rude. [308] When she prodded the speck, it shone through the mute grain, and inside appeared a root, furled like a question. [328] Delight tickled Nell like moss in a boot. [336] She read the leaves again, moving from monument to monument, and understood: the dead were not asking to be planted; they were asking her to stop discarding what did not resemble a proper beginning. [370] So Nell emptied her reject drawer. [376] Around each unwanted seed she built a monument to the thing it might become, using crooked twigs for crooked beans and warm ash for the scorched acorns. [403] For the mute grain she made a moon of her grandmother's brass, with the bright dust at its center. [422] The arbor shuddered. [425] Leaves poured down in soft green applause, covering her tidy trays until the ruler vanished and the tweezers looked delightfully silly. [446] Then the rejected seeds opened, not into trees, but into small doors leading down through the cloud roots. [464] From them came the lost tailor's needle song, the traveler's unfinished good-bye, and a thousand voices humming the ends of interrupted tunes. [486] Nell stepped among the doors and felt the hush enter her, patient as loam. [500] When she returned to her bench, the prophecy was fulfilled without horns or halos: she emerged transformed always, which meant she could never again mistake neatness for goodness. [528] Her hands still loved order, but now they ordered invitations. [538] She labeled the first new row UNEXPECTED, and left space between every seed for a name she did not know yet. [559] By spring, the monuments had softened under velvet moss, and forgotten things kept arriving to admire them: lost buttons, shy beetles, even the wind, which had misplaced a mountain. [588] Nell welcomed them all, sorted none, and saved the last empty pedestal for whatever she had forgotten about herself. [607] There, at the next hush, she intended to place a mirror, though a very small one, so the discovery would not seem intimidating. [630]</story>
