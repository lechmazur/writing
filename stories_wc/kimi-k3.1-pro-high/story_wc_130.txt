<story>After the orchard grew silent beneath the moon, Vero came alone to the cavern, carrying a lantern hooded against the dripping stalactites. [22] He was a conjurer by trade and secretive by necessity, though his tricks had never fooled grief. [39] Above him, silver light descended through a fissure and touched pear trees whose roots braided through the dark. [57] Their fruit had stopped swelling on the night his sister, Mira, vanished between one footstep and the next. [75] No door had opened, no cry had risen, and no body had returned to make loss simple. [92] Vero had come to understand the mathematics of emotion, believing absence must obey some law if love did. [110] In his pocket lay a taxidermist's glass eye pair, brown as Mira's eyes, bought from a woman who mounted dead foxes for grieving farmers. [134] He had told no one about them, or about the worn notebook where he measured sorrow by pulse, breath, and hours slept. [156] The orchard's silence had broken his equations. [163] Without the stalactites' patient ticking, grief had no denominator. [172] At the oldest tree, Vero knelt beside a mosaic stepping stone, its blue and ochre chips arranged around a blank center. [193] Mira had made it as a child, pressing broken crockery into lime while he invented gentle uses for useless things. [213] He was still gently inventive, though now his inventions concealed themselves: a hinge that sighed like her, a kettle tuned to her whistle. [236] The glass eyes were his latest device, lenses meant to reveal whatever the living overlooked. [251] He set them into two empty chips in the mosaic, and their curved surfaces caught the moon. [268] Through the left eye, the stone's chipped patterns became numbers; through the right, they became paths. [284] A missing blue square meant subtraction, a split ochre fan meant division, and three white crescents marked the remainder. [303] Vero laughed once, softly, because Mira had always been better at mathematics and worse at straight lines. [320] He read the directions via the chipped patterns on the mosaic stepping stone, stepping only where damage interrupted the glaze. [340] Each step took him deeper among the pear trunks, until he reached a living branch and a stone branch growing almost together. [362] Their ends were cut at matching angles, as if waiting for a carpenter. [375] Vero understood then why the orchard had nourished his hope while starving its fruit. [389] The trees had been trying to dovetail with the cavern, joining what grew to what merely endured. [406] He pressed branch to branch, fitting the angled wood into the stone's patient notch. [420] The joint held. [423] One drop fell from a stalactite, then another, and the whole orchard resumed its soft, uneven clock. [440] In that music Vero heard not Mira's voice, but the space where her voice might answer. [456] The glass eyes clouded, showing him a final figure: himself, old and bent, still tending trees whose fruit he could not explain. [478] No Mira stood beside him, yet neither did the figure stand alone. [490] Around his feet lay hundreds of white petals, each shaped like a door left ajar. [505] Vero removed the eyes, and the vision vanished. [513] He could have called it a message, a haunting, or the last trick of a tired mind. [530] Instead, he opened his notebook and wrote that grief was not a quantity but a shape, changing whenever love found somewhere new to fit. [554] He left one eye in the mosaic for Mira, should she return by the same impossible road, and kept the other for whoever next needed looking for. [581] Before dawn, a single pear ripened above the joined branches. [591] Vero did not pick it. [596] He simply sat beneath it until the moon withdrew, listening to water count what he could not, and finding, in every unanswered drop, a small place for morning. [624]</story>
