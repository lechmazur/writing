<story>Three nights after seeing my own grave, I woke in the mountain ranger cabin with snow on my tongue and the birch bark scroll open beneath my hands. [28] As a lucid dream chronicler, I had survived avalanches, false awakenings, and one wedding to a glacier, but the grave had been offensively specific: my name, my crooked teeth carved into the stone, and yesterday's date. [64] Outside, the radio hissed without words, and the shutter banged though I remembered nailing it shut next winter. [82] Being chronologically confused was not poetic; it meant checking whether the coffee was brewed before deciding if I had drunk it. [103] I kept the scroll because birch took ink even in dreams, and because its pale layers could be peeled, reversed, and read like a mountain that had forgotten which way was up. [135] My task, assigned by no one after the service stopped answering, was to distill chaos into narrative before the dreams learned to use my name. [160] That night I slept beside the stove and returned to the grave, where a younger version of me sat eating blackberries, stained and smug with all the summers I had wasted by surviving them. [194] She offered me one, and I hated her for believing the future was a room she could simply enter. [213] Then came the others: the ranger before her divorce, the child before the fire, the woman who had not yet mistaken endurance for a virtue. [238] They circled me with the delicate menace of wolves, and I understood that feral nostalgia was not longing but appetite, a wild thing fed on abandoned possibilities. [265] It had dug the grave not to kill me, but to give every past self a place to wait. [284] I could have run, but running in dreams only provides the landscape with exercise. [298] Instead, I began getting jealous of past selves on purpose, inspecting each bright regret until envy told me what I still wanted. [320] The child made me jealous of her rage, so I wanted honesty; the divorced ranger made me jealous of her empty bed, so I wanted room; the youngest made me jealous of blackberries, so I wanted to be here, bleeding slightly, alive. [362] One by one, I wrote those wants on the scroll, not in the order they happened, but in the order they released me. [385] The past selves snarled as their anecdotes lost authority, and the gravestone softened into a gray door. [402] Beyond it stood the cabin roof, impossibly far below, with every version of my life nailed to its shingles like bad repairs. [424] I stepped through, spread my arms, and let the cold take me; for once, falling remembered its other name, and I began to soar. [448] From above, the mountain was gorgeous and badly plotted, all avalanche chutes and sentimental meadows, and the grave was only a dark comma where I had paused too long. [477] I flew until the scroll stretched beneath me like a second horizon, its sentences knitting into a trail that led, with almost insulting simplicity, to morning. [503] I woke to a dead radio, cold coffee, and sunlight making the cabin look less haunted than poorly cleaned. [522] On the table, the scroll held one new line in my handwriting: nostalgia may keep my footprints, but it does not get my boots. [546] I dressed, radioed the district from the emergency set, and learned that yesterday had behaved itself while I was gone; no one reported me dead, which seemed bureaucratically encouraging. [575] Before leaving, I carried the scroll to the ridge and buried it beneath a stone cairn, not as an offering, but as a map for whichever self came sniffing back. [605] Then I went down to mend the shutter, disappointed to discover that wonder had survived the night, and faintly, jadedly grateful that it had. [629]</story>
