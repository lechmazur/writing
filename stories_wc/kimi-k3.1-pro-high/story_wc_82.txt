On the floating island tea garden, where jasmine brewed above an ocean of clouds, crafty lantern maker Veya sold light that could remember. [23]
Her patrons slept beside porcelain cups while she polished gems against her lanterns, each facet recording a dream until its colors could be poured out again. [49]
Beyond the trellises stood a glass basilica on the floating isle, transparent as held breath, its spire bright with other people's mornings. [71]
Veya alone refused to enter, for her brother Orin had fallen from the isle a year ago, leaving only a cloudy opal containing his final, unplayed dream. [98]
She had sworn to fold her grief into origami cranes, one for every memory, but each finished bird only made the opal heavier in her pocket. [124]
Then, one blue dusk, after her shadow detached from her shoes, it stole the opal and ran across the grass without bending a blade. [148]
It entered the basilica, and the whole building began its nightly miracle, descending while ascending, nave sinking into cloud as the spire rose toward the stars. [174]
Veya understood with an artisan's dislike of elegant traps: her grief meant to preserve Orin by sealing every recorded dream inside an endless night. [198]
She pocketed polishing cloth, diamond dust, and a paper crane, then followed, finding the basilica's stair descending while ascending beneath her, each upward step opening deeper into its luminous crypt. [228]
Thousands of gems burned in the walls, and their recorded dreams flickered over her: weddings, forgiven enemies, children laughing beside beds that had long been empty. [254]
The beauty hurt like staring at the sun, yet Veya climbed down and down, until her shadow awaited her beneath a bell of clear gold. [279]
It had grown Orin's face, and when it set the opal in the bell, the dead boy appeared in white fire, asking her to stay. [304]
Around them the island's ascent faltered, tea tables sliding as roots tore from the cloudstone. [319]
Veya nearly surrendered, but the apparition wore Orin's face without his impatience, and she knew this was not her brother, only grief pretending that love required a cage. [347]
Craftiness, not courage, saved her. [352]
She pretended to embrace the shadow and ground her diamond dust into its outstretched hand, making it flinch and drop the opal. [374]
Instead of snatching it back, she set it against the bell and began gem polishing with frantic, tender strokes. [393]
Cloudiness vanished; facets opened like small windows, and Orin's true dream entered the lantern she unfolded from her paper crane. [413]
He was not asking to return. [419]
He dreamed of the morning before his fall, when they had raced through the tea garden with a paper dragon, laughing because the wind kept stealing its wings. [447]
In the dream he turned and tossed her the creature, not in farewell, but because it was her turn to make it fly. [470]
Veya wept, and her tears struck the polished opal, splitting its beam into a hundred narrow roads of light. [489]
Along them flew every crane she had folded, their paper wings kindling without burning, until grief itself began to transform. [509]
The shadow pleated inward, edge over edge, becoming a black crane whose breast held the opal like a captive dawn. [529]
Veya lifted it into the lantern, and the bird's darkness did not vanish; it gave the radiance shape. [547]
With a sound like a thousand cups touched gently together, the basilica released its dreams. [562]
Gems dimmed, sleepers woke smiling or sobbing in the garden, and the isle surged upward as its glass roots rose home from the clouds. [586]
Orin's image thinned, but before leaving he made the paper dragon flap once, badly, exactly as he always had. [605]
Veya laughed through the luminous torment of losing him anew, and this time she did not mistake the pain for a chain. [627]
At dawn she returned to her stall, hung the crane lantern above the kettle, and offered patrons a new service: dreams recorded whole, with sorrow left in. [654]
Whenever someone asked why the black paper bird seemed to descend while ascending on its rising thermal, Veya served the tea and said that some things must carry weight to climb. [685]
Then she folded another crane, smaller than the last, and set it free into the fierce, shining air. [703]
