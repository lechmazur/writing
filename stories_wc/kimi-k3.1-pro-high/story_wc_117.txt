At Sage Ridge, where silver sage brushed the hospital garden and one old cottonwood held the hill together, Mara tuned her smallest drum. [23]
She was a music therapist, mellow enough to let a silence finish, but today silence felt like a door she might close too soon. [47]
In forty minutes, the solar eclipse would dim the ridge, and her mother, Iona, would arrive with Mara's daughter, Wren, who was twelve. [70]
Since forgetting names had begun stealing Iona's confidence, she had refused Mara's sessions, calling them kindergarten with better chairs. [89]
Wren had refused too, insisting that eclipses were geometry and drums were embarrassing. [102]
Mara wanted to nurture lifelong wonder in both of them, though wanting it so badly made her careful hands eager. [122]
She unpacked shakers, then removed them, leaving three hand drums, a chime, and the walnut box from her coat. [141]
Inside lay her keepsake baby hair curl, pale as dry grass, tied with thread Iona had sewn fifty years ago. [161]
Before the forgetting, Iona had called it proof that Mara arrived already singing. [174]
That was nonsense, Mara knew, but useful nonsense: a small invitation to listen. [187]
When they came, Iona studied the garden while Wren studied the path back to the car. [203]
No performance, Mara promised herself, and set the box between them like a stone. [217]
She tapped a slow pulse, stopped, and let the cottonwood answer with leaves. [230]
Wren gave one grudging tap; Iona rubbed her thumb along the chair arm, almost in time. [246]
Mara became softly adaptive, thinning her rhythm until their tiny sounds could change it. [260]

Wren looked startled, then pleased. [293]
Beneath the hush of dreaming roots, or so it seemed when the wind died, they waited with paper glasses ready. [313]

It was the lullaby Mara had thought lost, changed at every third bar, but stubbornly alive. [420]
