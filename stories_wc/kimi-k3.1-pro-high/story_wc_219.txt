<story>Forty days after making first contact, Mara Venn returned to the rainwashed tower in spring, wearing her new light like an accusation. [22] The Contact had silvered her skin, and the university had silvered its language, calling its capture protocol an artificial pilgrimage. [42] She, a luminous skeptic with an academically rebellious record, had agreed to walk its scented stations only to understand the chemistry of homecoming. [65] Why had the visitor's first message, a fragrance beneath the thunder, made hardened technicians weep for kitchens, cousins, and unvisited graves? [86] Director Saye met her beneath the tower's bronze bell, holding out a white mask. [100] "Wear it, and the Guest will follow your radiance into the observatory." [112] Mara refused; the mask smelled of camphor, the same sharp denial used in the containment tanks. [128] Instead she touched the fly amber at her throat, her mother's last unfinished sample, a golden tomb with one black wing folded inside. [151] Her mother had studied fossil resins here before the tower became sacred, before wonder acquired an office and keys. [170] Rain had washed the courtyard clean, but beyond the bell, the official path steamed with synthetic myrrh. [187] Mara turned the other way. [192] On the overgrown sculpture path, dusty footprints crossed the rain-dark moss, impossible unless someone had walked there before the storm carrying dry tower dust. [216] She followed them via their broken rhythm: a long step, a drag, three prints circling each headless saint. [234] They were her mother's prints; polio had given the left foot that tender hesitation. [248] Elin Venn had vanished three years earlier, condemned for stealing university samples and insisting that contact would begin with smell, not mathematics. [270] Now Mara found her beyond the final saint, kneeling in a bronze pavilion where wisteria shook rain from its buds. [290] There was no bronze quiet in Mara's cry. [298] Her mother answered by opening a furnace door. [306] Inside, no prisoner waited, only a darkness that shone back. [316] The Guest had been here since the first rain, not summoned but returning. [329] Elin had made first contact by accident while heating the fly amber, releasing compounds that the visitor recognized from its own ancient dispersal through Earth's carbon-rich clouds. [356] Its people had once seeded no world and conquered none; they had simply carried the chemistry of a dead home between stars. [378] The amber's resin, spring soil, and human grief shared enough molecular echoes to make a door. [394] "I hid because Saye wanted a specimen, not a guest," Elin said. [406] "I needed you to doubt the pilgrimage enough to leave it." [417] Mara almost hated her then, but anger was another road home. [428] Bells struck above them. [432] Saye's technicians were coming down the official path, their white masks blooming with camphor. [446] Mara saw the whole design: her light would draw the Guest upward, the scented gates would close, and homecoming would become a thesis owned by frightened men. [473] She unclasped the amber and set it in the pavilion's basin, then crushed it with a bronze pestle. [491] The fly vanished into glittering dust. [497] Mara added a handful of wet earth and cut her luminous palm, offering salt, resin, iron, and the electric chemistry of choice. [519] "Not a lure," she told the darkness. [526] "A farewell, if you want one." [532] The darkness opened like a pupil. [538] Around them, footprints, rain, wisteria pollen, and the approaching technicians seemed to converge without touching, each current altering the next. [558] Mara understood that homecoming was not one molecule but a pattern of recognition, the body's brief chemistry of enough safety to remember and enough freedom to leave. [585] The Guest rose, not into Saye's observatory but through the open roof, and its departure smelled of rain on every road Mara had ever called exile. [611] Her light went with it. [616] In the bronze quiet that remained, mother and daughter stood apart, neither forgiven nor captured, while spring continued its patient work of making the path green. [642]</story>
