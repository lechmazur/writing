<story>After luck became measurable, the city printed it on identification cards, and gardeners with poor numbers were advised to plant stones. [21] Mara Venn, whose card read eleven, ignored the advice because her maze had survived three owners, two frosts, and one fashionable plague. [43] She was its withered vine dresser, bent like a hooked root, with hands stained purple from years of coaxing grapes through the hedges. [66] Its new owner, Lord Orell, possessed a luck rating of nine hundred and intended to replace her with a crew whose numbers glittered. [89] At the autumn festival, he announced, the maze's sleeping heartvine must flower beneath his hand, or Mara and her unlucky knees would go. [112] He spoke beneath arbors where late grapes swelled even as their leaves browned, and the whole garden seemed to bloom and wither around his wager. [137] Mara could have won it easily. [143] For twenty years she had deceived Orell's father, opening hidden sluices and warming buried pipes whenever he demanded a miraculous flowering. [164] The old man paid generously for wonder, and her lie had kept the maze alive. [179] But repeating it now would only prove that gardens knew their deceivers, while Mara wanted to prove that gardens knew their gardeners. [201] Wisely pragmatic, she bowed to Orell and requested thirty days to prepare his luck for the heartvine's fussy temperament. [220] He agreed because preparation sounded expensive and therefore credible. [229] Each dawn she gave him some absurd ceremony: bathing his hands in rain, humming to the west hedge, or carrying compost without spilling a prophecy. [254] The ceremonies were pruning, feeding, and listening in ceremonial clothes. [264] Orell complained, blistered, and slowly stopped asking whether his card had changed. [276] Mara commemorated each small victory elaborately, as she always had, with a blue ribbon, a ridiculous speech, and a marble from the box beneath her bed. [302] Her collection of marbles was cracked from decades of frost, one for every cutting that had rooted, every nest spared, every worker paid on time. [327] When Orell saved a wind torn graft, she chose the clearest marble and told him to polish it until he could see his hands. [351] He rubbed longer than necessary. [356] By the twenty ninth evening, buds silvered the heartvine, and Orell had learned the maze well enough to notice which turns fell silent when Mara approached. [382] Actually, the silence followed him too, though he thought it was luck settling its debts. [397] Mara then led him to the sluice room and confessed everything, including the false ceremonies. [412] A deception could be mended, she said, only if its owner stopped admiring the crack. [427] Orell stared at the polished marble, then at the corroded wheel that had manufactured his father's miracles. [444] He dismissed her before sunset and ordered the festival gates opened. [455] Mara left by the service arch, carrying her marbles in a seed sack. [468] Yet at the entrance she turned back, not to work the sluices, but because the heartvine had never needed audience chamber manners. [490] Guests crowded the central circle as Orell laid his fortunate hand on the vine. [504] One bud opened, small as a confession. [511] The crowd cheered, but Orell did not, because beyond the hedges every leaf was turning toward the service path. [530] Mara stood there in her brown apron, neither hidden nor invited, and the heartvine flowed downward in a white tide, flower after flower after flower. [555] No sluice ran; no buried pipe knocked. [562] The maze simply recognized the footsteps that had carried water through it before Orell could pronounce its name. [580] Orell looked at his single blossom and understood that his month of tending had not purchased nothing. [597] He fetched Mara a chair, which was the first sensible miracle of his life. [611] Then he asked her to teach the lucky crew where the sluices were, and where the roots were, and why those maps differed. [634] At winter's end, they set her cracked marbles into the maze's paths, so visitors would stumble slightly and look down. [654] Each stone marked a modest rescue, not a fortunate birth. [664] Orell's own marble went beside the heartvine, still bright from polishing, with no inscription but the date his hands first blistered. [685] Mara kept her low luck card for propping open the greenhouse door. [697] Above her, new shoots greened the old wood, while dead flowers dropped softly onto the path, both endings feeding the dark, remembering soil. [720]</story>
