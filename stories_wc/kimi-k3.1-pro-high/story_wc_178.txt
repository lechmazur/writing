<story>Between regimes, when the old king's seal had been broken and the conqueror's had not yet arrived, the reimagined palace of Babylon kept its own dusk. [26]
Its ancient courts were an observatory of blue brick, its hanging gardens turned water through bronze orreries, and its throne room roof opened to the measured sky. [53]
There worked Nisaba, a frugal astronomer who split reeds for pens, saved lamp soot for ink, and wore one winter cloak until its stars showed through. [79]
Her colleagues had fled with the treasury, but she remained because the instruments belonged to no king, and because something above Babylon did not appear in any catalog. [107]
Each twilight it answered her measurements with a shadow where parallax promised light, a discrepancy too patient for error and too exact for fear. [131]
Nisaba trusted error more than omens, so she repeated the observations on clay, copper, and the palace floor, spending curiosity where others would have spent prayer. [157]
Her only treasure was a magic square tile, nine worn cells whose numbers summed to fifteen along every line, found by her mother beneath a demolished altar. [184]
She had kept it not for sorcery but because its symmetry made an excellent paperweight. [199]
On the seventh night without a ruler, soldiers beyond the gates began arguing over whose god should own the city. [219]
Nisaba heard them while aligning three schematics: the palace as seen from above, the orbit of the absent star, and the tile's numbered square. [243]
Through schematic representations rather than incantation, she discovered that corridors mirrored orbital arcs, while the square supplied missing angles like a key supplying teeth. [267]
The design was older than the palace, yet the palace had been rebuilt around it, generation after generation, by hands that mistook obedience for architecture. [293]
At its center lay not a throne but an invitation, addressed to anyone willing to read without commanding. [311]
Nisaba laughed once, softly, at the empirically ironic result: every test meant to discredit a haunting had instead proved that the haunting practiced better astronomy. [336]
Still, proof was not consent, and she would not trade Babylon's uncertainty for another crowned voice. [352]
She decided to seal a covenant with the unknown on terms a mortal could audit: no worship, no sacrifices, no kings made sacred by darkness. [377]
In return she offered her encrypted devotion, a lifetime of accurate maps whose errors would be confessed and corrected, hidden in drawings no pious plunderer would value. [404]
She ground her last lump of costly blue pigment, mixed it with soot, and drew the covenant across the floor in nineteen frugal lines. [428]
Then she set the tile where the throne had stood and rotated it until fifteen became the sum of doorway, shadow, and star. [451]
The palace did not tremble; instead, every lamp flame leaned outward, making room. [464]
With the hem of her ruined cloak, Nisaba wiped away the central line, choosing to uncloak the visitor rather than command its appearance. [487]
The missing star unfolded above the roof, not bright but deep, a darkness crowded with distances that moved together like reeds bending under one wind. [512]
From it came no voice, only a second shadow beside hers, matching each gesture as she redrew one flawed arc. [532]
When she corrected the arc, the shadow corrected a turn of the gardens, sending water into channels dry since the siege. [553]
Below, the arguing soldiers heard wheels and waterfalls, and by the time they entered they found no miracle to seize, only an old woman repairing a diagram. [580]
They demanded an omen for their factions, but Nisaba showed them the cisterns filling and asked which faction preferred thirst. [600]
Their silence ratified the first article of her covenant better than any seal. [613]
Thereafter, conquerors came and went, each receiving the same star charts and the same refusal to mistake precision for permission. [633]
Nisaba grew older, mended her cloak with schematic patches, and never spent a night expecting gratitude. [649]
At dusk she placed the tile on her latest drawing, where its numbers kept the paper flat and its hidden arithmetic kept the door open. [674]
