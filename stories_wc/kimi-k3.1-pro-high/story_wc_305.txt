<story>After Mara forgave her sister for the fire that killed their mother, she climbed into the umbral grotto above the clouds and waited for contentment to find her. [28] Instead, she found only the vineyard hush: dew ticking from leaves, roots shifting in dark stone, and far below, the slow surf of the cloud sea. [54] For eleven years she had been the vineyard's committed caretaker, tending vines whose fruit ripened only after their parent vine died. [75] Pilgrims called this rebirth; Mara called it work, and work had kept her hands from becoming fists. [92] Now the oldest vine, Mother Root, stood black and leafless, though one green grape shone among its thorns. [110] The keepers' book instructed her to cut the vine at moonrise, burn it, and plant the grape's single seed in its ashes. [132] Without that death, no root in the grotto would flower again. [143] Mara had read the words each winter, yet now her hand refused the axe. [157] Her mother had planted Mother Root, and some childish part of her still believed love could be preserved by refusing every necessary ending. [180] Seeking a clearer thought, she did what she had done since childhood: she climbed the cedar above the wine press. [200] Climbing trees to think better was not wisdom, her mother used to say, but it often made wisdom easier to hear. [221] From the highest fork, Mara saw smoke threading through the clouds. [232] Her kindly decisive nature, so reliable with injured vines and frightened novices, deserted her. [246] Then she saw that the smoke rose from the lower nursery, where a lantern had fallen against the winter straw. [266] She dropped, branch by branch, and ran. [273] At the nursery she found Liora, her sister, beating flames with a wet cloak, her silver hair loose around her burned hands. [295] Beside her lay the lantern she had dropped while smuggling food to a sick apprentice. [310] The old accusation rose in Mara, bright as the former fire, but she saw Liora's terror and chose differently. [329] "Bring water to the beds, not the flames," she said, and cut the burning straw away with one clean stroke. [349] Together they saved the seedlings. [354] When Liora tried to speak of guilt, Mara bound her palms and said, "Stay useful tonight; we can be sorrowful tomorrow." [375] It was absolution without ceremony, and it steadied them both. [385] At moonrise they returned to Mother Root. [393] Mara drew their mother's antique hairpin from her knot, a slender bronze vine with a pearl no larger than a tear. [414] Its two prongs had opened seed husks for three generations, never cutting, only loosening what was ready. [431] Mara pierced the green grape and scalloped its skin into a tiny bowl, exposing the seed without wounding it. [450] She expected a revelation, some final voice explaining where contentment lived. [461] There was only Liora beside her, frightened and willing, and the vine waiting for her mercy. [477] Mara understood then that she had been seeking a source as though contentment were a spring, hidden and pure. [496] Perhaps it was nearer to rain: received, shared, and given back through roots. [509] She kissed the dark wood and swung the axe. [518] The fall sounded like a long breath leaving the grotto. [528] They burned the branches, sifted the warm ash, and set the seed within it. [542] Before covering it, Mara hesitated, then fastened Liora's burned hair with the antique hairpin. [556] "Not a reward," she said when Liora began to weep. [566] "A task: help me tend what comes next." [574] By dawn, the other vines had put out small white flowers, something no living keeper had witnessed. [591] Their petals opened without sound, and the cloud sea brightened beneath them. [603] Mara felt no triumph, only a quiet appetite for breakfast, rain on her sore shoulders, and Liora asking which bed needed water first. [626] She answered, and found that choosing the next small care did not bring contentment to her; it made a place where contentment could return. [650]</story>
