<story>The moment Oren's spirit left the magnetic arbor, the ironwood trees stopped singing and the whole garden began to fall through the clouds. [23]

Below, white vapors closed over the lowest roots; above, the copper crown tilted toward a sky suddenly stripped of miracles. [64]
For twelve years, Sera had kept Oren there by reading a funerary tablet backward, calling her haunted ambition devotion because love was easier to confess. [89]
He had finally asked to cross over, and she, kindly methodical even in grief, had loosened one silver wire at a time. [111]
Now his absence was tearing the arbor from its invisible moorings. [122]
Sera could have reversed the wires and summoned him, but his last words had been, 'Do not make a prison of my going.' [145]
Instead, she opened his parting gift: the knowledge fruit they had shared the previous night, its rind bitter, its single seed warm as a pulse. [170]
Hairline wedges covered the shell, too regular for chance. [179]
Sera set it on the translation desk, pinned her sleeve against the wind, and began to read. [196]
The seed's message was not a spell but a problem: 'A field survives its source when departure teaches matter how to remember.' [218]
Around her, pots slid, and a young gardener clung to the mast, waiting for Sera to panic usefully. [236]
She almost laughed at that, then asked him to bring clay, a stylus, and the filings from Oren's empty room. [256]

She pressed the stylus into wet clay and lifted it away. [292]

She could almost feel his shoulder beneath her palm, near enough to touch, already beyond touching. [464]

'Remember the direction, not the hand,' she whispered, translating the sign into instruction. [492]
