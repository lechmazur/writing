<story>Mara returned from the coastal diaspora with two suitcases, a dead mother's letters, and a faith worn smooth by years of leaving. [22] She had crossed three oceans intending never to see Bellor again, yet each interrupted journey had ended with her dreaming of its buried bells. [46] Now the village council had hired her, an archaeologist trained abroad, to excavate the tomb beneath their ruined chapel before the spring road swallowed it. [71] On the first morning, she descended cautiously and found an orchard in the catacomb, pale trees fruiting without sun while their roots snaked between bones. [96] Warmly curious rather than afraid, Mara greeted the skulls by the names on their broken markers and asked what harvest the dead expected. [119] Only old Tomas answered, explaining that pilgrims once carried seeds here, planted them above loved ones, and trusted roots to complete journeys their bodies could not. [145] That trust had died when invaders sealed the tomb, leaving processions stranded on roads and prayers circling the locked hills like birds. [167] Mara's excavation therefore became more than a rescue of masonry; she meant to restore a path for faith, though she no longer knew whether faith included her. [194] For days she mapped vertebrae, lifted roots veiling ribs, and followed promising passages that ended in collapse, letting each detour teach her the orchard's design. [219] Her purposeful wandering revealed channels beneath the soil, all bending toward a central chamber where a sundial base lay split beside an empty coffin. [243] Its bronze dial and gnomon were gone, but carved rays pointed not to hours, rather to names of villages erased from every modern map. [267] Mara recognized Bellor and, farther out, the port where her grandmother had boarded a ship during famine, promising the child left behind that she would return. [293] The promise had become a family curse of departures, visas, missed funerals, and lullabies sung to screens across incompatible dawns. [313] Tomas said the tomb belonged to Saint Orra, patroness of interrupted journeys, whose worshippers believed no arrival was holy unless it made return possible for someone else. [340] Her empty coffin suggested either theft or resurrection, and Mara, trained to distrust wonders, found herself hoping inconveniently for both. [360] When the dig's funding vanished, the council ordered her to seal the chamber, but she rebuilt a fallen arch instead, working unpaid while villagers brought soup and arguments. [388] Rebuilding drew others: a mason who had buried his son abroad, sisters mending survey cord, children brushing dust from roots without snapping them. [411] Mara set the sundial base upright but refused to cement its fracture until she understood why the channels carried rainwater away from the trees. [435] She wandered again, this time above ground, knocking at farms whose owners remembered processions only as superstition or inconvenience. [454] At each door she asked warmly what people had stopped believing, then listened long enough for grief to disclose its practical shape. [476] The pattern led her to a quarry, where Orra's bronze dial had been reused as a cover for a communal well. [497] Its gnomon served nearby as a gate hinge, holding shut a field no one had cultivated since the last procession. [517] Returning both pieces required bargaining, apology, and the promise that the chapel well would remain common property, terms Mara preferred to miracles. [539] On the excavation's final evening, she fitted dial to base, leaving the old crack visible, and laid her mother's letters in the coffin as Orra's only relics. [566] Dawn entered through the rebuilt arch; the gnomon's shadow crossed Bellor, then traveled slowly toward the port as sap brightened in the nearest root. [590] No saint rose, no buried bell rang, and Mara was grateful, because enchantment asked less of her than the work remaining. [611] She opened the tomb gates to the hesitant villagers and invited them not to worship return, but to practice it. [631] They carried out bones for named burial, carried in tools and seedlings, and cleared the road one bend at a time. [652] Mara joined them without promising to stay forever; faith, she now suspected, was a road rebuilt by people who allowed one another to wander. [676] When spring traffic finally passed, its drivers saw an orchard flowering underground and a woman below waving them onward, as if every interrupted journey might still arrive somewhere kind.
