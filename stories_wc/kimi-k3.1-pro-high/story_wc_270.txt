She had led that raid against the Seed Wardens, whose surgeons implanted destiny in every embryo before birth, assigning rulers, diggers, soldiers, and mourners with serene efficiency. [111]
The victory had freed the village but killed her daughter, and Sera had spent the peace wondering whether destroying one cage merely taught people to build another. [138]

Sera wanted the child born without orders, but the seal had resisted knives, steam, and every insurgent code she remembered. [213]
The kite had snagged overnight in a tea shrub, its tail brushed with cryptic script that the shrub insisted tasted like Sera's daughter. [236]
Plants did not speak in words; they offered pressures of thirst, sunlight, and recognition, so Sera pressed her palm to the leaves and listened until grief became direction. [264]
The shrub had grown from a cutting above her daughter's grave, and its roots remembered the narrow ribbons she had buried with her. [287]
Sera laid the kite tail across those ribbons, matching tears to stains, and discovered the script was not language but instructions for folding. [310]

Below it waited the release sequence, disguised as a grandmother's lullaby, with rests where a singer must listen rather than command. [355]

He read his dead mother's name in the margins, lowered the pistol, and, at the final rest, listened. [466]
What he heard was not prophecy but the springs themselves: water worrying stone, roots answering water, and the embryo's minute heartbeat quickening inside amber. [490]

Tradition would survive, she decided, only if each generation pruned it deliberately, kept what sheltered life, and composted what merely demanded obedience. [671]
