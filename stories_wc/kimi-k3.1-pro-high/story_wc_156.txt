<story>Mara Vell arrived at the Sound Wave Garden wearing sunset overalls, three brushes in her braid, and a restorer's contempt for guesses. [22] She was famous for rescuing drowned frescoes and infamous for testing their cracks with tunes tapped from a hobo nickel. [42] The coin's worn buffalo had been re-carved into a sleeping eye, its reverse into an ear surrounding a question mark. [62]

The garden's commission seemed simple: restore its mural of a closed gate before the donors arrived at dusk. [80] Around her, silver reeds bent in sine curves, and convex walls returned footsteps as soft, intelligible notes. [97] Once, those reflections had made flowers open; now the beds held only green fists. [111] Mara intended to correct that with records, measurements, and the original painter's approved palette. [125] Certainty, she believed, was simply kindness that had done its homework. [136]

She cleaned the mural methodically, numbering each flake, then playfully hiding tiny painted beasts wherever the plaster allowed. [154] Beneath the gate's latch she uncovered not a keyhole but a mouth. [166] Its lips formed, "Do you know what sleeps behind you?" [176] The garden repeated the question from shell to shell, each reflection higher, until the reeds leaned toward Mara. [194] She opened her notebook to reply that nothing slept there except degraded pigment. [207]

In the pause between question and reply, the hobo nickel grew warm against her thigh. [222] The garden held that pause open, patient as wet plaster. [232] Its carved eye opened. [236] Mara did not scream; she set the coin on her palm and tapped three experimental rhythms. [252] The first returned as a brassy buzz, the second as rain, and the third as her own voice asking, "Behind whom?" [273] Only then did she notice that every painted figure faced her, not the gate. [287]

Her methodically playful habits supplied what doctrine could not: a test, a variation, and room for surprise. [304] She placed the nickel in the mural's empty keyhole, where it fit like a pupil. [319] Then she hummed into a curved shell and sent the note ricocheting through seven arches. [334] She meant to bewitch no one; she wanted only to wake the painted mouth enough to explain itself. [352] But reflected sound returned before her direct voice faded, braiding with it until she could not tell questioner from listener. [372] That braid of sound bewitched the gate. [379]

Its boards flowed into roots, its latch into a silver stem, and every green fist in the garden opened. [398] Within each flower stood a smaller mural, and within each smaller gate slept the same carved eye. [415] Mara understood with delight and vertigo that the awakenings were nested: coin, wall, garden, and now something behind her ribs. [435]

For years she had restored faces to their first expressions, believing people and paintings deserved one recoverable truth. [453] Yet the returning hum carried her mother's old question, the one Mara had answered by becoming useful: "What if you are wrong?" [475] She had built certainty to avoid the wilderness after that question. [486] Now curiosity entered her not as an answer, but as the wish to hear another variation. [502]

The painted mouth moved again, and the donors' bells sounded beyond the walls. [515] Mara could close the gate, restore the approved design, and present a miracle safely labeled restoration. [531] Instead, she lifted the nickel from its socket. [539] The garden shuddered, not dying but listening. [546] On the blank plaster where the mouth had been, she painted a small open door and, beneath it, a bench for two. [568] To keep it open, she had exchanged certainty for curiosity, and found the trade left her richer, not unmoored. [587] She signed neither her name nor the dead painter's, only a question mark shaped to catch echoes. [604]

When the committee entered, demanding to know whether the work was finished, Mara flipped the coin and caught it on her wrist. [626] "Ask the garden," she said. [631] They did, and in the pause between question and reply, she heard a hundred sleeping things consider waking. [649]</story>
