<story>During the lull of that wounded season, when frost had split the barley and war had taken the mule bells, Orin kept his locksmith's shop closed. [26]
Each morning he descended instead into the marble cavern below the village, carrying a cracked lamp and the broken timer mechanism from its singing gate. [51]
The gate governed the harvest of echoes, when buried veins of stone gave back the year's footsteps, rain, laughter, and music. [72]
Without it, the cavern would open only once, then seal its gathered voices until another year had passed. [90]
Orin was a reflective man, slow to answer and quicker to notice what stood beside a question than what stood within it. [112]
His apprentices had called him peripherally focused, usually while quitting. [122]
Yet that habit had taught him how a key could reveal its wards by the shadow it threw, and now it gave him a plan. [147]
He would not copy the ruined timer; he would reverse-engineer the lock whose hours it had once commanded. [165]
The mechanism lay on his bench, a nest of stopped wheels around a mirror no larger than a thumbnail. [184]
Its central axle pointed nowhere useful, but a hairline scratch on its rim caught light whenever the cavern breathed. [203]
Orin began studying reflection angles at different times, marking where that tiny gleam touched the walls at dawn, noon, dusk, and the black hour before dawn. [229]
The direct reflections showed only his tired face. [237]
At the edges of his sight, however, pale numerals surfaced in the marble, then withdrew as the sun moved. [256]
He understood that the timer had never counted hours; it had counted the cavern's changing posture toward the light. [275]
Across three still days he built a dial from key blanks, setting each tooth by an angle rather than a number. [296]
On the fourth day, the season's first thaw ticked through the cavern like a clock made of water. [314]
Orin fitted the dial to the gate and turned it toward the last numeral he had seen. [331]
Nothing happened. [333]
His old fear returned, that wonder was merely an error the world had not yet corrected. [349]
Then a drop struck his bench, and he noticed, almost beyond vision, the mirror trembling in his palm. [367]
The cavern was not awaiting sunlight but resonance. [375]
He hung the mechanism from a silver key string and struck his tuning fork against the bench. [392]
At each hour he had measured, he turned the dial until the mirror's reflection aligned with a wall mark and let the fading note finish the turn. [419]
On the final note, the gate opened without sound. [428]
The villagers entered carrying empty resonance jars, expecting Orin to release the old year's joy at once. [445]
Instead, he asked them to make new sounds: a boot heel for rain, cupped hands for wings, low voices for those who would not return. [470]
At first they resisted, for grief seemed more honest than music. [481]
Then Mara, whose mule had frozen in the traces, clicked her tongue twice, and the cavern answered with the lost bell's warm iron note. [505]
Laughter followed, ragged and startled, and every jar filled with a blue grain of light. [521]
Orin harvested nothing for himself until the others had gone. [531]
Alone, he spoke the name of his dead master into the gate, not hoping for a reply. [548]
The echo returned altered by distance, less a voice than a hand releasing his shoulder. [563]
He sealed that grain in an empty jar and carried it to his shop. [577]
There, beneath trays of screws and patient locks, he built a small cedar cabinet. [591]
On its door he wrote, Cellar of Reason, and set the jar upon its lowest shelf, leaving the one above it empty. [613]
He had not preserved the past, nor explained why stone should remember kindness better than men. [629]
He had only made a safe place for wonder, for later, when reason grew hungry and the world locked itself from within. [651]
Before climbing upstairs, Orin left the cabinet key in the lock. [662]
Some doors, he thought, should require a decision rather than a search. [674]</story>
