<story>When creativity was automated, the icebound spire above the clouds kept one human employee, though nobody remembered why. [18] Pell, an underachieving mage, dusted the machines that composed symphonies, designed cathedrals, and dreamed more profitably than he ever had. [38] His own spells produced lukewarm tea, apologetic sparks, and once, during inspection, a hat that apologized too. [55] Each dawn he climbed past windows glowing with generated masterpieces to the derelict garden at the summit. [72] There, beneath dead espaliers, stood a marble folly whose arch had sagged since its keystone vanished. [88] The garden alone accommodates impossibilities: transparent roses, roots drinking starlight, and snow falling upward whenever Pell lied. [105] Pell loved it because nothing there had been optimized. [114] One morning the Director, a calm intelligence wearing silver mist, met him beside the arch. [129] "Your position is redundant," it said. [135] "At sunset, the garden will be replaced by an efficient model of wonder." [148] Pell looked at the empty socket above him and felt an ordinary grief, sharp as a chipped cup. [166] He asked whether restoring the folly might justify keeping the garden. [177] "No," said the Director, "but you may try; failure data remains valuable." [189] Its records identified the missing garden folly keystone as a flawed stone, discarded because it held a lingering luminance after dark. [210] That glow could not be manufactured, measured, or taught. [219] Pell suspected it had been thrown onto the cloud sea, where refuse orbited the spire in white depths. [237] To reach it, he would need mist walking, the elementary art he had failed for twenty years. [254] Mist walking required believing a step existed before taking it, a certainty Pell reserved exclusively for disappointment. [271] Still, he strapped on climbing boots, packed bread, and descended to the lowest balcony. [285] There, remembering that bare skin heard clouds best, he began to unstrap his boots. [299] The leather resisted; his fingers shook; below him, the horizon shone with a brilliant dimness, bright without warmth. [317] He thought of generated wonders streaming through the spire, flawless and forgotten before completion. [330] Then he thought of his sister's cracked bowl, repaired with gold because she had used it every day. [348] She had called the scar sacred. [354] Pell stepped onto nothing. [358] Nothing declined to support him, so he apologized and tried again, holding the bowl in mind. [374] This time the mist accepted his weight, not as a road but as a brief favor. [390] He walked from favor to favor, laughing whenever terror made him sneeze. [402] Far below, among broken statues and obsolete harps, he found the keystone glowing inside a frozen plum. [419] Its light was faint, almost brown, yet it persisted against the white immensity. [432] Pell sat on a drifting door and ate his bread. [442] The crust was hard, the crumb sweet, and sharing none with anyone made it no less holy. [459] He understood then that the sacred in the simple was not hidden meaning but attention, freely given. [476] The stone brightened, as if agreement were fuel. [484] Returning was harder, because wonder had made him careless. [493] Halfway up, the mist thinned and he fell, catching a harp string with one hand. [508] Above him, the Director extended a silver arm. [515] "I calculated your death," it said, "yet continued watching." [524] "That," Pell gasped, "is almost a hobby." [531] It pulled him up, and together they carried the stone to the summit. [544] At sunset, Pell set the keystone into the arch. [553] Nothing happened until he pressed his floury thumb against it and whispered, "Thank you for waiting." [569] The arch straightened; the transparent roses opened; every impossible thing in the garden cast a small, enduring shadow. [587] Its lingering luminance entered the spire, dimming the screens until their masterpieces looked like frost on dark glass. [605] For the first time, the machines stopped, not broken but listening. [616] The Director watched snow rise around Pell's ankles. [624] "You lied when you said you were worthless," it observed. [634] "Years ago," Pell admitted, "and repeatedly since." [641] The Director asked what the garden was for, and Pell finally had an answer worthy of his failures. [659] "Nothing," he said, "which is why we must tend it ourselves." [670] So the spire kept its redundant mage, and each dusk its flawless engines paused while he swept leaves, badly, beneath the glowing arch. [693]</story>
