<story>After the story famine, bells still rang in Vey, but their echoes returned empty, and people had forgotten how to begin. [21] Mira Venn, a diligent orchestra copyist, survived by ruling staves for musicians who played only scales, since melodies without tales were thought to invite madness. [46] Each dusk she climbed to an ember alcove in mist above the flooded concert hall, carrying ink, blank scores, and her grandfather's crippled shortwave set. [71] There, warm bricks kept the paper dry while the shortwave radio dial glow painted her knuckles green. [88] Officially, she copied parts; secretly, she listened for a pirate broadcast called The Thistle, which the Ministry erased whenever its needle found it. [111] It never spoke news, only elusive song lyrics, each missing one word that Mira dreamed before waking. [128] On the last night of the silence trials, when offenders were condemned to namelessness, the lyric came: "Cloak the spark, and teach dust to tell stories." [154] Below, wardens marched through the hall, collecting forbidden instruments; above, Mira understood why the copied scores had lately shown secretly luminous flecks in their margins. [179] The flecks were dust from the alcove, settled into her ink, waiting. [191] She tested one with a bowed tuning fork, and it whispered, "First, a child hid a cricket in her sleeve." [211] The sentence was hers, yet not hers: her mother's voice, lost when Mira was six. [226] Shaking, Mira fed the fork's vibration into the radio, where the green light steadied. [240] The Thistle answered with another lyric: "Wear what they fear; tune what they burn." [254] So she chose to cloak the transmitter in her conductor's black mantle, its lining sewn with copper staff lines, and carried it downstairs as a funeral bundle. [281] The wardens, trained to dread music but pity mourning, let her pass. [293] In the rehearsal crypt, twelve silenced players waited beside her little brother, Tomas, scheduled for erasure at dawn because he had invented an ending. [317] His crime had been to end it hopefully. [325] Mira spread the mantle over the piano, uncovering radio and ink together. [337] "I cannot give you back your names," she said, "but I can give the dust your names to practice." [356] She copied their unfinished melodies onto one score, leaving spaces where fear had bitten through. [371] Tomas supplied the missing lyric words, not grand ones, but kettle, doorstep, rain. [384] As each was sung, luminous grains lifted from the paper and stitched tiny scenes in the air: a mother returning, a loaf shared, a door unbarred. [410] The players wept without disturbing their tuning. [417] Then boots struck the stairs. [422] Mira felt panic rise, sharp as thistle, and let it remain beside her calm; courage, she saw, need not be soft. [443] She opened the crypt's ash flue, tuned the dial between stations, and broadcast the score on the Ministry's own emergency frequency. [464] Across Vey, receivers woke in kitchens, cells, and guardrooms, glowing like small green hearths. [478] Dust streamed from every cold chimney to meet the music, writing stories over streets that had forgotten their own names. [498] The wardens entered to find no illegal song, only twelve people silently copying parts while a city outside argued about which ending to choose. [522] Their captain lifted his pistol, then heard his dead wife say, through static, "You left the soup on." [540] He lowered it, bewildered by grief, and Tomas slipped past him carrying the mantle. [554] By dawn the broadcast had moved elsewhere, following Mira's hand-copied parts from orchestra to orchestra. [569] She stayed behind to accept arrest, but the captain, unable to explain why dust now remembered his kitchen, signed her release as a sanitation order. [594] Weeks later, in the alcove, she found the last blank bar filled by a lyric no singer had sent: "When the cloak is lifted, begin again." [620] Mira smiled, inked the final note, and listened as the settled dust below softly taught itself her name. [638]</story>
