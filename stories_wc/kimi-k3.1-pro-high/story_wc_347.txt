Mara had kept the cursed orchard for eleven years, which was ten years longer than she had intended. [18] It grew in a cliffside labyrinth where the paths moved at dusk, and mechanical birds patrolled the walls with copper wings and borrowed human eyes. [43] Her mother had called the work sacred; Mara called it a life sentence with pruning. [58] Each autumn she meant to leave, and each autumn some traveler cried out among the terraces, making departure seem uglier than staying. [80] On the last clear evening of the year, she found three answers to her escape: a small nut, a forgotten blueprint, and voices below. [104] The nut lay in her dead mother's pruning box, black as a knot and heavy enough to pull the box from her lap. [127] The blueprint was folded beneath it, soft with oil, showing a machine assembled from wire, a mirror, and one orchard seed. [148] Across its margin, her mother had written: A HAND THAT TOUCHES THE PATH MAKES THE PATH LIE. [165] Mara understood that part; every marker she had hammered down had led someone in a confident circle. [182] Below, a man shouted, then a child answered him from farther away. [194] The birds lifted from the battlements, their wing joints ticking like sleet. [206] Mara looked at the seed compartment drawn on the blueprint and laughed without humor. [220] Of course it needs the only thing worth stealing, she said. [231] The nut was the orchard's heart: carry it beyond the cliff before night frost formed, and the curse would choose another keeper. [253] Use it in a machine, and it would be spent. [263] She put it in her pocket and assembled the rest anyway, because waste, unlike hope, could be decided later. [282] Her workshop yielded a cracked shaving mirror, kite wire, three wind cups, and the axle from a hated music box. [302] She worked with offhandedly exact motions, biting wire at four finger-widths, tilting the mirror eleven degrees, cursing each measurement when it fitted. [324] According to the blueprint, the device would not command the birds; it would interest them. [339] A seed's weight would turn the cups, the cups would flash the mirror, and the flock would chase what it believed was a wounded rival. [364] Their wings, beating close above the orchard's silver moss, would leave impressions without touching anything. [380] The curse could move stones and deceive footprints, but it had never learned to erase an impression made by wind. [400] A machine to make directions out of pursuit: Mara found its ugliness almost tender. [414] She carried it to the western wall as the sun thinned and frost began to silver the lowest leaves. [433] Once the night frost formed fully, the moss would stiffen, the paths would close, and the travelers would freeze among them. [454] From below came the child again, smaller now, saying, I can hear you. [467] Mara set the nut into its brass cup. [475] For one cowardly second she imagined the road beyond the cliff, the unremarkable rooms, the years belonging to no tree. [495] Then she shoved the mirror toward the last sunlight and said, Follow me, you dreadful pigeons. [511] The machine lurched along the parapet, flashing badly and beautifully. [521] Every bird in the labyrinth stooped after it. [529] Mara ran beside them, guiding the clumsy frame down the safe stair she knew by scar and root. [547] Behind the flock, wing wind pressed long shining arrows into the moss, each one pointing inward to the stair and outward to the gate. [571] When the machine snagged, she did not repair it gently; she kicked it straight, and the birds screamed approval. [590] The travelers found those arrows: a fisherman with a broken lantern, his daughter, and an old woman carrying a door. [610] They reached the gate just as frost knitted across the upper terraces. [622] The child looked back at the churning birds, the lopsided mirror, and Mara bleeding from one thumb. [639] Is it magic, she asked. [644] Badly, said Mara, who was offhandedly exact even then. [653] At her feet the nut cracked, spent, and a white root pushed through its shell. [668] Instead of taking the road, she planted it beside the gate, where anyone lost could find something living before the walls began to lie. [692] By spring there stood a crooked tree with copper leaves, ugly as a repaired kettle and loud with birds. [711] Mara still called her work a sentence, but she had started, without admitting it, to prune the orchard toward the sea. [732]
