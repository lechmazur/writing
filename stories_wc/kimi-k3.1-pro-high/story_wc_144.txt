<story>At the mist-coated rail depot, Sera Vale kept her repair shop open before dawn, though the last passenger train had gone and the harbor bells were counting toward a departure. [30] Blind since childhood, she oversaw the benches by touch and hearing, assigning each apprentice work from the music of a loose gear or a frightened motor. [56] Her calm drew people closer rather than making them hurry; even the steam pipes seemed to tick more softly under her hands. [78]

That morning, a naval courier brought her an artificial hummingbird drone in a cedar box, its emerald wings folded like leaves around a cracked brass breast. [104] It belonged to the Meridian, whose anchor was already lifting for the ship's maiden voyage beyond the fogbound strait. [123] The drone was meant to fly ahead, taste the beacon light with its prism eyes, and steer the ship away from the drowned rail causeway. [148] Instead, it had returned against the wind, stitching circles above the depot until its battery failed. [164]

Sera's youngest apprentice, Ivo, wanted to replace its guidance coil, but she felt a hairline tremor in the left wing and asked him to wait. [189] "If we silence the complaint before we understand it," she said, "the sea will finish the diagnosis." [206]

She had built her shop around water because water made light tangible to her trained fingers and useful to machines. [226] A locomotive fountain fed three shallow basins, each with a different rim of glass, copper, or salt-roughened stone. [244] By examining light refraction through water features, Sera could read a drone's feedback as direction: a warm band meant east, a cool double pulse meant the signal had split, and a sudden stillness meant trust nothing. [280]

Ivo set the hummingbird above the basins and lit its beacon lamp. [292] Its damaged prism scattered white needles across the fountain, while a second image trembled against the copper basin, pointing toward the rails instead of the harbor. [318] Sera trailed one finger in the water, feeling the warmed current bend left, then right. [333] "It is not lost," she murmured. [339] "It is obeying two maps." [344]

The depot's old military beacon, buried beneath the platform, had woken when the ship's new beacon answered it. [362] The hummingbird's cracked breast was letting their signals cross. [371] Sera could have disabled the old beacon, but that would also silence the depot's fog pump and leave the fishing boats blind outside the breakwater. [396] She chose to patch the drone instead. [403]

From a ruined ticket press she took a thin plate of nacre-lined tin, warmed it over her tea, and shaped it to the bird's breast with small, patient blows. [432] The patch did more than close the crack; its ridged lining separated the two wavelengths, turning noise into a choice. [452] Ivo offered to solder it, but Sera kept the seam herself, guiding his hand only when his breath quickened. [471] "A repair should leave courage behind," she told him. [480]

When the hummingbird woke, it hovered above her shoulder and dipped toward the salt basin. [495] Now the refracted light made one clean road across the water, east by southeast, while the old military signal faded to a harmless violet rim. [520] Sera smiled, not at the light, which she had never seen, but at the steadiness entering Ivo's hands. [538]

Outside, the Meridian's chain gave its final iron groan. [547] The courier reached for the box, but the drone was already gone through the open doors, a green spark sewing mist to morning. [570] Sera followed only as far as the platform. [578] She heard the ship's engines gather, falter, and then settle into the deep rhythm of a vessel that knows where it is going. [601]

For years she had believed direction was something sighted people gave her, a hand on her elbow, a warned step. [621] Now she understood her work differently: not to command the lost, but to listen until their confusion revealed a road. [641] When Ivo took her arm, he did not lead; he simply walked beside her back to the fountain, where the water kept shining for anyone patient enough to read it. [671]</story>
