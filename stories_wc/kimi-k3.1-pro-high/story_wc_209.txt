In autumn, when the amber pavilion glowed like a coal among the cathedral's brown gardens, Mara Venn returned with brushes, solvents, and her grandmother's collection of old postcards. [28]
She was a vivid mural artist, famous for walls whose thunderstorms seemed to rumble, yet privately she was quietly driven by one unfinished childhood question. [53]
Why did the flowers in her grandmother Lena's night garden bloom only after dark? [67]
Lena had died before answering, leaving the postcards in a cedar box and Mara a commission to restore the pavilion's faded mural of moonlit lilies. [92]
As a child, Mara had believed Lena withheld answers to seem mysterious; now the box felt less like a puzzle than a hand extended after death. [118]
The cathedral's dean called the mural Master Orson's work; Mara recognized Lena's impatient violet strokes beneath his gilded signature. [137]
Each postcard pictured the same cathedral window, but on their backs Lena had written dates, flower names, and arrows pointing toward particular panes. [160]
Mara began by day, cleaning lilies while visitors watched, and continued by night, when the pavilion held only shadow, turpentine, and the tick of cooling glass. [186]
The postcards' dates matched nights when their pictured flowers had opened, always beneath a moon entering the window's eastern edge. [206]
Following that geometry, Mara climbed the cathedral scaffolding and discovered that the window was not one sheet but several, separated by narrow seams of lead. [231]
Through the hidden layers of etched glass in the cathedral window, moonlight broke into faint images: roots, moths, folded petals, and a woman painting beside Orson. [257]
The woman wore Lena's crooked silver ring. [264]
On the coldest night, Mara carried the postcards upstairs and laid them against the panes in chronological order. [282]
Their arrows aligned the etchings, completing a sentence no single layer contained: What opens unseen still answers light. [300]
Below it, almost erased, was Lena's name. [307]
Anger warmed Mara more cruelly than the brazier; she wanted to strip Orson's signature away that instant and summon the dean. [328]
Instead, she studied the final postcard, which showed no window at all, only the pavilion under snow. [345]
On its back Lena had written, Orson kept me from the guild's rolls, but he could not keep moonlight from remembering. [366]
The revenge Mara imagined would make Lena visible by repeating his violence, scraping until nothing tender survived. [383]
She had to reckon with a harder restoration: preserving the injury without letting it remain the whole truth. [401]
Winter deepened. [403]
She cleaned around Orson's name, stabilized every fracture, and painted a narrow border of moths whose wings carried Lena's initials. [423]
In the pavilion's warm soil she tended the last night-blooming cereus, descended from Lena's garden, though its buds remained stubbornly shut. [444]
Reading beside it, Mara learned that such flowers opened for nocturnal moths, sparing nectar from daytime heat and trusting darkness with their fragrance. [467]
The answer was biology, yet it did not feel smaller for being true. [480]
By the week winter's last snow began melting from the cathedral steps, the dean agreed to add a plaque naming Lena as artist and Orson as patron. [507]
Mara refused the easier lie of replacing one sole genius with another. [519]
On the final evening, meltwater whispered through the gutters while she carried Lena's postcards to the pavilion. [536]
Moonlight crossed the restored window, passed through its patient layers, and touched the cereus just as its white petals loosened. [556]
A dawning hush gathered, though no sun had risen: the hush of a room before understanding enters it. [574]
Mara sat within that umbral tenderness, smelling the flower's vanilla breath and thinking of Lena working unthanked beneath Orson's gold. [594]
Night blooming was not a refusal of day, she finally understood, but an intimacy with whatever light remained. [612]
She sketched the open cereus onto the last blank postcard, addressed it to Lena, and wrote beneath it, Now the dark has a witness. [636]
She would leave it in the cedar box, not as another secret, but as the first page of the garden's true history. [658]
