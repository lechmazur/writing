Three weeks after the televised debacle, Deke Marsh took the job nobody else wanted: cataloguing the contents of a dead quarry's office trailer. [23] On air, before cameras and a weeping widow, he had certified a fake violin as genuine, and the clip traveled farther than any song he had ever hauled. [51] The trailer crouched at the rim of the flooded pit, and the whole valley ran on longing: every sound the wind dropped came back asking for more. [78] Mrs. Aldous, the owner's widow, met him with coffee and forty years of ledgers. [92]

He appraised antiques the way he had once packed trucks: gloves, foam, tape, facts. [106] Silverware, plate, not sterling; desk, oak, water-ringed; radio, Bakelite, one knob missing. [118] Facts were insulation; he packed them between himself and whatever might hurt. [130] Two years since June's funeral, three weeks since his career's, and nothing in between had explained why he should keep going. [151] Behind a filing cabinet, under a tarp, he found an amplifier. [162] The tolex was scuffed and the logo gone, but he knew the cab before he touched it, the way you know a shoulder in a crowd. [188] He had hauled this box through forty states for the Mercy Brothers, a bar band with one almost-hit and a singer named Jonah Wills who could make a banquet hall feel like church. [221]

Mrs. Aldous's son had bought it secondhand, she said, and carried it to the pit's edge to play, because the echo made one guitar sound like five. [248] "He said the valley answered him," she said. [256] "Nobody else did, after the quarry closed." [263] Her son was in the cemetery now, and that was all she could say about it steadily. [280] Deke knelt and examined the baffle, where the grille cloth had torn, leaving a ragged scrap still stapled at one corner: silver thread shot through black, the salt-and-pepper weave every amp on that stage had worn the night the Mercy Brothers ended. [322] He recited what he knew to keep his hands from shaking: woven in Passaic, New Jersey; discontinued 1971; half the bar bands in America had worn it thin. [350] The facts held, mostly. [354] The last show was a VFW hall in Ohio: Jonah sang like the building owed him money, the applause ran long enough to feel like forgiveness, and then it stopped, and after the applause there was only teardown; Deke wrapping cables while Jonah signed papers in a back room that ended the band. [407] Jonah went to Nashville and stopped calling; June came along years later, and then went too, in a hospice bed with the radio playing. [431] Deke had built his second career on the theory that objects outlast the hands that hold them, and his whole defense on never saying so out loud. [458]

It took him ten minutes and a flathead screwdriver to wrest the scrap free of rusted staples and dry rot, and the tearing sound was small and terrible. [486] She hovered at the trailer door, asking if it was worth something. [498] The violin had died of his certainty, so now he gave her the honest shape of the thing, confidently uncertain, a number with room around it for the truth. [527] "Three hundred as an amplifier, maybe, to the right buyer." [537] "But the provenance is real. [542] This cab played behind Jonah Wills. [548] Your boy played through the same speakers that carried the last Mercy Brothers show. [562] That, I can't price. [566] That, I can only tell you." [572]

Mrs. Aldous laid a hand flat on the dusty tolex, the way you touch a casket. [588] "He sounded good out there," she said. [595] "Tell me he sounded good." [600] He could not appraise her son's playing, but he knew what the valley did with sound, so he told her the truth: the echo did not answer noise; it answered something worth answering. [633] When he tried to hand the scrap of grille cloth back, she closed his fingers around it: "You knew it before he did." [656]

At dusk, left alone to lock up, he walked the cloth to the edge of the pit. [673] The water below held the last light like a struck cymbal. [684] He shouted Jonah's name into the valley, and June's, and the quarry gave each one back doubled, tripled, a long rolling applause no roadie would ever have to tear down. [714] Deke understood what he was for: not to set prices, but to carry precious things carefully from one life into the next, and to tell their owners what they had witnessed. [745] It was teardown work, after the applause, and he had been doing it all his life; he had only needed a hollow valley to hear it. [771] He folded the scrap into his shirt pocket, over his heart, and drove down out of the hills with the windows open, listening. [794]
