The plague was over, officially, and that word did more work in the ruins than any living man. [18] Marsh lived in the sewer of the drowned city, in the one dry gallery the tide politely refused, and he called himself a philosopher because nobody else would. [46] The shore folk called him a rat, which he accepted with abrasive calm, since labels were cheap and strata were not. [67] Above him the sunken city rotted in its lagoon: rooftops like broken teeth, bell towers listing, tide-flat streets where the priests of the Cleansing recited the official story on pilgrimage. [97] It was simple and load-bearing: God sent the plague for the city's pride, then in mercy sent the water to end it, and the shore survivors were the spared and the grateful. [129]

Marsh had doubts, and doubts, properly fed, become evidence. [138] So he dug. [141] He dug through sedimentary layers in the great conduit beneath Cornmarket, downward and backward through time, because silt keeps better books than priests. [164] Reading strata was an ancient method, half remembered, and preserving ancient methods was the only religion he could afford. [183] Top layer, gray ash: thirty years of memorial fires. [192] Beneath the ash, tan flood-silt; beneath that, the black band everyone called the Plague Year, wrath and water supposedly touching. [212] He counted the laminae like a miser counting coins, because the counting was the whole argument. [228] Then he counted what lay above the black band: the ordinary filth of a living city, pollen and hearth-ash, three quiet years of it, and only then the drowning. [257] The plague had ended itself, quietly, three years before anyone sent any water. [270] The Cleansing had drowned a living city. [277]

He agonized over that for a season, which is the correct verb, since agonizing is thought with the pain left in. [298] Rebellious by reason rather than temper, he refused to leap from a fact to a sermon; the leap was how bad histories were born. [322] Perhaps the water was mercy delayed, or murder, or a harbor tax enforced by tide; strata showed sequence, not motive. [342] He needed a date that no one could argue with. [352] In the fourth winter of digging, tweezers-deep in the boundary clay, he found a small brass cog, toothed like a tiny crown, corroded green at the roots. [379] A seafarer's chronometer cog; he knew it from a salvaged manual, from the chapter on keeping time at sea, the ancients' way of knowing where you were by knowing when. [409] It lay just inside the old tide-gates, driven into the clay with the first pulse of the drowning. [427] A ship of the fleet had been broken against those same gates at the moment of the Cleansing, and its timekeeping tooth dated the water like a nail dates a wall. [458] The priests said God sent the water; the cog said the fleet was anchored in the harbor when He did. [478] Two narratives, one channel, and the channel was his. [487]

He climbed to the shore at low tide, the cog in his pocket, and found the young priest Ossen drawing water for the sick-house, because even after a plague ends, someone is always weak. [521] "The Cleansing saved us," Ossen said, not unkindly. [529] "The sequence disagrees," Marsh said, and held out the cog, calm as a man handing over a knife he had already decided not to use. [554] "This is a tooth from a chronometer, a machine for knowing when you are, and it was rammed through your open tide-gates with the first of your mercy." [582] "Your water came three years after the plague died, and it came by ship." [596] Ossen looked at it and then at the ruins and said nothing, which was the honest sound of two narratives colliding. [617]

Here he could have preached, minted a rival story with himself as prophet, and he turned it over all the way home, tasting its sweetness and its rot. [645] A counter-myth is still a myth; it asks for the same obedience with better smells. [660] So he did the rebellious thing, the rational thing, the slow thing: he kept the cog and taught the method. [680] He set the cog on a shelf of driftwood in the dry gallery, labeled it as a question, and began teaching the shore children to count layers. [707] "Don't believe me," he told them, abrasively, calmly. [715] "Believe the sequence." [718] "Dig, count, and let the mud out-argue us both." [727]

The priests' story would keep its church, the strata's story would keep its silence, and the children, methodical as tides, would eventually have to choose. [752] Marsh could live with either outcome, because a method, unlike a story, survives being wrong. [767] In the sewer of the drowned city, that was as close to wisdom as anyone had honestly come.
