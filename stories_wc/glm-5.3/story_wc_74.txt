The smog lay on the city like wet wool, but Maren's rooftop greenhouse held the last clean air on the block. [21] She had not truly slept in four years. [29] Insomnia was her doctor's puzzle, her landlord's complaint, and her painting's engine. [41] While the city coughed in its sleep, she ground pigments that woke only under the moon. [57]

Between the saffron crocus pots she kept a ledger, and the ledger said: sixty-one words painted, forty nights worked, zero replies. [78] The brush had come from a stranger named Teodor, seven years ago, when a smog storm chased fourteen-year-old Maren into his stairwell. [100] He fed her barley tea, covered her with his coat, and taught her the looping letters of Riven, the language of his drowned valley, of which he claimed to be the last speaker. [133] In Riven, nobody said thank you; they said halveth, which means "I remember you." [147] When the storm cleared, he pressed his calligrapher's brush into her hands, its tip a pale filament of moonstone that seemed to drink light. [171] The winter storms took his building the next year, and Teodor with it, and left her a brush, an alphabet, and a debt. [194]

People in this city insisted kindness evaporated. [201] "You give, they forget, you die," her neighbor liked to say. [212] Maren meant to prove that strangers remember kindness, and she was methodically hopeful about it, the way a gardener is hopeful: not trusting, just thorough. [237] So every night while the city slept badly, she painted. [247] By day her letters were ash grey and invisible on the walls; under moonlight they burned amber, saffron in the dark. [268] On the night shelter's wall she painted velan, "rest." [277] Above the bus depot, sholai, "you are not alone here." [287] The hospital wall received miren, "morning will come." [295] Nobody could read them, and everybody could feel them, and on the nights she lingered she watched strangers stop beneath the glow, eyes drifting, faces going soft, roused into daydreams that the moonlit words seemed to spark like struck flint. [335] That was the whole wager: strangers would remember kindness they could not even name. [349]

Tonight the moon was waxing, three nights from full, and the smog had thinned enough to let its light fall through the greenhouse glass in long saffron bars. [377] Sleep never came for Maren, so she did her version of it: she sat among the crocuses, let her eyes half close, and waited for the daydreams that only moonlight could rouse. [409] They came the way they always came, sparked by moonlight: Teodor's stairwell, the steam off the tea, his hand over hers, guiding the moon-tipped brush through the letter for river. [439] Out of the reverie rose a word she had lost for years: seneth, dawn, the kindness owed to no one and given anyway. [462] She climbed down to the sleeping streets and painted it across the depot's shuttered face, the moonstone tip trailing light like a snail's silver. [486] Then she climbed back up, entered the sixty-second word in her ledger, and tried not to count the silence as an answer. [508]

Three nights later, the waxing moon nearly full, she found the greenhouse door ajar and two sets of bootprints on the stairs, one large, one small. [534] On the potting bench, in chalky phosphor that glowed the same amber, someone had written a word in Riven script. [554] Halveth. [555] "I remember you." [558] Below it, smaller, in a child's careful hand, was a word she had never painted and never taught: tenari, "beginning again." [579] Maren sat down hard among the crocus pots. [587] Teodor, then, had opened his door to other strangers in other storms; somewhere in the smog were more hands that held his letters, waiting, like hers had waited, for something to rouse them. [620] A language, she understood, is only lost when the last kindness written in it is forgotten. [636] She thought of the neighbor's arithmetic, the evaporating kindness, and smiled at the quiet evidence glowing on the bench. [655] Under the waxing moon, she opened the ledger to a fresh page and began a primer instead, letter by patient letter, halveth first. [678] Then she dipped the moon-tipped brush, methodically hopeful, and painted her reply on the greenhouse roof where every insomniac in the city could find it: the Riven word for "welcome," glowing saffron and quiet against the dark.
