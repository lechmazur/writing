Lena kept the night watch at Vesper Ridge Observatory, where the great chart of the sky grew one variable star at a time. [23] She recorded the stars that dimmed and flared, dimmed and flared, and the steady work soothed her the way rain soothes some people. [46] She was also, in the privacy of her sleeping mind, a chronicler of lucid dreams. [61]

Since her sister Ines died, Lena had dreamed one recurring temple: pale stone above a grass sea, its walls carved floor to ceiling with columns of script she could copy but never read. [94] Each morning she chronicled what she had seen, journal after journal, because she was certain the inscriptions meant something, and decoding them had become the current that pulled her whole life. [125] Ines had kept the southern station alone and died there in the first snow, and the dreams had begun that same week. [147]

On a clear midnight in March, Tomas, the courier from the southern route, came up the observatory road carrying her sister's effects, snow still on his shoulders. [174] Among the papers was a telegram Ines had written and never sent, a daylily pressed flat between its folded halves. [194] Ines had tucked a daylily into every letter she ever mailed, one bloom for one day, kept. [211] This one had been picked the morning she wrote. [220] The telegram read: WINTER WATCH TOO HEAVY ALONE STOP COME WHEN THE PASSES OPEN STOP BRING YOUR DREAM BOOK STOP INES. [241] So Ines had known about the journals, and had wanted them, and had never mailed the asking. [258] She had protected Lena from her own weight exactly as Lena had protected her, each sister polite about her darkness until the darkness won. [282]

Tomas could not descend before dawn, the road being what it was, so he sat across the chart room from her, and they kept the watch separately, the way the bereaved do. [314] Near two in the morning she could bear the silence no longer and slid a dream journal across the table. [334] Witness it, she said. [338] He turned the pages slowly, saying nothing, and she found that having him see the copied columns eased something in her chest she had not known was tight. [366] Then he stopped at a page and tapped a small glyph that recurred at the foot of every column. [385]

Lena pulled her ledger of observations alongside and felt the night shift beneath her. [399] The tallies in the inscriptions rose and fell in cycles, and the cycles matched her records of the red variable in Cetus, the star Ines had tracked for years. [428] The temple was a watch log. [434] Its ancients had charted the dimming and returning of stars on stone, and every entry ended with two names, two hands, because no watcher there had ever kept the night alone. [465] The recurring glyph was the refrain from the story Ines used to tell when they were girls, the story of the watchers: it dims, we keep the watch, it returns. [495] Her sleeping mind had built a temple out of Ines's stories and her own star charts, and had spent a year teaching her to read it. [521]

Tomas spoke at last, the words rising out of him like water from a deep well: he had watched Ines grow thin all autumn and had said nothing, keeping her secret because it seemed hers to tell, and he had carried that silence all winter. [566] Lena took his hand and gave him the only medicine she had, which was words. [581] A burden carried alone goes dark, she told him, the way a star does, and it comes back when it is shared; the temple knew it, Ines knew it, and now so did they. [615] He wept, which was its own kind of astronomy, and she witnessed it without looking away. [631]

When understanding dawns it rarely announces itself, but this time it did: the eastern sky went grey, then gold, and the night's work lay finished on the table between them. [661] Before the road opened, Lena filed an astronomical telegram, the kind every station that keeps the night watch receives: variable star, southern sky, period and position drawn from Ines's papers, discovered by Ines, to be entered on the great chart under her name. [704] It was the reply the unsent telegram had waited for, sent at last to everyone. [719] Then she lifted the daylily from the old telegram and pressed it into the chart volume at the page of Ines's star, where one day's bloom would keep a permanent watch beside a light that dims and returns. [757] The light reaching the observatory that morning had left its star years ago, while Ines was still laughing beneath it, and it arrived anyway, which is the oldest comfort there is. [788]
