Three weeks after the birth, Maren carried her daughter up the shepherd stair to the glade that hung above the clouds, where midnight pooled instead of falling. [27] The baby slept in a sling of grey wool, and Maren's pack held the two things she could not read from a distance: her deck and the tablecloth. [55] She was a cartomancer by trade, forty-two cards painted in her own onyx ink, ground from black stone until the pigment held shadow the way other inks hold light. [84] For anyone else the cards spoke plainly; for herself they only ever mumbled, and she had never once tried to make them clearer. [107] That was her discipline: she stayed willingly perplexed, because a reader who knows her own ending stops looking, and Maren meant to keep looking all her life. [134]

Tonight, though, she needed one answer. [140] Her mother had died in spring, four days short of meeting her granddaughter, and since then Maren had been haunted by arithmetic: visits postponed, long calls cut short, birthdays spent elsewhere. [171] All that lost time had to be somewhere, and she meant to find where lost time accumulates before her daughter grew old enough for such losses to begin. [199]

The glade was where her mother had brought her once as a child: a ring of pale grass, the cloud sea lying below it and glowing. [225] Above the clouds, her mother had said, repentance cannot fall back on you; it binds to the mist and comes down years later, somewhere far off, as gentle rain. [254] Cloudbound, she called it, and swore that every kindness in the valley arrived that way. [269] So this was where you brought what you could no longer say to anyone. [283]

Maren spread the birthday tablecloth on the grass. [291] It carried the family custom: every birthday, the child's hand was traced in pencil and embroidered the following winter, until the cloth held a ladder of hands climbing from small to almost grown, one rung a year. [328] She laid her cards along the ladder and asked her question aloud. [340] Where does lost time accumulate? [345]

The onyx ink stirred like silt in water, and the cards turned themselves in a slow spiral that closed, card by card, on the hem. [370] The hem had always seemed thick with decades of washing, nothing more. [382] With her thumbnails and then her teeth, she unfastened the old stitching, and something pale slid into the moonlight: a saucer from a dolls' tea set, thin as a wafer, cracked clean across its painted violets. [418] Her mother's childhood saucer. [422] Maren had cracked it at seven, playing tea after being told twice to stop, and had buried it in the rosebed and never confessed. [446]

She recognized the stitching she had just opened as her mother's: even, patient, the same thread that stitched the ladder of hands. [468] Her mother had found it, washed the earth from it, and hidden it here, inside the family's whole account of time, without one word. [492] Then Maren saw the rung from that year. [500] She had been too guilty that year to study the cloth. [511] Every birthday held one traced hand, hers, growing; that year alone held two, her small hand and beside it, for the only time, her mother's own, embroidered in the same violet thread as the saucer's ruined flowers. [548] The beauty of it stopped her breath the way thin air does, so that she could not tell choking from glory. [569]

She understood then where lost time goes, and laughed once, wetly, at how plain the answer had been. [587] It accumulates in cracks. [591] Not in ledgers, not in grief, but in the fracture itself: in broken things kept and sewn around, in the place where someone chose quiet love over accusation. [619] Every postponed visit her mother had forgiven had pooled right here, in this split porcelain, a whole reservoir of unlived time, kept safe. [642]

Maren knelt at the edge of the glade and held the saucer out over the cloud sea. [659] "I broke it," she said. [664] "I hid it, and let you be kind to me anyway." [675] The words left her mouth and bound themselves to the mist, as promised, to fall in some far year as the gentlest rain. [698]

Then she took the pencil stub from her pack, pressed her daughter's starfish hand flat on the cloth below all the others, and drew its first rung. [725] The baby stirred but did not wake. [732] Above the clouds, the midnight held them all, the old hands and the new, in its unbearable, beautiful mouth. [751]
