Mara Voss had built her career burying ghost stories, so the cursed orchard of the Ashfall Zone was irresistible. [19] The story went that a flawed diamond lay buried among the trees, and everyone who touched it died. [37] Mara debunked such things for a living; excavating truth from comfortable lies was less a job than a hunger. [56] Two years after the Liquidation, when the Provisional Authority had erased the zone's records and evacuated everyone in it, the orchard still bloomed. [79] Apricots ripened there while radioactive dust storms scoured the surrounding country to gravel. [92] That detail had bothered her for a year, and Mara was sharply observant to the bone. [108]

She hiked in during a lull between storms, goggles fogged, dosimeter ticking. [120] The ticking slowed as she entered the trees, then stopped altogether. [131] Inside the orchard the air hung windless and clean, warm as a held breath. [145] Above the canopy the dust still spiraled past on both sides, a river bending around a stone. [162] There was a stillpoint here, a fixed calm the storms had never once breached. [176] Science couldn't explain it, so the Authority had explained it with a curse. [189]

In the keeper's cottage Mara found the walls stuffed with shredded paper, thousands of strips packed in as insulation. [208] Reyes had shredded her own letters herself, hiding her testimony the only way it could survive a purge. [226] She spent eleven days at the orchard's edge, sorting strips by ink color and tear pattern, reassembling shredded letters a phrase at a time. [250] They were from Dr. Ilsa Reyes, the orchardist, written to her sister before the evacuation. [265]

Reyes had mapped the basin's wind geometry and guessed what the storms concealed: they self-organized around a stationary eye, and fallout could never settle where no dust crossed. [293] The orchard was not surviving the wasteland; it stood outside it entirely. [305] But proof needed measurements the Authority could not shred. [314] Flawed diamonds, Reyes wrote, were cheap and patient; their lattice flaws drank radiation the way blotter paper drinks ink. [333] She had buried a dozen at measured depths, planning to unearth one each year, heat it, and read its glow against her records. [356] Three letters reported three stones, and all three had come up dark, cleaner than her laboratory controls. [373] The basin was habitable; the wasteland around it was a convenience. [384]

Then the letters turned frightened. [389] Surveyors had come asking about minerals, and Reyes suspected why the Authority wanted the land declared permanently dead: dead land meant dumping contracts, cheap titles, and no returning claimants. [418] Her dark diamonds were a threat. [424] The last legible fragment broke off mid-sentence: if they come again, the stones are the evidence, and the stillpoint will keep everything alive until someone. [449]

Until someone what, Mara could only intuit; but intuition had already told her where to dig. [465] From her first hour she had noticed one tree standing at the orchard's exact center, a single pear among apricots, its leaves never wearing dust. [490] In one recovered letter, Reyes mentioned a tree her sister had planted on her only visit. [506] The two facts met like torn edges, and Mara walked to the pear with a trowel. [522] Two feet down, a glass jar held eight flawed diamonds and a ledger wrapped in waxed cloth. [539] She heated one stone over her lighter; it stayed dark, inert, innocent. [551] Reyes's ledger said the same in careful columns: stone after stone, year after year, dark. [566]

The curse made sense now, in the way curses always do, as economics. [579] After the evacuation, a scavenger had pried a glinting stone from these roots, carried it out through a storm, and died coughing in a border clinic; Authority radio named the diamond cursed, and the rumor kept every curious soul far from the basin. [622] Fear was cheaper than fences. [627] Comfortable lies travel light; truth needed a jar, a ledger, and eleven days of tape. [642]

Mara photographed everything, then sat in the windless quiet eating an apricot that no law of the wasteland said should exist. [663] The fruit was absurdly sweet, the kind of fact that ends arguments. [675] When the next storm rose, she sealed her mask, zipped the jar and the reconstructed letters into her pack, and walked out through the howling dust, counter chattering, the way the scavenger had gone. [709] She would carry the truth back the same way, through everything designed to kill it, and let the comfortable lies finally be the ones to die. [735]
