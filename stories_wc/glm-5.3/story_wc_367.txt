The last print newspaper on earth died screaming about weather, and after that nobody printed anything. [16] But up in the emerald lighthouse, Fen the storm herald kept his routine. [29] Every dusk he climbed the spiral stairs, laughing at his own jokes, and lit a lamp whose wick was not fiber but glass. [52] The wick was a strand of green glass spun finer than hair, and light that entered it never leaked out the sides. [74] It ricocheted along the inner walls, cornered through every bend, and emerged only where Fen aimed it. [91] The old keepers had called it total internal reflection; Fen called it teaching light to follow orders. [108] Either way, he could steer the beam anywhere: down the stairwell, out across the water, over the roofs of sleeping towns. [129]

Fen was a herald because storms sent him ahead of themselves; he laughed because the gift could not be helped. [149] Whatever he joked about while laughing had a way of coming true. [161] He never meant to prophesy; the laughter carried prophecy in it like static. [174] "Wouldn't it be funny," he once snorted into the wind, "if the ferry ran three hours early?" [191] It had, straight into the squall, and only laughing luck brought it home. [204] After the papers died, warnings became feeds, feeds became noise, and nobody downstream believed weather anymore. [220] So Fen shouted his accidental prophecies to an empty channel and climbed back to his routine. [236]

The routine was otherworldly, though he would have called it housekeeping. [247] At dusk he polished the emerald glass until stars stood in it. [259] At midnight he fed the glass wick a spoonful of starlight and watched it burn green along its coiled length. [279] And before dawn he unfolded the last newspaper, kept in oilcloth beneath the lamp, and read its single surviving page to the sea. [302] The final edition carried the oldest news there is: STORM RISING, TIDE AT NINE, SEEK THE HIGH GROUND. [320] Fen had bought it the morning print died, and he loved it the way sailors love land. [337] His motive, if anyone had asked, was simple: he wanted to shape a new dawn out of ancient shadows. [356] News people could see with their heads tipped back; news that arrived with the sun. [371]

The storm that came in the fourth autumn was bigger than his jokes. [384] He felt it three days out, the way you feel a stranger's eyes. [397] The barometer fell like a dropped plate, the gulls went inland, and the stars above the lantern looked bruised. [416] Fen broadcast the warning and the feeds laughed at it, burying it under a thousand brighter nothings by morning. [435] Downstream, the tide gauges said nine meters and the town said goodnight. [447] That night, alone with the singing glass, Fen laughed until he cried, and heard what his own laughter said. [466] "Wouldn't it be funny," he heard himself say, "if dawn came up with something to say?" [482] Prophecy, then, in his own mouth, and a plan taking shape around it. [495]

He worked through the dark hours like a man possessed by calm. [507] He coiled the glass wick along the gallery rail, clamped it fast, and aimed its far end at the storm's leading edge. [529] Then he clamped the newspaper into the beam's path, so the light had to pass through it. [546] He fed the lamp starlight; green fire ran the coil, bouncing, bending, refusing to spill, emerging tight as a nail. [566] The ink drank the light, and the ancient shadows of the type swung out across miles of cloud. [584] STORM RISING stood over the harbor in letters a kilometer high. [595] TIDE AT NINE marched along the headland. [602] SEEK THE HIGH GROUND climbed the eastern sky, right where the sun was rehearsing its entrance. [618] All night Fen steered the beam, one hand on the clamps, one ear on the wind, laughing and not entirely with joy. [640] There is a gladness that only grows in the dark, and it beat in him like a second heart. [659]

The town woke at first gray and read the sky. [669] By the time the tide stood nine meters tall, the streets were empty and the hills were not. [687] The storm took the lower floors, the fish market, the pier, and every antenna that had ever laughed at a warning. [708] On the hills, people watched the water take their harbor and found that they were alive. [724] At dawn the clouds tore where the last shadow stood, and the sun rose through SEEK THE HIGH GROUND, dissolving the letters into gold. [748] A new dawn, shaped out of ancient shadows, exactly as he had wanted. [761]

At noon the fishermen rowed out to thank him; Fen was wiping salt from the ruined paper, laughing. [779] "Wouldn't it be funny," his laughter said, "if the world got gentler?" [791] Inland, already, something gentler had taken root. [798]
