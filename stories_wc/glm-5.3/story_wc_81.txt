The blackout came at dusk, and the pumps that held the sea back from the sunken library fell silent for the first time in forty years. [26] Mara Voss, the last page indexer still employed in the forgotten library, felt the change in her wrists before she heard it. [48] The machine hum that had filled the stacks since the district drowned was gone, and what remained was a stillness so complete it felt less like absence than reclamation. [77] She stood in the dark of the reading hall and let it settle over her like silt. [94] Water would be rising in the lower galleries now, seeping through the cofferdam seams, but slowly; she had until midnight, perhaps later. [116] Long enough. [118]

For six years Mara had indexed the drowned collection page by page, assigning each leaf its logical place in the master catalog, and for six years one discrepancy had waited for her in the provenance records. [154] Every volume in the founding collection carried an ownership stamp attributed to Aldery Renn, the merchant-prince credited with saving the city's books during the flood. [179] That was the official origin: Renn the philanthropist, Renn the savior. [190] But Mara was tranquilly perceptive, which is to say she noticed things slowly and never once mistakenly. [207] The Renn stamps were not all the same stamp. [216] Under the electric lamps, with the pumps roaring, she had half-seen it. [228] Now, in the reclaimed stillness of the blackout, she could look properly. [240]

She lit the brass oil lamp she kept for emergencies, readjusted its wick low and steady, and carried it down into the vault where the founding collection slept. [268] A finger of seawater had gathered on the lowest step, and her lamplight crossed it in shifting, dappled coins. [287] She knelt at the provenance shelf, laid out the flood-year volumes, and began to compare the marks one by one. [307] The official explanation, printed in every catalog, was wear: a stamp aging, striking unevenly over decades. [323] Mara had never believed it. [328] Wear fades; it does not move. [334] The break in the left border of the flood-year impressions sat a full millimeter higher than the break in the later ones, and a stamp's flaws travel with the stamp or do not travel at all. [370]

So tonight she tested the alternative hypothesis she had been building in the margins of her notes for two years: that there had been two stamps. [396] The first, an antique harbor stamp, had marked the books when they were pulled from the flood by dockworkers and schoolteachers and carried up to the library by hand. [425] The second, cut decades later, carried Renn's arms, and beneath its confident ink Mara had glimpsed, on a dozen spines, the ghost of the older mark. [451] Glimpsed, and then doubted, because the lamps were always burning and the pumps were always roaring and truth requires quiet. [471] Tonight there was quiet. [475]

She worked through the founding collection volume by volume, tilting each spine to the low flame, and the pattern emerged as cleanly as one of her own indexes. [503] Under every Renn mark, faint as a watermark, sat the antique seal: an open hand above a wave. [521] Not Renn's gift, then. [525] The people's salvage. [528]

At the back of the shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, she found the stamp itself: a walnut handle, a brass face worn smooth at the corners, the open hand and the wave cut deep and honest. [563] A note in the old head librarian's hand was tied to it: 'They made me stamp the lie; they could not make me burn the truth.' [589] Mara held it for a long moment. [596] An index is a promise, her first teacher had told her: every entry sworn true, or the whole shelf lies. [616]

Somewhere above her the drowned city waited in the dark, and somewhere in the living city the power would return and men would ask what she had done with her night. [647] She would tell them. [651] First, though, there was work to finish, and she meant to trace the honest origins of every volume in that collection before the water reached the shelf. [678] She fetched her ledger, opened it to a fresh page, and pressed the antique stamp into her ink pad for the first time in forty years. [704] It struck clean. [707]

By lamplight, with the seepwater dappling the vault's far wall in slow silver leaves, she began to record the founding collection as it truly was: not a prince's gift, but a city's, carried up from the flood by hundreds of tired hands. [749] When the lights came back near dawn, the rescue crews heard a small, unhurried voice echoing up the flooded stairwell, reading numbers aloud. [772] She was still indexing, calmly, in the reclaimed quiet, and she was not finished. [786]
