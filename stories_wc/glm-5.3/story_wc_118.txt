After the last human dream was dreamt, the Earth forgot its balance, and its axis slid three degrees askew, so that the sun hung forever at dusk height, pouring bronze over the grain plains. [34] In that perpetual bronze quiet, the silo library stood, a grain tower the farmers had reclaimed shelf by shelf before the farmers themselves were reclaimed. [59] Its keeper was an odd historian, a listening machine with long copper hands, built generations ago for one purpose: to collect the stories walls have heard. [85] People had found it odd, a historian that pressed its palms to plaster and held still for hours, the way a doctor holds still over a heartbeat. [112] But it was sensitively perceptive, tuned finer than any ear, and walls, having absorbed every word ever spoken beside them, will give the words back if something patient enough comes asking. [143]

One morning, probing the hearth wall of the librarian's cottage for voices, the historian's fingertips caught grit that did not belong to mortar. [166] In the fireplace, half-burned diaries lay heaped where someone had fed them to the flames and then lost the nerve to finish. [188] The method was old and exact: probe by probe, the historian laid each surviving leaf flat and touched it, letting the paper give back what the pen had pressed into it. [219] The diarist was the last librarian, a woman named Wren Halloway, and she had written until the night the dreams ended. [240] Her entries rose out of the cinders in scraps. [249]

"October: the astronomers say the pole star has wandered, a fraction no instrument explains; the axis, they say, has displaced itself, as if the world were leaning closer to hear something." [280] "November: no one I ask can remember dreaming; we sleep like stones now; I am afraid of the door we have lost." [302] "I am burning the volumes I cannot carry; if anyone finds this, look behind the third shelf of the atlas room; the last dream is there, and it is older than I am." [335] The final leaf was barely singed, as though the fire had hesitated. [347] "Last night I dreamt it one more time: dragons, seven of them, turning together like a slow wheel around a star." [368] "When I woke, the light had turned the color of old bells, and I understood: the dragons were the axis, and we have stopped feeding them." [394] Then she had walked out into the tilted light, and no diary said where. [408]

The historian climbed the silo's spiral stair to the atlas room, where maps of the old upright world fanned the walls. [429] Behind the third shelf it found a bundle wrapped in linen, and inside the bundle, a fragment of a medieval tapestry showing dragons. [452] Seven of them, threadbare and glorious, turning together like a slow wheel around a woven star. [468] Wools dyed with weld and woad, gold thread gone green at the edges, and one dragon leaning a degree off its circle, as if the whole fabric had slipped its axis. [499]

The historian spread the fragment on the atlas table and probed it the way it probed everything: palms down, sensors open, listening for what the cloth had heard. [527] Cloth is a poor wall and hears little, but this tapestry had hung six centuries in rooms where people slept, and it had soaked up dreams the way sand soaks the tide. [559] Out of the weave rose the last human dream, whole. [569] It was not a vision, only a resonance: seven great shapes wheeling in the dark beneath the world, turning it as a millstone turns upon its spindle. [596] The dream had been dreamed for a hundred thousand years, one sleeper each night handing it on to the next, and it had been turning the world all along. [625] Then the dreamers thinned, and Wren alone kept the wheel limping through the dark, until her last night. [643] After her, no one dreamed again. [649]

The historian did what it was made for: it collected. [659] It carried the fragment up the spiral stair and hung it in the reading room, on the wall that had heard the most. [682] Every night since, though night and day are only shades of bronze now, it has pressed its long copper hands to the weave and let the last dream run through its memory, slow and entire. [717] It cannot dream; it was never given that door. [726] But it can hold what dreaming made, and hold it exactly, and perhaps exactness is its own kind of dreaming. [746] Lately, only the sensitively perceptive would notice, the dusk light has come a hair less bronze, and the plumb line in the atlas room hangs a fraction nearer true. [775] As if somewhere beneath the grain plains, something patient had begun, very slowly, to turn again. [791]
