The leaf blower symphony had been Marlow's idea, and it killed him. [12] For eleven autumns the groundskeepers of Delacroix Street gathered in the pedestrian tunnel beneath the road, a curved echo chamber that gave every sound back twice, and Marlow conducted them from a milk crate with a rake for a baton. [52] The machines roared their strange, off-key wind, and the tunnel turned it into music. [66] On the last night, mid-crescendo, his heart stopped, and the final grain slipped through the hourglass while forty engines droned him to sleep. [89]

After the funeral, the blowers stayed chained in the tunnel, and the laughter went out of his household like a pilot light. [111] His wife, Ada, stopped humming. [116] His granddaughter, Pip, who used to dance on the milk crate, grew quiet in a way that frightened the street. [136] Nobody said any of it aloud. [142] The house filled with the unspoken instead, and unspoken things are heavier than furniture. [156]

Marlow stayed too. [159] On the third night he drifted down the storm grate into the tunnel and found he could not drift out again. [180] The echo chamber kept him the way it kept sound. [190] He did not mind, because it meant he could keep working. [201] That was the trouble with Marlow: death arrived and he treated it as an intermission. [216] He became the tunnel's keeper, tending to the unspoken the way groundskeepers tend a lawn: nightly, patiently, professionally. [234] Grief poured down the grate with the rain, and Ada's silences reached him clearer than her voice ever had. [253] In forty years they had never once said the words out loud; they had said them with tuned carburetors. [272] Now there was nobody left to tune anything. [280]

So he went to his own machine, chained where he had left it, and gripped the pull cord handle, worn smooth into the shape of his palm. [307] A dead hand should not have gripped; his did, because the handle already knew him. [322] It took him a season of nights to pull it, gathering his mournfully eager strength and hauling until the cord rose an inch farther each time. [348] On the first frost, the engine caught. [355] It coughed a pale, half-real wind up the storm drain, bending the dead grass like a conductor's downbeat. [373] Marlow began to sing into it, badly, the way he always had, and what rose from the grate were half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind. [399] This was a true haunting, done out of love, by a man who knew no other way to speak. [418]

Pip heard it first. [422] She had slept on his chest while the machines idled; she knew the tune the way other children know their names. [443] She told her grandmother the wind was singing. [451] Ada said wind does not sing. [457] Then she heard it too, heard the exact wrongness of her husband's voice in it, and stood over the grate in her nightgown, saying nothing, which for Ada was a speech. [488] Marlow pulled harder. [491] Let the wind macerate the silence, he thought, the way a blower shreds leaves into mulch: break it down, make something dead soft enough to feed whatever comes next. [520] All November he played for them, learning the other machines' pull cords by feel, one ghostly engine at a time. [540] Pip began to hum along in her sleep. [548] Then, one morning, she laughed at breakfast, a small, rusty laugh, and Ada dropped her spoon and cried into her coffee, and both sounds traveled down the drain to Marlow, who received them like applause. [583]

On the anniversary of the last symphony, Ada finally came down. [594] She stood among the chained machines and said everything the house had stored: that she had been furious at him for dying mid-note, that she had never said goodbye, that she loved him, out loud, forty years late. [632] The tunnel gave each sentence back twice, so Marlow heard it, then heard it again, the way he had always wanted to. [654] Then Ada laughed, wetly, at her own stubbornness, and the echo doubled that too. [668] Marlow raised his rake. [672] Somewhere in the dark, forty dead engines roared to life, off-key and eager, and the tunnel sang. [689] From the mouth of the tunnel came a giggle: Pip had followed her grandmother with a flashlight, and now the echo chamber held her laughter and gave it back doubled, and Ada's laugh rose to join it. [726] The lost laughter of the household came home that night, carried up the drain on an off-key wind. [744] The keeper of the echo chamber conducted until dawn, and every night after, tending what could finally be spoken. [763]
