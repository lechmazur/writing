Dawn gathered in the verdant arbor like held breath. [9] Wren Bellowsmith knelt beneath the oldest apple tree, her cart of oddments ticking softly beside her. [25] She was a tinkerer by trade and a storyteller by stubbornness, and the villagers called her whimsical the way you call a storm surprising. [49] Before her stood the songloom, a crooked contraption strung not with wool but with vine tendrils, each one thinner than a promise. [71] She had harvested them at midsummer, coaxing each tendril from the arbor's grapes and ivy with a whispered apology and a copper pin. [94] Now, at the edge of morning, she tuned them one by one, listening for the note each tendril remembered. [113]

The orchard was failing, and everyone knew why, though nobody said it aloud. [126] For three autumns, no one had sung to the trees under the harvest moon. [140] The old songs, her grandmother's songs, had been judged quaint, then embarrassing, then forgotten, and the apples had turned thin and bitter in answer. [164] A sacred practice does not die loudly, only fades like a chord held too long to hear. [181] Wren had decided, somewhere between grief and temper, that a thing forgotten could still be remedied, if you caught it when everything aligned. [204] This was that hour. [207]

Overhead, the harvest moon, round as a struck bell, was setting in the west while the sun climbed in the east. [228] For a dozen breaths the arbor hung between them, lit by both at once, silver pouring down one aisle of leaves and gold up the other. [254] Her grandmother had called it the seam of the day, the only moment a song given to the vines would take root in both worlds. [279]

Wren drew the first tendril taut and plucked it. [288] The note was small and green and absolutely true. [297] She plucked another, then another, and the forgotten song of the first frost rose out of the loom like something stretching after long sleep. [321] Her voice followed, low and unhurried, an old woman's melody in a tinkerer's rough throat. [336] The remedy was not dramatic and never had been. [345] It was a woman, a loom of vines, a handful of songs, and the right hour. [361]

As she played, she did the other thing she was known for, the thing that had gotten her laughed out of the winter fair twice: she told stories. [389] She told the vines about the year the moon came down low to listen, about the orchard keeper who bartered three winters for one song, about the girl who planted a note like a seed. [424] They were not quite true, and they were not quite false, and they came out of her golden. [442] That was her gift and her oddity: she spun golden stories, and threads of them lifted off her tongue and drifted toward the tendrils like pollen. [468] Where a thread touched a vine, the leaf beneath it shone. [479]

She heard them before she saw them: miller's boys in unlaced boots, the schoolmistress with her shawl held shut, old Tam who had sworn the songs were nonsense. [507] They came quietly, the way you enter a room where someone is sleeping, and the hush that gathered them was its own kind of music. [532] Wren did not stop playing. [537] She nodded at the empty bench across from her, where a second row of tendrils waited, strung low for unfamiliar hands. [558] That, too, she had built, on the chance that remembering is easier done together. [572] The schoolmistress sat first. [576] She plucked a tendril badly, and the note forgave her, and by the third try she was crying and playing at once. [598] The miller's boys found the rhythm of the frost song. [608] Old Tam stood apart a long while, jaw working, and then he sang the low part, the one only he still carried, in a voice like a door unused for years. [639]

The moon touched the western wall of the orchard and began to go. [652] In the last of its light, every tendril on the loom glowed, and the songs ran down through the vines into the roots, where remedies must live if they mean to last. [684] Wren felt the story she was telling change under her, from a story about the past into one about next autumn, when these same people would come back on their own. [715] That was the whole point, after all: she had not built the loom to be its only player. [733] The sun cleared the eastern hedge. [739] The moon folded away like a letter being kept. [748] The hush settled over the arbor, a different hush now, the kind that gathers people rather than empties them, and the small crowd played on, learning the old songs the way you learn anything meant to be kept: slowly, together, out loud.
