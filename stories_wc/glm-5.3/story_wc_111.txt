After the Bureau learned to measure luck, it minted luck into glass, and people carried their fortunes in marbles. [19] Cracked marbles leaked, the Bureau stamped them worthless, and their owners flung them into the gutters. [35] Old Torv, vine dresser of the Merrow estate, walked the gutters every market day and gathered what luck had abandoned. [55] He was withered himself, hands like dry canes, and the neighbors said the maze had hollowed him to match its dead hedges. [77] That withering was his lie. [82]

When the measurers came, forty years ago, their meters had read the maze as luck-negative, and luck-negative land was slated for reclamation. [104] Torv, wisely pragmatic, had not argued. [110] He showed the inspectors the outer hedges, which he had pruned to skeleton and daubed with ash water, and he filed the report himself: dying, worthless, forget it. [138] The Bureau forgot the maze, and the deception became his fence, walling the blooming heart of the garden away from a world that only counted. [163] Inside, where no one looked, everything lived. [170]

And every time something small survived, Torv held a ceremony. [180] When a graft took, or a frozen runner pushed out one green leaf, he commemorated the victory elaborately. [198] He chose a cracked marble from his collection, polished it all evening with vine oil and wool until the flaw shone like a vein of quartz, named it for the victory, and set it into the hedge at that spot. [238] Then he recited the vine's name, the date, and what had nearly killed it, because he believed that what is honored gets repeated. [261] Over forty years the maze filled with them, hundreds of flawed spheres glinting in the hedges like strange fruit. [280] What nobody knew, least of all Torv, was that luck leaks. [291] Every cracked marble he had set in the hedges bled its small remainder into the soil, drop by drop, season by season, until the ground of the maze was soaked in salvage. [323]

The auditor came in autumn, on foot, with a meter and a mandate. [336] She was young, named Ila, and she had orders to remeasure every forgotten parcel in the district. [353] The outer hedges satisfied her at first, gray and skeletal, exactly what the old files promised. [369] Then her meter began to climb. [375] It climbed as she walked, steepest along certain paths, until at the maze's heart, where apricot roses scrambled over a sundial, the needle struck the top of the dial and trembled there. [407]

She found Torv among his vines and demanded an explanation. [417] He was honest, being too old for anything else. [426] He told her about the ash water, the false report, the forty years of pretending to wither. [443] "Report the deception," he said. [448] Ila meant to, but her eyes stayed on the meter, which was doing something her manual called impossible. [466] It fell when she walked alone and rose when Torv walked beside her, as though the ground itself leaned toward the old man. [489] "Explain that," she said. [493] "Luck is only attention, miss," Torv said, "and this garden has paid me attention for fifty years. [510] It knows its gardener. [514] Measure that." [516]

Fifty years he had tended, wanting only to prove that gardens know their gardeners, and here was the proof, trembling at the top of a dial. [542] Ila stayed three days and walked every path, and the meter told one story everywhere: the luck pooled deepest where Torv's commemorations were thickest, where the polished marbles crowded the hedges. [573] He showed her his own ledger, each victory named, each marble catalogued, the graft that took in a hard March, the rose of the bitter winter, the runner that lived. [603] "Luck-negative land," she read from his old false report, and laughed, or something near laughter. [618] The lie had mended itself into truth, which is the only way lies should end. [633] She filed the maze as a conservation site, protected for its anomalous luck, and she mended the deception in the record with a clause she invented on the spot: the estate had always been under observation. [669]

Torv died the next spring, quietly, among the first roses. [679] Ila came back for the funeral and commemorated his last small victory elaborately, the only way he had taught her. [699] He had kept one perfect marble all his life, saving it for a victory big enough to deserve it, so she cracked it herself against the sundial. [726] Then she polished it all evening and set it at the heart of the maze. [741] They left the outer hedges withered on purpose, a monument to the lie that had saved the garden. [759] And every year, inside the gray walls, the luckiest garden in the district bloomed along paths that only one man had ever known. [782]
