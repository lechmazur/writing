At twilight the mist came into the atrium first, pooling under the glass roof like something that had been waiting all day for permission. [24] Mara Voss clocked in for the dead call. [32] That was the trade's name for the hour between dusk and full dark, the only window in which the newly dead could still be reached. [57] She was a contact tracer for hearts, which meant she mapped the bonds the dead left behind and delivered whatever last words clung to them. [82] Her only instrument was her grandmother's hearing aid, a decades-old analog thing with a cracked earhook. [98] Modern gear filtered the dead out entirely. [105] The old frequencies survived only in equipment nobody had bothered to recalibrate. [117] So Mara walked the colonnade each evening, tracking faint pulses, and wrote down what they said. [133] She had taken the job for one plain reason: somebody had to protect vulnerable voices, the ones that could no longer speak up for themselves. [158]

Protocol said pulses under three hertz were unassignable, to be logged and left. [171] The pulse that found her that evening was barely one. [181] It came through the hearing aid like a fingertip tapping once against a far wall. [196] Any tracer with sense would have logged it and gone home. [207] Mara had never managed sense in her life, only a persistent, low-grade curiosity, and it bent her toward the east arch. [228] The mist thickened there, and inside it stood the residue of an old woman, dim as breath on glass. [247] The pulse tapped again, slower. [252] "Edith Aldous," read the intake slate in her pocket, "died this afternoon, stroke, no final statement, household of one lodger." [272] A stroke that had taken her words two years ago had finally taken the rest of her. [289] So she had waited to die in order to be heard, and now her pulse was nearly spent. [307] "I hear you," Mara said, though speaking to the dead was against etiquette. [320] "You count." [322] The tapping steadied, as if the words were a handhold. [332]

Something slid from the mist onto the bench beneath the arch: a photograph, curled where a dead hand had gripped it. [353] On its back, in a careful hand, was written the last photograph of June. [367] The front showed a girl of maybe nine at a bus shelter, a red duffel hugged to her chest. [386] Behind her stood a church with a cracked bell, and beyond it a water tower ringed with rust. [404] June Aldous, the slate noted, had been missing for six days. [415] It had been taken the morning the lodger drove her away, at the bus stop beside his church. [433] The hush of the atrium seemed to tilt around her, and Mara let it. [447] The pulse tapped faster, and the hearing aid's crackle resolved into something almost like a whisper: Vane Street, under the choir loft, he never leaves before ten. [474] Edith had known all along, Mara understood, and had been unable to tell a soul. [489]

Her voice had been stolen before the girl vanished, and the man watched the old woman too closely for her to write or type. [513] So Edith had died on schedule and saved everything she knew for the one hour he could not supervise. [532] "I have it," Mara said, lifting the photograph so the residue could see it. [546] "I'll carry your voice from here, and it will arrive louder than it left." [560] She said it to assure her, and the mist around the arch brightened, then thinned. [575] One last pulse came through the old hearing aid, clean as a struck bell, and then the channel was only weather. [596] Mara ran the length of the atrium, heels loud in the hush, and called the night desk from the steps. [616] By full dark, officers had found the red duffel under a choir loft on Vane Street, and June with it. [636]

They credited Mara with heroism. [641] She kept trying to explain that none had occurred, that she had followed a faint pulse only because she could not bear not to, and that any tracer with the same defect of curiosity would have done the same. [680] Nobody believed her, least of all the girl, who sent one sentence to the atrium office the following week: thank you for listening when it counted. [706] Mara taped it above the intake slate and returned to the colonnade at the next twilight, when the mist filed in and the dead called, faint and patient, one pulse at a time. [739]
