When the tide pools emptied, the village obeyed its oldest precedent and began to leave. [15] The sea had drawn back from the canyon mouth, stranding the centuries-old labyrinth that the first carvers cut into the red canyon walls, and a labyrinth without water at its heart, everyone agreed, was only ruin. [51]

Only Sef, the wharf weaver, stayed. [57] She was hauntingly optimistic, the kind of woman who stitched praise into old nets and called the retreating sea a teacher. [78] "Water is leaving so we learn the shape of the ground," she told the empty harbor, and she kept weaving. [98]

Her one treasure was a storybook illustration plate, a copper engraving her grandmother had saved from the drowned press. [117] It showed the labyrinth as it once was: pilgrims descending the red corridors, tide pools like scattered coins, the whole design glowing with purpose. [141] At night Sef inked the plate with lampblack and printed the image on cloth until her small room floated with the old reverence. [164]

Then the sleepwalking began. [168] The few families still packing their carts rose in the dark and walked, eyes open and vacant, into the canyon. [188] Precedent said never wake a sleepwalker, never follow one, and so the parents who woke at dawn in their own beds said nothing, and called it grief. [215]

Sef broke the precedent. [219] She followed. [221]

Night after night she trailed the sleepers with hooded lanterns, mapping their turns on linen with tailor's chalk. [239] Their paths overlapped - the fisherman's son crossing the ferryman's daughter, the old midwife doubling back through them both - and where the paths crossed, the ancient corridor design repeated itself, corrected itself, became legible again. [273] The old route had needed the tide, she realized. [282] The sleepers were sewing a new one. [289]

So she sewed. [292] She unpicked her nets, all that leftover purpose, and reeled the thread through iron needles. [307] Following the overlapping sleepwalking paths, she stitched their lines into the dust seams of the canyon floor, knotting each crossing so the pattern would hold. [332] Where a path doubled, she doubled her thread. [340] Where three sleepers converged, she braided. [346] The work took a season, and her hands bled, and she composed verses about it as she worked, because that was her nature: poems about salt leaving stone, about grief as a loom. [379] At every seventh knot she sewed a printed cloth from the illustration plate, so the sleepers' route was marked with the image of the labyrinth's first purpose: ruin and reverence on the same red wall, joined by thread. [417]

Word spread, as words do, even among people leaving. [426] One dawn the ferryman's daughter woke with red dust on her feet and followed the stitched line down into the canyon, and found what Sef had found at the center. [456] The emptied tide pools had become basins of brine-warmed silt, and in them, impossibly, orchids had taken root - pale, salt-tolerant, blooming out of what the sea had abandoned. [484] The heart of the labyrinth, which the carvers had meant to hold water, now held flowers, and the sleepwalkers' overlapping paths ringed it like a stitched garland. [511] It was solace of the strangest kind, orchid-pale and unearned, and no one could look at it and keep calling the place ruined. [534]

The village stopped packing. [538] They walked the sewn route by daylight, tracing Sef's knots, reading the printed cloths like pages of a storybook. [557] Where the old precedent said reverence required the sea, they made a new one: reverence requires only the walking. [576] Children copied the illustration plate onto slate. [583] The midwife, who had sleepwalked farthest, began leading a slow procession at dusk, and the labyrinth, ruined and blooming, filled again with purpose. [606]

Sef remained at the wharf, weaving. [612] When travelers asked how a path had been carved from ruin to reverence without a single chisel stroke, she showed them the worn copper plate, then the thread-scarred canyon, then her bandaged hands. [645]

"The sea wrote the first story," she said. [653] "The sleepers stitched its sequel. [658] I only set the type." [663]

Some precedents, she believed, exist to be broken the way a pod breaks - not destroyed, but opened, so the seed inside can be carried somewhere new. [689]
