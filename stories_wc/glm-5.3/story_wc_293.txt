Maren was a bridge-song fiddler, which is how she came to be at the mountain scenic pullout above the Kessler gorge with an empty passenger seat and a stranger's glass in her hand. [33] The hitchhiker had been standing at the rail when she stopped, hat low, thumbs nowhere, looking down at the old iron bridge the way relatives look at hospital beds. [62] He had asked, politely, if she was really the fiddler who kept a tune for every crossing. [79] She was. [81] In the car he hummed a work song she had never heard, a rhythm like hammers passed hand to hand, and then, somewhere between two mile markers, between one breath and the next, he was simply gone, the seatbelt still swinging, the heater blowing warm at no one. [129]

On the seat he had left a flake of glass no bigger than a thumbnail, and she knew it at once, because her grandfather's trunk held a broken plate just like it: a wet-plate collodion glass flake, silver image floated in varnish, sharp as frost. [174] Against the morning sun it showed one gray eye, one suspender strap, half a jaw, a man forever about to speak. [195] She recognized the jaw. [199]

Down at the gorge floor, the old trestle was being restored, its whole broken flank zippered into scaffolding wrapped in printed mesh: a blowup of the 1891 crew photograph, forty men enlarged until their faces dissolved into dots. [237] For a hundred and thirty years the town had repeated the company line, that the crew skimped rivets and drank the payroll, that when the center span dropped it was justice of a kind. [271] Now the county had scanned the plate at low resolution for its centennial apology page, and the pixelated amnesty stretched in banner letters across the mesh: WE WERE WRONG ABOUT THESE MEN. [303] But an apology issued in pixels is a soft thing, and it had left one man out entirely: far left, where the plate had shattered long ago, the archive showed only a blur and the caption UNKNOWN WORKER. [341]

Maren was dynamically observant, a fiddler whose eyes caught the small motions other people averaged away, and now they caught the wrap breathing. [364] Wind moved the printed mesh in long waves, and each wave re-sorted the dots, so that for half a second at a time the pixelated faces drifted into new constellations. [394] She watched in luminous disbelief as the flutter mapped a secret: the dots aligned, again and again, along one line, and the line ran from the missing corner of the crew photograph up the gorge wall to a shelf of stone at the old cable anchorage. [440] It was absurd and obvious at once, a secret mapped by scaffold wrap that flutters, wind for ink, light for paper. [461] Her grandfather, a riveter's son, used to say nobody would ever sing for the men who fell, and she had built her whole strange career sculpting possibility from the ashes of that never, one bridge song at a time. [500] So she climbed. [503]

Above the pullout the trail followed the rim, aspens hammering gold light against the sky, and at the anchorage shelf she found a loose capstone, and under it a tin box, and inside the box a paymaster's ledger wrapped in oilcloth. [544] The ledger, in a cramped clerk's hand, held the truth the company had burned: iron ordered, iron delivered, the difference pocketed, sixteen tons of it, the reason the span dropped. [574] Under the last entry, a different hand, unhurried: IF I GO IN THE RIVER, THE IRON GOES TO JAIL. [593] On the back of the glass flake, scratched with a nail point, faint but legible: E. FERRO, FAR LEFT. [612] Maren sat with flake and ledger in her lap, laughing and crying at once, because none of it could be happening and it was. [636]

Two weeks later she stood at the county hearing and recounted it all: the vanished rider, the humming, the flake, the fluttering wrap, the ledger, every number read aloud twice. [666] The handwriting was verified, the resolution passed, and the archive page was quietly edited: UNKNOWN WORKER became Elias Ferro, 1866 to 1891, cleared of everything, his face restored from her flake, the amnesty finally resolving out of its pixels into a man. [708]

That evening she stood at the rail of the pullout where he had waited, and played the work song he had hummed, hammers passed hand to hand, down into the dusk. [739] Far below, the scaffold wrap breathed in the last wind, forty pixelated faces rippling, and for half a second she could have sworn they bowed. [764]
