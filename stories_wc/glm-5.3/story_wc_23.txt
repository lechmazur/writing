The hydraulics failed in October, the same week the sea took my sister's boat and did not give it back. [20] After that, the great lamp at the signal station froze mid-sweep, pointing at one fixed patch of dark water like an accusation. [42] People call me the patchwork navigator because I guide boats through the reef channels using charts stitched from my grandmother's quilt scraps and my father's torn survey maps. [70] Lately the patches I navigate are all interior: the shelf where her coffee mug sits, the channel of her empty chair, the shoals of the hours between two and four. [100] Grief, it turns out, is a geography, and I mean to cross it with instruments. [115]

The first midnight call came three weeks after the failure, on the station's dead landline, a number the telephone company swears has been disconnected for a decade. [142] Static, then a voice like wind through a porthole. [151] "Count the rivets on the west stair," it said. [160] I counted forty, then hung up and shook for an hour. [171] The calls came again, midnight after midnight, cryptic as tide tables. [182] "Bring the bronze," the voice said on the fourth night. [192] I knew exactly what it meant, which frightened me most of all. [204] My sister kept a patina bronze censer foot on her boat, a doorstop salvaged from a drowned chapel, green as shallow water. [226] A trawler dragged it up in her wreck's net-lines a week after the search ended, and it was all anyone recovered, and I had not touched it since. [254] That night I carried it up the tower stairs and set it under the starlit dome of the lamp room, and the patina began to change. [280] Waxing, the verdigris paled to silver; waning, it deepened toward black. [291] The bronze foot was a lunar dial, its surface fluctuating with the moon phases, and the midnight voice knew it. [311]

"Build," it said. [314] So I built. [317] With no hydraulics, the lamp needed another way to sweep, and the voice, call by call, gave me the geometry in riddles I had to earn. [343] "Twelve turns of rope to one of iron," it said one night. [355] "Hang the foot above the lens by the full of the moon, and weight it with everything you have not said." [376] I hung the censer foot as the driving weight on a rope wound around the lens axle, so the bronze sank slowly through the dark and turned the beam as it fell, and I rewound it every hour with aching arms. [417] Full moon, the patina silver, and the light stretched all the way to the Gullet where her boat went down. [437] New moon, the bronze gone dark, and the beam barely cleared the harbor mouth. [451]

That autumn the terns had done a strange thing: when the station light froze, the flocks turned around mid-ocean and flew back north, out of season, chasing a wrong star. [481] The birders called it reverse migration and blamed the dead beacon. [492] I called it proof that lost things refuse to stay where the world files them. [507] On the night of the December full moon, the phone rang its dead ring, and the voice was not cryptic at all. [529] "Sweep the Gullet," it said, and beneath the static I heard my sister's laugh, the way you hear a harbor moving under ice. [552] I wound the rope and let the bronze fall, and the beam walked out across the black water, and the terns rose out of the dark from the wrong direction, hundreds of them, silvered, flying home south at last with the light beneath them like a road. [599] One bird broke off, circled the tower three times, close enough that I saw its eye, then followed the light out over the Gullet and did not come back. [628] That was the last call. [633] The line has been silent since, though I still lift the receiver at midnight, the way you try a door you know is locked. [657]

The censer foot still swings above the lens, silvering and darkening with the moon, and I chart its moods in the margins of my patchwork maps, because the geography of loss is not a place you cross once. [695] You navigate it phase by phase, night by night, and some nights the light reaches all the way to the far shore, and some nights it barely clears the rocks. [725] Either way, I keep the lamp moving. [732] That is the whole solace I have to offer, and it is enough: something built from wreckage, swung by moonlight, showing the lost a way home, or at least a way through. [764]
