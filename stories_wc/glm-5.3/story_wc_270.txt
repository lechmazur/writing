During the reconstruction, when the volcanic hot springs above Emberfall finally stopped smelling of ash, Grandmother Oma climbed the steam-veiled terraces with her grandson Pim, back to the gardens where her family had grown pepper vines for nine generations. [39] The elders waited at the temple pool with the Amber Womb: a fist-sized lump of hardened resin holding the embryo of the first pepper tree, preserved, tradition said, with the family's destiny already implanted inside it. [75]

The village still called her the insurgent grandmother, because during the uprising she had run codes for the rebellion, carrying ciphers woven into her gray braid and a pistol she had finally buried on armistice day. [111] She was also, though she rarely admitted it, accidentally telepathic with plants. [123] It had happened in the third winter of the war, when a shell collapsed the greenhouse where she was hiding and the sap of a dying pepper vine seeped into her open wounds. [156] Ever since, the green world murmured to her: vines complaining of cold, moss gossiping about the mineral content of the springs, trees holding long grudges against lightning. [183]

The ruined terraces recognized her before she reached them, whispering that the ground stayed warm, that they had waited. [202] The elders began the old argument at once. [210] "The Amber Womb must be unsealed," said the eldest, "so the destiny our ancestors implanted can be planted again; the reconstruction cannot truly begin until the first tree returns to the soil." [242] Oma lifted the amber and felt the embryo inside, a faint presence pressed thin as paper, a destiny with no room to breathe. [265] "A destiny implanted without asking is just occupation by another name," she said, and she would know, having spent seven years fighting the occupation. [289] But she could not simply refuse, because the ritual was the last thing holding the village together, and because the unsealing verse had been lost when the shells pulverized the temple wall where it was carved. [325] The elders demanded that the famous code-breaker find it again. [335]

That night, walking the terraces, she found her son's kite tangled in the upper vines where the war had left it, abandoned for a decade, its paper ribs split, its tail of prayer ribbons bleached gray. [371] Her son had been a boy who copied temple inscriptions for fun. [383] He had grown into a courier and died at twenty-two, but the vines had kept his kite the way the living keep the things of the dead, gently and without meaning to. [415] She pulled it down with shaking hands and read, by lantern light, the cryptic script inked along the ribbons of its tail. [437] He had copied the unsealing verse, all of it, playing at holiness the way other boys played at war. [456] Except the old scribes had written the verse as a substitution cipher disguised as liturgy, and a boy's hasty copying had scrambled it further. [480] Oma worked through the night while the vines leaned close to murmur encouragement, and by dawn she had decoded it. [500] The verse was not a command at all. [508] Translated, it asked: what will you become, if no one tells you? [520]

She carried the kite and the Amber Womb to the hottest pool, where steam rose like the mountain's slow breath, and spoke the verse aloud with her son's ribbon wrapped around her wrist. [553] The amber softened in the mineral water, turned clear as glass, and the embryo slipped into her palm: a small pale seed, humming, awake. [577] She heard it the way she heard all green things, a thin new voice saying, "I was so long in the dark," and then, uncertain, "what am I supposed to be?" [608] "Whatever you intend to become," Oma told it, "and nothing else." [619] She planted the seed at the pool's edge, in soil the volcano kept warm, and pressed Pim's hands into the earth beside it, because tradition, she had decided, should evolve with intent, the way a living thing does. [657]

The elders protested that the rite had been performed wrongly, and Oma, who had once helped topple a government, told them gently that rituals, like governments, survive only when they learn to listen. [690] By spring the seed had become a sapling, and this new tree, planted by consent instead of decree, talked freely to anyone who stood near the springs with hands in the soil and mouths politely shut. [726] Pim asked it what he was meant to become, and the sapling, young and honest, answered that it did not know, that he would have to tell it. [754] Its first fruit tasted of pepper and of something no one could name, because no one had named it yet. [774]
