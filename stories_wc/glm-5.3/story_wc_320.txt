Marisol Vane climbed the third bridge at moonrise, when the perigee moon pulled the gorge wider by a hand's width and made the cables sing. [25] Above her, Antenna Row hung like a chandelier nobody had switched off: an abandoned city of radio towers suspended over the gorge, chained to the cliff, humming dead frequencies. [54] She was the last celestial signal interpreter licensed in the city, which meant she was also the first, since licensing had ended with everyone else. [79] Perigee was the working night. [84] When the moon came close, tides of static rose with it, and drowned signals surfaced briefly, like wrecks at low water. [105] Marisol climbed because the interpreters' rule was old and practical: never trust a reading taken through a roof. [123]

In her satchel she carried the tools of her trade: solder, felt, a logbook, and two objects no academy would certify. [144] The first was a shard of hush, harvested from the air the instant the city's final broadcast ended. [162] Hush is what a signal leaves behind, and it keeps well if you store it in felt. [179] Hers was five years old and faintly warm, serenely electric, the way still water is before lightning finds it. [198] The second object was a fragment of a Hollywood star, broken from that famous sidewalk on the far side of the world, pink terrazzo with a brass point like a claw. [229] Her mother had carried it inland when the coasts went quiet, saying every star on that walk was a beginning someone had poured into concrete before anyone knew how it would end. [261] Marisol had spent her whole career testing that claim, and tonight, on the anniversary of the Last Broadcast, she intended to prove it. [284] The Last Broadcast had been her mother's. [291] It cut off mid-word when the last live tower failed, and the whole city heard the hush close over it like a lid. [314] Since then Marisol had worked one problem: every signal is an ending that arrives late, so somewhere inside it, running backward, there must be a beginning. [340]

She reached the master tower's tuning deck, unrolled her felt, and set out the shard and the star fragment side by side. [362] The great antenna overhead was tuned, as all antennas are, by weight: not lead, but assumptions. [378] Every belief the old engineers held had been cast into slats and hung in the core, and the sum of them bent the tower's ear toward what they expected to hear. [409] Expectation is ballast; that was the second rule, and the reason the abandoned towers still heard only their own echoes. [429] To tune deep, you did not add; you subtracted. [438] Marisol warmed the core housing with a torch, because slats come out easier warm, and began to tune the tower by discarding assumptions, one by one. [464] "That sound is noise," she read, and dropped it into the gorge; the static thinned by half. [481] "Dead stars are silent," she read, and let it fall; a chorus of old light rose underneath the static like heat off stone. [504] "The city is abandoned," said the next slat, and when it fell she heard the towers breathing on their cables, waiting for something to answer. [529]

She kept going until one slat remained, and that one was not the engineers'; she had cast it herself, five years ago, from the one belief she could not work without. [560] It read: "What ends is gone." [566] The moon stood at its nearest, the static had gone transparent, and Marisol held her own assumption over the gorge for a long time. [590] Interpreters do not believe; they weigh. [596] She let it go. [600]

The antenna's hum dropped through registers until it passed out of hearing, and the whole row of towers lit at once, soft as windows in a street where everyone has just come home. [633] Marisol set the brass point of the Hollywood star into the shard of hush, the way you seat a needle, and turned it gently against the spin of everything. [662] The hush opened. [665] Inside the ending, exactly where the word had broken, she heard the breath her mother drew before the first word of that final broadcast, and beneath that, faint and enormous, the first syllable of the first light, still arriving. [704] It was not a message. [709] It was a beginning, and it had been there the whole time, wrapped in the hush like a seed in its shell. [731]

Marisol laughed once, took out her logbook, and opened a fresh page. [743] At the top she wrote the date, the perigee, the coordinates, and then, in the interpreter's plain hand: Endings keep. [763] Then she oiled the hinges of the transmitter room, because tomorrow the city would need its doors, and every door, properly tuned, opens on the beginning. [789]
