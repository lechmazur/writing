Mara went down the stair with a burned tarot card cupped in her palms, the way other girls carried candle flames through wind. [23] Half-lit corridors glowed ahead of her, mosslight clinging to the brick, and in that glow the orchard opened: apple and pear trees rising out of the sleeping dead, their roots snaking between ribs and finger bones like patient silver eels. [63] She had lifted the card from Vane's counting room at dusk; now she meant to give it back, and to take back everything else. [87]

Below the city, destinies were fruit. [93] Each soul arrived in the catacombs with its card, painted at birth, and each card could be bartered away by the living who envied it or the dead who no longer needed it. [126] Mara's mother had traded hers for bread during the famine years, and died with a hole where her future should have been, an emptiness that whistled when the wind came down the corridors. [159] Mara had heard that whistling all her childhood. [167] So she had become a mender, one of the few who could patch holes in the soul, and she had been reckless about it, giving away pieces of her own thread until her card was mostly ash. [204] Everyone called her an idealist; they meant it as a warning. [215]

Tonight's card was not hers. [220] It was the Sun, scorched at every corner, the destiny of a stillborn girl named Pell, traded by a grieving father to the orchard's keeper before the child's bones were even warm. [252] He had wanted a better future and sold the one thing he could not use. [267] The keeper, Vane, hoarded such cards in the oldest vault, destinies strung like lanterns, and priced them in years, in memories, in the small unremarked goodness of a life. [296]

Mara's method had always been inlay. [302] Others stitched souls with plain thread and watched the patch hold for a season. [316] She inlaid meaning everywhere: silver words set into bone, the names of kindnesses beaten into fine wire and laid in grooves she carved, so that a mended soul could not help but know what it was for. [353] Meaning, she had learned, outlasts thread. [359]

In the vault corridor, Vane was waiting for her; keepers always know. [371] "You have eleven lines left on your own card, mender," he said. [383] "Barter those away and there will be nothing of you left to bury, let alone mend." [399] "Then I will spend them well," Mara said. [407]

She knelt at the vault's threshold and began the work she had planned for years. [422] She cut grooves into the burned Sun with her awl, curved lines to catch the mosslight, and inlaid them with silver wire, writing into the card every meaning a stolen life could still hold: dawn, warmth, a door left open. [462] Then she pressed the card to the earth and asked the roots for help. [476] Roots were the orchard's trammels, nets that held the dead in place, but Mara had learned they could be asked to trammel other things. [500] "Hold him," she whispered, and the roots rose from the bone-woven earth like slow lightning and trammeled Vane's ankles, his wrists, patient as gravity. [524] He cursed her in the language of ledgers. [532]

She took the string of stolen destinies down one lantern at a time, and for each burned card she performed the same surgery, grooves and wire, meaning inlaid over every scar of fire, until the vault was full of small bright mornings. [574] Then she went among the dead and gave the cards back, sliding each one beneath the right collarbone, her hands fiercely gentle, tucking the Sun against little Pell's ribs. [603] Souls with patched holes do not rise, not exactly; they settle, the way a house settles when the foundation is finally sound. [625] But that night the whole orchard sighed, every tree leaning toward its sleeper, and the wind in the half-lit corridors stopped whistling. [647]

Vane, still netted, watched her with something like awe. [656] "What will you take for my release?" he asked. [665] "Your ledgers," she said. [669] "Every debt, every barter, unwritten." [674] He wept then, because a keeper without ledgers is only a man in a hole in the ground, but he agreed, and the roots let him go. [701]

Mara climbed back toward morning as the mosslight dimmed, her own card a lace of ash in her pocket, eleven lines left, maybe fewer. [725] She was not afraid, or not only afraid. [733] She had spent a life learning that a soul is not its thread count but its pattern, and she had inlaid her pattern everywhere she could reach, in silver, in bone, in the settled sleep of a thousand mended dead. [773] Whatever came for her, it would find her full of meaning, and it would have to read every line. [792]
