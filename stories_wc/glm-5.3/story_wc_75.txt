Nine winters had passed since the beekeeper's last visit, and in all that time the monumental falls hung frozen over the mouth of the grotto city, a white wall taller than the cathedral they had watched burn. [37] Anka rose before the bells to bake. [44] In her family, one baked bread shaped like initials: letters of the dead, dusted with flour, sweetened with rosewater, so that names could be held in the hands and eaten warm. [75] This morning she shaped a B for Bohumil, the beekeeper, and set it aside, uneaten. [90]

Her oven was the museum's old brazier; the museum was the city's chapel; and the city itself was made of displaced persons who had fled up the river carrying whatever could be carried: looms, sheet music, grandmothers. [127] Anka gave the morning tour at nine, as she had for nine years, in the low, courteous voice of a woman once trained to lecture in marble halls. [155] Before the war she had been a docent in the capital; now she was the sole guide of the last museum of a murdered country. [180] "Everything here has a date," she told the children, "because a people without dates is a people without a spine." [200] She pointed out the lacemaker's pillows, the shadow puppets with singed hands, the hymnal with its bullet holes, giving each a year the way a doctor gives a pulse. [229] Her true work was the rescue of dying arts: she copied recipes for dye, coaxed songs out of the old ones, taught children their letters in dough before ink. [258] What could not be saved was kept behind glass. [267] In the center case lay a single emerald-tinted opera glass lens, cracked at one edge. [282] Her grandmother had sung second soprano at the National Opera until the night the chandelier fell; the lens, she said, had been tinted green so singers could watch the royal box without being blinded by its crystal. [319] Through it, Anka liked to tell the children, the whole world looked as if seen from underwater, which was exactly how a refugee sees the world. [345]

At noon a boy came running down the salt galleries with water on his sleeves. [360] "It's weeping," he said. [364] The falls had begun to thaw. [370] All afternoon the citizens stood in the spray-lit dark, listening to the ice give up its hold in long, tearing sounds, like cloth, like hair, like a language returning to a tongue gone dry. [404] A thaw meant the beekeeper might come again, with seed cakes and news and queens in little wooden boxes. [423] A thaw also meant that whatever had driven them up the river could follow it down. [439] The elders still spoke of the smoke rising off their old cities, a second, dirtier waterfall. [455] No one said the word soldiers, but it moved among them anyway, the way the smell of rosewater moved through the museum from Anka's cooling bread, sweet and wrong at once. [486]

That night she could not sleep. [492] She took the lens from its case, an act somewhere between theft and prayer, and walked to the ice. [511] Green glass against green ice: through her grandmother's lens the falls became a curtain of leaves, of bottle glass, of the theater's domed ceiling on the last beautiful night before the chandelier fell. [544] Water ran down the melting face in threads, and far off, past the green, she thought she saw a figure on the high path, small as a bee, patient as one. [575] She lowered the lens. [579] Whoever it was would arrive by morning, or would not. [589] Either way, the city would have to decide what it had become: a vault, or a garden. [606]

Anka went back to her oven while the falls wept above the streets. [619] She worked the dough until it forgave her, folded rosewater into it, and began shaping letters: an A for herself, an M for the museum, and then, one by one, the initials of every dying art she still meant to save. [660] There were so many. [664] By dawn the chapel smelled of bread and flowers, and the first loaf she drew out was the letter B, risen high and golden, still too hot to hold, which she held anyway. [697] Outside, the falls opened thread by thread, and green morning light came through the water like a stage coming up on an empty house that might, just might, fill again. [727]
