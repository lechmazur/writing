The daydream folded around her at the ticket window, between the second and third stamp of the clerk's purple ink. [20] Marisol had once been the most vivid mural artist in the low cities, a woman who could make brick walls bloom, and now she waited for a train she did not care about, her joy gone missing like a cat that leaves for days and returns acting innocent. [68]

In the fold of the daydream, the train took a wrong line and let her off somewhere the maps surrendered to blankness: the orchard district, ruined and lost under three centuries of fog. [101] The fog was not gray but pearled, patient as a debt collector, and it pressed against the dead apple trunks like a rumor refusing to clear. [127] She walked because dreamers walk, and her boot struck something small and stubborn in the mulch: a fresco fragment, a chip of plaster no bigger than a hymnbook, bearing half a painted apple blossom and one laughing mouth. [165] The mouth was mid-laugh, as though the painter had caught the exact second someone remembered they were happy. [183] Marisol felt something in her chest unclench, an old homesickness for a place she had never seen. [200] Here, she realized, was a museum the fog had been hiding: whole walls painted for blossom festivals, all of it swallowed. [221]

She was not the first pilgrim; outside the fog ran a knowledge network, a scattered guild of archivists and retired lanternists who traded letters about fog timetables, plaster chemistry, and the names of trees no one alive had tasted. [260] Their letters found her the way letters find people in daydreams, arriving on bats or in the pockets of crows. [280] From them she learned that the orchard folk had painted with light: they ground glow-lime into plaster and, on festival nights, held lenses to the fog so the murals burned faintly through the mist, a town broadcasting its own happiness. [320] Marisol had no glow-lime, but she had her trade's one trick left: she still painted with light, the slow way, propping open the shutter of an old bellows camera and moving through the frame with lamps and colored gauze while the film drank the scene whole. [366]

For one dream-day she worked: she traced the vanished avenues in amber, hung blossom-glyphs of green and pink above the dead branches, and sang the district's murals back into being on the negative's dark canvas. [401] She laughed at herself once, hoarse and delighted, when a crow perched on the camera and had to be painted around. [422] When the frame was done she closed the shutter like ending a prayer, and the exposed film held what fog had hidden: an orchard in impossible bloom, the laughing mouth from the fresco fragment recreated forty feet tall and mid-giggle. [462]

The archivists had one standing rule, written at the bottom of every letter: light hoarded goes out, so whatever the fog gives up must be displayed. [488] So Marisol climbed the ridge at the fog's hem, strung a bedsheet between two dead pear trees, and displayed the film with a projector she had daydreamed into her satchel, because daydreams are generous that way. [524] The image spilled past the sheet and onto the fog itself, and the mist, which had stood in as scenery for centuries, took the light meekly, the way snow takes footprints. [555] For a while the whole drowned district glowed with blossom and laughing mouths, and somewhere in the pearly nothing, Marisol felt joy sidle back to her, apologetic, ears back, ready to stay. [587]

Then the loudspeaker cleared its throat, and the fold of the daydream opened like a hand. [603] Platform three, said the station; her train, said the clock; nothing, said her portfolio of half-finished murals back home. [622] But her hands were warm as if they had carried lamps, and her pocket held a small chalky weight she did not remember picking up. [647] She drew it out: a chip of plaster, no bigger than a hymnbook, half an apple blossom, one laughing mouth. [667]

The clerk stamped her ticket and looked twice at her grin. [678] Madam, he said, you look like a woman who has somewhere wonderful to be. [692] Marisol stepped onto the train, the fragment laughing softly against her palm, and began, already, to plan the display. [711] Some joy, she had learned, is not lost, only fogged; it waits, mid-laugh, for someone patient enough to hold the shutter open. [733]
