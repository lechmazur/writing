The state killed me with a rubber stamp seven years ago, misspelling my name on a death certificate meant for a drowned sailor. [23] Since no office would resurrect a dead woman, I stopped being her and moved to the port city, where shipping containers stack into a towering labyrinth, eleven tiers of rust. [53] The dead keep odd hours; I measure mine in tides. [63] At high water the harbor drowns the mudflats and the cranes idle; at low water the flats reappear, silver and pocked, offering up whatever the city has thrown away. [92] Twice a day, that rhythm; twice a day I walk out among the wrecked things and read them. [110]

Being erased changes how you see. [116] Where dockers read a container's stencil code, I read the ghost beneath it: the fishing village the terminal buried, the shanty lanes, the corner shop whose owner vanished when the bulldozers came. [148] Official reality sits on top like fresh paint; I filter for what bleeds through from underneath. [164] My method is simple: I collect fragments of overheard stories. [174] I crouch behind container walls at shift change and take in what the crews let fall: a bosun's half-sentence about a fever ship, a clerk muttering that the archive fire was no accident, an old stevedore warning a boy which gate not to guard after dark. [220] Each fragment is a torn ticket from a history the port has pulped. [233] In my container, between two walls that never see sun, I layer them: dock tickets, scrap paper, a singed chit from the burned records office, each sheet pinned over the last until the wall becomes sediment. [269] The deepest layer is always truest. [275]

A chaplain asked me once, before he learned to avoid the dead, why I bothered. [290] I told him the truth: I wanted to discover the texture of memories, the grain of them, whether a remembered afternoon has weight the way wet rope has weight. [319] Facts are flat; memory has nap, like corduroy, like rust. [329] A name on a certificate is a fact, which is why mine could be erased so easily. [346]

Then came the spring tide that pulled the flats back farther than I had ever seen. [362] Out past the old pilings, in black mud, my boot struck glass. [374] A bottle, whole, its paper label softened but legible: a skull, a company name half gone, and the word POISON in a careful serif. [398] The name matched one I had heard through steel walls two winters back, on a night when old men drank tea against the cold. [422] One had said the chemical works gifted bottles of pale syrup to the shanty town before the eviction, that the fever which followed was convenient, that the list of the dead went missing with the archives. [458] The others shushed him, and the wind took the rest. [468] At the time, it was one layer among many. [477] Now it had an anchor. [482]

For a month I worked the rhythm differently: eavesdropping at the canteen window, riding the ferry queue, sitting where retired dockers play dominoes under a tarp. [508] A woman remembered her aunt's cough; a crane driver remembered being paid extra to load drums at night; a boy remembered his grandmother forbidding him to dig near the pilings. [538] None of it was testimony; all of it was texture. [548] Each fragment I pinned made the erased town feel more like a place and less like a rumor. [566]

On the night of the next spring tide, I peeled the label from the bottle in one careful piece and carried it home dry. [590] I pinned it at the center of my wall, then layered the fragments around it until the shanty town stood up in paper: coughs and drums and forbidden mud. [619] Somewhere under the stacks, that town still has texture; the port paved it, but could not sand it smooth. [638] Standing back, I understood my own file at last. [647] My death certificate is only a label pasted on the wrong bottle; peel it away and the person underneath is still whole, still walking, still listening. [673] The tide turns; water climbs the pilings; a watchman calls the hour to men who never ask my name. [692] I have stopped needing one. [697] Namelessness is only the first layer, and over it, fragment by fragment, tide by tide, I lay the histories of others until what was erased can be felt again under the thumb, rough and real. [732]
