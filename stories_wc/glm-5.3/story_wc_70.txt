The blackout entered its third night, and the lightning collection tower stood dark for the first time in living memory, a black needle against a sky gone sweet with stars. [30] Wren kept the toll gate at the tower's foot, as she had for twenty years, waving through the same faces at the same hours, charging the same six coins. [59] She had been honest by force since a stray arc leapt the fence and burned the crooked out of her tongue; now truth dripped out of her whether or not she offered it. [92] Travelers detoured miles to ask her one question and receive one unsoftened answer. [105]

The tower drank storms through its copper anthers, and a hive of work-drones carried the charge down to the cells like nectar, gold-bodied, tireless, humming. [130] Then the drones failed, all at once, the way hives fail: quietly, and then completely. [145] The cells drained, the city went dark, and the council needed someone to blame. [159] On the third dusk, Ilsa the hive-mistress came down the tower road with soot on her wrists and no explanation in her hands. [182] "Tell me what passed this gate before the failure," she said, "because everyone else's story changes when the council is listening." [203]

No one else had dared ask her, and the truth left Wren now before she chose to give it, warm and unsweetened. [225] "Asa," she said, and could not stop. [232] "Asa kept the hive for forty years; Asa told the council the new white floodlights would blind the drones to the amber dusk-lamps they steered home by; Asa was retired within the week, thanked for his service, escorted past this gate on a morning that smelled of cut hay." [281] "He carried out one lacquer box, black and smooth as a beetle's back, and left it on my counter." [300] "Keep the wonder in the toll," he told me, and I have tried. [313] She had kept his tradition ever since: any traveler could attempt the riddle for a free crossing, and none had solved it, and the trying made the routine sweet. [342]

"Show me the box," Ilsa said. [348] Inside the lacquer box, nested in velvet gone soft with age, lay a single matchbox. [363] Wren slid it open: eleven matches and one blank slip of paper. [375] The riddle appeared only under flame; Asa had written it in juice that heat woke. [390] Wren struck a match, its small sun trembling, and held it beneath the slip until words rose brown as honey. [410] The riddle read: "I am the hour the hive spends and cannot save; burn me white and the workers lose their door; I fall gold every evening and am never missed until I am gone; what am I?" [448] "I never solved it," Wren admitted, which was true, and therefore easy. [460]

Ilsa read it twice, and her face did what faces do at sunrise. [473] "Dusk," she whispered, "the drones steer by amber, and we lit their sky white and stole their dusk." [491] "They are not dead; they are scattered in the fields, circling, waiting for a signal they can trust." [509] "Then we give them dusk by hand," Wren said, and the truth of it rang like a struck coin. [528]

They climbed together into the nectar dusk, Ilsa with the matchbox, Wren with a lantern of amber glass. [546] Along the tower's great rail they planted matches, one to a bracket, and lit them one by one, ten small amber doors in the failing light. [572] The first drone came out of the west like a struck spark, then fifty, then hundreds, gold bodies heavy with storm-charge, humming home along the ladder of little flames. [601] They poured their nectar into the cells, and far below, street by street, the city lit like a jar of fireflies filling. [623] Ilsa swore the white floods would sleep through every dusk from then on, and the council, facing the one witness who could not soften the truth, agreed. [650]

At dawn Wren returned to her gate and the six coins and the same faces, but the lacquer box stayed open on the counter from then on. [677] Every traveler got a match and one attempt at the riddle, and the toll, for all its sameness, never tasted routine again; it tasted of matches, of amber, of the evening the city learned what the hive had always known: wonder is not the opposite of routine, but the nectar hidden inside it. [730]
