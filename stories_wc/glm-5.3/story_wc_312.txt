They caught the last fish on a Tuesday, and the world has been quietly wrong ever since. [17] The sea gave everything she had, and the sky, which had never learned to say thank you, kept her water anyway. [38] That is the cosmic imbalance, as the old tide-keepers explained it: a debt the whole world owes and no one intends to pay. [61] The ship's bell broke in the same instant the last fish came over the rail, cracked clean through like a held breath finally let go. [86] Nobody thought that mattered, except me. [92] My name is Fenn, and the Academy rated my fire lukewarm at best, fit for tea and not much else. [112] I believed them, mostly, the way you believe weather. [121] But I had also, naively, earnestly, kept my grandmother's ledgers, and the ledgers said the bell had a job. [140]

Every dawn, the sapphire viaduct across the harbor mouth caught the first light and wept it back into the sea, drop by patient drop. [164] Forty-one drops a minute, said the ledgers; a pint an hour; a tide every eleven years. [180] The bell rang once for every hundred drops, keeping the count, and the sea, hearing herself remembered, agreed to keep existing. [201] With the bell broken, the count stopped, and the sea began, quite sensibly, to leave. [216] The fishermen called it bad luck and moved inland, because devotion, it turns out, is a brittle metal: it holds everything until the exact moment it holds nothing. [244] Mine is brittle too, I admit it. [251] It cracks whenever the drops falter. [257] So on the first dawn after the last fish, I carried the two halves of the bell up the viaduct stairs and set to work rebuilding it, because it was the only debt-paying I knew how to do. [295]

Here is what I understood that wiser mages did not: heat is the enemy of a mended bell. [313] Great fire welds bronze hastily and leaves it brittle, and brittle bronze is how you get a broken bell twice. [333] But dawn light through sapphire burns cold and slow, and a lukewarm mage can tend a slow fire forever without once cracking the metal. [357] My weakness was the exact size of the job. [366] So I warmed the crack to blood temperature, no hotter, and laid the sapphire lens the tide-keepers left behind across the seam, and let the first light in. [394]

The drip governed everything. [398] I had measured the viaduct's rate for nine years, every season: forty-one drops a minute in spring, thirty-three in winter, when the light comes grudgingly. [423] A drop fell; I waited; the seam softened exactly as far as one drop's worth of patience. [440] Rushing the rate scorched the bronze. [446] Ignoring the rate left the seam cold. [453] Understanding dripping rates was, my grandmother wrote in the margin of the last ledger, the whole of magic, and everything else is ceremony. [476]

It took eleven dawns. [480] On the fourth dawn my devotion cracked, just a little, a hairline of doubt: the sea was still leaving, the fishermen were still gone, and here I was warming metal like a fool. [513] I mended it the same way I mended the bell: at blood temperature, without hurry, one honest drop at a time. [534] That, I think, is the secret nobody writes down: devotion was never meant to be unbreakable, only repairable. [552]

On the eleventh dawn the seam closed. [559] The bell was not beautiful; you could see the scar where the world had broken it, and the sapphire had left a blue vein through the bronze like a river remembered in metal. [592] I hung it from the viaduct's middle arch, where the drips gather, and struck it once. [608] The note came out cracked and true at the same time, the way honest things usually are. [625]

One. [626] The viaduct answered with a hundred drops, right on schedule, forty-one to the minute. [640] Far below, at the base of the pilings, the water paused, considered, and rose one inch. [656]

One inch is not a tide, and I want to be honest about that, because honesty is all a lukewarm mage has. [678] It will take eleven years of dawns to pay back a single tide, and some four thousand tides to refill what the sky took. [702] I have done the arithmetic; I did it before I climbed the stairs, naively, earnestly, and then I climbed anyway. [722] This morning a fisherman's daughter came back from inland to see the madman on the viaduct, and stayed to count drips. [743] The sea rose another inch. [748] Somewhere out past the pilings, in water that has no business holding fish yet, something silver turned over, once, and was gone. [770]

I struck the bell again. [775] Two. [776] The count continues. [779]
