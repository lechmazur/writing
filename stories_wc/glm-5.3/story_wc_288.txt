Marisol remembered her death first: the cold of the river, then the warmth of being sung into a new body. [20] After that reincarnation memory surfaced, she stopped grieving the past and started climbing toward it. [35] The climb ended at the Tidal Library, an archive anchored above the clouds, where memory rolled in like weather. [54] Twice a day, a silver tide of forgotten voices rose through the stacks, and twice a day it withdrew. [73] Marisol was its only librarian. [79]

In her first life, four centuries ago, she had been a contemporary of the last honey-voiced auk, a seabird whose song could stitch fog into lace. [105] She had fed it from her palm the winter the colonies fell silent. [118] Now she was the last living creature who had heard that song, which made her, in the library's records, a primary source. [140]

Solitude pressed on her at first like deep water. [149] But she had chosen this post, and she meant to translate that isolation into independence: no council, no caretakers, only her own hands re-shelving the drowned voices. [176] The library rewarded self-reliance. [180] Its catalog had been designed for one reader at a time, because the dead do not queue. [197]

At the center of the reading room stood a brass sundial with no gnomon and no shadow, polished until it was atomically reflective. [220] It did not tell the hour. [225] It told the tide. [229] Look into its face and you saw not your reflection but your composition: the drifting lattice of atoms that had once been river water, bird bone, someone else's breath. [258] Marisol read hers every morning. [263] The pattern confirmed what the reincarnation memory had promised: nothing in her had been lost, only rearranged. [280]

Above the stacks, on the library's highest terrace, stood a ring of standing stones older than any nation below the clouds. [301] When the tide turned, the ancient stone circle hummed, a low chord that made the mist shiver into shapes. [320] The shapes were the extinct. [325] By the hum of the ancient stone circle, the living and the vanished could coexist for the length of one tide. [346] They did not speak. [349] They simply stayed, and their staying was the whole message. [359]

Marisol learned quickly that these awakenings were brittle. [367] They came thin as ice on a spring puddle; ask a ghost a desperate question and the whole shape cracked to powder. [389] She had shattered the auk twice with her own eagerness, once by reaching for it, once by weeping. [407] Grief, she decided, was a kind of grabbing. [415] So she trained herself the way she trained her hands for the shelves: slowly, steadily, alone. [431] Independence, it turned out, was a craft. [438] She learned to mend the tide-damp spines without help, to read the brass sundial's atomic ledger, to sit inside the hum without asking it for anything. [464] In return, the brittle awakenings lasted longer. [471]

One evening the tide rose higher than the records said it could, silver voices flooding the terraces, and the hum dropped into a register she felt in her teeth. [500] The mist gathered into one small, upright shape. [508] The auk stood on the terrace stones as it had stood on her palm four hundred winters ago. [526] It did not sing. [529] Marisol kept her hands at her sides and her questions locked in her chest, and because she asked for nothing, the shape held. [552] She simply breathed beside it, two eras sharing one patch of cloud-light. [564] When the tide began to fall, the bird walked a slow circle around her, the way its kind had once circled sun-warmed ledges, a living shadow for a sundial that had never owned one. [598] The hum held the shape of that circling long after the mist withdrew. [611]

Afterward, she descended to the reading room and pressed her palm flat to the brass sundial. [627] The atomically reflective face showed her lattice shifting, a few bright points drifting inward from the edge of the pattern, atoms the mist had left behind, or that she had imagined it had, and it no longer mattered which. [666] She copied the pattern into the catalog under a heading no other library would recognize: coexistence, witnessed. [683] Then she climbed back up to wait for morning, one librarian, entirely her own, keeping the tide's hours above the clouds. [704]
