The static shock snapped from the doorknob into Cass's palm, sharp enough that she dropped her keys on the studio steps. [21] She flexed her stinging hand, unlocked the light painting studio, and did not yet know that the whole city had felt the same white snap at the same moment. [50] After the static shock, everything changed. [56]

The Unison recall was three months old by then. [65] The telepathy pill had actually worked, seeding conductive mineral lattices along the nerves, until the regulators pulled it because users could not stop feeling strangers at bus stops. [93] Cass had never taken one. [98] She was an affectionate skeptic, the kind of woman who remembered every client's birthday and rolled her eyes at every miracle, and she had told her wife Dee that Unison was expensive fairy dust. [132] Then the recall came, and Dee grieved the silence between minds like a death, and Cass felt ashamed of her own relief. [154]

On the workbench, propped where Dee had left it years ago, lay the muscovite geode sliver from their first date, thin as a fingernail, glittering like frozen water. [182] Under the magnifier its layers branched into smaller branches into smaller branches still, fractally precise at every scale, and Cass had once joked that it looked like a photograph of the nervous system. [215] Dee hadn't laughed. [218] She had said that was exactly what Unison felt like from the inside. [231]

They started arriving the day after the static shock. [240] Ex-users, mostly, hands trembling, describing ghost flickers: goosebumps when a stranger nearby was about to cry, warmth before a friend laughed. [261] The winter air had dried the whole city into one enormous capacitor, a physicist on the news explained, and the leftover lattices in people's nerves had woken a little. [290] Dee woke up gasping, saying she could almost feel Cass dreaming. [301]

Cass did not believe in vibes, but she believed in instruments, so she set out to devise something honest. [320] Her studio already turned invisible things into light: long exposures that caught a dancer's swung torch as a ribbon of gold. [341] What she built was a rig of sensors, taped gently to wrists and collarbones, that worked through somatic markers, the pulse, the breath, the skin's quiet voltage, feeding each one to the light wands. [375] No mind reading, she told her classes, holding up a waiver. [386] Only the body, which never learned to lie, painting in real time. [398] She mounted the muscovite sliver in front of the projector, and the laser came through it split into a hundred thin glittering sheets, so that every trail someone painted was already layered with the mineral's patient branching. [435]

It worked better than she had dared hope. [443] When two people in the rig resonated, when one's heartbeat slowed to meet the other's, their trails braided into a single helix, and the whole dark room could see the hidden pathway between them illuminate. [478] Ex-users wept and called it telepathy come back. [486] Cass called it evidence, and loved them too much to argue. [497] Her motive had never really been science, anyway; it was to illuminate hidden pathways, to make the invisible cables between people visible for one long exposure, so nobody had to take a pill on faith again. [533]

[711] Cass moved the muscovite sliver to the windowsill and hung the print of the braided trails beside it, so the morning sun came through in sheets and filled the room with willowglass light.
