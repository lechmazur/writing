Every autumn, during the migration of clouds, when the whole sky went south in long gray ropes, Mara took the coast road home. [23] She was a firefighter in the city, but the town still called her the arsonist's daughter, since her father had given it more fires than festivals. [49] His last fire had been the willow grove that crowned the dunes, above the obsidian lagoon where the water lay black and glassy and gave back no sky. [77] She had been nine that night, hiding among the trees, and she never learned whether he had known she was there. [98] Skin grafted from her mother's hip had closed the burn along her shoulder, a patch that never tanned and never forgot. [119] As they led her father away, he pressed a gift into her palm: a foreign coin, drilled through and strung on red thread, from a country she could never find on any map. [152] "Wear it," he said, "and remember that thread is for mending." [163]

The town waited for her to become him. [171] Instead she became the opposite, joining the city brigade at nineteen, because she had grown up listening to fire and knew its moods like a sibling's. [197] On the fireground she was gracefully alert, reading shifts of wind before the smoke showed them, the way other people read weather in their knees. [222] She carried a girl out of the Harbor Street fire alive, but not unmarked. [236] The girl's name was Wren; surgeons grafted new skin over her neck and arm, and afterward Wren stopped speaking, as if the fire had kept her voice as a trophy. [266] At the burn clinic Wren hid her scars under wool in July. [278] Mara had watched her a long time, then asked Wren's grandmother for one autumn day. [293] She meant to offer one proof, the only one she had ever found: that scars can sing. [310]

They walked the volcanic beach first, and the black sand gave softly under their boots, warm as a held hand. [330] Mara knelt and filled a small tin with it, the way she did every year, for the pale patch on her own shoulder. [353] "The volcano made this," she told Wren. [360] "Whatever burns can become something you can stand on." [369] They climbed the dunes through the cloud migration's long shadow, the sky moving south above them in ribbons, and came down into the hollow where the lagoon lay. [397] Obsidian edged its rim where the dunes leaned close, and the water inside was dark and still, a mirror that refused reflections. [419] Around it stood the willows, scarred trunks black with old fire, and alive, because Mara had come back every autumn to graft them. [442] She cut scions from the unburned trees by the road and set them into the wounded wood, and the wounded wood accepted, because that is the nature of the living. [472] Seven years of grafts, and the grove sang now whenever wind came off the sea. [487]

Mara rolled up her sleeve and showed Wren the pale patch on her shoulder without shame, then took the girl's wool sleeve and folded it back too. [514] She rubbed warm black sand over the graft on Wren's arm until the scar shone like poured glass. [532] "Scars are not the end of skin," she said. [541] "They are where the story of you keeps its loudest part." [552] The clouds thickened overhead, and the wind arrived the way it always did during the migration, in one long exhale. [572] The water shivered; the sand hissed; the lagoon began to sing, a low ringing hum, obsidian answering obsidian, willows whistling through their grafts. [595] Mara had heard it all her scarred life and never once gotten used to it. [610] Beside her, Wren's mouth opened. [615] What came out was small, a hum at first, then three notes, then a child's song about rain, rough-voiced and unbroken. [636] The lagoon carried it out over the black water and gave it back. [649] Mara lifted the red thread over Wren's head and let the drilled coin fall against the singing sand. [667] "It has a hole in it," she said, "so the grief has somewhere to go." [682]

That night she wrote to her father, as she did every year and he had never once answered. [700] She did not write out of hope; she wrote the way she fought fires, with a compassion that refused to quit. [721] "The clouds went south," she wrote. [727] "The lagoon sang." [730] "I gave your coin to a girl whose scars sing now." [741] "I am not angry anymore, and I will keep not being angry, every year, until one of us is done." [761] Outside, the migration of clouds went on, slow and gray and forgiving, and the sea kept its kelp-drift peace beneath it. [782]
