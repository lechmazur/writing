The leash broke at dusk, the way old things break, without violence, with a small sigh of worn metal. [19] Bess, who had spent nine careful years never straying past Mara's knee, lifted her head as if hearing a far-off bell and ran. [42] She ran up, not along the road, toward the ridgeway the villagers called veiled because mist clung to it even in August, a white hem above the estuary. [70] Mara followed, breath ragged, boots sodden with bracken, while below her the tide went out and out, hushing the shingle like a mother who means to say nothing and says it all night. [103]

The mist on the ridge was not empty. [111] Three figures walked it, lanterns swinging low, and Mara knew them at once from her grandmother's tellings: the ferry guides, the wise dead of the old crossing, who had rowed travelers over the estuary for two hundred years before the bridge was built. [154] They still kept their vigil in twilight, the grandmother said, for anyone crossing in the dark between one life and the next. [176]

"You've lost your dog," said the eldest, a woman with salt-white braids, and it was not a question. [194] "She went toward the water," said the second, "but the straight way down is the bridge's way, and the bridge belongs to carts and grief." [219] "We traverse by indirect routes," said the third, gently persistent as surf. [231] "Come." [232]

So Mara traversed the veiled ridgeway in their wake, by sheep tracks and loops through gorse, by a hollow where blackbirds were settling, by a stile her own boots could have found in the dark and never had. [270] Every detour mattered, the eldest explained, the way a lullaby matters, each return of the wave carrying something the last one dropped. [292] They asked her nothing, and somehow she told them everything: fifteen years indoors with her mother, who had died in spring; the wages of caution banked and never spent; the ticket in her coat pocket even now, a ferry ticket from a schoolgirl crossing, from the day she had meant to slip over the side and swim the last stretch and had gripped the rail instead. [358] "You're looking for her," the eldest said, nodding downhill toward the dim water, "but you're looking for where your unused courage waits, and it has been waiting a long time, patient as a dog." [392]

Their lantern hung at the old ferry landing, burning as it had burned every twilight since the bridge made it useless; and across a channel of returning water, on the far spit, Bess stood small and still, waiting. [430] The guides could not fetch her. [436] "The water will not carry us twice," said the second, not sorrowful, only stating the tide's arithmetic. [453] The channel between was not deep, only dark, only cold, waist-high on a woman of fifty-one who had never in her life gone in past her knees. [480]

The eldest held out her hand, and Mara, understanding without being told, gave her the limp ferry ticket she had carried for forty years. [504] From her coat the guide drew a brass ticket punch, worn smooth at the handle, the kind the old boatmen once carried, and pressed it through the paper with a click like a small door closing. [540] The hole it left was a star. [547] "Passage paid," the eldest said, "not in coin, but in the going." [559]

Mara took off her shoes, and the third guide, gently persistent still, said, "The water remembers you; you were young in it once; let it say so." [586] She waded. [588] Cold climbed her calves, her thighs, her held breath; the mud took her feet kindly, the way the guides took her story, and the far shore came to her one step at a time, indirect, unhurried, certain. [625] Bess swam to meet her halfway, which felt like forgiveness, or like teaching. [638]

They came out dripping into the lantern light, and the three ferry guides stood in a half circle, nodding, the way old women nod at weather that has finally turned. [668] Behind them the estuary breathed, in and out, in and out, a lullaby with no words left in it. [687] "Keep the ticket," said the eldest. [693] "When you wonder whether you are someone who goes in past her knees, hold it up to any light, and the star will let the courage through." [720]

Mara walked home along the tideline with the leash, snapped, looped useless around her wrist like a bracelet, and Bess trotting free beside her, close as a heartbeat, no rope required. [751] The mist on the veiled ridgeway thinned behind them, keeping, as mist does, only what it came to keep. [770]
