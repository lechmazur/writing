Vess built carts that refused to touch the ground, which was sensible, because the ground was acid. [17] Her workshop rode the Vitriol Sea on pontoon cogs, a fat brass broom of gears that ticked and turned and sweated oil into the yellow fog. [43] The sea ate iron in a week, wood in a day, and arrogance in about a minute. [60] Vess called herself a cart glider, the first and so far the only, and she said the word pioneering with the smugness of someone who had welded it to her own name. [92] Her neighbors were two hundred miles of acid and, on clear days, a smudge of far shore where hunters lived. [112]

The hunters fired arrows across the water in high, lazy arcs that fell short and drowned, shafts dissolving, all but the tips. [134] A hunter's arrow tip was grey flint the acid could not chew, and steadily curious Vess fished them up by the bucket, though it took her years to ask why anyone would shoot so long at a raft that never shot back. [176] The flint lined her cart skids like teeth, and a skid-shod cart could skim the Vitriol's skin the way a wasp skims a pond. [200]

Her masterwork was the yearning algorithm, a stack of differential gears that took in distance, wind, and cargo weight and spat out one number: how badly the cart wanted to leave. [231] Engineers measured lift and drag; Vess measured want, because want, she insisted, was the only force that had ever carried anything anywhere. [253] The night she fed her newest cart's numbers into the works, the algorithm chattered and returned a yearning value of nine, its ceiling, which she chose to interpret as the machine voting. [285]

Then came the arrow that did not fall short. [294] It thunked into the workshop wall, and its flint shoulder was scratched with half-pictures, curves and hooves and wings cut off at the spine. [318] Vess held the tip inside her kaleidoscope, the mirror-drum she used for checking gear symmetry, and the mirrors did what mirrors do: they finished things. [343] Each half-glyph met its reflection and closed into a whole image, a horse, a wave, an open mouth, and the tumbling glass rearranged the images into order, a sentence built in mirrors. [375] So the hunters wrote to be read through a kaleidoscope, which meant they expected a mirror-minded reader, which meant, Vess thought, they had been waiting a very long time. [404] She transliterated the images into her own alphabet, sound by sound, whispering at the glass: horse is hah, wave is ohh, open mouth is a shout. [430] The message, in her letters at last, was four words long: WE RING TOO, SISTER. [445]

Every dusk for thirty years Vess had rung Old Tooth, the great bell bolted to the workshop's spine, cast so low that not even she could hear it, only feel it, a hum in her teeth. [481] She rang it for ears that did not exist yet, which she considered the only honest kind of audience. [500] The hunters could not hear it either, and the acid fog ate every frequency, and still they had guessed, the way deaf dancers feel music, that someone out here was ringing. [531] She struck Old Tooth once more, loaded the cart with arrow-tip flint and dried fish, and let the yearning algorithm choose the wind. [554] The cart skated the acid for eleven hours, yellow fog peeling off the skids, Vess shouting apologies to the sea she was cheating. [577] The hunters waited on their shore with a second bell, and no words, and no need of any. [595]

What happened next took the rest of time, because this is a tale that runs throughout the evolution, all of it. [616] The two shores stitched themselves together with gliding carts, generation by generation, the designs breeding and improving like gulls. [635] They towed Vess's workshop to mid-sea as a bell-buoy, cogs still turning, still sweating oil. [650] The script evolved, images flattening into letters, until children read kaleidoscopes only for fun. [664] The acid thinned as the ages turned, and the fog loosened its grip on sound. [679]

And after ten thousand dusks, and ten thousand more, ears on both shores had deepened and grown patient, the way ears do when a people spends forever listening. [707] One evening a child on the far shore stopped mid-step, turned toward the water, and heard Old Tooth at last, vast and unhurried, ringing across the sweetened sea. [735] Behind it, faint as gear-teeth catching, came the hunters' bell, and then a third and a fourth, the whole lineage of cart gliders ringing down the years. [762] Nobody had heard them yet, and now, at last, gloriously, everybody did. [774]
