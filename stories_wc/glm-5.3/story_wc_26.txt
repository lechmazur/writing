After dreams became shareable, people learned to perform them, and the last honest hopes had to be kept somewhere cold and high. [22] So they built the Cloudshelf, a library above the clouds where archived dreams bloomed instead of sitting on shelves. [41] Wren worked there as a dusk noticer, sharp-eyed by trade, because the blooms opened only in the seven minutes when day forgot itself. [64] Each evening she walked the aisles cataloging flowers of other people's sleep, their petals holding the exact colors of longing. [84] It was quiet work, and she was good at quiet. [94]

The trouble began with the footage. [100] Restoration had unearthed two reels of the library's early days, silent video footage from decades apart, and Wren spent a week comparing them frame by frame. [126] The older reel showed the first blooms, small and uncertain; the newer one showed aisles gone tall and strange. [145] She laid one image over the other and noticed what a lesser eye would have missed: a shadow that moved against the light. [168] It fell the wrong direction in both reels, cast by nothing, owned by no one. [183] Sixty years apart, the same wrong shadow crossed the same aisle. [194] An unbound shadow, she thought, and her hands went cold on the desk. [207]

Every archivist knew the stories: when a person's hope came loose, truly loose, its shadow detached and wandered, looking for a shape to remember itself by. [233] They dissolved at dawn unless they found an anchor. [242] That dusk, Wren walked the aisles with her eyes narrowed against the fading gold, and there it was: a darkness drifting between the bloom-lamps, thin as spilled ink. [270] It did not flee her. [275] It moved toward the western sill, where a single river stone sat half-buried in moss, dark and smooth, older than the library itself. [298] The shadow curled around it like a hand remembering a hand. [309]

The old reel held the answer in its grain: a girl carrying a river stone to the western sill, pressing it into the moss, then walking away lighter on one side than she should have been. [345] The ledgers named her Ilsa Marten, a first dreamer, one who had refused to share. [360] Her hope had begun to come loose, the way all private things did in that era, so she anchored its shadow to the one object that had never asked anything of her. [392] Then she died, and the shadow, unbound from any living hand, kept returning each dusk to the stone, waiting to be remembered. [414] Wren understood waiting. [417] She had given her own dreams to the archive long ago, before she understood what the sharing cost, and she had been trying to preserve the shape of hope ever since, one cataloged bloom at a time. [454] This shadow was hope's original shape, unperformed, unbroadcast, and sixty years of hiding had thinned it to lace; the stone could shelter it but not renew it. [481]

She worked through the night, softly deliberate, the way she did everything that mattered. [495] She carried the river stone to the deepest aisle, where the soil was richest with old dreamfall, and set it down with both hands. [519] The shadow followed, patient as weather. [525] "I can't give you back to her," Wren said. [534] "She's gone, and the footage is silent, so I never even heard her voice. [548] But I can give you a body that blooms." [557]

The first light came up through the cloud floor like water through cloth. [570] The shadow touched the stone and began, at last, to metamorphose. [581] It thinned, gathered, folded itself greenward; a stem rose from the stone's shadowed side, and petals opened in a color Wren had no catalog number for, something between dusk and forgiveness. [612] Inside the flower, visible only when you squinted the way a noticer squints, a small dark figure stood at a riverbank, arm cocked, forever about to skip a stone. [641] Wren cried, which surprised her, and did not stop watching, which did not. [654] She had preserved the shape of hope, and it was not the same as saving the girl, and she would carry that difference for the rest of her life, glowing. [684]

The bloom opened only at dusk, seven minutes each evening, and Wren was the only one who stayed to see it. [705] Visitors asked her why she kept such strange hours, and she told them the truth: that hope keeps its shape best in fading light, and someone sharp-eyed ought to be watching. [736] Some nights, when the petals opened and the little figure lifted its arm, she whispered to it, sorry and grateful in the same breath. [760] The stone stayed beneath the flower, holding them both, the way rivers had always held everything that ever skipped across them: briefly, brightly, and then let go. [787]
