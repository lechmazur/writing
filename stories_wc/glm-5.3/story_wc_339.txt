Eleven days after the sonar image of Amelia Earhart's Electra appeared in the news, I took the submersible Petrel down to a wreck nobody was looking for. [27] The world called it closure, a word I have never seen survive contact with salt water. [43] I dive to study the physics of letting go: I measure how long the sea holds a thing before it opens its hand. [66]

Mara rides in the copilot's seat, where my wife Lena sat until the flooding off Kanton nine years ago. [85] Mara is the one people call Death, and we are on a first name basis because she introduced herself while I was four minutes dead and used my name first. [115] Wren, she said, you have unfinished arithmetic. [122] She sent me back up and kept Lena, and we have been perfectly polite about it ever since. [140] Every dive since, she takes the empty seat; every dive I ask whether it is time; every dive, not yet. [160]

We settle at 58 meters on the Circe, a copra steamer that carried a lending library through the Phoenix Islands and sank in a squall in 1940, forgotten by all but me. [192] Her library sits aft, a long room of shelves and brass lamps, and the water entered it the way water enters everything, patiently, like a tenant. [218] Books line the shelves the way coral lines a reef after it has eaten the reef. [234] I note the temperature, two degrees above the seasonal mean, and I note that nothing down here hurries; this is how I grieve, quietly, with numbers. [260]

On the librarian's desk, upright in the silt, stands a snow globe containing the Circe herself, brass hull and single funnel, a souvenir from Suva before the war. [288] The ship inside the ship: thirty years of diving, and I have never seen the ocean make a better joke. [308] My thruster wash lifts it and the flakes rise instead of falling, slow as ash, because the globe is full of sea now, and grief, like snow, obeys the medium it is given. [341] I give it one deliberate shake, and the white comes down over the brass decks, the first snowfall these islands ever had, settling on a deck nobody will sweep. [370] I note the settling rate in my log and leave the globe where it stood. [385]

What I came for lies beneath the borrowing ledger: the radio log of Anani Tuilagi, the operator who copied the signals the whole Pacific was chasing. [411] Mussels have laced it to the shelf with byssal threads, and I free the book by untangling threads one at a time, an hour of patient work, delicate as braiding a child's hair. [444] Mara watches and says nothing, which from her is a kind of applause. [457] The log opens to July 2, 1937, in a steady hand. [468] Anani copied her last transmission on 6210 kilocycles: we must be on you but cannot see you, gas is running low, we are on the line 157 337, running north and south. [500] Nobody answered her properly, because nobody could give a bearing; a voice at the end of its fuel does not need to be heard, it needs to be answered. [529] Below it, in darker ink, the Circe's last words as the squall opened her plates: taking water forward, the library is holding, tell Suva the books are all right. [558] I photograph every page and put the log back, weighted with lead, because raising paper is only a slower way of drowning it. [581]

Then I do the thing I planned and never admitted planning. [592] I key the Petrel's transmitter on 6210 and answer, 88 years late: Electra, this is Petrel, we have you, position confirmed, come down, come down now. [618] Radio does not pass through sixty meters of sea, which is the correct physics, and Mara reaches over and rests her hand on mine anyway. [643] Then it assembles itself: letting go is not an event but a rate, one electron at a time, rust as patience, dissolution as mercy, love the constant you divide by. [673] I ask her, one last time, whether it is time. [683] Mara nods once, as if I have finally turned in my arithmetic. [695]

Before flooding the ballast I write a last note on the dive slate and leave it on the librarian's desk: found, read, answered, you may let go. [722] This is my last transmission and my last dive; when I tell the surface so, nobody up there asks why. [742] Mara stays below as we lift away, as she always does; she has never once seen the surface. [760] I rise through water that lets me go reluctantly, then all at once, into a light that asks only to be borne. [782]
