On the last night of midsummer, Ilya Kest climbed the velvet stair of the Empire's observatory with her instruments, a cup, a saucer, and a flask of black coffee. [29] She was the finest provenance researcher in three provinces, summoned because the young republics had begun asking inconvenient questions about the Empire's founding relic. [53] The observatory was a dome of mulberry velvet, dyed, the histories said, from the berries of the conquered groves, and its ache followed her up every stair, purple and patient. [83] At the center, on blue silk, lay the relic: a violin, wholly transparent, made of ice crystal, holding two centuries of light the way water holds a drowned sky. [112] The Empire claimed it was carved from the heart of the Glacier Mother, proof that the first Empress was born of ice and entitled to everything warm. [139] Curator Vell met her beneath it with her commission in both hands, the salary written in luminous gold ink that still glowed through the paper like a lamp under skin. [169] Sign the certification before dawn, he said, while the promise still has its glow. [183]

Vell did not know that Ilya was telepathic with plants, ever since the greenhouse accident at university, when a shelf of ferns collapsed on her skull and she woke hearing them scream. [215] The gift had arrived by accident and stayed like a scar, useless mostly, since plants rarely discuss anything but light and thirst. [237] But the ivy strangling the observatory's railings was shouting before she reached the relic, and beneath its gossip ran an older voice, faint as roots in dark soil. [265] The ivy said: listen to the planter. [272] By the wall stood a marble planter of imperial flowers, and under the flowers, half buried, lived the mother stump of the conquered groves, kept as a trophy. [300] Mother and child were speaking to each other across the room, and the child was the violin. [317]

When she lifted it, cold as a held breath, its voice flooded her: I was not carved, I was grown. [337] In its crystal rings she read the burned history: a gardener named Anselm had propagated the last cutting of the rebel mulberries in a basin of meltwater that never thawed, and the sapling drank cold until its heartwood ran clear as ice. [379] He shaped it into a violin so the grove would keep singing after the soldiers finished, and he promised the tree its song would outlive the Empire. [406] That promise was the faint blue glow inside the crystal, and it was dimming with every midsummer. [423] When the glow died, the voice said, the crystal would be only ice, and no ice can be propagated. [442] But while the promise held its glow, the bud at the scroll's end, no bigger than a fingernail, could be broken off and pressed into living soil, where it would root like any cutting and the grove would begin again. [482]

So she stood at midnight weighing a dream against an empire: the grove's dream of singing on against the Empire's heart of ice, with her own bright directorship laid on its pan. [514] Undecided, she did what her grandmother had taught her, the only divination she trusted: she poured coffee into the saucer, swirled it, tipped it out, and read the future in the stain. [546] Where the coffee had pooled, the future lay solid and unbroken, a brown empire with her name stamped in it. [566] But at the rim the stain had dried into a chain of separate drops, rings like a tree's years, each touching the next at a single point, and lamplight came through the gaps between them. [601] Destiny, said the saucer, is not a sealed page; it is perforated, and light gets in at the holes, and a woman with steady thumbs can tear along the line. [631] She tore. [633]

The bud snapped off with a sound like a frozen note, and she pressed it into the soil beside the mother stump while the ivy crooned and the stump, waking, wept sap. [665] By bicentennial dawn a transparent sapling stood in the planter, rooted in ice crystal, and no glacier in the world does that. [687] She wrote the true provenance in her formal hand: grown, not carved; mulberry, not glacier; a gardener's promise, not an Empress's birthright. [709]

Vell read it in silence while the gold ink of her commission dimmed like a lamp running out of oil. [729] Somewhere behind him, in the planter, a faint blue glow brightened. [740] The directorship went to a more careful liar, and the observatory kept its mulberry ache. [755] But every midsummer afterward, passersby heard a violin tuning itself inside a small transparent tree, and Ilya heard it best of all: the grove, singing on. [781]
