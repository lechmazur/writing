The city kept time by the giant's breathing, and tonight the breathing stumbled. [13] Mira, natural history illustrator to the Canal Guild, felt the stutter through her oar: the rooftop canals that wove between the towers sagged mid-tide, held like a skipped heartbeat. [42] Beneath the foundations slept the giant, and his slow nap was the city's only clock; the bell-ringers counted breaths, not hours. [63] When the cadence broke, so did everything that moved to it. [74] Mira had one nap's length to fix it. [82] If the giant woke arrhythmic, the towers would shake their canals dry. [94]

Her gift was the reason the Guild tolerated her odd commissions: whatever she drew true, she sculpted time itself. [113] A heron sketched mid-dip kept that morning from ending; her field notebooks were jars of held seconds. [130] At her throat hung her grandmother's infinity symbol pendant, a brass loop with no beginning, and time drawn through it could be borrowed and returned without breaking. [157] Her grandmother had rung the breath-bells for sixty years, and on her last night had whispered a single word that never became an echo. [181] That was why Mira rowed toward the high towers, where the oldest maps marked, in faded ink, the place where echoes are born. [204]

In this city, echoes were not accidents of sound; they condensed out of the giant's exhale, newborn and luminous, carrying the last words of the dying out over the rooftops. [234] For three winters, none had been born. [241] The stutter and the silence shared one address, the maps said: the Windcage at the city's crown. [258]

She rowed all night through the rooftop canal system, past weirs where water stood trembling on the hinge of a stalled breath, past markets asleep in the same pause. [287] Twice the giant's chest caught, and the whole skyline flinched; twice it laboriously resumed, and the water remembered which way was down. [309] In the deep middle of the nap, the towers closed overhead like ribs, and she heard it: a thin, trapped keening, a nursery of echoes with no door. [337]

The Windcage was a brass lattice clamped over the vent where the giant's exhale left the world, and behind it the breath backed up like floodwater behind a dam. [366] Trapped in the lattice, pale as moths, the unborn echoes clung and shimmered. [379] The Horologists' seal on the lock explained everything: they had caged the breath to meter it and sell it, and the giant's lungs had learned the cage like a stutter. [409] Her grandmother had died three winters ago, the very night the hammering began on the high towers; Mira had never once thought to join those dates until now. [437] But the lock was breathed: it opened only on a full, smooth exhale, and the giant had none left to give. [458]

Mira moored the skiff, opened her satchel of notebooks, and threaded the pendant's loop through a drawing of a heron at first light. [481] The page whitened; the morning it had held poured into the present, one long, seamless minute. [497] This was borrowing time: no beginning, no end, only a loop drawn tight around the stutter. [513] Inside that borrowed minute she turned the lock, and it turned as if the giant breathed easily in his sleep. [533] The Windcage swung wide, and Mira, natural history illustrator, did the strangest work of her career: she uncaged the breath of the world. [556]

The exhale rolled out unbroken, and the echoes came with it, hundreds of them, spilling down the canals like light made of voice. [579] They were born the instant they crossed into open air, and she saw the truth the maps had only guessed at: echoes are born wherever a breath is finally let go. [610] One echo, older than the rest, drifted back against the current and spoke in her grandmother's voice. [627] "Live," it said, and the word was out at last, three winters late. [640]

Below, the giant's breathing eased into its old, long cadence, the canals surged, and the waking bell-ringers began to count again. [661] Mira owed the pendant a debt of minutes now, and she would repay it the only way she knew: drawing mornings and giving them back, one sketch at a time. [691] The giant slept on, hours from waking, and the whole city moved to his breath again. [707] In her last blank notebook she drew the newborn echoes lifting off the water, each one a small forever, and the drawing did what true drawings do: it held. [736]
