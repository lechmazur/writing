The Meridian Opera House had been drowning for three years, and Ines had been drowning beside it in her own quieter way. [22] Tonight, the eve of demolition, she slipped past the chained doors with her viola and a tin of matches. [41] The flood had never drained; the river had simply moved in and stayed, turning the orchestra pit into a well of black glass. [64] She was a pit musician, third chair viola, and she had spent her whole working life in in-between spaces, not quite stage and not quite house, wedged beneath the lip of the world while sopranos blazed overhead. [101] The pit had always suited her, that neither-here-nor-there. [109] But lately something in her had changed. [116] Lately she had been inspired, of all impossible things, waking at three in the morning with phrases already in her fingers, melodies that felt borrowed, as though someone else were lending them out. [149] Tonight she meant to find out who. [156]

She moved along the promenade lighting the old wall lanterns the preservation society had left oiled for tours that never came, and each flame woke flickering and doubled itself in the black water, so that the ruined hall glowed twice over, above and below. [200] Between flickers, the house looked exactly as the river had left it. [212] The water had come through the walls mid-rehearsal, and Maestro Aldous Vane had sent the company out ahead of it, then gone back for the score of his unfinished opera and never come up. [246] The last act had never been played. [253] Ever since, the house had held its breath amid the hush of unstruck chords, and so, Ines suspected, had its ghost. [274]

His letters were waiting in the box office, in a crate of antique programs the flood had chewed and kept. [294] A century of seasons, every page blooming with water stains, and over her secret visits Ines had begun to see that the damage was not damage at all. [322] The flood had run the old printer's ink, pooled it, coaxed it outward into images: a staff line here, a clef there, noteheads like small dark moons scattered across tributes to sopranos decades dead. [356] She spread the programs across the counter, ordering them by season, and the stains reached for one another, bar lines joining across the years, a melody the water had spent three years writing on a hundred years of paper. [395] This was why she kept trespassing in her own ruined workplace: to unravel the code left by a ghost. [414] She had learned to check her reading the only way available. [425] Perched on the pit rail with her feet trailing in the flood, she would play a phrase and watch the lanterns; when she had it right, every flame in the house stood taller and the water at her ankles went still as held breath, and when she had it wrong the light shivered itself nearly out. [481] It was a conversation without words, question and answer, bow and flame, and it was the truest dialogue Ines had ever kept. [503]

Tonight the final page had made itself legible: the house's very first program, 1897, its stain a rising figure that resolved the whole phrase the way a held breath resolves into speech. [535] The finale of Aldous Vane's drowned opera, dictated by a dead man through the twin mediums of water and paper. [555] She carried it down to the pit rail, set bow to string, and struck the first of the unstruck chords. [575] The sound disrupted everything. [579] The water shivered into rings, the drowned reflection of the hall shuddered and swam, and three years of held breath broke at last. [602] She played the code as she had read it, phrase by phrase, and the lanterns rose and steadied, and somewhere in the dark above the stage a presence leaned in the way a conductor leans. [637] Ripples crossed the flood in strict tempo, keeping time for her. [648]

Only now, hearing the melodies she had been waking with pour out of her own fingers, did Ines understand where her inspiration had been coming from: he had been teaching her his handwriting while she slept, so that someone alive would be able to read him. [694] She reached the final bar, the rising figure from 1897, and held the last chord until the lanterns burned white and the flood lay still as glass. [721] Then, one by one, the flames bowed themselves out, and the house exhaled. [734] By morning the water had drained away through the aisles like an audience filing home, and the demolition crews found the doors standing open and the pit empty, while Ines walked the gray streets with a crate of ruined programs under her arm, carrying a finished opera out of the in-between spaces and into the waking world. [791]
