Vera had inherited three things from her grandmother: the stargazer's tower on Cape Aurel, a keyring of constellation charts, and a conviction that magic was nonsense. [26] Etta had been the coast's last stargazer, a woman who left the tower's roof open in every weather so the sky could come and go as it pleased. [54] Vera kept the roof open too, though she told herself it was for the lease, not the woman. [72] She was a mathematician, a skeptic of the sympathetic kind, the sort who doubted politely and drove all night anyway when a neighbor wrote that the whales were early. [101] Every October she came for the migration, because Etta had come, and grief, like arithmetic, respects repetition. [118]

It was the boy who brought the tile. [126] Sam was nine, ferried up the cliff stairs by an aunt who worked the lighthouse, and he carried the ceramic square in both hands the way children carry things that are actually carrying them. [160] "My mom left it," he said. [166] "She said your grandma promised the tower would be open." [176] The tile was old, blue-glazed, four rows of four numbers pressed into it, every row, column, and diagonal summing to thirty-four. [197] A proper magic square, and etched on the back, in Etta's hand, the instruction: TRANSCRIBE THROUGH THE FORGOTTEN PATTERN. [216] Vera had seen it once before, years ago, in the hands of a woman climbing the tower stairs at dusk. [236] "It doesn't do anything," she told Sam gently. [244] "Then why are you scared of it?" he asked. [253] Because Etta had died in this tower mid-transcription, chalk in hand; because Sam's mother had followed in spring; and because a skeptic with no doubts is only a person who has decided not to look. [288]

Below the cliff, the first humpbacks of the season were already moving south, singing at frequencies the human ear mostly cannot hold. [310] The tower hummed with it, an unheard symphony pressing against the glass of the world. [325]

That night Vera found the pattern. [331] It was worn into the tile's glaze, a threadbare route linking the sixteen numbers in an order no book recorded, using every cell exactly once. [356] Her grandmother's fingers had walked it so often the ceramic remembered them. [368] Etta had called such things forgotten patterns, ways of going that people stop walking and then stop remembering. [386]

And here was what Vera did not say aloud: she had not come to the tower only to mourn. [405] She had come to sculpt space for reflection, to hollow out a room in herself the way Etta had hollowed this tower, open at the top so something could move through. [436] So at midnight, with the roof open to the cold wheel of stars, she did the unreasonable thing. [454] She set the tile on the observatory desk, took Etta's chalk, and began to transcribe. [469] Sixteen numbers, copied in the forgotten order, one chalk figure at a time onto slate. [484] Nothing happened at one. [488] Nothing at two. [491] At four she smelled coffee and old wool, Etta's smell, and thought she was imagining it. [507] At eleven she was not imagining it. [514]

The memories arrived entangled, braided, nobody's alone. [521] Etta at this desk one last October, transcribing with cold hands while a whale sang beneath the cliff; and threaded through that memory another strand rising, seen from elsewhere: a hospital window, spring light, an old woman's face, a young voice asking Etta to promise that the tower would stay open for her son. [575] Sam's mother. [577] The tile did not store the past; it wove rememberers together, each memory arriving through the other, the way the square's lines cross at different angles yet all hold the same sum. [609] Vera's chalk shook, and she kept writing, because stopping felt like leaving someone alone in a dark room. [627] Courage, she decided, was mostly kindness that refuses to sit down. [638] She finished at sixteen. [642] When the last figure closed, the tower filled: whale song from below, wind and stars from above, and beneath it a low human humming, Etta's tune, surfacing in Vera's own chest as if it had always been hers. [680] She wept at last, inside the room she had built. [690]

In the morning Sam climbed the stairs with his aunt's lunch pail, and Vera, who owed him honesty, showed him what she could. [713] She set the tile before him, chalk beside it. [722] "It's a path," she said. [727] "Copy it slowly, and if your mother comes, don't be afraid of what she remembers with you." [744] He transcribed all morning while the whales went by below, and Vera stood in the doorway listening to the symphony no one could hear, believing, for once, every note. [773]
