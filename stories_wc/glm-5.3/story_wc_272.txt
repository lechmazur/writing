The decommissioning notice arrived on the same day the sea took the ferry dock, and Marguerite understood, with a crestfallen calm, that the world was coming apart at the seams. [30] She was a portrait artist, or had been, until her sitters' faces began to slip, cheekbones migrating like dunes, eyes drifting toward the edges of the canvas, as though reality had grown tired of holding still. [66] Her aunt's lighthouse held a forgotten library, three spiraling floors of salt-warped shelves wedged beneath the lamp room, and Marguerite climbed into it the way others enter churches, hoping to be small enough to be overlooked. [102]

On the seventh shelf, behind a drowned man's atlas, she found a broken tarot card box. [118] The lid had split along one corner, and the cards lay scattered like teeth. [132] She recognized the deck; her aunt had read it every storm night, laying out futures to keep the past quiet. [152] But the deck was incomplete, and something worse: each image had fractured, the Moon broken into three moons, the Tower sprouting from the middle of the sea. [179]

Outside, the horizon had begun to fold. [186] Or perhaps it was the library shrinking, shelves leaning inward, the walls closing in with the patience of ice. [205] Was this what the end looked like, not fire but erosion? [216] Had the world always been held together by nothing more than attention, and had everyone simply stopped paying it? [235]

Then she saw the threads. [240] They hung everywhere, gossamer filaments strung from shelf to shelf and card to card, frayed, knotted, some already snapped. [259] Attention, she realized. [262] Every thread was a moment of noticing: her aunt reading the deck, a sailor memorizing the atlas, a child tracing coastlines with a jammy finger. [287] The world was a loom, and its weavers had all gone. [298]

Mending would not be enough; the old futures were obsolete. [308] The deck needed to be redesigned, redrawn for a world that had changed its shape, and the threads she needed lay below, in the lighthouse's stone root, in the dark she had sworn never to enter again. [345] Her aunt had died in that dark, fallen on the lowest stair with cards scattering around her like black birds, and Marguerite had never gone past the seventh step since. [375] The lamp above had hung dark ever since. [383] Was there any other way to grieve than by refusing to? [394] She descended anyway, because the darkness was necessary now, and necessary things do not wait to be invited. [412]

The stairs wound down through cold into older cold. [421] Her hands found the wall, then the banister, then nothing at all. [433] At the bottom, in that necessary darkness, she knelt and felt for the cards her aunt had dropped three winters ago. [454] The missing threads were wound through them, knotted around the final card, and when she lifted that card to the small window's gray light, she saw it was not Death, as she had feared, but the Star. [491] Untangling them was patient work, fingertip work, the work of a woman who had spent thirty years blending skin tones from minerals and bone, and with every knot she loosened, a memory came free as well: her aunt lamplit, laughing, saying that a portrait is only a future you can look at. [543]

She carried the threads and the Star up into the library, laid the deck out, and began to redesign it, restringing a thread through the split box so the scattered cards hung together once more. [578] With a sable brush and pigments ground from the library's own dust, she painted the three moons back into one, returned the Tower to dry land, and drew new figures for the unnamed cards, her sitters, the ferryman, her aunt, herself, a deck that would answer to the people still willing to notice the world. [633] The walls were close now, close enough to touch, and dust sifted down like snow. [648] Would it be enough? [652] Was one woman's attention ever enough for anything? [660]

She climbed to the lamp room, wound the last thread around the lamp's ancient wick, and set it alight. [679] The light turned. [682] The walls paused, then eased outward, like a breath released after years of being held. [697] Below, the sea smoothed its fragments into one gray sheet, and the horizon remembered where it had left itself. [716]

Marguerite sat in the turning glow, ache and light braided together, and began at last to paint her aunt's portrait from memory. [738] The walls would close in again someday; all walls do. [748] But she would be here, brush ready, asking the dark her questions, for hadn't the light always been something you had to make yourself? [772]
