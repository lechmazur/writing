The island of Vael lost an arm's length of shore every year, and Mara, who had coiled rope on its docks since she was seven, knew exactly where the sea kept it. [32] She was the most graceful worker on the docks, out of necessity rather than vanity: the island tilted, faintly, with every breath of the thing beneath it, and clumsy feet ended up in the harbor. [67]

Nobody called the thing a whale aloud, though everyone knew, the same way everyone knew why knives stopped cutting skin forty years ago, when the whales sang the Quiet into the world's waters and violence became impossible. [104] Pirates had beached their ships that same season, among them Mara's grandmother, who traded her cutlass for a chisel and spent her last years carving up the whalebone she had taken in her raiding days. [139] The finest piece she left to Mara: a pirate's whalebone scrimshaw, polished dark as a hazelnut, showing Vael riding on the back of a whale, and on its reverse a small carved heart, hollowed with the shape of a keyhole. [179] Her mother, the harbormaster, kept it locked in her chest, along with everything else on Vael that might unlock a guarded heart. [201]

Because Vael was never settled, in the old violent years; it was captured. [214] The founders drove a heart-stone into the harbor, ran a great hawser down through the rocks, and shackled it into the whale's old harpoon scar so their island would stay put forever. [246] After the Quiet, the whale could not fight them, so it dived instead, gently, year after year, wearing itself against the line, and every dive dragged another arm's length of Vael under. [278] That was the disappearing, Mara understood one night with the scrimshaw warm in her stolen fist: not an island dying, but a whale grieving against a chain. [305]

She took the scrimshaw on the night of the lowest spring tide, and her mother caught her at the harbor stair, as she had known she would. [332] "You'll unmoor us all," her mother said, gripping the rail, gripping her own arms, gripping anything but her daughter. [351] "We're already unmoored, Mama," Mara said. [357] "We've been drowning politely, an inch a year, and you keep calling it home." [371] "Your grandmother carved that bone as an apology," her mother said, "not as a key." [386] "Then it can't hurt to try," Mara said, kissed her mother's furious cheek, and went down into the water. [405]

The tide had drawn back farther than she had ever seen, laying bare the sublittoral fringe: ribbons of kelp, anemones clenched like fists that could no longer strike, the hawser lying fat and green down the shelving rock. [443] At its end, sunk into the whale's scar, hung the shackle, forged in the shape of a heart and pierced by a keyhole exactly the size of the one on the scrimshaw. [475] Mara pressed bone heart to iron heart, keyhole to keyhole, and felt the island holding its breath beneath her knees. [495] Nothing happened. [497] Bone could not unlock iron; her mother was right; an apology was only an apology. [512]

Then the call of a whale rose from the deep, so low she heard it in her teeth rather than her ears, and the scrimshaw began to sing. [540] The note passed through both keyholes, bone to iron, and somewhere inside the heart-shackle a pin shivered, turned, and slid. [561] Mara detached the hawser with both hands, and the great line fell away into the dark like a held breath finally let go. [584]

Vael lifted. [586] Not drowning, not dissolving, but rising, the whole harbor tilting as the whale beneath it rose and rolled its shoulders free after a hundred years. [611] Water poured off the lower streets, and lanterns came alight in doorways, and her mother came stumbling down the rocks with her skirts kelp-wet, shouting her name in a voice that cracked halfway between fury and joy. [648] "You horrible, wonderful girl," she said, which on Vael meant thank you, and I love you, and I have been so afraid. [670] The whale sounded once, softly, and the island moved. [679]

By morning, Vael had vanished from every chart, a disappearing island at last keeping its promise, riding the whale's back past the shelf and out into the open sea. [708] Mara worked the moving docks as gracefully as ever, reading the tilt of each whale breath, and found that freedom felt less like leaving home than like admitting what she had always been: her grandmother's blood, the sea's tenant, a creature made for carrying and being carried. [755] When the whale called from the deep, which it did most nights now, the whole island answered like a bell, and no heart on Vael stayed locked for long. [784]
