Every dusk, the great dial above Vesper Crossing drank the real sky and gave it back as pale green fire, so the town called it borrowed starlight. [27] Halyna Ivarsdottir, choral librarian of the astronomical clock tower, kept six hundred hymnals in three tongues and knew every verse the prairie had ever argued over. [53] North bank, the Icelandic elevator co-op; south bank, the Ukrainian one; between them the Silt River and seventy years of silence. [74] Tonight the grain elevator talks convened for the fourth and surely last time under the hush of borrowed starlight. [93] Halyna came carrying a brass pinecone the size of a fist, the missing cuckoo clock weight, found that morning folded inside her grandmother's red hymnal. [118] The founders' Black Forest cuckoo had hung speechless since 1954, when the weight vanished and each bank accused the other of stealing it. [141] Silence, not the river, was the true border. [149]

In the tower chamber, the only ground neither bank owned, Old Stefan and Widow Marta sat armed with ledgers and grievances. [170] The rail company would fund one spur only, on one bank, and whoever lost it would watch their wheat rot. [190] Halyna set the weight on the table between them. [199] "Whoever holds this speaks," she said, "and everyone hears them whole." [210] Stefan gripped it and thundered about quotas; Marta answered in kind; the old anger stood up and stretched. [228]

Halyna, whose whole trade was harmony, tender as a choir director and twice as stubborn, lifted her hands. [246] "Rotting korn," she said, "rotting wheat, hnyle zhito." [254] She gave each side the other's fear in its own language, then again, interspersing the tongues until Stefan's grandson on the north benches and Marta's nieces on the south began finishing sentences they had not started. [290] "Bridges, bruar, mosty," she said, "bridges from silos," and laid her sketches flat. [303] Not rhetoric, but drawings: the two derelict silos that leaned on either bank like bad teeth, unbolted into steel staves, those staves latticed into a span. [329] A bridge built from the co-ops' own bones, wide enough for grain carts, joining both elevators to the single spur. [349]

"You want to tear down our silo," Marta said. [358] "I want to rehabilitate it," Halyna said, "into the reason your grandchildren stay." [371] She had the engineer's estimate already, the rail company's letter already, the schedule of shared loading days drafted in her own hand, because empathy without decision was just weather. [400] Stefan read the numbers twice. [405] "Who pays for the bolts?" he asked, in Ukrainian, badly, and the south bank laughed for the first time in years. [426] "We split them," Marta answered in Icelandic, worse, and the north bank laughed back. [440] That was the hinge; Halyna felt the room turn on it. [451]

While the clerks scratched out the split, she climbed the tower stair with the brass pinecone and hooked it, at last, to its chain. [475] Grandmother had found it in the hymnal returns in 1954, hidden it inside that red hymnal, and left one line: wind it home only when both banks can share one room. [506] She wound. [508] Gears woke like a congregation. [513] The little door snapped open and the cuckoo sang ten into the borrowed starlight, and both banks, spilling onto the stair to hear, stood shoulder to shoulder without noticing whose shoulders. [544] Someone began an old round; it traveled across the landing in three languages, verse answering verse, and the tower held the harmony. [566] By midnight the bridge was voted, the silos deeded, and the weight hung gleaming like a small brass moon. [585]

Harvest came loud that year, carts rattling across silo steel with a vigor the valley had forgotten, and quiet tenderness underneath it, hands steadying old enemies over the new planks. [615] The spur belonged to everyone, and the debt, and so did the argument about whose turn it was, which was, Halyna noted, a kind of music. [641] That spring she cataloged a new hymnal, one round composed for the bridge's opening, its verses Ukrainian, Icelandic, and plain prairie English interleaved so no voice sang alone. [669] On clear nights she climbed past the sleeping bird to the dial, where painted constellations glowed on what the day gave them, and she thought of her grandmother, who had hidden a small heavy truth for seventy years until the town grew hands gentle enough to hold it. [717] Even now the cuckoo calls the hour in no language at all, and both banks answer, and the borrowed starlight is enough. [739]
