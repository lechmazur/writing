The city of Sargasso lived in the throat of a gargantuan shell, and the shell never slept, and neither did Vess. [21] She was the youngest of the night-singers, an insomniac poet sworn to keep the Blossom Armistice alive. [38] Three centuries before her birth, the Coral Quarter and the Pearl Terraces had ended their salt war by planting one seed in the scar of the old bombardment. [66] When its vine flowered, the killing stopped; when grudges stirred, the vine grayed, and only a lullaby could coax it back to bloom. [89]

Vess knew exactly one lullaby, and she only half knew that. [100] It had ridden the shell's wind for generations, handed from singer to singer, and the wind itself sang flat, so every note arrived bent, apologizing. [125] That was the method, her grandmother had taught her: half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind, because perfection sounds like a promise, and a broken note sounds like a person. [155] Vess stammered in daylight and blushed at doorways; at night she was shyly unstoppable, a small woman with a voice that would not lie down. [180]

She kept one treasure beneath her pillow: a galaxy postcard, printed with a spiral of stars, mailed by her great-grandmother's beloved on the last ship out of the shell's mouth. [210] He had promised to write when he reached the lights he saw in dreams, and he had, once, this once, a single card. [233] On the back, above the stamp, one unfinished line: "The stars are a shell we have not yet learned to hear." [254] For a hundred years the sentence had waited for its ending, the way the vine waited for spring, the way Vess waited for sleep. [278] The card had outlived its sender and its first reader both; longing in Sargasso moved across centuries, patient as tide. [298]

Then came the dry summer the Terraces dammed the dew-wells, and the Quarter threatened fire, and the vine's blossoms closed like fists. [320] Gray crept up the stems the way it had in the oldest engravings of the war. [336] The elders debated embargoes. [340] Vess, who could not order soup without rehearsal, went to the core of the shell. [355] There, in the smallest whorl where the wind was born, she ensconced herself among fern and fallen scale, small and stubborn as a hermit crab in a borrowed throne. [384] She set the galaxy postcard upright against a stone, so the spiral faced her, star-mouth to wind-mouth. [401]

All night she did what her line had always done, what she was for: she photosynthesized pain into beauty. [420] Grief, her grandmother said, is only light that entered you at a bad angle; the work of a singer is to turn her face until it feeds you. [448] So Vess turned. [451] She sang the half-known lullaby into the birth-wind, and the wind, off-key as ever, bent her crooked notes through every chamber of the great shell. [476] The sound arrived in the Terraces like a mother who could not carry a tune; it arrived in the Quarter like a father humming outside a door he was afraid to knock on. [509] Hard people lay down in hard beds. [516] They dreamed each other's thirst, each other's burned gardens, each other's children. [528] No one called it surrender. [533]

By dawn the dew-wells were open, and the vine had put out buds the color of the inside of a shell. [554] They opened at noon, and the Blossom Armistice renewed itself, as it had done and would do across centuries of longing, one shy voice at a time. [581] Vess slept, finally, at the core, and the wind kept humming her scraps for her, off-key, tireless, a lullaby's echo learning to live alone. [605]

When she woke, she took up the postcard and finished the dead man's line with a poet's theft. [623] Beneath "we have not yet learned to hear," she wrote: "but someone is singing in it, and the shell answers." [643] She propped the card in the vine's new blossoms, facing the harbor mouth, addressed to no one, which is to say to everyone. [666]

The joy in Sargasso was never loud; it was reef-shadow joy, the kind that flickers under overhangs where the timid fish live, quick and bright and easily missed. [694] But Vess saw it from the core that evening: lanterns drifting down both terraces, old enemies nodding across the vine, her crooked lullaby still loose in the wind, slightly wrong, entirely enough. [726] She opened her notebook, and, being shy, wrote the city a love letter it would never know was addressed to it. [747]
