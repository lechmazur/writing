The gold would last seven minutes, and Wren meant to spend every one of them kneeling on the ice. [19] Her azure coat was government issue, stiff with salt, the only warm thing the drift-ice marina had given her that still fit. [41] Beneath her boots the ice had come early, rafted and frozen into a crooked white street between the trapped boats, and beneath the floes the drowned were knocking. [69]

That was the trouble. [73] Every evening at sunset she sounded the lighthouse horn, and the tone rolled out over the pack like a verdict of mercy, and the knocking stopped. [99] Tonight the horn had sung the old note and the knocking had only quickened, small fists working the underside of the ice, patient and terrible. [124]

The crypt keeper's key lay against her sternum on a cord, brass warmed by her own blood. [141] Mother Sabine, the old crypt keeper, had pressed it into her hands the winter she died: the key that opened the crypt beneath the lighthouse, where the first drowned lay in ordered rows, waiting. [175] It was a sacred thing, cut from the bell that had gone down with them, and the marina's whole liturgy of sound depended on it. [200] Wren was not the keeper. [205] She was the engineer. [209] But Sabine had chosen her anyway, and Wren had understood, in her cold arithmetic way, that refusing would be a kind of theft. [232] The harbor office had written twice demanding she surrender the key for scrap, certain the old rites were obsolete; if tonight's mercy failed, they would be right. [259]

She knelt. [261] She pulled off her gloves, laid her palms flat on the ice, and the flesh went instantly to fire. [280] Logically, she told herself, grief is only the body measuring distance. [291] She measured now. [294]

The knocking had a frequency, and the ice had a frequency, and the old absolution tone had been tuned for open water, before the pack came in and changed everything the sound crossed. [327] The dead were not louder tonight. [333] They were mismatched. [336] Sonic absolution could not land on a soul it could not reach. [348] So she would adapt. [352]

She bowed her head and listened through her skin. [361] The tremor came up her wrist bones like a struck string: low, arrhythmic, human. [375] Her brother had knocked like that, once, on her workshop door, two beats and a pause and two beats, the winter before his boat went down. [401] Her grief rose, and she did what she always did: read it like an instrument panel, let it tell her the number she needed. [425] Love, plotted honestly, was only a reference tone. [433]

She climbed to the horn room, the sunset already thinning to a rind of copper on the horizon. [451] She took the key from her neck and struck it once against the rail. [465] It rang, not like brass but like the bell it had been cut from, a drowned note, a note the dead already trusted. [488] That was the sacred function Sabine had never explained: the key remembered them. [501] Match the horn to the key, and the horn would match the ice, and the ice would match the souls. [521] Vibrational matching, the manuals would have called it, if any manual admitted the dead. [535] She set the key singing on the console and worked the horn's slides and baffles, bending the great voice down by feel, by marrow, until the two hums closed on each other like hands. [569]

A wire of gold remained. [574] She pulled the cord. [578] The tone went out low and immense, not a verdict now but an arm laid across a shoulder. [596] It moved through the drift-ice as a shudder, and the shudder matched the knocking, and where the two met the knocking softened, then spread, then smoothed, like fists unclenching all down the marina. [629] She ran down and knelt on the floe before the echo settled. [641]

Beneath the ice, faint, she heard two beats and a pause and two beats. [655] She pressed her mouth to the frozen surface, breath blooming back at her. [668] "It was the storm, not your calculations; you were never to blame; go to sleep," she said, logically, lovingly. [687] The answer came once more, softer, then not at all. [697]

The dark came up off the water and took the last of the gold. [711] Wren put the key back against her sternum, pulled her gloves over her burning hands, and opened the ledger to write the new frequency, so tomorrow's mercy would know where to land. [743] She was, she understood now, both trades at once: the engineer who kept the light and the keeper who kept the key, kneeling each evening above the forgiven, her body the last warm instrument in a cold, singing harbor.
