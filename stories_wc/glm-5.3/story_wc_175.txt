Vessa counted wingbeats the way other people counted breaths, because rhythm was the only honest language left. [17] She was an esoteric realist, which meant she built altars and then measured their shadow lines. [33] The verdant grotto above the clouds held her work: a bowl of moss and epiphyte, green to the rim, floating on white. [55] Epiphytes lived here the way she did, transiently rooted, holding on without owning anything. [69] For eleven autumns she had built temporary structures as signals, towers of seed husk and twig that dissolved in the first rain. [91] The pattern of each tower recorded something history had stepped over: a beetle no journal had named, a frog whose last call she alone had transcribed, a moth. [119] The moth wing lived in a matchbox against her heart, gray and dusted, taken from the ridge's last erratic moth nine years ago. [142] Nobody had mourned it, so she did, annually, quietly, with geometry. [153] This autumn the world's last monarch butterfly was migrating south, a single animal carrying a species on its back, and every keeper on the flyway had agreed that Vessa's grotto sat beneath its probable final arc. [189] They called the chain of them the Quiet Line, building signals nobody official would ever read. [205] On the third morning a fleck of orange crossed the cloud sea, and Vessa raised her binoculars and counted. [224] Eight wingbeats, glide, eight wingbeats, glide: the cadence every old recording had promised. [237] Then it broke. [240] Five beats, a stall, an ugly sideways drift, four beats. [250] She had watched enough dying rhythms to know what an interrupted cadence meant. [263] The monarch was faltering, and a faltering monarch looks for height and green, which meant the grotto, which meant her. [283] Her realist mind ran the numbers: body fat spent, a storm front behind it, four hundred kilometers since the lowlands. [303] Her esoteric mind ran the pattern: a species ending its story at the one place built to receive it. [322] Both minds agreed. [325] She began to build. [329] Not a monument, nothing permanent, nothing that would presume to own the moment. [342] She lashed sunflower stalks into a low spire, wove sweet flag through the ribs, and set a dish of sugared water at its base, a signal that doubled as mercy. [372] Then she took down the matchbox. [378] The moth wing had waited nine years for a purpose larger than grief. [391] She threaded a grass fiber through the wing's fragile fringe and tied it to the spire's apex, finishing with the same fisherman's knot her grandmother had used on mourning wreaths, three loops pulled snug. [425] The knot held the wing so it could tremble without falling, which she felt was the correct relationship between history and the wind. [448] At dusk the monarch came over the rim like a small burning letter. [461] Its broken rhythm read clearly now: seven, stall, three, drift. [471] It circled the spire twice, found the dish, and drank, wings opening and closing in a slow apology. [489] Then it climbed to an epiphyte pad beside the moth wing and settled, feet gripping moss, transiently rooted at the end of an ancient road. [514] Vessa sat on her heels and did not touch it. [524] Cloud peeled off the valley below in slow pink sheets, and a blushing stillness filled the grotto, the hush of a room where someone sleeps who must not be woken. [554] She recorded everything: temperature, wind, the monarch's torn left hindwing, the exact minute of its landing. [570] Honoring overlooked histories, she had learned, was mostly this: paying attention on behalf of everyone who had not. [588] The moth wing trembled beside the monarch all night, gray beside orange, the forgotten dead attending the famous dying. [607] At dawn the butterfly opened its wings once, fully, a stained window letting the light through. [623] Then the cadence stopped altogether, and stillness of a different weight settled over the moss. [638] Vessa worked through the morning with steady hands. [646] She logged the death for the Quiet Line, built a final tower of seed husk and monarch-orange petals, and carried the moth wing over to knot it at the top. [676] The monarch itself she left on its moss pad, where it had chosen to end, returned to the grotto's keeping. [696] The first rain would take it all, spire and petals and fiber, and that was correct too. [713] Signals are not meant to last; they are meant to be caught. [725] Before she climbed down, she touched the knot once, three loops snug against the morning light. [741] Then she spoke the only liturgy she trusted: the moth's name, given by her since no one else would, then the monarch's, then her own. [766] The clouds below had gone rose, the grotto held its breath, and somewhere behind her the wing trembled on its fiber, still signaling, still caught. [791]
