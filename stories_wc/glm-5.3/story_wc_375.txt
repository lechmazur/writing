Vesna waited in the dark beneath her mother's house, charcoal still ghosting her fingertips, until the laughter of the mourners upstairs thinned and the cave gave back its final echo. [30] Silence now, except for the mineral drip of water and the slow breathing of light. [45] The crystals lining the walls burned without heat, green deepening into violet, violet into a green so pale it hurt, like the aurora her mother had chased across thirty winters of canvas and never caught. [80] It was grief-light, Vesna thought, the kind that makes sorrow visible. [91]

Upstairs they had laughed, her aunts and the neighbors, remembering Mother as the woman who painted the harbor so nicely, so tamely, and they had laughed harder at the one wild canvas propped beside the coffin, the aurora one, calling it a scribble. [134] Vesna had not defended it, because she had spent forty years not defending anything, least of all the itch beneath her own charcoal. [157] She was, the whole town knew, the portrait sketch artist of the Saturday market, the woman with charcoal-blackened fingers who could draw any face in the square in eleven minutes flat. [188] Nobody suspected what the town called diligence was flight. [197]

Vesna remembered everything; that was her curse and her craft. [207] Every face she had ever drawn still hung inside her skull, ten thousand likenesses in perfect graphite order, and she could reproduce any of them with her eyes shut, which was exactly the problem, because a memory that perfect leaves no room for invention. [251] She had watched, at age six, her mother's wrist spin the combination of the paint cabinet: twenty-seven left, four right, sixty-one left, and the memory had never dimmed. [279]

The cabinet stood where it always had, at the foot of the easel, and the lock still clung to its hasp. [300] She knelt, spun the numbers out of the deep cellar of her mind, and pulled. [315] Nothing. [316] The dial turned sweetly, the numbers seated with a click she felt in her teeth, but the shackle did not lift; the mechanism inside had rusted through and broken, years ago probably, long before it mattered to anyone. [354] It was a joke with a long fuse: she had kept the combination safe for forty years, perfect and useless, because memory cannot unbend sprung steel. [380]

Vesna laughed once, a cracked sound the crystals caught and passed around the dark until it came back to her sounding like her mother's voice, and then she let herself weep. [411] When her eyes cleared she saw the easel properly. [420] On it hung the harbor commission, the safe picture, boats and gulls and a polite gray sky, the painting the town had loved her mother for. [446] But the frame hung a finger's width off true, tilted the way nothing of her mother's was ever tilted, and behind its lower edge Vesna saw the corner of something that was not stone. [480] She lifted the painting off the easel. [487] Behind it, wedged into a seam of the crystal wall, hidden for who knew how many years behind the tame picture the town had demanded, leaned a sheaf of canvases and a wooden box of paint tubes, brushes, and a folded note. [529]

The canvases were auroras. [533] Green rivers of it, violet wounds, skies poured on sideways, dozens of them, all painted here in this cave where the light itself moved like the northern sky, the true work her mother had hidden the day the town first laughed at it. [576] The note said: The lock was for them, dear one, not for you; I knew you would remember the numbers, and I knew they would not help you; a perfect memory is a door that only opens backward; look behind what you are shown. [620]

Vesna understood that her mother had left her not an inheritance but a task: a wrong to rectify, and the tools to rectify it. [644] Two wrongs, in truth. [648] Tomorrow she would carry the auroras up into the daylight and hang them where the laughers could not avoid them, would let the town see what it had mocked into hiding. [679] But tonight belonged to the second wrong, the older one, the cork she had driven into her own chest at eleven years old when the laughter had first found her sketches. [710]

She squeezed a tube of violet onto her palm, then a green, and smeared them together with her thumb, and the charcoal of forty years looked at the color and did not recognize itself. [744] There was no escaping it, she saw now; the calling had simply waited, patient as crystal, for the last echo of laughter to fade. [768] She set a blank canvas on the easel and, with hands still black and now beginning to bloom green, painted the sorrowful light. [791]
