The lunar observatory stood above the clouds, where the air was too thin for bells and too honest for echoes. [20] Mara had kept its cameras for thirty years, a sage repairer of lenses that watched the moon the way widows watch doors. [42] Her sister Ilsa had left on the night ascent, when the rockets in the valley burned escape velocity out of the mountain's bones. [65] That same hour, the brass hand of the great clock began spinning and never stopped. [80] Time had simply refused to behave since. [87] Eleven years, and the hand had not slowed once, the only steady thing left in a station the world had forgotten. [108] The government's letter called the observatory condemned. [115] The glacier beneath it was melting, and every week the foundations shook, sifting dust through the dome like gray snow. [135] When the foundations shook hardest, Mara climbed the stair with her tools, because tonight Ilsa's ship would cross the face of the moon. [158] Once a year the ship passed in silhouette, a black seed of metal, silent, small enough to miss if she blinked. [179] Its radio had gone quiet in the second year; its orbit had not. [192] Her motive was simple and impossible: to harness the ephemeral, to hold one heartbeat of proof that her sister still existed. [213] The great camera had been built for comets, its shutter tuned to explosions of light. [228] A crossing ship gave almost nothing. [234] So Mara had spent the winter learning to restructure it, trading force for listening. [248] She rebuilt the trigger around a resonator plate no larger than a coin and taught the mechanism to wake to the softest signal in the world: by the sound of a leaf falling. [281] The leaf was pressed in Ilsa's field journal, carried up from the valley forty years ago, brown as an old eye. [302] There were no trees above the clouds; this one leaf was the entire history of green the observatory owned. [321] Mara laid it on the plate and waited. [329] The moon rose, enormous, pouring out light eight minutes old and changelessly constant, obedient to the same law since the first dawn. [351] Ilsa used to say that light never lied, that it crossed the universe at one unbargained speed, carrying each moment faithfully away. [373] Every photograph, she said, was a reunion. [380] At midnight the mountain shifted; the dome groaned, and dust fell through the moonlight like slow rain. [397] Mara did not flinch. [401] She had folded the orbital tables for years like love letters, and the crossing needed one clean second. [419] At 2:14 by a clock whose hand spun without meaning, a mote crossed the silver. [434] Mara lifted the leaf a finger's width above the plate and let go. [447] It turned once in the dead air. [454] It landed with no sound any ear could name, but the resonator heard it, and the shutter opened like a held breath released. [477] Light poured through glass Mara had polished for three decades and wrote the moon onto the film: craters, silver seas, and a small dark seed crossing them, faithful and far. [507] She counted the full second like a vow, then closed the shutter by hand. [521] The dome groaned again, deeper, and a crack ran across the ceiling like a signature. [536] Mara lifted the leaf from the plate with tweezers, the way one lifts a relic, and returned it to the journal between the pressed years. [561] The clock hand spun on, marking not hours but momentum, all her years fleeing upward at escape velocity. [579] Let it spin, she thought. [584] A clock that refused to settle was a truer instrument than any that pretended time could be kept. [602] She descended at dawn with the film canister warm in her coat, past walls shedding plaster like bark. [620] In the valley darkroom the image surfaced under red light, the moon blooming on paper, and there, sharp as grief, the small ship. [643] The shadow was minutes old: sunlight that had crossed ninety-three million miles just to carry her sister's shape down to her. [664] Mara pressed the photograph against the window where morning was beginning, a luminous ache she could finally hold. [682] Some things must reach escape velocity to be free of us. [693] And some, falling as gently as a leaf, come back. [703]
