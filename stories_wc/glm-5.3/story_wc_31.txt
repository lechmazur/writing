Mara was the pier's late-shift lamplighter, and on the last night of summer she lit the lamps from the landward end outward, as she had for nine years. [28] The work asked nothing of her ears anymore. [36] Deafness had been coming for her the way tide comes for sand, patient and total, and she had slowly rearranged her whole life around the sounds that remained. [64] Light she could keep. [68] Behind her, the town was folding paper lanterns and writing vows on them, because a vow written honestly stays luminous, and a luminous vow, the old people said, can be read even from under the sea. [104] Mara carried no lantern. [108] She carried a brass skeleton key on a worn tag stamped MERIDIAN HOTEL, and when anyone asked about her slow, searching walks down the pier, she answered with the plain truth: she was checking her lamps. [144] The truth hid her neatly. [149] No one guessed she was mapping the tide, waiting for the one night each year when the water pulled back far enough to bare the black ribs at the pier's end. [180]

The Meridian had been a grand hotel once, and when it closed, its library was crated aboard a freighter bound for auction overseas. [203] A storm sank the ship within sight of shore, and the town later built its pier directly over the grave. [223] Mara's grandmother had been the Meridian's night clerk, who swore she could see sounds as colors, and she had pressed this key into Mara's palm with one instruction: when the lanterns fly, bring it home. [258] That winter her grandmother died; her hearing began to go; and she understood the two losses as one inheritance being settled. [279] What she wanted, before the silence finished, was to learn the color of sound. [293] Her grandmother's glossary of it lay somewhere in that drowned library, and Mara meant to find it while the sky filled with light. [316]

The first lanterns rose just as the tide reached its lowest ebb, and she took her hand-lamp down the pier ladder into the wreck. [340] Inside, tilted decks had become corridors, and the burst crates had long ago resolved into something impossible: shelves, upright and orderly, silted but unbroken, a forgotten library keeping itself in the dark. [372] A man in a clerk's coat moved between the stacks, not floating, only walking, though his feet found no floor. [392] For sixty years, he said, he had wandered these shelves, and the wandering was purposeful: every book reshelved, every page aired, the catalogue held ready for whoever came. [420] She asked if he was the librarian, and he said he was the man who had been reading when the water came. [442] She asked if he was dead, and he said he had not been hungry since the storm. [459] He hid behind truth, she realized, the way she hid behind her lamps: every answer honest, none of them whole. [479] Then she held up the skeleton key, the tag caught what little light there was, and something in him settled. [499]

"That signifies a claim," he said. [505] "The master key has come home; the debt closes tonight." [515] He led her to a reading desk where a notebook lay, its pages dry, its cover stamped with her grandmother's initials. [536] The glossary filled every page: a high G is sea-glass green; rain on a tin roof is the silver of fish scales; a mother's voice warming is copper catching light. [566] And folded at the back, a slip of paper still glowed, a vow written decades ago in honest ink: I will teach my granddaughter to see her own name. [595] Above her, through the broken deck, the released lanterns began to pass, hundreds of them, a slow constellation climbing. [614] Their light fell through the gaps and lay across the pages, and Mara, matching color to entry, began to hear the way her grandmother had. [639] A red lantern crossing overhead was a trumpet; the blue ones were children calling; the gold ones, rising fastest, were laughter. [660] Every color signified a sound, the whole sky was speaking, and she could finally see it. [676]

The final entry was blank, and it was hers to finish. [687] Her own name, she saw, was the color of her hand-lamp two heartbeats after first light. [703] She wrote the entry, and beneath it her vow, and the words took light: I will light a lamp for this library every night I live. [729] "A claim honored and a new vow lit," he said. [739] "My shift is over." [743] He was gone between one word and the next, only silt where his coat had been. [759] Mara climbed back up into the meteor hush, carrying two names' worth of light.
