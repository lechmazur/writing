Mara Voss had taught navigation in the benthic city of Cirra for twenty-two years, and in all that time she had never once shown her students the sky. [28] No one down here had. [33] Cirra clung to the abyssal plain beside the Lattice, the alien structure discovered thirty-one years ago, and the city existed because of it the way a mission town exists because of a cathedral. [66] The Lattice was a scaffolding of dark mineral, older than agriculture, silent, and unread. [80] In the first greedy year a salvage crew tried to cut it, and it sealed itself, folding inward like a closing hand. [102]

Mara's subject was edge geometry, the discipline of fixing your position by angles: where the thermocline's faint lid met the black water, where the plain's lip dropped into the trench, where ridge lines crossed. [136] The deep has no landmarks, only edges, and she taught three hundred students to read them. [152] At the center of her classroom hung a single boat anchor chain link on a thread of braided line. [171] It had come down from her mother, a surface sailor who anchored the founding ships above this very spot and never once came below. [195] Her mother was three years gone now, buried at sea the way sailors are, somewhere above the classroom's plumb line. [215] Steel sinks honestly, she told her students, so this is our plumb line, the last true vertical from the world of sky. [237]

Mara was, her colleagues agreed, balancedly curious, meaning she wondered about everything and trusted nothing but angles. [254] That balance was why she noticed what the salvage crews and the shouting professors had missed. [270] The Lattice's facets did not meet at structural angles; they met at sighting angles, the kind you take between a star and the horizon's edge. [295] The aliens had left a star map, and a star map is legible only to someone who can navigate by starlight. [316] No one in Cirra could. [321] The old celestial skill had died with the surface fleets, and Mara, born under the waves, wanted it the way the drowned want breath. [345] So she learned. [348]

She studied her mother's water-stained manuals, memorized fifty-seven navigational stars, and once a year requisitioned the survey submersible for a night ascent. [370] The ascent was the part she never managed to describe. [380] As the submersible rose, the sea's edges opened one by one, darkness thinning into blue, blue into a ceiling, and then the ceiling broke and the horizon unfolded, all the way around her, a perfect circle where water met sky. [420] She practiced her sights on that circle with a brass sextant, star by star, until her angles stopped trembling. [439] On the seventh such night she found the match: a sight between Vega and the northern horizon that equaled, to the arc-minute, the angle between two facets of the sealed Lattice. [470]

She descended for three hours in silence and walked straight to the artifact with the anchor link swinging from her fist like a censer. [494] She hung the link on its line before the facets, let it settle into true vertical, and read the angle aloud, quietly, the way you speak to something that might be listening. [526] The Lattice expanded. [529] Not violently, not with machine hunger, but the way a flower opens at dusk: facet after facet swinging outward, a geometry unfolding into the dark water until the thing stood four times its size and the whole city was awake and staring. [571] The new facets were horizons. [576] Each ring of angles was a sky seen from a different world, a chain of sightings stretching outward like waypoints, and Mara, reading them one by one, understood that she was looking at a route. [611] The final facet's star was wrong, though, dim and swollen, a dying sun. [624] The travelers had fled their ending, crossed the dark, and left this one gift folded shut on our floor, waiting for someone patient enough to ask in the right geometry. [654]

Mara's curriculum expanded that same season. [660] Celestial navigation joined edge geometry in the course catalog, and the first class of deep-born children learned the names of stars they had never seen, taking practice sights against the thread of an anchor chain that still hung, plumb and honest, at the front of the room. [707] Some nights she stands at the newest viewport with the link in her pocket, looking up through half a mile of black water toward a sky she can finally read, and the feeling is somber and marvelous at once, like standing inside a horizon that is still unfolding. [755]
