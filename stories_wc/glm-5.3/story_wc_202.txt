The alchemist's market hung high above the city rooftops, its stalls strung between chimneys on cables and lantern chains. [19] People climbed rope ladders to buy moonwater and burnt honey. [29] Mara's stall was the smallest, a plank desk beneath a stained canvas awning. [42] She was a between-worlds translator. [47] The dead who had been overlooked in life could not speak plainly, so they spoke through hidden details in paintings. [67] A window left open in a family portrait, a dog waiting at a door, a shadow where nobody stood: these were sentences, and Mara read them. [93]

Painters stared for hours at what the world ignored, and their brushes remembered. [106] Her grandmother had taught her the trade. [113]

On the day Mara turned eight, Grandmother pressed a lighthouse lens fragment into her palm, a wedge of ground glass no bigger than a tooth. [138] "The lighthouse guided drowned sailors home for a hundred years," she said, "and when they tore it down, I saved this." [159] Intuition, she explained, drifts like a boat without an anchor. [169] The fragment was Mara's anchor of intuition: when she held it to a canvas, what she sensed became what she could see. [191] Grandmother had her own reason for the work. [199] Her mother appeared in a famous portrait, a washerwoman in the background, unnamed in every ledger. [215] "Honor the overlooked histories," Grandmother said, "or they stay unfinished." [225] She died when Mara was nineteen, and Mara inherited the stall, the awning, and the wedge of glass on its leather cord. [247]

Now a woman named Pell climbed the ladders, carrying a portrait wrapped in a bedsheet. [262] "My great-grandfather's house," she said. [267] "Behind him stands a woman with a ring of keys." [277] "No record names her." [281] "My family called her the housekeeper, and that is all we know." [293]

Mara unhooked the lantern overhead so its light fell flat and steady. [305] She lifted the lens fragment on its cord and held it near the painted woman. [320] Through the glass, the hidden details woke. [327] Her ring held seven keys, though the portrait showed six. [337] The woman's shadow pointed toward a door the room did not contain. [349] And in the lace at her cuff, worked so small that no casual eye could find it, ran letters: I kept this house standing when the fever came. [377] Mara translated aloud, and the market noise thinned as though the whole rooftop world had leaned in to listen. [396] Something elusively tangible filled the space beside her cheek, like the warmth of a hand through a curtain. [414]

"There's more," Mara said. [418] "Under the paint." [421] She could feel it, an intuition anchored deep, dragging like a hook. [433] "The original canvas was painted over." [439] Pell went pale. [442] "May we?" [444] Mara turned the frame face down and worked the small nails free with a whalebone pick. [460] She unfastened the backing board, panel by panel, until the canvas lay open like a page. [476] Then she pressed the lighthouse fragment flat against the linen and waited. [488]

The moment stretched and refused to end. [495] Up there among the lanterns, time did not pass so much as hold its breath; the instant turned eternal, a jar's clink two stalls away still hanging in the air, unfallen. [526] Beneath the top layer, an underpainting rose through the lens: the same woman, alone, seated where the master sat, the keys in her lap and a ledger open before her. [556] Household accounts, doctor's bills from the fever years, burial fees for children she had nursed: every line written in her hand. [577] "She ran everything," Mara whispered. [582] "The master couldn't write." [586] At the underpainting's lower edge, a signature: Agnes Rill, 1841. [596] Pell put both hands over her mouth. [603] "Agnes," she said. [606]

The warmth against Mara's cheek pressed closer, then loosened, satisfied, like a knot finally unfastened by patient fingers. [624] Mara sat back. [627] The eternal instant released them; a jar clinked, a seller laughed, and the cables creaked in the wind. [645] Pell paid in silver and climbed down carrying Agnes's name like a lamp. [658]

That night, Mara cleaned the lens fragment with her sleeve and hung it back on its cord. [675] Between childhood and now, she had translated cobblers and scullery maids, drowned sailors and unnamed midwives, each one speaking in brushstrokes. [696] Grandmother had been right about anchors. [702] Intuition alone was fog. [706] Anchored, it became a lighthouse. [711] And every overlooked history, brought into the light for one eternal instant, stayed lit. [725]
