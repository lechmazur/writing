The last ship sailed on a Tuesday, taking with it three hundred names, and after that the harbor belonged only to gulls and gray water. [25] Maren arrived that evening, as she did every autumn, walking up from the empty pier into the shadowed garden behind the old palace. [48] The garden was all copper leaves and long blue shade, its hedges clipped into shapes nobody remembered the names of. [68]

She was a traveler who wrote obituaries for the living. [78] Not for the dying, and never for the dead. [87] Her clients were the ones left behind by the ships, people carrying absences no one had explained to them. [106] A son in the copper mines across the sea is not dead, but he is gone, and grief without a shape will rot a house from the inside. [134] So Maren sat with the bereft in their kitchens, an intimate hour, her hand sometimes over theirs, asking them to say everything. [156] How he held his spoon, what his laugh did to a room, the smell of his coat in rain. [175] Then she wrote him down so vividly that, reading it aloud, she could project his presence back into the room for one held breath. [199] The families wept, and afterward they slept, because an explained absence is a door you can walk around, a landmark on the map of yourself. [224]

This was her motive, if anyone had asked: to understand the geography of healing, the way pain occupies a person like a country, with borders you can learn and eventually cross. [255] She had learned it the hard way, at nineteen, when her father sailed and simply never became past tense. [274] Nobody had explained that absence, so she had spent years inside it, and she had decided no one else should have to. [296]

The palace made her work possible, and stranger than it would otherwise be. [309] Its great hall held a carpet woven two centuries ago as a map of the city, every street in dyed wool, every district a different shade. [335] The steward, an old man named Ely, kept the doors open out of habit and loneliness. [351] For every obituary Maren finished, Ely pulled a single weft thread from the carpet, from the street where the departed had lived, and she tied the pages with it. [380] It was the city's own law, older than anyone could argue with: when a person leaves, the map must lose the thread that held them. [405] By November the carpet was thin as old lace, sun coming through it in long wounds of light. [423]

The last obituary of the season was for Berrit, a sailmaker's daughter whose mother could not stop setting two cups on the table. [446] Maren sat close beside her in the garden, their shoulders touching, and asked about the girl's hands. [463] The mother talked for an hour: needle scars, ink stains, the way Berrit fanned a finished sail like a wing. [483] When Maren read the page aloud, the garden smelled briefly of tar and canvas, and a girl seemed to stand in the blue shade, laughing, before the wind took her. [513] The mother pressed the paper to her chest like a warm stone. [525] "Now she is somewhere," the mother said, "and now I know where." [537] That was the geography Maren meant: not cure, but location. [547]

Ely called her into the hall and knelt at the carpet's edge, where the garden itself was woven, box hedges in silver thread against green wool. [573] "One thread left in this quarter," he said, "and I believe it is yours, at the traveler's lodging by the gate." [594] Maren understood, and did not refuse. [600] She wrote her own obituary that night by candlelight, vividly, truthfully: a woman who had crossed forty harbors, who was missed somewhere and never mourned. [625] Ely drew that weft thread out and she bound the pages with it, and for one breath the whole shadowed garden filled with everyone the ships had carried away, present, patient, lit by nothing but autumn. [661] Then it was only a garden again, and she was only a woman in it, and somehow that was bearable now, because it was explained. [686] In spring the ships would return, and she would sail with them, leaving a city that could finally name what it had lost, its map frayed to splendor, its grief given streets. [718]
