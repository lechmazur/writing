After the story famine, the concert hall kept its silence polished. [11] Wren had spent eleven years as the orchestra's copyist, inking the approved scores in a hand so clean the inspectors called it bloodless. [34] She was diligent the way a lock is solid, and the inspectors trusted her for it. [50] During the famine, stories were confiscated like grain, and songs with words were burned in the yard behind the library, the wind carrying ash across the gray city. [78] The famine ended the way sieges do: laws repealed, nothing left in the pantry, the airwaves still licensed, the old songs still ash. [101] That ash never settled. [105] It hung in the light of every window, dust that had once been lullabies and ledgers and last letters, and Wren could not stop thinking it was waiting. [133]

Her mother had been the head librarian. [140] When the burning ended there was nothing to bury, so Wren filled a jar with the yard's finest gray and made herself a quiet, impossible vow: she would teach the dust to tell stories. [174]

By day she copied. [178] By night she made ink. [183] She stirred a spoonful of that dust into lampblack and scrapings of match heads, and the ink behaved like a small mercy: in daylight it dried to ordinary black, under any lamp it stayed invisible, but in a room with no light at all it glowed, faint and green, secretly luminous. [234] Only in true darkness did the hidden verses surface between the staves, drifting up like drowned things coming back for air. [255] The words she wrote were lyrics, but they never sat still. [266] She borrowed a line here, bent a rhyme there, dropped a verse down a half step and hid another inside a dynamic marking, so no two pages matched and no informer could swear to the same song twice. [304] Musicians began to notice. [308] A cellist hummed something at rehearsal that was not in the score, and the conductor marked it as a fault of memory, because memory had been declared a fault. [337] Those who found the verses read them with the lights off, heads bowed to the faint green. [354]

But paper travels slowly and burns quickly, so Wren went further. [365] Behind the hall's boiler room lay an ember alcove, a hollow of bricks the furnace kept warm long after midnight, where river mist came up through the grate and hung like gauze. [397] She kept an old shortwave set there, salvaged from her uncle's lighthouse, and in the small hours, when the patrol vans were parked and the censors slept, she keyed the transmitter and let the dial glow up amber, the only light in all that gray. [442] No license named her signal; it was a pirate broadcast, born and gone in a night. [458] Each night she cloaked the glow with her coat, leaving just a coin of it to tune by, and each night she broadcast on a different frequency, elusive as the lyrics themselves. [490] Her calm was thistled: soft as seed-down on the surface, spined underneath. [502] She never spoke. [505] Speech can be traced to a voice; a song can only be traced to everyone. [520] She sang half of an old story one night and the other half three nights later on another band, and the city, which had forgotten how to listen, learned to keep the fragments and trade them at market. [558] When the censors finally recorded a full song and came to ban it, it had already changed its clothes, and what they banned was a version nobody sang anymore. [587]

It worked like frost, invisibly and everywhere. [594] A child in the flour queue whispered a verse about a fox that argued with winter. [610] An old man wept at a bus stop over two lines about a garden and could not say why. [629] The dust noticed too. [633] A room where someone sings gathers less silence; the particles stir, lift, and turn as if reading. [650] Wren liked to believe some of the library rode the city's breath now, telling itself back to whoever listened. [669]

One mist-heavy night she sat in the ember alcove, coat over the dial, singing the second half of her mother's favorite song, the one about the lamp that outlived the lighthouse keeper. [701] Far off, a patrol van hissed past on the wet road, slowed, and hissed on. [716] The dial glow dimmed as the set went quiet. [725] She uncloaked the light and let it lie open on her hands, and out in the drifting mist, faint as breath on cold glass, a hundred dark windows were not empty, and in them the dust was turning, turning, remembering.
