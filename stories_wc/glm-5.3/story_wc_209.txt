Vera painted walls in colors loud enough to be seen from passing trains, yet she had crossed the country for something no one had ever looked at twice. [28] She came in autumn, when the amber pavilion beside the cathedral stood knee-deep in fallen leaves, and sat there with her grandmother's collection of old postcards spread across her lap. [58] Forty-one cards, postmarked across four decades, each showing the same pavilion in the same season, amber light pooling like honey across its boards. [81] The handwriting on the backs was Alma's, her grandmother's, and it never once mentioned the cathedral looming behind the pavilion in every frame. [104] Only the last card broke the pattern, and it said: Look through, not at. [118]

Vera was quietly driven; she had spent her whole life finishing what other people abandoned. [133] So she asked the verger for keys to the east transept, where the great window held daylight like a held breath, and she began. [157] The window was plated glass, three panes stacked a hand's width apart, each etched with fine lines that meant nothing alone. [178] In full daylight the front pane showed a conventional garden: roses, lilies, obedient flowers of noon. [194] But when Vera slid her lamp along the glass at a shallow angle, the etchings on the middle pane woke. [214] Shadows gathered in the scratches and resolved into petals: moonflower, evening primrose, night jasmine, a garden that opened only in the dark. [236]

She spent the whole winter with those panes. [244] Snow sealed the streets; heat was rationed to a whisper; and Vera worked in gloves, breathing on her pencil to keep it moving. [267] She traced every line, matched every hidden bloom to its species, and set the postcards' postmarks against the years the middle pane must have been etched. [293] The dates aligned perfectly. [297] Alma had been a glazier's apprentice when women were never credited in the records; the ledger named only the master, and the flowers of noon were his design. [325] But the night garden layered behind them in etched shadow was Alma's, drawn bloom by bloom while the workshop slept, announced each autumn by a single postcard mailed home. [354]

Why flowers that bloom at night, Vera kept asking the empty nave. [366] Her own murals were famous for vivid, unignorable color; she understood the shout of pigment, work made to stop traffic. [386] She did not understand making something exquisite that no one was meant to see. [400] The answer, she sensed, waited in the third pane, the deepest layer, which resisted her lamp all season. [418] Its etching was too faint for lamplight; only the sun, she realized in February, could read it, and only once it climbed above the rooftops. [443] So she waited, which was the hardest work she had ever done. [455]

Winter's last snow melted slowly, taking weeks, unsealing the world stitch by stitch. [468] On the first clear dawn after the thaw, Vera carried the postcards in and sat beneath the window with her coat still on. [491] The hush that comes with daybreak settled first, that dawning hush when even the birds concede the sky to the light. [512] Then the sun cleared the rooftops and struck the glass low and level, as it does only in earliest spring. [532] The three panes aligned. [536] Noon roses, night jasmine, and the secret third layer fused into one garden that existed nowhere on earth, only in the light's passage through glass. [561] And the deepest etching resolved into an inscription cut so fine it was nearly a rumor: Because the night was the only garden they let me tend. [588]

Vera sat very still, the postcards trembling in her hands. [598] She had come to reckon with a grandmother she had thought small, and here was the reckoning: Alma was never diminished by hiding. [621] The night had not been her cage; it had been her canvas. [633] Flowers bloom at night, Vera understood at last, because darkness is not always absence; sometimes it is shelter, and some beauty can only open where no one is watching. [662]

She stayed through the spring doing something no one had commissioned. [673] In the amber pavilion, on the ceiling where morning light never reached, she painted a garden of night blooms in pigments that stayed mute and gray by day. [701] Then she made a postcard of her own. [709] It showed the pavilion in autumn, amber leaves on the boards, and on the back she wrote to whoever came looking: Come at night, and bring no lamp. [737] The garden on the ceiling opened only after dusk, when streetlight struck its hidden glazes, and the townsfolk who noticed spoke of it softly, as if in church. [765] Vera liked that best: her loudest work, blooming in the umbral tenderness of the hour her grandmother had loved. [784]
