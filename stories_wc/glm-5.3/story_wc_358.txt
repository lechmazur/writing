After the sirens stopped and the town went silent, Rhee walked into the container yard alone. [16] She was an accident investigator, the last one still working a case the world had filed under silence. [34] The chemicals aboard Cargo Lifter 9 had emptied the port city; three weeks on, the silence held. [51] Around her the shipping containers rose in a towering labyrinth, rust-red and blue and bone-gray, canyons of steel stamped with the names of ports that no longer answered their radios. [81]

Light came down between them in slats. [88] Dust hung in it like sediment, exact and unmoving, as if the air had been shocked into stillness. [106] Gulls wheeled overhead without crying. [111]

She began her survey at the point of impact, where the lifter had come down and been cut away, leaving a furrow of folded steel that the port had not yet disguised. [143] Her method was simple and close to ritual: documenting wind patterns between the buildings of this accidental city. [161] She taped ribbons to the corrugated walls at fixed heights, released chalk dust at the corridor mouths, filmed smoke pencils drifting, and entered every reading into a ledger in a hand so neat it looked printed. [197] The haze in her head, fatigue and grief and all that silence, she kept outside the numbers. [214]

The wind here was not weather; it was architecture. [223] Each canyon took the sea breeze, bent it, squeezed it, accelerated it. [235] And it was rapidly adapting: heat soaking into the steel, the turn of the tide, one crane swinging its boom, and the corridors reshuffled the air within minutes. [263] A reading taken at noon was a lie by 12:20. [273] So she adapted faster, running transects at ten-minute intervals, learning to feel the shift in her teeth before the instrument caught it. [295]

On the fourth day, wedged in the buckled door of a container half a kilometer from the scar, she found the flight engineer's patch. [319] Embroidered wings, a propeller, gold thread gone dull with ash, torn loose along a shoulder seam. [335] She photographed it, logged the position, then held it longer than procedure required. [348] Then she pinned it inside her jacket, over her own shoulder seam, where she could feel it while she worked. [368] The recorders said the flight engineer had left his station in the last ninety seconds. [383] Nobody could say why. [387] By the sixth day, Rhee could. [393]

Her ledger had become a map of invisible streets, and when she overlaid it on the lifter's final track and the patch's position, she saw the singular convergence. [421] Three currents had met above corridor seven: the sea wind off the harbor, a thermal rising off the sun-baked roofs, and the downdraft of the lifter's own rotors. [449] At 14:47:12, for four seconds, they made a shear no aircraft that size could survive. [464] The pilot fought it; the flight engineer had unstrapped to reach a latch or a valve, and the same shear broke the airframe and scattered it, and him, across half a kilometer of steel. [498] Shift any one of the currents by fifty meters or forty seconds and nothing collides; the lifter lands, and the town never goes silent. [522] That was the horror and the clarity: no failed part, no villain, only a conspiracy of everything at one point in one moment. [545]

On the inside cover of every ledger she kept, Rhee had written her reason for the work: to cultivate foresight in action. [567] Hindsight was grief with footnotes. [572] Foresight meant standing in the wind while it was still only wind. [584] So she did what investigators rarely do: she left her instruments anchored and running, ribbons fluttering on the walls like flags for a future she would not witness. [612] She marked corridor seven on every harbor chart with the coordinates and the time window, so that whoever returned would inherit her numbers and the wind would have to get through her first. [645]

On the last afternoon the sea wind rose, the roofs gave their heat to the sky, and the steel began its low organizing hum. [669] Rhee stood in corridor seven, beneath the singular point, and watched every ribbon rise at once, pointing to the same invisible summit. [691] The air went hazy with churned dust, but her hand was steady and her page was precise: 14:47, the first two currents closing, forecast true. [716] The third current, when it came, would have engines. [725] Under her jacket the patch pressed warm against her shoulder, as if it remembered altitude. [740] She wrote a final line, "wind is a language and I have begun to learn it," and walked out while the town behind her stayed silent, waiting for someone who could read the air to come home. [777]
