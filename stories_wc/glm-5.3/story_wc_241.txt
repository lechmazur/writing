Midsummer rain washed the tall windows of the Halloway Library all afternoon, and Professor Vera Solano let it. [17] Three weeks after her father's heart attack, she was grading probability finals by green, underwater light. [33] At six the storm took the electricity, and the reading room went the color of deep clover. [50] From her satchel she took her father's candle lantern, whose little window was not glass but mica, a stone sliced thin enough to see through. [75] He had kept it burning on the kitchen table for forty years, sorting racing forms by its amber glow while she, age nine, did long division beside him. [103] The compulsive gambler had raised a professor of probability, and both of them found this funny, in a wounded way. [123] Vera had a private term for the shape of her life: lucid contradictions, held all at once, like cards. [142] She proved daily that the dice have no memory, and still whispered his superstition at every fourth red light. [161] She knew the lantern was only wax and mica, and she lit it anyway, the way you visit a church you don't attend. [184] The flame behind the pane softened everything it touched, and she graded by that kindness until visiting hours drew near. [204]

Her father lay eleven stops west at St. Ambrose, and tonight, as always, she would traverse the city to him by interpreting cryptic scrawls on subway seats. [231] Some anonymous hand had been leaving them for a decade, arrows and digits and half sentences, ballpoint ghostwork on orange plastic. [252] A three meant go north; a wavy line meant transfer at the river; an eye above a question mark meant, she had decided years ago, keep going. [279] The system was nonsense; she had published a paper proving it was nonsense; she followed it anyway. [296] Her father used to call her theoretically rebellious, his revolutionary daughter who overturned ideas instead of rules, who argued against the house edge at his kitchen table and then went home to floss. [329] Following scrawls was her one true disobedience, and even that she did responsibly. [342] She granted the marks their power only after dark, and only for places she was already going. [359]

She zipped her satchel, cupped the lit lantern, and carried it out through the fire door into the rain. [378] Dusk came down sweet and grey, and the mica pane held its small gold against all that water. [396] In the subway car, an old woman glanced at the flame and smiled as if remembering something. [413] Vera checked the seat backs, her old ritual. [421] Near the door someone had scrawled a four-leaf clover, then another, then another, a whole row of them marching toward the west-facing seats. [444] Her breath went strange. [448] Luck was her father's religion, the little four-leaf charm he traced on every ticket, and here it was, drawn by a stranger's hand along the route to his hospital. [477] She knew the mathematics, one chance in ten thousand, meaningless, random, and she sat down in the west-facing seat anyway, hollowed and grateful. [500]

At St. Ambrose he lay propped up, grey and smaller, watching rain ladder down the window. [516] "They say no more betting," he said. [523] "Doctor's orders." [525] "Good," she said, and set the lantern on his table, and the mica pane threw soft amber shapes across the white ceiling. [547] She told him about the clovers on the seats. [556] He looked at her a long time. [563] "Somebody knows," he said, which was exactly, beautifully wrong. [572] She did not correct him. [577] Her life's work, she realized, was this: to widen perspectives responsibly, to open the field of chance so wide that people could stand inside its vastness without betting the rent on it. [609] So she took his hand instead and said, "Dad, I keep thinking about the clover field behind Grandma's house." [628] "You hunted four-leafers for an hour, found none, and said the field was still winning." [643] He laughed, then winced, then laughed lower. [650] "That field held ten thousand clovers," she said, "and maybe three were ever four-leafed, and you walked it every summer anyway." [671] "You want me to be the field," he said slowly. [681] "I want you to see how big it is," she said. [692] "The whole green field, Dad, not the ticket in your fist." [703] Visiting hours ended at nine. [708]

She rode back east with the lantern warm in her lap and got off, out of habit, at the library stop, where the storm had rinsed the steps and the air smelled of cut clover. [743] Inside, the power was still out, so she set the lantern on her table among the ungraded exams, and the mica pane did what it always did. [770] It refused to show the world exactly, and showed it truly anyway, green and softened and worth reading by. [789]
