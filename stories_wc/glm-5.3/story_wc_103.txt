<story>

Marla Voss designed hedge mazes the way other people prayed: slowly, on her knees, with dirt under her nails. [19] She had come to the living bridge garden on a rumor: that its bridges, grown from woven strangler figs, remembered every foot that crossed them. [44] She wanted, more than anything, to discover the texture of time. [55] A maze designer knows every material by touch; time was the only one she had never held. [72]

On her first evening, resting a palm against the oldest bridge, she fell backward into a hundred years. [90] She watched the bridge-tenders of 1824 weave saplings into the beginning of the span, felt their calluses as her own, and could not find the door out. [117] That was the trap: retrocognition, the keepers called it, and those caught by it never fully returned. [134] A wanderer who touched the roots too long simply stayed, body kneeling in the moss, eyes flickering through centuries. [153]

The keepers sold her a halo fragment for this reason. [163] Long ago, they said, a ring of light had circled the garden's founding tree, humming the present tense like a struck bowl. [185] While it hung, the bridges remembered but did not seize. [195] When it shattered, the garden's now broke into its pasts, and visitors began to fall in. [211] The fragment, warm as a sparrow, sat in her pocket and pulled her back to the surface whenever she sank. [231] But the keepers warned her the shard was dying. [240] It was disintegrating through hyperbolic geometry, they said, and she did not understand until she studied its cracks under a loupe. [261] Each fracture branched into smaller fractures, and each of those into more, until the edge of the shard held more divisions than the shard held substance. [287] It was becoming all maze and no garden, all boundary and no ground. [300]

She recognized the pattern. [304] She had drawn hyperbolic hedge mazes for eccentric clients, labyrinths where every path forked faster than a walker could choose, where the plan outgrew any possible walker. [331] Now the mathematics was eating her anchor, cracks doubling, redoubling, the fragment thinning toward pure outline. [347] The keepers had also given her photographs, brown and curling, one fixed image for each century of the garden. [366] The photographs were fading. [370] Silver dimmed, hedges ghosted, faces went anonymous; and as each picture forgot its era, that past grew unstable, flooding the minds of the trapped. [394] She checked them nightly by lantern, and nightly the bridge of 1824 had fewer leaves, the tenders fewer faces. [413] When the last photograph blanked, she understood, there would be nothing left to return to; the trap would close like a hedge growing over a gate. [439]

So she stopped designing and started wandering, shard in one fist, photographs in the other, looking for the founding tree. [459] The keepers had never dared approach it; the pull there was absolute, and they were already half gone, dreaming in the moss. [481] She found it at the garden's crooked heart, where every bridge converged like ribs around a breath: an ancient fig with a scarred ring of bark where the halo had once rested. [513] Here the retrocognition was worst. [518] She knelt, and the garden's pasts poured through her like weather: tenders weaving, lovers quarreling mid-bridge, a child dropping a lantern into the water, a funeral, a wedding, a drought, centuries stacking in her chest until she shook. [556] Quaking, half in reverie, she pressed the halo fragment into the scarred bark, letting the geometry finish. [573]

It flared, and its hyperbolic dying accelerated. [580] She watched the mathematics bloom: each crack forking into four, each fork into four, an exponentially unfolding lace of edges spreading through the wood. [604] The fragment was disintegrating, yes, but its disintegration had direction; the infinite edge it was becoming traced itself into a circle, the only curve with enough circumference to hold what the halo had held. [638] The last sliver of substance winked out, and light stood in the air again: a new halo, an edge without an inside, ringing the old fig. [664] The stacked centuries in her chest quieted, each settling into its own place on the ring, and the shaking in her hands slowed. [687] She was still trembling, but with wonder now, the way she trembled at the first bud of a hedge she would never live to walk. [712]

In her other hand, the photographs stopped fading. [720] Better: they began to return, faces surfacing like divers. [729] And in the hush under the new ring she finally felt it, the thing she had crossed the world for: the texture of time. [753] It was not a river and not a line; it had grain, like a woven root, ridges running one way, memory another, and it was warm where the present pressed through. [784] She laughed once, quietly, a designer among her materials. [793]
