In the winters after humans learned to hibernate, the coast went quiet, and only the wakers stayed moving. [18] Mara was a sensory map maker, one of twelve people certified to chart the emotional weather of places in heat. [38] Her commission that season was the coastal cave network beneath the sleeping town of Greyhaven: forty chambers of limestone that no instrument had ever translated. [63] She worked alone, which suited her, because grief reads clearer without an audience. [76]

Her method was heat-mapping emotions. [81] She would float in the flooded passages, arms wide, and let her skin register what the water carried: fear pooling in cold blue pockets, old joy threading the stone in warm amber veins, sorrow drifting through the dark like slow currents. [122] Every temperature was a sentence. [127] She transcribed them into maps so precise that readers claimed they could feel their own hearts move. [144]

In her dry bag she carried an elm leaf fossil, its veins pressed into tan stone forty million years ago. [164] The Registry of Anomalies listed it as factually mystical: verified, measured, and unexplained. [177] Though stone, it was always warm to the touch, and in water it floated, riding the surface like a living leaf in June. [200] No laboratory had ever accounted for either property. [208] Mara did not need them accounted for. [215] The fossil had been her grandmother's, given to her before her first hibernation at six years old. [232] The sleep-doctors called such things transitional objects: bridges a child builds between the self who sleeps and the self who wakes, between inner and outer worlds. [258] Adults were supposed to outgrow them. [264] Mara never had. [267] Every autumn, before the cold pulled the town under, she pressed the fossil into her palm and told herself the warmth would hold; every spring she woke with it still there, and the waking self and sleeping self touched fingers across the gap. [310]

On the ninth night of the season, she floated into a chamber the surveys called Unmapped 31. [327] The water bore her in on a current that tasted of iron and rain. [341] Her skin lit with signatures she had never charted: a vast slow warmth, layered, multitudinous, like a thousand small suns banked under ash. [364] She stopped paddling and let the heat-mapping happen to her. [374] The chamber was dreaming. [378] The signatures had the rhythm of dreams. [385] The hibernating town above was leaking its sleep into the limestone, and the cave had been collecting it for years, the way caves collect water. [410]

She drifted among them for hours, naming what she could: a child's dream of running, incandescent; an old man's terror, a cold knot tucked beneath a shelf of rock; and threading through everything, a familiar warmth she followed like a rope. [451] It pooled in a hollow near the far wall. [460] She knew its signature before she touched it. [468] Her grandmother had gone into her final hibernation three winters ago, the sleep you do not wake from, and her ashes had been given to the sea, and the sea had given them to the caves. [504] Mara floated with her hand in that warmth until her fingers went numb, and understood what she had to do. [524]

The unknown deserved a voice. [529] More than anything, she wanted to become the voice of the unknown, and the caves had just handed her the words. [550] Her maps had always aimed at this: not to shrink the world's mysteries but to speak for them, so that what could not speak for itself would not be erased. [580] When the town woke in spring, they would find her new chart waiting: not walls and water, but the geography of their own sleeping hearts, the dreams they had made and never known. [613] Grief would be marked, and company. [619] On the map's border she printed a small elm leaf beside the legend. [632]

She left the chamber in the blue dark before dawn, pausing at the mouth of Unmapped 31 to set the fossil on the water. [656] It floated, warm, impossible, turning slowly, a fact the world had decided to keep mysterious. [671] "Hold the door," she told it, the way her grandmother used to say it. [685] Then she swam toward the light, carrying the heat of everyone she loved, ready to translate. [701]
