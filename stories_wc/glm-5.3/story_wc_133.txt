Mara painted at night because night was the only thing the water wars had left her whole. [17] Sleep had gone the way of the aquifers. [25] She lived on Fenwick Skerr, a tidal island that the sea took twice a day, and she had become its insomniac painter, its unreasonable witness. [50] She painted, she told the gulls, to thresh meaning from experience, though lately the chaff was winning. [67] After the wars, water kept the calendars. [74] Rain meant something now; it was currency, grief, and weather all at once, and when it fell it wrote. [93] Every storm drove small rivers down the skerr's flank, and Mara, who could not sleep anyway, followed them. [111] This was the method the islanders had taught her before the last of them left: to find anything, follow rain trails. [132] The water always ran toward whatever the land was hiding. [142]

In the autumn of the seventh ration year, a storm stripped the turf off the island's west shoulder. [160] Mara went out at three in the morning with a lantern and her contempt for bed, tracking the braided runnels through the dark. [183] Every trail on the skerr, she had long noticed, bent toward one hollow above the tideline, as if the ground itself converged there on purpose. [208] That night the rain had dug it open. [216] What the storm unearthed was a convergence, and not one convergence but three, stacked like sediment. [232] On top lay the junction the wars had been fought over: the brass manifold where the mainland aqueduct had married the island cistern, stamped with the old utility's eagle. [261] Beneath it, a ring of standing stones, the moot circle where the first islanders had met to divide the fish. [281] And beneath that, oldest of all, a shelf of dark clay studded with amber, raw resin from forests drowned before any war. [303] Mara stood in the rain with her lantern guttering and felt the specific vertigo of the very tired: everything that had ever converged here was still converging. [330] She laughed, once, haggard with awe. [336] "Of course," she told the stones, wryly earnest, "you were a meeting place before you were a battlefield." [354]

In the clay at the circle's center, one piece of amber had been worked long ago into a brooch, its pin fused shut by centuries of salt. [381] Inside the stone a midge hung suspended, wings lifted, caught mid-escape from a world that had been wet enough to grow amber forests. [404] Mara held it to her lantern and understood that she was looking at her own profession: the preservation of a fleeting thing in resin. [428] "Brother," she told the midge, "you are the best painting I have ever seen." [442]

At the next high water, Deun's barter barge came over the causeway with its weekly cargo of salt filters, rumor, and pigment. [464] Deun was a fat, unkillable woman who had ferried contraband through the wars and now ferried almost nothing else. [483] Mara offered her the brooch. [488] She wanted, in exchange, resin enough for varnish, all the ochre Deun carried, and one answer. [504] "Ask," said Deun, turning the amber so the midge caught the light. [516] "They fought thirty years over that junction," Mara said. [525] "Was any of it worth a midge's wing?" [533] Deun weighed the question longer than the brooch. [541] "The wars were worth nothing," she said at last. [550] "But you, painting them awake all night for nothing - that might be worth everything." [564] They shook on it, an exchange with no ledger, and Deun rowed the past away toward the mainland while Mara carried its meaning home. [588]

She worked through what would have been a night's sleep and the next one too, grinding ochre, boiling resin, threshing the week like grain to get at the kernel of it. [619] The canvas that came out of her was the hollow itself: manifold above, stones beneath, clay at the root of the world, and over it all a lattice of rain trails converging like veins returning blood to a tired heart. [659] In the smallest corner she painted a midge with its wings up, forever leaving. [673] When it was dry she carried it to the hollow and leaned it against the manifold, an offering to the meeting place. [695] That night, for the first time since the ration years began, the tide came in and Mara slept, haggard, awed, and finally empty of witness. [720] The sea closed over the causeway, keeping the island for itself, as it always had. [735] In the morning the rain began again, writing its trails toward the hollow, patient as everything else that converges there. [755]
