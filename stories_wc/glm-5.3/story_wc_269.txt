The sea level monitoring station stood on pilings above the atoll's eastern reef, and Maren had lived there eleven years, moored to her instruments while her mind drifted out over the water. [32] The fossil fuel ban was twelve years old now, and still the seas climbed, patient with all the heat humanity had already banked. [55] Her work was simple: read the gauge, log the number, tap the rhythm, repeat. [69]

The tapping was a practice older than memory, fingers drumming patterns on whatever surface was near -- table edges, railings, the brass housing of the stilling well. [95] Long, short, long -- rest -- long, long, short. [102] She had learned it from her mother, the atoll's cartographer, who never once explained it. [117] When her mother died, Maren inherited a single torn atlas page, kept flat in a sleeve of oiled cloth. [136] Her mother had ripped it from its binding decades earlier -- the page she had defaced but could not bring herself to burn. [158] It showed the atoll at one to twenty-five thousand, every footpath inked in her mother's fine hand. [175] Or almost every footpath. [179] Where the coastal trails should have run, there were only erasure scars, pale ghosts pressed into the paper. [197]

That question was the reason she had chosen this posting over a comfortable desk in the capital: to discover why some paths disappear. [220] Not whether the sea would take them -- everyone knew that now -- but why her mother had taken them first, quietly, with a rubber eraser, before anyone spoke of rising water. [250] Maren had been sixteen the summer the reef bleached. [259] She swam out at dawn and found the coral bone-white as far as she could see, a whole city emptied of its color overnight, and she surfaced screaming for her mother, who swam out, held her, and said nothing at all. [300] That was the year her mother began erasing. [308] Maren had spent the thirty years since as the island's bleaching witness, the one who swam the reef transects each spring and logged every dead head and every pale survivor. [338] For thirty years she assumed the two facts were coincidence. [348]

In the station's dry room, on long quiet evenings, she began to assemble the old paper tide ledgers, shelf after shelf of them, back to 1911. [374] The work was meditative, almost musical: lift a ledger, smooth a page, tap the rhythm, enter the figure. [392] Months passed before she noticed that the numbers fell into the same cadence her fingers had always played. [410] The flood years -- the years the gauge had topped the old trail markers -- came grouped the way she tapped: clustered, a breath, clustered again, a long rest. [437] Her mother had not invented the rhythm; she had transcribed it, decades before Maren was born, from a hundred years of water. [459] The old keepers' logs mentioned her, the cartographer who came each spring to copy figures, humming, tapping, back when the station was the loneliest job on the atoll. [487]

Maren sat with that for a long while, the torn atlas page beside the assembled ledgers, and the delayed thing finally arrived, the way meaning arrives in music only at the last bar. [520] Her mother had known the schedule of loss. [528] She had read the water, periodically eternal -- always receding, always returning, keeping its appointments -- and she had erased the doomed paths so gently that no one would waste years waiting for them. [560] It was not denial; it was mercy performed with an eraser. [571]

In the morning she swam her transect, as she had every spring since she was a girl. [588] Since the ban, the water had stopped warming so quickly, and on the shallow flats she found what she had stopped daring to look for: knots of young coral, faintly brown, faintly alive, the reef taking its first careful breath in thirty years. [631] The sea was periodically eternal in its cruelty; perhaps it could be periodic in its mercy too. [648] That evening she spread the torn atlas page on the chart table and did not grieve the blank spaces. [667] They were not absences, she understood at last; they were room. [678] She took up her mother's fine pen and began to draw new paths -- higher, narrower, honest to the water's schedule -- tapping the old rhythm on the table as she worked. [708]

Outside, the station sat moored on its pilings while the tide drifted through its endless period, going out and coming back, going out and coming back, keeping time. [736]
