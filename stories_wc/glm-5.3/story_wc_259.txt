Veya took her saffron veil from its hook each morning and became, for the archive's purposes, no one at all. [20] The veil marked her order: historians of the lost civilization, sworn to catalogue its leavings and never to feel them. [40] The leavings were atoms of nostalgia, mote-small crystals sealed in the deep vaults, each one holding a single remembered moment of a people ten thousand years dead. [67] At the archive's heart stood the bronze prism pagoda, and at twilight its seven tiers caught the last light and split it into blades that could wake the atoms. [96] To wake them was forbidden, and the knowledge they held was the only thing Veya had ever truly pursued. [115] Her brother had pursued it before her, and the Keepers had scraped his name from the rolls, so that was reason enough. [137]

The atoms were steadily unpredictable; the same mote, shown the same amber ray, might sing of bread and ovens one night and of drowning the next. [163] The Keepers called this corruption and answered it with ritual. [173] Veya, cataloguing by day, began to suspect the opposite: the atoms were honest, and it was the rites that made them lie. [195] Every Keeper rite was repetition; every rite fixed the motes in one shape, the shape the Keepers chose. [213] To purify an atom, she reasoned, she would have to approach it by avoiding patterns: never the same stair, never the same hour, never the same question twice. [241] So she made her life erratic on purpose, and the other historians whispered that grief had cracked her, which suited her fine. [263]

Her work took her across the end of innocence, the four years in which her own city learned what its founding had cost. [286] The first year brought drought; the second, bread riots; the third, ploughs turning up bones beneath the river farms. [305] The city's story of its own goodness was coming apart, and Veya was assembling, mote by mote, the story that would replace it. [328]

She stole twilight hours in the pagoda, entering by a different tier each evening, humming a different wrong note, holding each atom to a different facet of the prisms. [357] An atom greeted with routine told her pretty fables: saints, harvests, obedient dead. [370] An atom greeted with strangeness went quiet, then clear, like water settling. [382] Through the purified motes she lived stolen evenings: a grandmother teaching a bread song, two boys racing paper boats down a canal, a whole street singing while the lamps were lit. [413] The joy in them was unguarded, and it hurt her like a stitch in the side, because she knew what was coming for them and they did not. [441] The later motes darkened: dust on the canals, refugees arriving with empty jars, her own ancestors' faces at the gate, gaunt and reasonable. [464]

She purified the last atom on the fourth autumn, in the topmost tier, with the whole sky burning amber above the bronze prisms. [487] The memory inside was their final evening. [494] The wells were already sealed. [499] They knew, and knowing it, they carried the children up to the rooftops, lit every lamp in the city, and sang the bread song together, badly, laughing at the wrong notes until the light failed. [534] That was their answer to her ancestors: not a curse, not a plea, just joy, offered at full price on the way out. [557] Veya came down the seven tiers weeping and grinning like a madwoman, which by then she perhaps was. [575]

She carried the atom into the plaza, where the elders had gathered to speak of the bones, and held it up to the last of the twilight. [602] It caught the light and woke, faint but enough: everyone standing there felt the rooftops, the lamps, the laughter, the wrong notes, the end. [626] The confession the elders had prepared died in their throats. [636] Nothing needed to be said after that; the atom said it in joy, and the joy was the accusation. [655]

Afterward they made her First Keeper of the archive, an honor shaped like a cage, and she accepted it, because someone had to guard what she had let loose. [684] Now each evening she climbs the pagoda with the atom of the last twilight, and each evening she takes a different stair and hums a different wrong note, so the memory will stay clean. [718] It never plays the same way twice. [725] Some nights the song is ragged; some nights it is almost perfect; once, she swears, her brother's voice was in it, laughing at the wrong notes with the rest of the dead. [757] That is what forbidden knowledge turned out to be: not a secret but a weight, the anguished joy of the dead held in two hands, with no way to put it down and no wish to. [793]
