By day Alden Ferrow surveyed firm shores, driving his rods into the banks until the numbers proved the earth would hold. [21] People trusted a surveyor who never flinched, and they were right to, though they mistook his stillness for stone. [40] He felt everything; he simply filed it where no one could watch him feel. [54] At dusk the drawbridge rose for the last freighter, the lock gates held, and the town settled into its island quiet. [75] Only then did Alden row out, because only then was the water exactly wrong. [89] Wrong meant high. [92] The new drawbridge had come with locks, the locks had lifted the river, and the Starling Pavilion, which had stood on pilings above the shallows for eighty years, went under in a single spring. [126] No one had fought it. [131] The town simply stopped dancing, stopped renting the stage, stopped rowing out, and told itself the water was progress. [150] Alden had surveyed that new shoreline himself, and the numbers had felt like a sentence read aloud at someone else's trial. [171]

The first night he waded in, lantern low, water at his ribs, he found the inkwell in the silt of the flooded ballroom floor. [195] An antique traveling inkwell, brass hinged, its screw lid still sealing a crust of violet ink, the kind dancing men once carried in coat pockets to sign promises at midnight. [225] He had to wrest it free with both hands, the mud holding on like a creditor. [241] When it came loose the water closed over the hole without a mark, and Alden understood that his profession had been lying to him. [265] Nothing holds, the lake said. [270] Everything is absent from somewhere. [275] What he wanted, he decided that night, was to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams. [290] That kind of archeologist works in reverse; he buries what he wants found. [303]

He bought oilpaper and a trowel and learned hydraulic mortar from a mason who assumed he was fixing a seawall. [323] Then he went door to door on a surveyor's pretext, measuring gardens, and let people talk. [339] They talked about anything but the pavilion, which is how he knew where to dig. [354] The baker had meant to sing tenor on that stage. [364] The lockkeeper's wife had meant to leave her husband at the pavilion's last masquerade, and did not. [381] Alden listened with his still face and his filing heart, and he had to wrest every dream loose, because each one had been mourned as dead already, and nobody hands a corpse to a stranger. [416] He wrote them at his kitchen table in the recovered ink, violet lines on oilpaper, and rolled each note tight. [436] After the drawbridge rose he rowed out with lantern and trowel, waded the flooded colonnade, and knelt at the north footing, where the stone had begun to slide. [464] He packed the joint with mortar, pressed the note into the wet gray, and sealed it over, so the repair and the record became one act. [490] Every night the pavilion stood a little firmer and held a little more. [503]

Absence ran through the work like rebar. [510] Each dream he sealed was a thing missing from a life, each life was missing from the pavilion, and the pavilion was missing from the town, which kept its lamps lit on streets that bent, politely, away from the water. [550] The absences interlaced, held each other up, and Alden, stoic as bedrock, sensed every one of them. [567] He did not call it grief. [573] He called it load-bearing. [577]

Widow Groll he saved for last, because her silence was the roof beam of the whole structure. [594] Eleven visits, tea going cold, her hands flat on the table like gulls refusing flight. [609] On the twelfth she was dying, and she wrested it from herself for him in one sentence: she had been asked to sing at the pavilion's opening, in a white dress, and her father had said no, and she had said nothing for eighty years, and the no had grown into everything. [661] Alden wrote it in ink drawn from almost the bottom of the well. [674] That night, after the drawbridge rose and the last freighter cleared, he waded in with her note and stood a long time beneath the drowned chandelier, lantern raised, black water carrying its small fire. [708] It was the quietest blaze the town would never hear about, a fire making no sound and no smoke, spreading anyway, footing by footing, note by note, through the bones of everything the water had taken. [744] He pressed her dream into the last open joint and smoothed the mortar with his thumb, the way a surveyor closes a page. [767] One note remained, his own, unwritten. [773] The inkwell was not empty yet. [779]
