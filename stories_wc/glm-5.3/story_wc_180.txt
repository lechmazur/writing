The mender climbed the ladder with a bag on her hip and a quartz eye that caught the sun like a hard little lie. [24] Above her spread the orchard of the abandoned Avers Cathedral: apple and pear trees grown up through the broken floor, their branches braided across the rafters, green and secret under a wounded roof. [57] Her name was Pell, eighty-one and endearingly feisty, which is a polite way of saying she argued with magpies and usually won. [79] "Move," she told the magpie on the top rung. [88] "I've been thrown off better ladders by better people." [97] The magpie moved. [99]

Below, her granddaughter Wren stood on the flagstones with her arms crossed, sixteen and furious. [114] "They signed the papers in March," Wren called up. [123] "The quarry bought the cathedral." [128] "The crane comes Thursday." [132] "You're mending a tree in a building that won't exist." [143] That was the breach of contract, though the council never called it that. [156] The founders' charter had bound the town to tend this orchard, and the orchard to hold the town's vows, for as long as leaves returned. [181] No keeper had tied a vow in three springs, so the council called the charter lapsed, sold the stone, and considered the matter closed. [205]

Pell settled onto a rafter-wide branch and opened her bag. [215] "The contract's breached," she agreed. [220] "Doesn't stop me." [223] "I mend what's put in front of me; I don't consult the calendar." [236] Her quartz eye, the one her own grandmother had set in the socket after the pox took the real one, saw hairline cracks. [259] Cracks in bowls, cracks in beams, cracks in granddaughters standing below pretending they had come for the walk. [277] "Come up," she said. [281] "Or stay down and practice shouting at people's backs." [290] "It saves them turning around." [295] Wren came up, muttering. [299]

Pell was already working: a storm had split a young graft, and she bound it with waxed cloth, firm and crooked, the way her mother had shown her, the way her daughter Mara had shown nobody. [335] "Why do you bother?" Wren said. [341] "Because the branch didn't ask to be split." [349] Wren looked out over the vow branches, where spring ribbons should have hung thick as blossom and hung thin as winter instead. [371] One ribbon did hang: brown, curled, three summers dead, tied at the far end of the pear bough. [389] "That one never fell," Wren said quietly. [396] "Auntie Bess says a vow that won't fall is a debt." [407] "Auntie Bess talks," said Pell. [412]

She reached for grafting tape, found the pocket empty, and dug deeper into the bag's forgotten bottom: a pencil stub, a thimble, apple seeds dried in a twist of paper, and, folded small, a red hair ribbon gone soft with age. [453] Mara's ribbon. [455] Pell had tucked it in on the funeral morning, three years back, and had never opened that pocket since. [474] She sat with it a while, the orchard ticking with bees. [485] "That's Mother's," Wren whispered. [489] "It is." [491] "Why is it in your bag?" [497] "Because I forgot it there," said Pell, "and because I didn't." [508]

She pressed the seed twist into Wren's hand. [516] "My mother's pocket seeds." [520] "Orchard stock, old as the charter." [526] "If the crane wins Thursday, we plant these where the crane isn't." [538] "And the ribbon?" [541] Pell nodded toward the dead brown vow on the pear bough. [552] "Go read your mother's." [556] Wren crawled out along the bough, careful as a cat, untied the withered ribbon, and read the vow inked on it in her mother's hand. [581] "That Wren learns the orchard's way of promising, from somebody stubborn enough to teach her." [596] The wind chose that moment, as winds do, lifted the brown ribbon out of her fingers, and dropped it, soft as a leaf, into her lap. [622] "It fell," Wren said. [626] "After three years, it fell." [631]

"Deciduous vows," said Pell, as if that settled it. [640] "They're built to fall." [644] "Falling isn't failing, girl; it's how a tree turns the page, and a person too." [659] "A vow holds till it's done, then lets go, and you tie it fresh next spring." [675] "It's yours now," said Pell, and held out the red ribbon. [686]

Wren wiped her face with her wrist and asked what to write. [698] "Nothing clever," said Pell. [702] "I never wrote a clever vow in my life, and look how wise everybody thinks I am." [719] Wren laughed, wet-eyed, borrowed the pencil stub, and wrote: I will learn. [731] Then she tied it to the bough with a knot Mara taught her before she could write. [748] But contracts had been breached before, stubborn women had persisted before, and orchards, given seeds and somebody to argue with magpies, had a habit of coming back. [775]

"You knew all along," Wren said. [781] "I knew no such thing," said Pell. [788] "I was talking about trees." [793]
