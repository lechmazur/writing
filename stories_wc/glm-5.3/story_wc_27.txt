The rebellion had fallen into a lull, quiet as a held breath, and Mara stayed anyway. [16] She lived in the abandoned spaceport repair bay at the edge of the Kest fields, where wrecked ships lay open like dissected moths. [39] The occupiers had bombed the towers and left, believing the port dead. [51] Mara knew better, because she had taken off her boots on her first morning here and felt, through her bare soles, the floor humming. [75] Beneath the bay, older than any colony and older than any skyport, stood a ring of standing stones. [93] The engineers who built the port had poured their deck plates right over the ancient stone circle, as if paving over a heartbeat. [116]

Mara was a stargazer, not a soldier; her war had been spent charting escape routes through the dark. [134] Now she stayed on alone, to discover meaning in the mess: she cataloged shards, named constellations through the shattered roof, and argued cheerfully with herself about both. [161] The others had laughed at her once, and she had laughed too, apologized for laughing, then insisted she was right. [181] That was Mara all over: awkwardly confident, a woman who tripped over spanners and then corrected your orbital math. [200] Her only instrument was a pair of vintage opera glasses, brass and mother-of-pearl, dug from a liner's crushed first-class cabin. [220] The magnification was pitiful for astronomy, but she pressed them to her eyes every dusk anyway, watching ships crawl like sparks across the last band of light. [247]

It was how she saw them first: three occupation frigates holding position above the northern ridge, waiting like patient wolves. [267] It was also how she saw the last rebel cells gathering in the hills, starving, silent, unsure whether to fight again or scatter forever. [291] They needed a sign, the old signal from the port's beacon tower: light the lamp and the rebellion reignites; stay dark and it ends. [315] The grid was dead, though, and the reactors were slag. [325]

On the third night she stood barefoot on the humming deck, closed her eyes, and finally understood what the vibration was trying to tell her. [350] The stones were resonators, and their hum climbed whenever the margin of daylight touched the horizon, that sliver-hour between night and morning. [372] The ancients had tuned their circle to the dawn, and the port's induction coils, she realized, ran beneath the same deck, near enough to couple with the song. [400] Meaning, she decided, was exactly this: wreckage plus attention. [409] She spent two days dragging cable, apologizing to the dead ships as she cannibalized them, assuring each one that it would be part of something. [434] She was not certain it would work, and she told the stones so, twice, in case honesty mattered to them. [454] Then she waited for dawn's first sliver. [461]

The horizon went from iron to violet, and the hum rose from a murmur to a chord. [478] She stood barefoot between two gutted engines, opera glasses hanging from her neck like a medal, one hand on the coupling lever, feeling the sound climb her ankles, her shins, her teeth. [510] The stones sang the oldest kind of song, the deck plates rang in sympathy, and the induction coils woke like sleeping dogs. [532] Meters that had been dead for a year twitched, then blazed. [543] She threw the lever with both hands, staggered, caught herself on a strut, and shouted an apology to no one. [563]

Above the bay, the beacon tower's ancient lamp caught. [572] Light unfolded across the whole valley, gold over the wrecked hulls, gold over the hills where the rebel cells waited. [592] The frigates began to move, and somewhere in the dark a thousand hidden people looked up and reached for their rifles, and the rebellion reignited by the hum of an ancient stone circle, of all things, of all absurd, holy things. [633] Mara felt something she had no better word for than serenity, vast as weather, like standing inside a struck bell and being unharmed. [656] The light was thunderous; her own heart was quiet. [665]

Through the opera glasses she watched the hills, and she saw small fires answering, one by one, like stars being born on the ground. [689] She had come to the mess looking for meaning, expecting to find it in starlight, in inventory lists, in the patient arithmetic of what survives. [714] Instead she had found it in the soles of her feet. [725] When the sun finally cleared the ridge and the stones' song settled into a low, contented drone, Mara sat down among the sleeping engines, put the opera glasses to her eyes, and watched the sky fill with returning ships. [764] Let the frigates come, she thought. [770] The morning was loud, and golden, and completely calm. [779]
