Vell was the Ashen Arbor's seed sorter, and she was orderly about it. [13] Every pod, pip, and winged husk that the wind shook loose passed through her hands and into drawers labeled by weight, sheen, and temper. [37] The Arbor itself hung in the sky, a whole forest adrift beneath ashen clouds, with no memory of soil and no wish for any. [61] On her sorting ledge she kept her other habit: tiny monuments to forgotten things. [75] A cairn of hulls for the lost word for rain-before-dawn. [85] A spire of split stones for the ground itself. [94] A little arch of seed-wing for the Quiet Cartographer, whom the old folk still argued about at wash-lines and ovens. [114]

The folklore went like this: when the world was young, the Cartographer lifted the Arbor into the sky to map the clouds, and her one eye was a moonstone cabochon, and when the forest tore free of its roots the stone cracked with grief. [158] The dust of that eye, they said, still drifted through the seed-fall, and whoever caught a speck could read one true leaf. [180] But the folklore also said this: every reader who went into the Hush came out changed, always, and no exceptions, so sensible folk kept to their drawers and let the leaves fall unread. [213] The Hush came once a year, in the hour where past met prophecy: last year's leaves, still falling, crossed paths with this year's leaves, already knowing how they would fall. [243] In that hour the whole Arbor held its breath, and the leaves hung still enough to read, if anyone dared. [263]

This year, Vell meant to decode the message in the falling leaves, because they had begun to fall in patterns that matched her own drawer labels, and she could not bear an unread letter in her sky. [300] That morning she was sorting the winged and unburied when a speck caught the light like a moon behind ash. [320] She knew it at once, and her heart went as soft and damp as moss. [335] Vell did not grab it. [340] Grabbing was for gulls. [344] Instead she took her finest sorting needle and gave the moonstone cabochon dust speck the gentlest prod, and it hummed like a struck cup, and the Hush folded in around her, eager to see. [378] Around her, ten thousand leaves stopped mid-fall and hung there, patient as held breath. [392]

Here is what her years of monument-building had taught her: a leaf that lands on bare wood stays silent, but a leaf that lands on something remembered will speak. [421] So she worked, quick and quiet, building tiny monuments to forgotten things in a row along the great branch: a hull-cairn, a seed-wing arch, a little tower for her grandmother's second name, a doorstep for the door it belonged to. [461] She set the moonstone speck on the tallest monument, balanced like a lantern, and let the leaves come down. [480] The first leaf touched the cairn and said, in a voice like dry pages, 'We remember the rain.' [498] The second kissed the arch and whispered, 'We remember her eye.' [509] And the last leaf, the one that had been falling since the previous Hush, drifted onto the doorstep and spoke the message it had carried all that way: 'We are still below.' [541]

Vell understood then that the ground, the great forgotten thing beneath the ash, had been writing to the Arbor all along, and the Arbor had answered with nothing. [569] Not once in a hundred years had a single seed been allowed to fall. [583] 'Send seeds,' said the leaf, and went to dust. [592]

Vell laughed, and it felt like moss spreading over a stone. [603] She opened every labeled drawer she owned and tipped them all over the edge of the world. [620] Seeds fell like green snow into the ash, the Arbor's first letter in a hundred years. [636] When the Hush lifted, Vell emerged transformed, as readers always do. [647] Her shoulders wore a shawl of lichen; her footprints, they say, still sprout ferns. [661] Her eyes had gone the pale of moonstone. [669] She kept her drawers, but now she sorts seeds by where they would most like to grow. [686] And every autumn, when past and prophecy cross in the air, she builds one more tiny monument on her ledge, in case the ground has anything else to say. [715] The Arbor, for its part, has begun to lean, very slightly, toward home. [728]
