The antique survey airship Petrel was older than anyone aboard, and everyone aboard could fit around one dinner table. [19] Tam, the ship's curious map apprentice, sat at that table every evening beside Captain Reyes, and the two of them talked companionably about weather and ports while the empty chairs listened. [50] That was the arrangement: a sociable kind of isolation, speech without echo, company enough to keep a person human. [69] He had inherited his post from Professor Voss, along with her polyglot's phrasebook, its corners soft as cloth. [87] One page stayed dog-eared, always, through years of being smoothed: the page where Danish gave up its word for a starling murmuration, sort sol, black sun. [113] Voss had died believing the birds kept a geography nobody had drawn, and Tam, diligently curious in the way she had trained him, meant to draw it. [140] He wanted, more than air, to chart the next great discovery. [151]

In late October the Petrel crossed the marsh country at dusk, and the starlings came up around her like smoke given a mind. [174] Thousands of birds folded and opened above the envelope, and where they turned, light stayed behind. [190] Not reflection, not lantern: stranded luminance, threads of it, hung in the cold air along every path the flock abandoned, slow to fade, like silk snagged on the sky. [219] The old pilots had a name for it and a superstition against chasing it, which Tam noted in his logbook, because he noted everything. [243]

That first night he simply watched from the observation deck, beside a hopscotch board painted there decades ago, when the Petrel carried refugee children instead of instruments. [270] Dust of chalk and years had settled into its squares. [280] He found it comforting, that ghost of play, and told the captain so at dinner, and the captain said lonely ships keep whatever company they can. [306]

On the second night the murmuration passed low, and the flock's shadow swept across the deck. [322] It broke over the hopscotch grid and, for a held breath, darkened its squares one after another, in order. [341] Tam stood up so fast his chair skidded. [349] Coincidence, he told himself, and then spent the third dusk on his knees with a pencil, and the fourth, and the fifth. [371] By noting the secret patterns in that dusty hopscotch board, the sequence the shadows chose each evening, he found they were not random at all. [396] The grid had ten squares. [401] The sequences ran long, twenty numbers, thirty, always different, always precise. [412]

Numbers wanted a language, so he reached for Voss's phrasebook, and it fell open, of course, to the dog-ear. [431] Beside sort sol, in Voss's handwriting, ran the Danish counting words, en, to, tre, fire, fem, and beside those, an instruction he had taken for a doodle: the birds count in the language that names them. [467] Tam read the shadow sequences aloud in borrowed Danish, and they stopped being numbers and became bearings and steps, a route written in hops. [491] He plotted them. [494] The line of his pencil ran off the chart's edge and pointed across the marsh, and when he looked up through the glass, the stranded luminance was hanging there in exactly that direction, a long seam of light fading north. [534]

It was not decoration, the light. [540] It was ink. [543] The starlings wrote their route on the sky each dusk, in luminance their passing stranded there, and the shadow-script on the hopscotch board was the key to the hand that wrote it. [575] Voss had guessed a geography. [580] It was an atlas, whole and nightly renewed, and no cartographer alive had ever copied one. [596]

He worked until the small hours, transcribing sequences, smoothing the dog-eared page flat, pressing it back into its fold, a small ceremony. [618] At breakfast he slid the new chart across to Captain Reyes, north line inked, margins crowded with Danish numbers. [637] "You realize we are two people and a boiler," the captain said, "and you are proposing we follow birds." [656] "I am proposing," Tam said, "that we stop being the only two readers of the oldest atlas in the world." [676] The captain looked at the chart, and the empty chairs, and the light already gathering over the marsh for the evening's writing. [698] "Hold course north," he said at last, and refilled Tam's cup, and Tam understood that his isolation had just doubled its society. [720]

That evening the murmuration rose again, black sun over the old ship, forty thousand wings keeping their ledger, and the Petrel turned north beneath them, the first vessel ever admitted to the reckoning, while an apprentice stood at the rail below the hopscotch board, pencil ready, delighted, translating.
