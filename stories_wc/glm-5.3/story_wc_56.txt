Twilight settled over the barge canal like a held breath, the water carrying the last light in long copper bars. [20] Wren Hasse knelt at the gunwale of her salvage scow, line taut in her gloves, letting her hands read the canal bottom the way other people read faces. [48] Ten years ago she was a legend seeker with a fellowship and a map; now she was errant, unaffiliated, following rumors down waterways that had no wonders left to give. [78] On the cabin radio, Canal Four murmured old songs to the night barges; when it fell silent at dawn, the lock keeper would open the sluices for the final draining, and the current would strip the bed bare before the canal closed forever. [121] Whatever she meant to salvage, she had until the silence of the morning radio. [135]

Her ruin had a name, the Horologist's Heirloom: an antique pocket sundial said to lie where an old clockmaker drowned, still marking true time on the silt for the honored dead. [166] Her mentor, Doctor Halvard, had authenticated the clockmaker's letters; Wren had written the book that made the legend famous; then Halvard was caught forging relics and vanished, and her book became a warning, a joke, a wound. [203] What she wanted now was not the relic; it was the architecture of lies, the exact design of the one that had held her, because if she could not see how it had been built, she could never trust her own eyes again. [246] So she had come to the canal as a salvager, and for two years she had trained her instincts on this water, learning the vocabulary of the line: the shrug of a bicycle frame, the stubbornness of anchor chain, the small dense politeness of brass. [291] That training had taught her something no archive could: a lie is built to be found, because a lie without a discoverable heart dies of neglect, and Halvard had always been a careful builder. [325] The forged bargeman's diary placed the drowning exactly where her anchor now sat, and so, in hindsight, the place to plant the proof had always chosen itself. [352]

The line whispered up through her gloves, her instincts answered before her mind did, she hauled, and the dripping net gave up a small brass box, hinged, round, older looking than any honest object should be. [388] She opened it in the failing light: the antique pocket sundial, gnomon folded like a sleeping bird, its face engraved with hours and a motto she knew by heart, having printed it on her ruined book's cover. [425] Tempus neminem manet, it read, time waits for no one, and the letters were beautiful, and the brass was wrong. [445] Her trained hands knew the smell of acid, the greasy sheen of hurried age, and she tipped the gnomon up and found beneath its hinge a date punched into the brass, a date from within her own lifetime. [483]

And there it was, hindsight arriving with the clarity of a sundial read at long twilight, when every shadow the day has cast lies down plainly at once: the forged diary, the authenticated letters, the drowned clockmaker, the door of discovery built to her exact measure, because a legend needs a seeker the way a trap needs a foot. [542] The light around her had gone thoughtfully clear, the way late light sometimes does, as if the whole day were reviewing itself before dark, and she sat inside that clarity while her life quietly overturned. [577] She was not angry; that surprised her most, the serenity of it, the upheaval arriving as stillness, and with the sundial in her palm she could finally walk through the whole false building: the diary as foundation, the letters as walls, the planted relic as keystone, the beautiful impossible motto for a roof. [630]

The radio played on, songs thinning as the sky grayed at the edges, and when the last song ended the announcer said goodnight like a man locking a door, and then came the silence of the morning radio, and far down the canal the sluices groaned open. [677] She held the dial toward the east, where the sun was coming, though a sundial can only tell you the hour you are already losing. [702] Wren dried it, wrapped it, and wrote in her logbook the words she had wandered errant for years to earn: not found, salvaged; not a legend, a blueprint. [730] The current came and scoured the bed clean, and she started her engine, calm at last, having learned the one honest trade: take lies apart, beam by beam, and keep only what the water truly gives.
