Marek had been colorblind all his thirty years, which the townsfolk called a tragedy for a painter and Marek called a gift. [22] He painted what others missed: the bruise of shadow beneath a jaw, the tremor in a supposedly straight eave, the way grief bent a shoulder. [47] Once a year, in the glassblower's courtyard at the edge of town, the phoenix rose. [62] It was glass, of course, a bird the size of a chapel door, fired through the shortest night, and at dawn the first sunlight struck it and every onlooker swore the thing caught flame from the inside. [99] Then it shattered itself on the courtyard stones, as it did every year, into ten thousand flawed shards. [117]

The morning after the phoenix rises is a holiday of grief and scrubbing, and the glassblower, Mother Iva, takes her vow of silence until the shards cool. [144] Nobody speaks to her in those three days. [152] Instead they leave messages by arrangement: cut stems and seedpods laid on the flagstones in patterns every child learns, a grammar of flowers in which a rose means love and yarrow means war. [185]

Marek came to the courtyard that morning carrying his grandfather's concertina and, in his fist, a single brass reed tongue pried from its belly, the one that gave the instrument its sour, human buzz on every low C. [223] For among the town's quiet customs, the strangest was the barter of awakenings: to have Mother Iva coax a shard of phoenix glass into new life, you paid not in coin but in something that had once awakened you, and she passed that opening on to the glass. [271] The reed was cracked and worthless, which was exactly why he loved it, and exactly why he was offering it. [291] It was that sour note that had woken him at nine years old, sobbing over a failed canvas, when his grandfather played a broken polka and showed him the off note was where the music lived. [327]

He did not know the flower grammar, not truly. [336] Rose and carnation, iris and marigold looked, to his eyes, like the same gray smoke, so the meanings everyone traded on were invisible to him. [361] So he arranged what he could see: a thorny stem bent into a circle, a broken fern frond, the seedpod that rattled like the bones of the concertina's chord. [390] If the arrangement meant nothing, so be it; he had been told all his life that his way of seeing meant nothing, and he had stopped believing it. [418] He laid the reed tongue at the center, placed the concertina beside the gate as proof of provenance, and went home. [439]

At dusk he returned and stopped dead. [446] His arrangement was untouched, but beside it lay another: a curl of glass shaving, a dropped poppy head, a shard of the phoenix no bigger than a tooth, their stems and edges aligned into what he could only read as a question. [488] He answered by rearranging everything: the thorn circle broken open, the reed tongue laid across the gap like a bridge. [508] Broken thing spanning broken thing. [513]

For three days they conversed in plants while the shards cooled, and Marek understood she was asking not what he wanted but why, and he answered with asymmetries, with the crooked line he could not stop loving, with a fern's flaw made the focal point, until the third evening, when she stood in her doorway and broke her silence with laughter. [574] People said the painter must have tricked her into speaking early; they were half right, though there was no trick in him, only the innocent cunning of a man who solves problems sideways because the front door is invisible to him. [615]

She took the reed tongue in her scarred fingers and pressed it into a gather of phoenix glass at white heat, and where the brass met the melt the glass refused to clarify; it set cloudy, veined, imperfect. [653] She blew it into a small bird, no bigger than a fist, its wing warped, its belly streaked with the ash of the reed. [677] When Marek held it up to the morning light, it was all gray to him and glorious anyway, which was fine. [698] Then he tilted it and the embedded reed caught the draft and sang, one sour, human buzz on a low C, exactly the note his grandfather made when he laughed. [728] He stood a long time with that broken music in his palm and felt something open in him, gently, the way glass opens in fire. [753] It was what he had bargained for all along, though he could not have named it: not the bird, not the craft, but proof that the flawed thing, rightly loved, is the one that wakes. [788]
