Marisol had not written a word in three years, though she heard them constantly. [14] They were never spoken. [18] They were the other kind: her father's swallowed apology at the funeral, her mother's unasked question every Sunday, and beneath them her sister's goodbye, which Liv had died before managing. [48] The doctors called the hearing a symptom. [55] The moon, when it rose, called it something else, and Marisol had always been the sort to believe the moon. [75]

Her grandmother, a night watcher before her, left her an astrolabe, a brass astronomical instrument older than any observatory on the coast. [97] The will held one instruction, written small: find where they land. [108] Marisol obeyed it the way the sleepless obey anything, gratefully. [118] If her verses had fallen silent the way everything falls, then falling stars could be traced, and what is traced can be found, and what is found might be freed. [148]

So each clear night she traced the path of falling stars, sighting meteors through the astrolabe's ring, logging every angle of burn and descent, drawing the line down to where the earth must have caught the light. [185] The work kept her awake, which she counted as medicine. [195] Across an unwritten calendar of such nights, months she had torn from the kitchen wall rather than count, the lines on her map converged like rivers deciding on one sea. [225] Every path landed at the ruined priory on Lark Hill, where a single stone archway still stood against the weather. [245]

On the last night of that uncounted year, the sky above the moor broke green. [260] Aurora was rare that far south, a storm of suns arriving uninvited, and it gathered strangely at the archway, pooling inside the stone curve as though the old builders had intended exactly this. [293] Marisol stood beneath the aurora-lit arch, and the unspoken words grew loud. [305] Her father's apology circled her like a moth. [313] Her mother's question scratched at the stones. [320] And beneath everything, patient as weather, Liv's goodbye. [328]

She knelt where the traced paths crossed and dug with the astrolabe's edge, ruining the instrument and not caring, until her fingers found a seam of dark grains colder than the soil around them. [362] Stardust, the old kind, fallen before priories, before grief, before names. [373] It had crossed the dark for longer than her sorrow had existed, and when she lifted a palmful toward the aurora it glittered like the memory of a fire. [402] Then the remembering came. [406] The grains against her skin remembered being inside a star, remembered the furnace and the forging and the long bright falling, and Marisol remembered it with them, because the dust was not other, had never been other. [443] Her bones were stardust remembering. [448] The words she heard were stardust remembering. [455] Everything unsaid was only matter waiting to be shaped, and everything shaped had once been falling, and everything falling could be traced. [477]

She understood then that she had traced the paths of falling stars not to find the dust but to be found by it. [500] Her verses were not gone. [505] They were unspoken, and unspoken is a kind of orbiting. [515] But knowing this did not loosen her throat. [523] She had traced every falling star, and still the words only circled. [535] Then, from somewhere older than her grief, came a simpler instruction, her grandmother's voice or the stardust's or her own: rest. [556] Not surrender, but rest. [560] She had been moonstruck so long she had forgotten that even the moon keeps a dark half. [577]

So Marisol lay down in the aurora-lit archway with the stardust cupped against her heart, and she rested. [595] For the first time in three years she slept without dreaming of falling. [608] The aurora moved over her like a slow green tide, and the unspoken words quieted, the way children quiet when they are finally allowed inside. [633] She woke at dawn with Liv's goodbye resting in her like a stone in a stream, not gone but settled, the water able at last to pass around it. [662] The words came while she was not reaching for them, which is the only way words ever come. [680]

She wrote her first line in the dust with the astrolabe's ruined edge, a line about a sister, a falling star, and a door of light, and she was laughing while she cried, rapture with a shadow in it, joy that knew exactly what it cost. [726] Above the moor the sky had gone ordinary again, and the archway held only stone and morning. [743] But Marisol carried the stardust home in her coat pocket, and after that she wrote and rested and wrote, and when her father finally spoke his apology aloud she was there to hear it, and to answer.
