When the anesthesia wore off, the tower was already graying toward dawn. [12] Mara Voss lay still and let the quiet settle over her like snow. [25] Outside the window, the last stones of the meteor shower fell without a sound, and the whole city seemed to hold its breath beneath them. [50] She opened other people's wounds for a living, cutting carefully along old scars so they would heal into cleaner lines, and her patients called the results closure. [77] She could give it to everyone but herself. [85] Last night, for the first time since the fire, someone had opened hers. [98]

On the bedside tray sat a paper cup, ringed four times with dried coffee, the circles overlapping like tree rings. [118] She had learned to read such marks at nine, at the kitchen table after the fire, while the grown-ups whispered in the doorway and told her nothing. [145] The detectives left rings of their own, and she studied them until the stains told her a story: how long they stayed, who leaned forward, who could not. [173] That was how the method began, and it was why she had spent her life trying to learn to read the stories in scars, believing her own held one sentence she had never been allowed to hear. [210] She examined the rings now: four hours of vigil, the last one smeared, as if the drinker had gripped the cup when the monitors changed. [235] Her mother had come, then, after twenty-three years of silence. [245]

Something rustled, and it was not the vent. [253] The pothos on the windowsill was thinking at her again, as it had for weeks, faint as a radio in another room. [275] She had never asked to hear plants; it had simply happened to her one day, the way allergies happen, and in the meteor hush of the tower it was suddenly, unbearably clear. [307] The east wing burned, the pothos was saying, in the way a plant says things, all leaf and patience. [326] We remember. [328] The east wing had been sealed since the night it burned, the night a nurse carried nine-year-old Mara out through the smoke of the pediatric ward and her sister Lark did not come out at all. [364]

Everyone called her survival a miracle, as if miracles could never be misdelivered, and Mara had spent twenty-three years certain it was a displaced miracle, meant for Lark, landed on the wrong sister. [397] In her bag, wrapped in a sweater, waited Lark's hourglass, its glass clouded white by the heat of that night, its sand fused so that no grain had fallen since. [427] She had carried it to the hospital intending, finally, to decide its fate, and had gone under the anesthesia without deciding. [448]

The ivy remembers the way down, the pothos said. [457] Our roots run the old coal tunnel beneath the north stair, under the sealed doors, out to the garden where the copper beech still stands. [482] It wears the fire. [486] She should have called a nurse; her shoulder was stitched and raw. [498]

Instead she dressed slowly, pocketed the hourglass, and slipped past the sleeping ward into the service corridor, where ivy had pushed through a broken grate and hung like green handwriting. [528] The tunnel was black and cold, and she tunneled through it on her knees, one hand on roots that ran the ceiling like a rail, murmuring encouragement in a grammar older than language. [561] Frost silvered the bricks, and the hourglass, riding against her heart, fogged whiter, as if the years were catching up with it. [583] Then the far grate gave, and dawn was there. [592]

The memorial garden held its breath beneath the last mute meteors, and the copper beech stood vast in the middle of it, its trunk split and healed over into a great gray lip of scar. [627] Mara laid her palm against the scar, and the tree spoke the way the pothos spoke, and what it said was not a sentence but a fact: it had never closed its wound. [660] It had carried it, ring by ring, outward, laying new wood around the hurt every year, so that the wound rode at the center of everything the tree grew. [689] Not a verdict, she thought. [694] A history. [696] She knelt and set the frosted hourglass in the crook of the roots, and when the sun cleared the tower's shoulder and came slanting through the branches, the sand inside the clouded glass showed plain, all of it resting in both chambers at once, held rather than stopped. [744]

Somewhere above, the tower was waking, and her mother would still be sitting by the empty bed with her cooling coffee. [765] Mara touched the fresh stitches in her shoulder, gentle as a page, and turned back toward the tunnel, ready at last to read. [788]
