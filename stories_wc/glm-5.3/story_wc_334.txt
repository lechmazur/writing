Maren Kestrel had spent forty years kneeling in other people's fields, and her knees carried the permanent proof. [18] A folklorist who works from armchairs collects versions; she collected rings, walking them at dawn with a tape measure and a soil kit, tasting the earth where the circles grew. [48] Her grandmother had called the trait chronically curious, the way a doctor names a fever. [63] It was the family flaw, and it had a shape: a hole where her ninth summer should have been. [82]

In the photograph from that year she stands grinning beside a boy nobody else has ever been able to see. [102] Saltmere had grown its apples under glass, a great greenhouse on the harbor's lip, and the ships that came to grief in the storm of that summer now ringed the sunken ruin like a crown of teeth. [139] She remembered nothing of the night the sea came in, only waking with a dried mushroom clenched in her fist and a brother-shaped absence in every mouth that spoke. [168] Marasmius oreades, she labeled it decades later, when her hands had learned the trade: the fairy ring mushroom, a small tan parasol that dries out and keeps for years if the ring it came from wants remembering. [205] Hers had never rotted. [209] Her grandmother said that was why the theft had never finished with her: the ring could not close its circle around a girl holding one of its own. [237]

Her fieldwork handed her the law she needed by accident, the way fieldwork does. [251] Fairy rings migrate, but nobody could ever say where they went. [262] Maren found that they moved toward broken patterns: rounding out churchyard arcs that had lost a grave, closing gaps where wells had caved, mending whatever circle the world had left unfinished. [293] A ring, she wrote in her notebook, cannot abide an incomplete thing. [305]

So she went looking for the largest broken pattern she knew. [316] Her grandfather's harbor chart drew the wrecks around Saltmere's greenhouse as a constellation, the Fleet, each mast a star in a slow wheel of rust. [341] Over the decades the sea had knocked its stars down one by one, and by this year's survey the wheel was broken in exactly one place. [367] There was her detector: by studying the broken constellation, she could detect where the ring had gone to mend it. [387]

There was one more rule, the oldest in her card file, taken from a hill woman who would not give her name: the ring returns what it has stolen, and only when habits break. [421] The fairies keep stolen memories wound inside repetition, she had written, and only the patternless can unwind them. [439] For fifty-three years Maren had touched an iron nail each morning before she spoke, a habit stitched on the day of the theft. [462] This year the habit had broken on its own; retirement had unstitched her dawns one unmarked morning at a time, and she understood that the door stood open for her now, decades later, precisely because nothing in her repeated anymore. [502]

She waded out on the night of the year's lowest tide and found the ring exactly where the missing star would have stood, a pale hoop of parasols among the drowned apple trees, glowing like spilled moonlight through the broken glass. [543] She set the iron nail on a stone outside the ruin, the way you set down a word you have carried too long. [566] Then she stepped over the pale hoop, the dried mushroom still in her fist, and the greenhouse held its breath. [586]

The stolen summer came back all at once, not as story but as weather. [600] Tam laughing with apple juice running down his wrist; Tam daring her to say the ring's name three times; Tam stepping in first because she was afraid and he never was. [631] The glow rising around his ankles like water; the real water coming after; her hand reaching for his and closing on one small parasol as the circle shut. [659] The sea had taken the boy and the ring had taken the grief, so the grief arrived now, fifty-three years late, punctual as a tide. [684]

She sat down among the mushrooms and let the summer wash through her, every green hour of it, because a folklorist knows that what is kept is not lost. [713] In her fist, the mushroom had finally begun to rot. [723] When she climbed out under the masts' first stars, the ring had already begun to walk, mending its way toward some other gap. [746] At dawn her old habit found her anyway, like a gull to a piling, and she opened a fresh notebook and wrote the first line of the rest of her collection: his name was Tam. [781]
