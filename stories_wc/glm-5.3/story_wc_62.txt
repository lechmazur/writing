The first monarchs reached Harrow Bend in September, and Mira Sorn followed them uphill to the ancient greenhouse at dusk. [20] She walked the quarry path her grandfather had walked, because that was the bargain he left her: retrace his steps exactly, and the door opened. [45] Three autumns dead, Aldous Sorn waited inside among the roosting butterflies, and he had been waiting since the first wing crossed the ridge. [68] The town called it a haunting; Mira, a thoughtful carver of gravestones, called it mentorship, the only kind he had left to give. [91]

In life he had been fiercely meditative, a man who could sit before a slab of granite in perfect stillness until the stone confessed its first line. [118] That stillness was the first thing he taught her; the second was the family custom of trading favorite poems at dinner, one line each, before anyone was allowed to eat. [148] Every line ever traded was inked onto a ribbon and woven into the great roll Mira carried under her arm, forty years of dinners wound around a dowel of ash. [178]

She set it on the potting bench between them and unrolled a hand's width. [192] "The river keeps no names," she read, "but the reeds remember." [203] A thousand wings lifted together, and his voice arrived inside the sound, the way it always did. [220] "Ask the loom," he answered, "what the thread cannot see." [230] That was their grammar, her ribbon and his reply, dusk after dusk, for the migration would last a month and so would he. [253] When the last monarch lifted from the valley, the greenhouse would go quiet, and Mira did not pretend otherwise. [272]

From the first night, she brought him the town's problem. [282] The March flood, when the dam let go, had taken forty-one people from Harrow Bend, and the town had paid her for one memorial stone to hold them all. [311] She knew how to carve a single grief in her sleep: a doorway for a widow, an unfinished chair for a carpenter, a struck bell for a choirboy. [339] But forty-one griefs braided into one another were a country she had no map for, and the stone stood blank in her yard all summer, waiting. [365] "I need the shape of it," she told him, "the shape of collective grief." [379]

The wings stirred, unhurried. [383] "Walk the water," he said. [388] So all month she retraced the flood's path from the broken dam down through the drowned streets, asking at every door the question her family asked at dinner: what line would you trade for your supper? [424] The ferryman's widow traded "the kettle sings for no one." [434] The miller's boy traded "the wheel turns without me." [443] Each line went onto a ribbon, each ribbon onto the roll, and the roll grew heavy the way grief does when it stops being only yours. [469]

The monarchs thickened on the glass like stained windows breathing. [479] Some nights thunder rolled along the ridge and the air never moved, and Mira came to call that sound by its true name, thundering calm, the noise a town makes when it is holding on. [514]

On the last evening of the migration she invited the whole town to dinner in the greenhouse, and they came, and they traded poems among the roosting butterflies until the glass fogged with their voices. [549] When the lanterns burned low and the living went home, she stayed to say goodbye. [564] Aldous stood among the wings, thinner than a month before, with monarchs hanging from his shoulders like a coat of light. [585] She read him his own ribbon, the one her grandmother had woven the night he proposed: "Whatever I lose, I lose toward you." [608] The wings rose once, all together, and inside the sound he gave her his last line: "Grief shared is not divided; it is woven." [632] At dawn the last monarch left the valley, and the greenhouse stood empty, and Mira did not pretend otherwise. [651]

She carried the great roll to the blank stone and unrolled it the whole way, forty years and forty-one doors of it, a river of ribbon across the grass. [680] Then she looked at it the way he had taught her to look at granite: fiercely, in total stillness, until it confessed. [702] She carved through November, retracing every ribbon line onto the granite, hundreds of fine interlacing grooves that crossed and held. [722] Up close, the stone held forty-one separate griefs, each with its own door, its chair, its bell. [739] From the road, the weave resolved into wings, thousands of them, crossing the granite together the way the monarchs had crossed the valley: many small griefs, one long current, going somewhere together. [771] And when people asked how she had found it, she only said she had once had dinner with her grandfather. [791]
