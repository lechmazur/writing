Mara counted steps the way other people counted regrets: endlessly, and without any real hope of finishing. [17] The staircase climbed forever, and it also descended forever, and she was somewhere in the middle of that forever, walking. [37] There were no walls, no ceiling, only landings every hundred steps, identical as proofs of the same theorem. [55] That openness was the cruelty of it: she could see forever in every direction, and infinity itself was the wall. [75] She was a grim optimist by constitution: she expected each landing to be her last, was proven wrong every time, and kept expecting anyway. [99]

She had been a mathematician, once, on a world with weather. [110] Her masterpiece had proven, through pure mathematical proofs, that no closed system could sustain motion forever. [126] The proof was celebrated, and it was airtight, and the morning after the celebration she woke up inside it. [145] This was the joke: she had disproved perpetual motion, and her disproof ran on her legs. [161] The stairs existed because she climbed them; her walking was the unceasing motion of a machine built from the one proof that said such machines could not exist. [189] The universe, it seemed, enjoyed irony more than rigor. [198]

In her pocket she carried a pair of dice, one chipped along a single edge. [213] Sefa had pressed them into her palm at a train station, years ago, on the last night either of them was young. [235] Roll with me, Sefa had said, meaning the dice, meaning the train, meaning everything. [249] Mara had closed her hand without throwing, and chosen the proof instead, because proofs do not depart at midnight. [268] She had spent the years since trying to recover the texture of that missed opportunity: the exact weight of an unwritten future, the temperature of a door left unopened. [297] That was why she climbed: not escape, though she wanted that too, but recall. [311] Every step she took, she hoped, walked her closer to feeling that platform again, dice warm in her fist, chance still alive. [333]

Between landings she audited her own work, because every proof has assumptions, and assumptions have edges. [349] She was balancedly daring as a scholar: she leaped boldly between ideas, but only after checking the ledge. [367] Each step was engraved with a line of her proof, and she read them as she passed, hunting for the flaw she knew must exist, since she was standing inside its consequence. [399] On step two hundred thousand and one, she found it. [409] Lemma nine: the system is closed; all outcomes are fixed; nothing enters from outside. [423] A deterministic world, sealed like a coin. [430] But the night at the station, the unthrown dice, Sefa's held breath: those were variables she had never accounted for, chance itself, the open door her equations had quietly bricked over. [461] If she could introduce one true random event into the proof, the seal would fail. [476]

She sat down on the cold step, took out the dice, and shook them, listening to them click like a train pulling out. [499] Her daring had always been balanced like this: she would stake exactly one step, no more, on an infinity that could spare it. [522] She threw. [524] The perfect die settled on a four. [531] The chipped one rocked, wobbled, and came to rest on the chip itself, standing at an angle the geometry of dice forbids, showing a face that did not exist: a seventh outcome. [563] The staircase heard it. [567] A rupture opened along the steps, a seam of white tearing upward through the engraved mathematics, splitting lemma from lemma, and through the gap she saw rain, a platform, a train with its doors still open. [603] Not the old night, she understood, but a new one, still unwritten. [615] The proof had finally met something it could not contain: the sound of dice hitting stone, which is the sound of a future being shaken loose. [641]

She stood and picked up the dice, both of them, even the impossible one. [655] Behind her the stairs were already stalling, step by step, a perpetual machine finally permitted to stop. [672] She stepped through the rupture sideways, the way one boards a moving train, and the white seam closed behind her like a held breath released. [697] Rain on the platform, real and cold. [704] She walked toward the open doors, rolling the dice across her knuckles, and this time she did not close her hand. [725] Wherever the train was going, she would find out by going there, which was, she thought, the only proof that ever mattered. [747]
