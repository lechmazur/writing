The eclipse would reach the crater at noon, and the whole frightened hemisphere was holding its breath for it. [19] Yeva had been holding hers for years. [26] Forty-one winters ago a star had fallen here, punched a deep bowl into the subarctic tundra, and poisoned the summers that followed. [48] The orchard planted on the crater floor had withered tree by tree in the poisoned summers, and in spring the families had drifted south, leaving only the flash-paper alchemist behind. [78]

Her craft was the most impatient substance on earth: nitrate a sheet of paper, dry it, and it bloomed into fire and nothing, gone inside a heartbeat. [105] A woman who works with the instant must practice its opposite, so she had taught herself patience in her body, not her mind. [128] She warmed her acid baths one degree an hour. [137] She stood unmoving while frost climbed her sleeves, because a shudder could spoil a dipping, and a spoiled dipping was a day of light lost. [162] All winter she had hung the finished sheets on the dead apple boughs each clear night, letting the paper drink the subarctic sky, and by morning each sheet held a pale gray shimmer she called safekept starlight. [199] Starlight was the one debt she meant to repay. [208]

Her heart was failing now; the doctor who had left with the others had counted its stumbles and given her two winters at most. [232] So she had begun to translate her heartbeats into a map, one beat counted, one object placed. [249] The evacuees had left the crater littered, and she gathered their leavings: tin cup lids, a sled runner, jar rings, buckles, the ribs of an umbrella. [275] Between the withered trees she laid them in patterns, row upon row, following the image she knew by heart: the storybook illustration plate of an orchard in blossom, a printer's copper block rescued from the empty town. [312] Every tin lid was a blossom; the sled runner made the river at the orchard's edge. [328] It had taken four hundred days, and she regretted none of them. [340]

Liv had been nine when the families left, and she had gone south clutching a storybook whose best page was the blossoming orchard. [363] The picture had been Liv's favorite, the page she traced with one finger every night, asking whether real blossoms had shone like that before the star came down. [391] A letter had gone south in the autumn, timed to arrive before the eclipse: Come home by the day the sky goes dark; stand at the crater gate at noon, and see what blossoms here. [426]

Now the day had come. [431] Through the long partial phase she knelt at the pattern's edge and worked without hurry, laying one sheet of safekept starlight beneath every tin blossom, weighting each corner with a pebble, moving to the count of her heart: beat, place, beat, place. [473] She pressed the illustration plate gently into the last sheet, so the copper orchard would leave its raised lines in the paper, then set the plate back in its cloth. [503] Then she sealed the plate inside a biscuit tin with candle wax, pressed her thumbprint into the warm seal, and hung the tin from the crater gate where a returning girl could not miss it. [538]

At noon the moon finished its slow crossing, the wind forgot to blow, snowflakes hung midair, and the world held its breath. [560] Stars came out in the sudden dusk, the same stars whose light slept in her paper. [576] She touched the match to the first sheet at the pattern's corner, and the blossom flared once, brief as a pulse, and went out. [600] She limped along the rows, lighting sheet after sheet, and each white flash was a heartbeat spent, and the spent heartbeats drew themselves into a map. [626] From the rim, the flashes among the tin lids would read as an orchard in flower, a constellation laid on the crater floor saying here, she was here, come home. [656] The light the paper had drunk all winter went back as fire, and she felt the old debt of the fallen star paid down, note by note. [683]

Then the moon slid aside, daylight poured back, and the world exhaled; birds resumed somewhere far south, and radios crackled with relief. [705] In the orchard only the pattern remained, tin blossoms among ash, still as held breath. [720] Yeva sat down among the discarded things that had become a garden and listened to what was left of her heart, slow, unhurried, patient as it had learned to be from her. [752] Whether or not Liv saw it, the map said what maps are for: you can find your way back to anything you loved, if someone keeps a light burning for you. [783]
