I am the person who notices what slips away by inches. [11] My neighbors called it morbid, my habit of cataloguing creeping normality: crosswalk paint fading one rainy season at a time, the courthouse oak losing a limb a year until it was just a pole. [45] Worst of all was Anselm's bakery. [51] No one could name the day it closed, because it never closed; it dimmed. [65] The loaves grew smaller, the chalk menu smudged, the morning smell of rye arrived later, until the windows were papered over and everyone said the place had been gone for ages. [96] It hadn't. [98] I had watched it vanish in slow motion, and no one believed the speedometer of my grief. [115]

So I garden. [118] By night, with seed in my pockets and a trowel apologetic in my sleeve, I practice guerrilla gardening on the city's wounds: vacant lots, traffic medians, the graves of buildings. [148] I do it to find the sacred in the ordinary, because the ordinary is the only scripture I trust. [167] When they razed the bakery's shell, I climbed the fence at two in the morning and sowed winter wheat into the rubble dirt, a yellow amen. [193]

After the birds flew south, the city went quiet enough to hear itself. [206] Swallows gone, swifts gone; the air had the hollow, swept feeling of a house after the guests leave. [224] Walking home past the old palace, a hulk the city stopped maintaining decades ago, I smelled bread. [241] Warm bread, where nothing had baked for years. [249] I found the gap in the palace's iron fence that I had ignored since childhood, crossed its overgrown courtyard, and followed my nose to a low wooden shape swallowed by wisteria. [280] I pulled the vines aside like a curtain, uncloaked what they had hidden, and there stood a secret tea house, small and patient, breathing smoke from a tin chimney. [309] On a shelf inside the window sat a petrified loaf, scored with three diagonal slashes I knew like my own name. [330] It was his final loaf, the one he could not give away, bread from a vanished bakery kept like a relic. [351]

"Sit," said the man at the stove. [358] Anselm. [359] He poured tea before I could accuse him of anything, which was its own absolution, tenuous as steam. [377] He had fled creeping normality too, his own. [385] His wife Lena had grown tired in increments, and he, who could feel a dough's readiness through his palms, never felt her dimming until she was gone. [412] He kept baking her loaf every morning and giving it to strangers, until the shop itself became an accusation. [431] So he hid here, where no one could watch him fail her daily. [444] I confessed my own small crime: I had watched his windows dim for three years and never asked him why. [464]

He answered by refilling my cup, gentle the way he kneaded, by rule of thumb. [479] That was his whole liturgy now, tea for whoever found the door. [491] Not gentleness from principle, but from heuristics: water warm, never hot; voices low, never hushed; hands open before questions. [510] Heuristically gentle, I thought, a kindness tested a thousand times on dough. [522]

I told him about the wheat. [528] He wept, or nearly, with a baker's economy of grief. [538] Come summer, he said, he would harvest it and bake again in the tea house's little oven, bread owing nothing to any shop. [561] "No one can pardon me but her," he said. [570] "Then take the tenuous kind," I said. [577] "The kind that holds like warm bread, as long as nobody squeezes." [589] He laughed then, a sound the courtyard had not heard in years. [601]

We drank tea while the first snow considered falling. [610] The stale loaf stayed on its shelf, sacred now, not supper. [621] That is our arrangement: ordinary things, noticed, enthroned. [629] I still garden by night. [634] And I still notice, including the noticing itself: how mercy also arrives by inches, and how a ruined courtyard goes from overgrown to tended so slowly that only someone like me will ever see it happen.
