Wren came back to the siskin alder grove in the ninth silent spring, the morning the hedgehog finally uncurled. [19] It had rolled itself tight the day the Hush fell over the valley, and it had waited beneath the roots of her sculpture like a small brown fist. [47] Only after the hedgehog uncurled, spines settling like rain easing off a roof, did she kneel and let herself look up. [68]

The sculpture had been her masterwork: nine alder trunks carved into a rising spiral of siskins, wings overlapping wings, a choir with open beaks. [92] Wind used to pour through it, and the grove had sung, thin and bright, like water over stones. [110] Then the Hush came, and every sound that tried to travel simply died. [123] Wind still moved, but it made nothing of its moving; voices faded an arm's length from the mouth; the birds fell silent, and the valley learned to live without being heard. [154] The tree-song sculptor had walked away and let the world forget her choir. [167]

She had not come back to mourn. [174] She had come because something was loosening, hedgehog spines and alder catkins and her own stubborn hands. [191] She had come to make music in a silent world, because a world that still aches to be sung to has not surrendered. [214] Nine frosts had not been kind. [220] It was webbed with stress cracks, fine as hair, running down every carved wing. [234] Anyone else would have seen ruin. [240] Wren was symphonically minded; she could not help reading cracks the way she had once read scores, every line at once, hearing the whole. [264] A crack, she knew, was a record: the place where the wood had strained under some old note and finally given way. [286] By analyzing the stress cracks in the long-forgotten sculpture, she could read the grove's music: which pitches had shaken the grain apart, which ones the wood had loved enough to hold. [317] The cracks were a score, written in damage. [325]

The wire found her before she went looking. [333] An old telegraph line had once crossed the grove, and its rotted poles had leaned, letting their cargo down gently into the leaf litter. [357] Near the sculpture's base she uncovered the first porcelain insulator, still threaded on a length of wire, its brown glaze crazed to the soft gray of old teeth. [385] As a girl she had pressed her ear to a humming telegraph pole and felt messages pass under her cheek, and she remembered it now like a hand on her shoulder. [416] Sound died in air, but it lived in solid things; the Hush had never thought to check the wire. [435] She tapped the porcelain with a fingernail and held it to her ear, and something small and high completed the short journey: a star of sound, the first music in nine years. [467] Her hands began to shake, and she made them be still. [478]

The instrument took her three days to build. [486] She strung salvaged wire from alder to alder and mounted each line on porcelain insulators at both ends, because porcelain was hard enough to pass a vibration along without swallowing it. [517] Then she tuned, and this was where the cracks came in. [528] For every crack she matched a wire, tuning it to the exact pitch that had once strained the grain, so that when one wire sounded, the cracked wing beside it would tremble in answer. [562] Instrument makers had a word for that: sympathy, one string grieving when another grieved. [576] Wren thought it was really empathy in motion, one body answering another's trouble, and she thought music had always been exactly that. [598] She was not forcing the grove to sing; she was giving each wound back its own note, gently, and letting it resolve. [620]

On the fourth morning the wind rose and did nothing, as always. [632] Wren laid her cheek against the nearest alder, reached out, and strummed. [644] Nothing happened in the air. [649] Everything happened in the wood. [654] The note ran down the wire, crossed the porcelain, entered the trunk, and spread through the grove like a shiver of light, and against her cheek the whole sculpture began to hum. [686] One by one the cracked wings woke and trembled in sympathy, nine years of stored grief turning at last into harmony. [707] It was the quietest music ever made, a music you had to lean your whole body against the world to hear, and it was enough. [732] The hedgehog, nosing through the roots, pressed its low body to a singing trunk and stayed. [748] A siskin dropped onto the highest wire, and its small weight bent the note slightly, kindly, the way a second performer leans into a duet. [773] Wren closed her eyes and strummed again, softly, and the silent world leaned in close to listen. [790]
