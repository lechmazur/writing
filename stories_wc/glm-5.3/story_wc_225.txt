Marek came back to Halden during the lull of distant thunder, while the storm held its breath over the hills. [20]
The village had not moved in seven years. [28]
Wagons hung mid-rut, rain stood in beads above the cobbles, and a baker leaned eternally toward an oven that would never warm again. [51]
The sorceress-weaver Ivesa had frozen Halden at the exact moment Marek's king fell. [64]
He had played chess for the village against her, champion by trade and tutor by calling, and he had lost on a gambit everyone begged him not to try. [93]
Her sentence was precise and cruel: Halden would sleep inside a hush she had woven, and its exiled tutor would walk outside it, remembering everything. [118]
She had left him one thing: a platinum labyrinth small enough to close his fist around, its channels fine as capillaries, a single pinhole at the center. [145]
"Solve it," she had said, "and come home." [153]
He had carried it every day since, and his hands had never once been still. [168]
Exile taught him to fidget productively: while his mind rehearsed the losing game, his thumbs wandered the grooves until he could map every turn of the tiny maze blindfolded. [197]
He walked now through the frozen market, past neighbors paused mid-greeting, and felt the old fury bank itself into stillness. [217]
In the great hall, the tapestry still hung where Ivesa had worked it: Halden rendered in thread, every roof and face and hanging raindrop, and around it all a border dense with knots. [250]
Kneeling, he saw what seven years and a hundred rumors had missed: the border was fraying. [266]
Strands had lifted, snapped, coiled loose, and their raised ends pointed -- he leaned closer -- in patterns. [282]
His thumbs knew those patterns. [287]
The border was the platinum labyrinth writ large; every frayed strand lay along a channel his hands had memorized without his permission. [309]
So he set to work by decoding the frayed threads in the tapestry's border, reading them as he read the maze, by touch and turn. [334]
The knots spelled moves, he realized -- the whole of the lost game, knot by knot, woven in order. [352]
And the frayed strands were the lies. [359]
Where a knot claimed she had moved her rook, the loose thread beneath it told the truth: a knight, placed out of turn during a candle change. [386]
She had cheated, and then, weaving her triumph, she had frayed her own deceptions so that anyone patient enough could winnow them from the true cloth. [412]
A confession, and a dare. [417]
Marek's breath went slow and even, the way it did over a board in the last hard hour. [435]
Rage was a luxury; repose was a weapon. [443]
With a needle borrowed from his own collar, he began to winnow, easing each frayed false strand free of its knot without disturbing the true weave. [469]
It took the length of the lull. [476]
His fingers trembled, then steadied, then worked with the calm of a man who had already punished himself worse than any sorceress could. [499]
Because the truth cut both ways, and he made himself look at the other edge: she had cheated, yes, but she had needed to. [523]
On the honest board, his position had been winning until pride whispered that winning was not enough; he wanted her humiliated, chased, crushed. [546]
The gambit was his sin, whatever her crime. [554]
"I knew better," he said to the frozen faces in the thread, "and I did it anyway." [571]
Saying it aloud did not feel like absolution, but it felt like the first honest move in seven years. [590]

The anchor of the hush. [622]
He threaded its end into the pinhole of the platinum labyrinth and let the maze take it, channel by channel, the way water follows a designed course. [649]
The labyrinth had never wanted a probe or a key; it wanted this: the hush itself, rewound into its center. [669]

He was not forgiven yet; he was forgiving. [771]
