The fjord lay quiet under a sky the color of old tin, and Signe went out at first light to feed her clocks. [23]
She was the clock shepherd at the horizon measurement station, the last posting on the last finger of rock before the water took over the world. [49]
The station kept three hydraulic clocks, tall brass creatures that ran on snowmelt piped down from the ridge, and her work was simple: keep the reservoir full, keep the channels clear, keep the flock watered and ticking. [86]
She had come for one reason, and she had told it to the interview board plainly: she wanted to understand what clocks really measure. [110]
They had given her the post because nobody else wanted the quiet. [122]
Around her neck, on a cord, hung a small key, its teeth worn to razor edges by fifty years of Sundays. [143]
It was her grandmother's mantle clock key, all she had taken from the farm, and it fit nothing at the station. [164]
The mantle clock itself stood dark in the empty kitchen, stopped the winter her grandmother died, and Signe had never wound it again. [187]
Protocol at dawn was fixed: blow the whistle, count backwards from sixty, and let the echo off the far cliff tell you where the horizon was. [213]
She blew the whistle, long and flat, and the sound went out over the water like a bird with nowhere to land. [235]
After blowing the whistle, she counted. [241]
Sixty, fifty-nine, fifty-eight, down through the still air, and the echo should have come home at zero. [258]
It came at three. [262]
Three counts late, and thin, as if the far cliff had moved away in the night. [278]
She climbed the tower and squinted along the brass horizon wire, one eye shut, the fjord pressed to a single line. [299]
On ordinary days that line was a razor edge, so clean it seemed you could shave the sky with it. [319]
Today there were two edges. [324]
A second horizon hung above the first, faint and floating, as though the world were being cut twice. [342]
The hydraulic clocks disagreed with the optical gauge by eleven seconds, and the logbook demanded an explanation. [359]
So she did what she always did with a problem: she went backwards. [372]
She carried the ledger stack to the table and counted backwards through the years, volume by volume, 1974, 1973, 1972, looking for another morning that had refused to behave. [401]

Under Remarks he had written two words: eminently peculiar. [430]
Nothing else. [432]
No explanation, no theory, just a man long dead admitting the world had shown him its seam. [449]
Signe sat with that for a long time, the fjord quiet around her, the doubled horizon burning pale above the water. [470]
Then she took the key from her neck and turned it over in the cold light. [486]
Fifty years of Sundays had honed those teeth like little razors, and every one of them had been a cut: Sunday from Saturday, tick from tock, grandmother from girl. [515]
She understood it then, the way you understand something that has been standing behind you all morning. [532]
The horizon was not a thing. [538]
It was a cut, made new every dawn out of water and sky, and it moved when the air moved, and no instrument would ever pin it down because there was nothing there to pin. [573]
A clock was the same. [578]
It did not hold time; it cut time, tick by tick, each second a razor edge slid between what was gone and what was coming. [603]
And what a clock measured, she saw now, was the same thing the whistle measured: distance. [619]
You sent something out, a sound, a tick, a drip of snowmelt, and you counted until it came back to you. [640]
Her grandmother's clock had measured the distance between Sundays, between the kitchen and the grave, between the woman winding and the girl listening. [663]
It had never been telling the time. [670]
It had been calling across a fjord of its own, and counting. [682]
Signe opened today's ledger and wrote the numbers plainly: echo late by three, horizon doubled, clocks adrift by eleven seconds. [702]

Clocks measure distance. [718]
Time is the far cliff. [723]
