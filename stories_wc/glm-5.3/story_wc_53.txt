The kinetic lagoon hung above the clouds, its water gone glass-still, and Wren scavenged its drowned deck chairs and dropped pennies as she had every day since the carousel halted. [30] She was a resolute scavenger, and resolute was the only word for someone who kept working a lagoon whose tide had died. [52] She had been the carousel's apprentice once, the girl who memorized every horse's chipped teeth and swore she would wind its brass heart herself. [76] Ambition, she had learned, erodes the way the old tide once eroded the fog: patiently, from the edges inward. [95] The day the motor died she was three hundred miles below, selling bus tickets, and the dream had already worn too thin to grieve. [119]

The halt had stopped the tide, the birds, the cotton-candy fog, the visitors mid-laugh, but it kept everything, so nothing could be forgotten, least of all her. [146] On the carousel's lead horse sat a girl only Wren could see: her own ghost, twelve years old, still holding the ticket Wren had promised to use. [173] Wren came home within the month and climbed to the platform every dusk to negotiate, and every dusk the ghost said nothing. [195]

So Wren had become a scavenger of different debris: she collected fragments of overheard stories. [210] The visitors' final sentences hung in the stopped air like flies in amber. [223] She had copied hundreds into a ledger: "and then he said the thing about the lighthouse," "I never told her about the money," "hold my hand when it starts moving again." [254] That was how she found the camera. [261]

It lay in the silt beneath the teacup ride, a disposable camera with a cracked shell, but its film-advance wheel still clicked. [283] Each click of the disposable camera wheel moved the stillness one frame forward. [296] But the frames obeyed no order she could trust. [305] She had clicked herself into a Tuesday nine years before the halt, into a storm from after it, into a birthday that had not happened yet. [331] She stopped trusting sequence; she had become chronologically confused, unable to say which Wren had come first, the ticket seller or the apprentice, the woman or the ghost. [359] The overheard fragments became her calendar; she matched sentences to frames the way other scavengers matched keys to locks. [378]

Tonight she had four clicks left of the original twenty-seven. [388] She spent three to reach the halted day at dusk, the sky the rose of stopped motion, the visitors leaning at the angles of interrupted laughter. [414] She crossed the planks to the carousel, wheel in one fist, ledger under her arm. [429] She opened the negotiation the only way the ghost permitted: in borrowed words. [442]

"Hold my hand when it starts moving again," she recited, like a lawyer citing precedent. [457] "I never told her about the money." [464] "You said you'd be back before summer." [471] Each fragment was a piece of the day the girl had waited through, and Wren assembled them aloud: a girl saves a ticket, her mentor dies, she descends the long stair to the world below for honest work, meaning to return in a season. [515] Time, unmoored from motion, closed over that departure like water, and the self who stayed aboard became a ghost. [534]

"You want an apology," Wren said. [540] "I have said sorry four hundred times, and it bounces off this stillness like a coin off ice." [558] The ghost turned her head slightly, the only motion anyone in the lagoon still owned. [573] Wren understood at last. [577] Sorry was cheap, eroded currency. [582] What the ghost wanted was regret, the heavy kind, the kind that arrives with an inventory attached. [599]

So Wren gave it. [603] She named what the years below had bought her, a rented room, a pension, a coat with working buttons, and priced each against the brass heart she had never wound. [633] Then she said the sentence she had dodged for twenty years, not "I meant to come back," but "I chose, every day, not to, until choosing stopped feeling like a choice." [664] That was regret without excuse, and the frozen fog leaned in, because even stopped stories know that sound. [682]

The ghost held out the ticket at last. [690] Wren took it, raised the camera, and spent her final click on the girl on the lead horse, the rose sky, the whole uneroded dream. [715] The wheel refused to wind again; the film was finished. [725] That was the release. [729]

The lagoon had been waiting for its story to reach the last frame, and the stillness fractured like ice giving way. [750] The tide remembered itself; the visitors' laughter finished, one beat late, and rose into the clouds. [766] The horses trembled on their poles; the lead horse stood empty. [777] Below the platform a brass heart waited, and Wren, scavenger no longer, rolled up her sleeves. [793]
