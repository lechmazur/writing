The orchard had been forgotten so long that even its forgetting was forgotten, which was why the silence liked it there. [21] It had come raveling out of the bamboo forest like thread pulled from a hem, and where it passed, sound unknit itself: birdsong loosening into nothing, names fraying mid-call. [50] Tacey, the loom spinner, lived at the bamboo's edge and understood raveling better than anyone, because her trade was the opposite of it. [73] Every morning she watched the quiet widen across the forgotten orchard, and every morning she thought of Wren, who had walked into it a year ago carrying a pruning knife and a promise to return by dark. [110] No shout crossed that distance; the raveling silence took voices apart before they could finish meaning anything. [127] So Tacey grew wistful the way amber grows: slowly, in layers, sealing grief inside something that could hold light. [146]

It was at the village rummage sale, in a box marked three coins or best offer, that she found the puzzle. [167] The picture on the lid was the orchard itself in blossom, painted before the bamboo closed over it like a held breath. [189] Inside, the jigsaw pieces were all edges, tabs and blanks worn smooth as river stones, and one space at the heart of the lid's image where a piece should have been and was not. [223] She carried the box home beneath her arm and did not haggle. [235]

That night she fitted two edges together by lamplight, and the seam they made gave off a sound: one cricket, stitching. [256] She joined a third piece, and a fourth, and each fitted edge recommenced some small sound the silence had unraveled, the way a patch restores a fraying cloth. [284] She began to understand that through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry ran the seams of everything lost, and that the puzzle's edges found them the way a needle finds a hem. [316]

Tacey worked for weeks, her wheel idle, her fingers busy, building the orchard back one interlocking border at a time. [336] Sound followed her progress: first crickets, then wind, then the ghost-creak of the old press in the barn where Wren had pressed cider every autumn. [361] When the borders were whole, only the heart-shaped gap remained, and she knew what it had been waiting for. [380] From her workbasket she took the amber bead, sap from the orchard's oldest plum tree, hardened around a single year of summer and stoically sparkling even in the dark, the way patient things sparkle when no one is watching. [419] It had been Wren's, worn on a cord at her throat since they were girls; Wren had pressed it into Tacey's palm at the orchard gate, saying, "Hold this, so some part of me stays outside." [455]

Tacey set the bead into the gap, and it fit the way the last knot fits a weaving. [473] Then she leaned close and gave the amber her message, every word she had stored through the long quiet: that the barn was mended, that the press was oiled, that she was not angry about the unfinished scarf, that Wren should follow the fitted edges home. [519] A message carried in the mouth unravels; a message carried in amber, which is time made solid, keeps. [537] But sound must pay for sound. [543] She felt her voice leave her the way thread leaves a spindle, evenly, without complaint, winding itself into the bead until the sparkle at its heart brightened and her throat held only the hush she had spent a year fighting. [583] She had guessed the price the moment she saw what the box was; every weaver knows that cloth is always paid for in thread. [607]

In the morning she carried the finished puzzle into the orchard and set it down where the raveling silence was thickest, beneath the oldest plum. [632] The fitted edges caught the light and ran with it, seam by seam, a bright line stitching away through the bamboo toward wherever the tapestry had come undone. [660] The light gathered into the amber, and the amber sank through the picture like a stone through water, taking her message with it across the impossible distance between the living and the lost. [693]

Tacey stood in the hush afterward, in the orchard deep in the bamboo, dusted with plum petals that had no sound left to fall with, and waited. [720] She was still wistful; that never raveled. [727] But one evening, deep in the hush, the wind moved wrong, against the grain, carrying back a sound the silence had not been given: a latch lifting, a gate swinging, footsteps on fitted stone. [761] Tacey could not call out, and did not need to. [771] She took up her wheel and set it turning, so the house would be making something when Wren reached it. [791]
