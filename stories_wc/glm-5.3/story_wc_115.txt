The hush began the instant her grandfather's sudden confession ran out. [11] It settled over the hospital room like snowfall, and into that silence, twenty minutes later, Mara took the car keys and left. [33] Her mother would call it desertion. [39] Mara called it fieldwork. [43] For six years she had crawled into dead mills -- flooded ones, burned ones, ones the county had buried in kudzu -- and never broken anything that mattered. [69] Recklessness was just curiosity with its seatbelt off. [77]

What he had said, in a voice like a dropped quilt: in sixty-six the mill ran a ghost shift, off the books, nine men; the water was rising and he shut the valve on the tunnel and never opened it. [117] Then he looked at her, the family skeptic, the one who rolled her eyes at psalms and psychics alike, and said: you weigh things; go find the weight of what I swallowed. [149]

She drove to the Brindle tunnel, the dead freight line that had fed the mill, and ducked the chain at one in the morning without telling a soul. [177] The county's sign promised a third of the bore had collapsed; she went in anyway, because that was the whole point of her. [200] Rails still ran down the middle of the floor, slick as eels. [212]

In one ear she kept a radio, because three a.m. in a dead tunnel can put you to sleep standing up. [233] Only one station reached this far down, sliding along the concrete like water: a psychic call-in show, The Sixth Hour. [253] Ordinarily she kept it on to mock it. [261] She did not believe in ghosts, heaven, psychics, or the hereafter; she believed in rust, ledgers, and the specific gravity of water. [283] But tonight the callers were strange, and her drowsy mind snagged on them like a sleeve on a nail. [302]

A woman, frantic: "He clocked in in sixty-six and never clocked out, and I still hear the looms at three in the morning." [325] The host soothed her toward spirits and rest; Mara, sloshing through ankle-deep water, translated differently: not a haunting, a bookkeeping error -- men erased from the payroll and therefore, on paper, never dead at all. [359] An old man, near tears: "My mother strung her rosary on a wheel down in the dark, so the water couldn't take it back." [383] A wheel, in the dark. [388] That was how she appraised the ghost shift, she understood, half-dreaming and wide awake at once: the psychic heard visitations, and she heard directions. [412]

The calls led her down the side gallery where the tailrace ran, and there it stood in her lamplight: a valve wheel the size of a steering wheel, pinned to the wall, strung with beads. [447] Nine strings of them, looped through the spokes, mineral-furred from fifty years of drip. [461] Rosary beads -- prayer, technically, though to her they looked like abacuses for grief, counting and counting on their wires. [480] She did what she was good at, what he had asked for: she appraised it. [495] Rust lay over the hub in one unbroken skin, which meant nobody had turned the wheel in decades; a loop of wire sealed the stem shut, twisted in a double twist she knew from a hundred jars in her grandfather's garage. [536] He had closed it; he had never opened it; the confession was true. [549] She hung her weight on the rim to be certain, and the wheel did not give, and something in her chest did instead. [572] So that was the weight of a swallowed truth: nine strings of beads, one rusted wheel, the full lean of her body. [594]

In her ear the show rolled on, caller after frantic caller, and she finally understood what she was hearing: fifty years of the ghost shift phoning in, family by family, night by night, while a psychic mistook them for hauntings. [634] Not ghosts -- erased men, whose only clock-card was a string of beads. [646]

She fished out her phone -- one trembling bar of signal -- and called the station, because a truth only has weight once you set it down somewhere. [672] The host picked up, breathless. [677] "You're live, caller -- what's on your mind?" [684] "Nine men," Mara said, into the last of the hush, her voice steady, tired, and absolutely awake. [701] "The mill ran a ghost shift in sixty-six; my grandfather sealed the tunnel; I'm standing at the valve now, and it's beaded like a rosary, and it hasn't turned since." [731]

Somewhere behind her a board creaked, or the water shifted, or the looms, hallucinated by fifty years of callers, turned over once in the dark. [756] Mara did not believe in ghosts. [762] But she stayed until dawn with her hand resting on the beads, listening, wide awake. [777]
