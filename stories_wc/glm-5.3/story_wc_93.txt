No one had touched Mirren in nine years, because no one could find her twice. [15] The night the second sun collapsed, its dust had settled over the world like a second skin, and friction went with it. [37] Now everything slid: silt, boats, the eels she herded. [46] The same dust had cursed her, giving every moment a shadow she could not stop seeing: a few heartbeats of the future, always, whether she wanted it or not. [75] When villagers climbed the bluff to ask her blessing, she saw them coming before they had decided to come, and was gone by the second bend of the path. [104] They called her the elusive eel herder and left gifts at the tideline, but she stayed on the bluff for one thing only: the promise her sister made before the sky fell. [136] "Keep the eels until I come back," Wren had said, her boat already sliding out on the frictionless tide. [155] Mirren had begged her not to go, because the curse had already shown her the shape of that leaving: long, far, and unfinished. [178] Without friction, nothing on the water could stop or turn; Wren's lantern had simply kept going, a spark sliding west for nine years. [201] The promise lingered, patient as a tide. [208]

Their grandmother had taught them that the world ran on sacred patterns -- the spiral of a shell, the primes in a starling flock -- and Mirren clung to that teaching now, hunting the pattern beneath the catastrophe. [244] Because the eels could no longer grip the mud, she herded them the only way left: by placing painted rocks in mathematical sequences across the flats, channels of numbers that deflected their endless gliding away from the open sea. [283] The paint was her own invention: collapsed star dust, sifted from the aurora's fall and mixed with eel oil until it glowed the color of a dawn the world no longer owned. [315] The dust veiled the sun, so the living worked and wept by aurora light -- a borrowed dawn, bright enough to see by, never warm enough to wake to. [343] For nine years the curse had shown her one image more often than any other: a spiral of glowing stones on the bluff, and the aurora bowing down to touch it. [374] She read it the way her grandmother would have, asking what the sacred pattern wanted rather than what she wanted from it. [396] The answer came slowly, eel by eel, stone by stone: the sequence could harness the dust itself -- call it down out of the sky and give the world back its grip. [427] Grip, she came to understand, was not a law but a loan; the dust had called it home, and the right pattern could call it back. [453]

On the ninth anniversary of the fall, she climbed the tidal bluff with two hundred painted stones and began. [472] The aurora hung low that night, green and violet, close enough to taste. [485] She laid the spiral by feel and by foresight both, the curse finally useful, showing her each next stone before her hands had found it. [510] Fibonacci at the rim, primes threading inward: the two signatures her grandmother had called the oldest words in the world. [530] When the last stone settled, the bluff went silent. [539] Then the aurora leaned down, slow as a tide reversing, and its light poured along the spiral and pulled. [558] Dust came out of the sky like snow remembering how to fall, powdering her hair, her shoulders, the stones. [577] Where it touched, the world gripped itself again. [585] Mud held mud; the wind stopped skidding across the grass; her boots found purchase on stone for the first time in nine years. [608] Below on the flats, ten thousand exhausted eels stopped sliding, settled into the still pools, and began, softly, to rest. [628] And far out on the dark water, a lantern that had been sliding west for nine years caught, slowed, and stopped. [649] The curse, gracious for once, showed her the moment before it happened: Wren standing in the bow, older, salt-burned, turning the boat east. [672] Mirren sat down on the gripping stone and laughed until she cried, or the reverse; the curse never showed her which came first. [695] By the time the sky paled, the veil had thinned enough to let the sun through, and a true dawn came up over the sea -- not borrowed, but returned, like the grip, like the eels, like the promise she had finally been allowed to keep. [740] There would be years of honest work: eels to gather, a sister to bring up the bluff. [757] She was already painting new stones -- a welcome laid in numbers, running down to the shore. [773]
