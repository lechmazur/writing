Mira had been gone thirty years before the letter found her, ink faded, asking returnees to help open the old tombs. [21] The village buried its dead beneath the apricot orchard, where roots had learned to thread between ribs and femurs like slow gray rivers. [44] She came back not to pray but to see whether faith could be recovered like a lost object, because the gods her grandmother named had gone quiet the night the boat left. [76]

The dig committee gave her the surveying work no one wanted: walk the lower passages, chart what the collapse had exposed, and mark the bones that needed naming. [104] They called it wandering; she made it a discipline, each day a different corridor, each corridor a question. [122] She was, to her own surprise, warmly curious down there, kneeling to read epitaphs with her thumb, asking the old foreman why this family faced east and that one held coins. [153] Many of the dead were pilgrims, she learned, travelers who had been interrupted here by fever or flood and buried facing the road they never finished. [179] Her mother had been one of them, in a sense: the family promised a pilgrimage to the spring at Kelter if the harvest held, and then the war came, and then the boat, and the promise went into the water with everything else. [222]

In the fourth week, her wandering led her to a sealed chamber behind a root-webbed wall, and the foreman declared it a proper tomb, single occupant, stone lid intact. [251] Opening it took eleven days, chisel by chisel, while apricots ripened overhead and dropped sweetness through the shaft's grate. [270] Inside lay a woman the epitaph named Sela, the village sundial-maker, buried with her tools and an unfinished commission. [289] Beside her, wrapped in oiled linen, sat a carved sundial base of honey-colored stone, its hour lines half-cut, the socket for the gnomon empty. [313] With the foreman's help, Mira translated the inscription: Sela had gone to the coast for the bronze rod that would cast the shadow, but fever had interrupted her twice on the road, and she came home to die with the base still waiting. [356]

Mira distrusted the small awe that moved in her chest; she had learned abroad that old things are usually just old. [377] But she found herself returning to the chamber each evening, lamp low, tracing the half-cut hours. [393] On the eleventh day, when the lid came fully free, she asked the committee for the base, and for permission to rebuild it. [416] The foreman shook his head at her plan and then helped her anyway, and so began her second, stranger wandering: purposeful, but aimless-looking, through the village and its margins. [445] She walked the old sun-ritual paths her grandmother had half-described, timing shadows against the town clock; she found a broken plowshare that a smith agreed to draw into a rod; she asked the oldest woman in the village how the festival hours were counted before clocks came. [492] Each discovery fed back into the stone: she finished the missing lines above ground, hour by hour, kneeling where roots held the bones of the people who had first kept those hours. [524] Doubt kept pace with wonder the whole time, and she let it; enchantment that cannot survive questions, she told herself, is only decoration. [547]

On the morning the tomb was declared fully recorded, she set the rebuilt dial on a flat stone above Sela's chamber, fitted the rod, and waited for noon. [575] The shadow crossed the new-cut lines like a finger finding a page, and the villagers who gathered made a sound she remembered from childhood, half gasp and half hymn. [604] Something in her, quiet for thirty years, turned over. [613] It was not the gods speaking, she thought; it was older and plainer than that: work stopped could be worked again, roads broken could be walked to their ends. [642]

That evening she walked the six dusty miles to the spring at Kelter, filled a flask, and poured a little on the roots above the pilgrims, a little on Sela's stone, and drank the rest. [677] The pilgrimage her family had promised was finished, a generation late. [688] The faith that returned to her was small and specific, like a shadow that keeps its appointments, and she found it was enough. [711] In spring, when the dig closed, she stayed. [719]
