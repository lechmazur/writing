The last wish in Paper-Lantern Cove was granted on the first frostbitten night of autumn, and by morning the town was empty except for the alchemist. [26] Her name was Vess, deserted now by every soul she had ever served, and she had stayed because someone had to close the doors. [50] For sixty years the Cove had granted wishes: sailors wished for safe harbors, widows wished for one more morning with their dead, children wished for wings, and the lanterns, fueled by her luminous oil, gave each wisher exactly what was asked. [91] That was the trouble with wishes, Vess had learned; they answered the words and abandoned the heart. [108] When the final lantern guttered out, the last wisher packed her suitcase and followed her granted miracle inland, and nobody thought to wonder who would sweep the ash. [136] Vess swept it. [139]

On her workbench sat an amber bottle with no opening, seamless as a teardrop, heavier than any glass had a right to be. [162] It was the sediment of every wish she had ever distilled, the residue the lanterns left behind when they burned hope into the salt air. [187] Alchemists know how to seal what cannot be poured out. [197] She knew, too, that one hard strike against the anvil would crack it open, and that a single drop on a wick would light a wish of her own. [226] She could wish the Cove full again. [233] She could wish the harbor bright with boats and the bakery warm with bread, could stand in the square and believe, for an evening, that she was not alone. [262] Vess put the bottle down and stepped away from it, because comfort that vanishes at dawn is just grief with better lighting. [284] She refused to be the last person fooled by this town. [295]

Instead, she sat at the bench each morning and did the harder thing: she remembered. [310] Not the soft, drifting kind of memory, but working memory, the kind that holds a whole thought steady while the hands do something with it. [335] She kept the fisherman Halloran's face in mind while she rehung his dock, plank by plank, recalling where his crates sat and how deep his mooring ran. [362] She held Marisol's bread recipe in her head, all of it, water and salt and the long patience of the second rise, and baked it in the cold oven until the kitchen smelled like a reason to stay. [400] Memory, she discovered, is a workshop. [406] Whatever you can hold whole in the front of your mind, you can rebuild with the back of your hands. [426] Through the winter she worked the cove the way she had once worked mercury, patiently, transmuting recollection into timber and glass. [447]

But she refused to restore the town exactly as it had been. [459] The old Cove ran on wishing, and a town that runs on wishing empties the moment it pays out. [478] What she meant to conceive was something that had never existed here: a settlement where nobody needed to hand their life to a flame. [502] A schoolhouse, then, instead of the wishing shrine. [510] A distillery for honest lamp oil, pressed from gorse and storm-wrack, so that light could be bought rather than begged. [530] A ledger by the harbor door where newcomers wrote what they could do, not what they wanted done for them. [550] She was not rebuilding a ghost town; she was reimagining a civilization from its first principle up, and the first principle was this: no more wishing. [576]

Word travels strangely along a coast. [582] In spring, a family of net-menders arrived, drawn by real lamplight in real windows, and asked what the town charged to stay. [604] "Work," said Vess, "and memory." [609] They stayed. [611] Others followed: a wheelwright, two sisters with chickens, a boy who had once wished for wings and now built windmills instead, which was better, because windmills lift whole towns. [640] By autumn the Cove had voices in it again, and none of them had been conjured. [656]

On the first frostbitten night of the new year, the boy asked Vess about the amber bottle on the high shelf, and why she kept something that could never be opened. [687] "Because it can only give people what they ask for," she said, "and I have stopped asking." [704] "These days I conceive things instead, and then I build them." [715] The boy considered this, then hung a paper lantern, unlit, above the schoolhouse door as a reminder, and went back inside to his books. [739] In the workshop, the sealed bottle caught the lamplight and glowed like a trapped sunset, full of every wish that had ever been granted here, and Vess let it glow. [769] The town did not need it anymore. [776] Neither, for the first time in sixty years, did she. [786]
