After seeing my own grave, I stopped trusting paper. [9] I rented the last ranger cabin on the mountain and moved my whole life into it: one cot, one stove, one tin of ink. [33] For thirty years I have been a lucid dream chronicler: seventeen volumes of my own nights, a syndicated column once, readers who wrote that my recorded dreams had predicted their divorces. [64] The work leaves you with jaded wonder, which is still wonder, just tired. [77] I never answered those letters, because the truth was stranger and less useful: my dreams arrive out of order, and after enough decades the waking hours arrive out of order too. [108] I am, in the clinical sense, chronologically confused. [116] I remember the cabin before the lease and the grave before the dream that found it. [132]

The grave came to me in flight. [139] I was soaring off the ridgeline, and thirty years in, the only verb I still trust is soar. [157] The valley opened under me like a held breath. [166] Below the pines sat a fenced square of stones. [175] I dropped the way you drop in dreams, without deciding to, and the granite said my name. [192] All of it, first to last, spelled right, which is how I knew this was no coincidence. [209] The dates slid around each other like fish and refused to be read. [222] I woke and did what a chronicler does: I hunted for the setting. [235] Six weeks of maps and buses later, I stood at a real fence behind this cabin, in an old ranger cemetery, reading my name on real granite. [262] Maybe the stone belongs to some dead ancestor who shared the name. [274] Maybe it is mine, waiting for me to catch up. [284] Either way, I came up here to make sense of it the only way I know how, by writing it down. [305]

Paper mildews at this altitude and mice eat it, which is presumably why the cabin's previous tenant kept her records elsewhere. [326] Under the third floorboard by the stove, I found a tin box holding forty-one birch bark scrolls, each curled like a shaved moon. [349] They were dream records, ink pressed into bark in a hand very like mine, signed with my own name. [368] She was the first ranger's daughter, born here a century before me; the family named me after her without telling me anything that mattered. [392] Her scrolls were numbered by nights, not dates, so naturally I read them in the wrong order. [409] For a week I believed her story ran from despair into wonder, and I resented her for it. [427] Then I found scroll one, and her whole arc reversed: wonder into despair, which was somehow worse. [444] Her final entry describes soaring over this valley and dropping toward a fenced square of stones where the granite says her name, above dates she cannot read. [471]

In the middle of the stack, on a scroll I first took for nonsense, she explains the trick that kept her going. [493] You cannot chase a dream backward; it runs. [501] But getting jealous of past selves works, she writes: envy them so precisely that the dream turns inside out to show you what you are missing. [527] I tried it. [530] I summoned the eight-year-old I once was, whose first flight dream was pure unearned joy, and I envied her until the dream folded backward around us both. [557] It worked. [559] It worked the way feeding a wild animal works. [568] Nostalgia is supposed to be a lap cat, something warm that purrs on the porch of the mind. [586] Mine came feral. [589] It did not purr; it stalked me waking, hunting me toward the porch light of 1979, hungry for me to stay with the child and her cheap, easy soaring. [618]

Last night I went back one final time, envying her so hard my chest rang, and when I reached her she was not flying. [642] She stood on the ridgeline looking up, and her face was my face, and it was starving. [659] She was jealous of me. [664] I still had nights she had never dreamed. [672] The feral thing looked from her to me, found nothing left to hunt, and slunk off the edge of the dream. [693] This morning I woke alone, in the good way. [702]

Today I peeled bark from a windfall birch, smoothed it flat, and began scroll forty-two. [717] Thirty years of chaos, unread dates, a grave wearing my name, and slowly the ink drags all of it into a line with a beginning, a middle, and me. [746] That is the craft, such as it is: distill chaos into narrative, because a story can hold what chronology cannot. [766] When it dries, it goes back in the tin, under the floorboard, for whoever dreams here next. [783] The stone gets my name; the scroll gets my nights. [793]
