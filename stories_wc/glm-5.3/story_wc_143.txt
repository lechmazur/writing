On the moonlit summit of crater Heraclides, Inspector Mara Voss knelt in earthlight, her boot prints the only scars on a plain older than oceans. [25] The space age had grown greedy, and her job was to certify craters for the strip-miners who followed. [43] This one, Heraclides, was the last untouched basin on the northern shelf. [55] Tomorrow's deadline would end it. [60] Mara was purposefully curious, the kind of inspector who measured twice and lingered over anomalies, and tonight the anomaly was time itself: fourteen days of lunar night had nearly passed, and dawn was coming to end the quiet Moon forever. [100] In her glove, a sealed vial held a single grain of sand. [112] It had fallen, one turn of an hourglass ago, from the last watch her grandfather ever kept. [129] He had been a nightwatch at the Atacama dark-sky station, guarding the sky itself until launch corridors and city glow made the stars obsolete. [153] On her eighth birthday he had taught her his secret: chemical reactions, mixed just so, that bloomed into light hours later, like letters that arrived on schedule. [180] A dash of oxidizer, a delay reagent, and a message would glow awake at midnight to say goodnight, or happy birthday, or hold on. [204] That was how he said goodbye, too, without saying it: one last ampoule glowing empty on the windowsill the morning he died. [226] Now Mara unslung her kit and began to work. [235] Along the crater's rim she planted two hundred tiny ampoules, each loaded with a different delay, the slowest clock reactions the old man had ever written down for her. [264] They would bloom in sequence, one by one, a line of cold fire marching toward the summit, timed to the minute the sun would crest the eastern wall. [292] A message no one would read, addressed to a dawn. [302] Also, a timer for her camera's long exposure, since her helmet clock had failed on day twelve. [319] Company dispatch had ordered her out three days ago. [328] She had answered with silence and settled in to outlast the night, rationing suit power to a trickle, eating cold paste in the dark. [352] To outlast was her grandfather's word for hope. [360] She wanted one photograph: the last sunrise that would ever fall across unbroken Heraclides, shadows long and blue, the basin still whole. [382] The last of something precious, which was, she supposed, simply the last of quiet. [396] The final hours were the hardest. [402] Earthlight silvered every stone, and the silence pressed so completely that she could hear her own heart, her own breath, the tick of chemicals waiting inside their glass. [430] She held the sand grain up beside the Earth, one last speck of one last hour, and it caught the light like a second, smaller world. [456] Then the first ampoule flared. [461] It burned amber, then dimmed as the next one woke, and the next, cold fire hopping along the rim in perfect rhythm, her grandfather's chemistry keeping time his hourglass no longer could. [493] The line of lights slowed, as designed, converging on the summit, and just as the last ampoule bloomed white at her boots, the sun broke the eastern wall. [521] There came the dawning hush she had crossed a fortnight of darkness for: that instant when light arrives but sound has not yet been invented again, when the shadows lean and hold their breath, when even the radio stays dead out of respect. [564] Mara opened the shutter and let the light pour in. [574] The photograph would show it plainly someday, when the crater was a pit and the quiet Moon a quarry: dawn, whole and unhurried, the last of its kind. [602] When the sun stood full on the rim, the comm channel crackled awake with dispatch shouting her name, and the miners' landers crawled over the horizon like weather coming. [631] The hush ended; hushes always do. [637] But she had outlasted it into morning, and the picture was hers. [649] Before the first lander touched down, she mixed one final ampoule, the longest-delay formula in her grandfather's notebook, and buried it at the summit with the hourglass grain pressed into the wax seal. [682] Someday, decades from now, it would bloom once in the dark for no one, a goodnight arriving on schedule. [701] Then she shouldered her camera and walked down into the light to meet them, the last quiet morning already fading behind her like breath on glass. [727]
