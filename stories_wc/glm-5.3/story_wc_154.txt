The mist in the hospital atrium thickened as twilight settled, softening the fountain, the ferns, the glass roof far above. [20] Mere Rakanui sat with her hands folded, waiting for word that her daughter was under. [35] The nurse had come out ten minutes ago, smiling: the anesthetic had kicked in, Lani was sleeping, the bone could be set. [57] An hour, the surgeon had said, maybe less. [65] Mere had spent her life inside other people's hours like this, in waiting rooms from Bougainville to Honiara, translating between peacekeepers and villagers who trusted no one's words, least of all their own. [98] Words had been her instrument and her burden. [106]
In her coat pocket, her fingers found the brain coral fragment, worn smooth on one side, maze-pitted on the other, the size of a plum. [131] A boy named Joseph had pressed it into her palm outside a clinic in Arawa, decades ago, and asked her to translate one word to the soldiers: enough. [159] She had said it softly, then louder, and the roadblock had opened. [171] It was the only time her words had ever stopped a gun instead of accounting for one. [188] She kept the coral because Joseph's hand had been small and certain, and because certainty was something she had never managed to grow. [211]
The surgery was a revision; swelling had shifted the bone, so they would re-break and reset it, cast and all. [231] The nurse had brought out the old plaster, cut free and warm, its signatures blurred by a sudden downpour on the day the class had signed it. [258] Mere laid it across her knees like a text. [267] Decrypting smudged handwriting was her gift; she had read rain-soaked manifests and smoke-stained letters in three languages, and now she read the looping charcoal of eleven-year-olds. [293] Get well Lani ran under a heart whose halves had slid apart in the wet. [308] "Don't be scared of the water," another said, which Mere understood was really the writer saying, I am scared of the water. [330] A boy named Tavita had written only my dad is gone again, no punctuation, the words thinned to gray where the rain had touched them. [355] Line by line the smudges gave up their fears: the rising tide that had eaten a classmate's yard, the dog that bit, the mother's cough, the dark, the moving away. [385] Children signing a cast, Mere thought, were really asking to be signed themselves, asking whether anyone would hold their small knot of dread up to the light. [412] Here, at last, were anxieties that could be shared without gunfire around them. [425]
Near the wrist, in the smallest hand, she found Lani's own message to herself, half-drowned in rain: it is only a bend, not a break. [450] The girl had known about the surgery for six days and had cried once, quietly, then asked her mother, shrewdly innocent, whether translators were allowed to change a sentence if it came out too sad. [485] Mere had laughed and said strictly no, and Lani had said, then I will write my own better one, and had. [506]
Now, in the mist, Mere took the pen she had signed consent forms with and began, gently, to unknot. [525] Beside each rain-blurred fear she wrote its truer translation, the way a gardener grafts a living stem onto an old cut. [546] Beside "don't be scared of the water" she wrote, the water is learning your name. [561] Under Tavita's gray line she wrote, gone is not gone; it is far, and far can be reached. [579] The tide that ate the yard became: the sea is asking us to dance, and we are a people who can move. [601] It was not lying, she told herself; it was the other duty of translation, the one no one paid for: to graft new meaning onto old wounds so the wounds would carry weight without carrying rot. [637] In Bougainville she had translated a threat once, precisely, and precision had cost a village its boats; she had been carrying that sentence for twenty-five years, its knot drawn tight every time it rained. [671] Tonight, at last, she was unknotting something. [678]
A nurse leaned over the balcony rail and called her name; the bone was set, the girl was awake and asking for her mother, and the lotus in the atrium pond had opened one white flower in the dark, as if it too had decided that ache was a kind of soil things could grow from. [734] Mere stood, pressing the coral fragment into her palm until its maze printed her skin, and carried the translated cast toward the doors, a warm plaster lantern against the mist. [764] She would read every fear aloud, and after each one, its new sentence, and watch her daughter decide to keep both. [785]
