The storm took the oldest yew in the hedge maze last spring, and when the roots tore loose they lifted a bundle of woven twigs and wax shaped like a baby. [31] It wore the Whitlock face, perfect down to the cleft in its chin. [44] The village called it the substitute body: proof of what they had whispered, that the fairies took the real Whitlock infant and left one of their own. [71] I had raised that left-behind child for eleven years and never once believed in fairies. [86] I believed in rickets, in fever, in bones gone crooked from want of sunlight. [100] I was the village bone-setter, and I set the changeling's twisted legs myself, splint by splint, the winter they left her on my step in a basket of thorn cuttings. [130] They had meant for me to refuse her. [138] A forgiving skeptic takes in what the faithful throw out; that is the whole of my religion. [155]

I named her Wren, for a small brown bird that sings louder than its bones should allow. [172] She grew shrewdly innocent: a child who never lied, but dealt her truths like a card sharp, one at a time, watching your face to see which one stung. [201] At six, over the splinting of a sparrow's wing, she asked why I buried every child's baby tooth in my garden instead of tossing them out. [227] Old custom, I said; buried teeth grow straight successors. [236] When her first tooth came loose, she pulled it herself and held it out on her palm: a pearl with roots like a seed's white threads, nothing a human jaw ever made. [268] I filed it under anomalies and slept badly. [276] I had spent fifty years closing clefts in bone, and I knew how they go: quiet, quiet, then a weight lands and the thing splits through. [302] My certainty had been whole all my life; the tooth was the first crack, and the substitute body was the weight. [323]

The vicar buried the little wax thing with honors, and the village left bread at my door like people feeding a stray they feared to touch. [349] Wren stopped asking her sharp questions. [355] She began instead to yearn, openly, shamelessly, the way only the young can: toward the gap where the yew had stood, toward the night road, toward whatever had made her and, she believed, mislaid her. [390] A mistake, she called herself, the one word I could not set right. [403] But she had an answer for mistakes, one she had been inventing for years behind the hedge maze, through a gate only we two knew, in the secret garden where I grew my herbs. [437] There she practiced her strange version of my trade: splinting sparrows' legs with matchsticks, freeing the ones that healed, and laying the ones that died anyway on the flagstone, bone by tiny bone, into patterns she named. [474] The Sparrow's Wing, she called one; the Mouse That Got Away, another. [486] When I asked why, she answered plainly: you set mistakes straight, and I turn them into constellations. [503] I laughed, the first time she said it. [511] I am not laughing now, remembering. [517]

After the substitute body was found and the yearning settled into her like weather, I took down the teacup from my shelf of anomalies and led her into that garden at dusk. [549] Wrapped in wool inside it lay the changeling's baby tooth, still pearled, still faintly humming against the porcelain. [567] I told her the truth at last: that I did not know what she was, that I had doubted her word for eleven years, and that I was sorry for every day of it. [601] A skeptic's apology is a rare coin; she took it and did not spend it. [616] Then she asked the sharpest of her shrewd, innocent questions: if buried teeth grow straight successors, what would grow from hers? [637] So that night we planted it in the herb bed, and by morning a silver sapling stood there, leaves like small white teeth, and the last cleft in my certainty split clean through, and it did not hurt. [675] Some fractures are doors. [679]

Wren stayed, not because the door was shut but because she aimed her yearning at the garden instead of the road. [700] Last week the Whitlock mother came shaking to the gate, and Wren met her with bread and honey and forgave her before she could kneel. [725] I have forgiven the fairies, if fairies there be, their theft; I have forgiven the village its fear; most days I have even forgiven myself. [750] And on clear nights we sit beneath the silver sapling, laying the bones of our old mistakes on the flat stone, star to star. [774] Wren has named the newest constellation for the lost Whitlock baby, so that child has a place in the sky at last. [796]
