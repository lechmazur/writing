The first storm had bled itself out over the mountains, the second was still massing behind them, and between the two the world stood in a hush so saturated it pressed on the skin like damp cloth. [37] Wren, second-year and the youngest rune scholar ever permitted onto the tea ceremony rock, knelt on the floating platform forty feet above the drowned valley and drew one long breath. [67] She held it. [70]

Master Oyen called this span the breath between storms, the only honest time to inscribe sky-runes: too soon and the retreating weather soured the ink, too late and the coming rain drowned the work. [104] Her assigned masterwork was the Smoothing, the rune that erases flaw. [115] Three times she had tried, and three times her attunement had betrayed her, because Wren's gift crystallized under stress: the ink froze into lace-thin crystal the moment her hand remembered to be afraid. [148] The shattered runes had left their splinters in her palms, and the scars still glinted there, seams of glass beneath the skin. [170] The academy surgeons had twice offered to cut them out. [180] Wren wanted something harder than removal: she wanted the scars turned into stories, lines someone could read and be comforted by. [201]

She performed the rite her grandmother had left her, warming the cup over the brazier and feeding the first pale drop of tea to the wind, for the ones aloft. [231] That was superstition, and she knew it now, being a scholar trained in doubt; she did it anyway, the way you keep a promise to the dead. [258] The crow arrived on the second pour, landing on the platform's rim, storm-wet and unbothered. [273] One for sorrow, said the counting rhyme her grandmother had trusted more than weather. [287] But her grandmother had also taught the counter-rule of Wren's childhood: when a crow is near, hold your breath, and the bird will mind your words for you, carrying them safely from one storm to the next. [324] So she was already obeying, already holding, when she lifted the quill. [336]

It was the crow quill her grandmother had given her at nine, its tip trimmed so often across the years that the point had become a scarred little stub, faithful and nearly worn through. [370] She set it to the stone, and her hand began its old trembling. [383] At the nib the ink clouded and granulated, the first frost of crystal, because the Smoothing asked her to think of her hands as mistakes. [408] Inside the held breath, memory moved slowly, saturated, the way steam moves when the world has agreed to wait. [427] Her grandmother at the turning of each season, silver scissors, small ceremony: trim the quill and give the trimming to the wind, and the crow takes back what is hers. [457] Nothing kept, everything shed and returned; that was the whole of her grandmother's wisdom, and Wren had spent two years in the academy failing to learn it. [484] She looked at the worn stub of the trimmed tip, all those years of small defenses, and understood what she had been holding along with her breath. [511]

She snapped the old tip from the quill, a clean small break, and shed it over the rim. [529] The crow hopped once and took the trimming in its beak, patient, as if superstition were an appointment it had kept before. [551] With her tea-knife Wren cut a fresh point, angling the blade the way her grandmother's hands had once angled hers. [571] Then, instead of the Smoothing, she began to write the other thing. [583]

Not a seal to erase flaw but a record of it: a rune that unspooled like something told aloud, each stroke laid along a scar, the glinting seams of her palms becoming the glyph's bones. [618] The first shatter, the refused surgeries, the years of trembling: line by line, the telling left her, and the ink stayed liquid. [640] It stayed liquid because she was no longer performing perfection; she was telling the truth to a crow, which is the oldest and safest kind of telling. [667] The rune finished itself under her hand as the far thunder leaned closer. [680] She let the breath go. [685]

The second storm broke over the platform in the same instant, and the rain, which would have drowned any unfinished line, found the rune complete and set it deep into the floating stone the way a kiln fixes glaze. [724] The crow lifted into the downpour with the shed tip in its beak, carrying the old scar away between the storms, and her grandmother had promised that a single crow always returns one true word. [759] Wren drank the cooled tea in the rain, her scars reading now as nothing more or less than a story, her hands, at last, only hands. [785]
