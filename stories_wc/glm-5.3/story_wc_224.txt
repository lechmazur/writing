The bookstore had been cut into the mountain so long ago that no one knew which shelves were carved and which had grown there. [24] Its corridors turned without warning, a labyrinth that rearranged itself overnight, and travelers spoke of rooms that existed only for certain griefs. [46]

At the summit lived the mercury-glass seer, an old woman named Ilma who answered questions by breathing on a tall mirror backed with quicksilver. [70] The glass gave back not reflections but evenings. [78] For a price, a petitioner could watch tomorrow simmer into shape, silver and unreliable. [92]

For no price at all, each night, Ilma let the glass substitute her own reality. [107] This had begun after remembering became painful. [114] Her town had burned in the year of the dry lightning; her brother had spoken to her in their shared language as the smoke rose, and she had answered in the conqueror's tongue because soldiers were listening. [151] After that, memory arrived like a boot to the chest. [161] So nightly she breathed on the mercury and let it build her a softer world: the kitchen, the lamp, her brother laughing with his whole unburned body. [188] The substitution was merciful, and it buried her. [196] A talent kept in darkness grows down instead of up, and hers had been buried eleven years: Ilma had once been a reader of the feather-script, the lost language written not in ink but in the scored barbs of quills, a script you read with your fingertips, like blindness, like devotion. [247]

On the twelfth autumn, a girl climbed nine thousand steps carrying a stone the size of a hymnal. [265] She would not leave the mirror room. [272] "Scholars in the lowlands gave up," she said. [280] "They say the language is dead." [286] "But my grandmother swore this stone named our town, and our town is gone, and I want to know the shape of the place I am from." [313]

Ilma took the stone to humor her. [320] The moment her palm covered it, she felt the border pattern, a hummingbird curl, the same one her mother had carved into the doorframe, and thunder she had hidden for eleven years moved in her chest. [356] She set the stone down too quickly. [363] "Come back at dusk," she said, "if you must." [372]

All afternoon she thought of escape. [378] The glass would offer its usual mercy tonight. [386] But mercy was the problem; the substitution buried the very hands that could read. [400] If the stone named her town, then somewhere beneath eleven years of borrowed evenings lay the shape of home, and she was the last person alive who could dig it out. [431]

At dusk she refused the mirror. [437] The glass hovered, silver, useless. [442] The pain arrived on schedule, and she let it. [451] She remembered the smoke, and the soldiers, and her brother's mouth shaping the feather-words while she stood silent, fluent in cowardice. [472] "Once more," she told the stone, as if it could hear. [483]

She sent the girl to the Ornithology Wing, and between the pages of an old folio they found a pressed grebe's feather, flat and patient after a century. [511] Ilma laid the quill against the stone's grooves and drew it slowly, the way her mother had taught her, letting the texture of the feather catch each scored notch. [540] The letters did not rise to the eye. [548] They rose through the skin. [553] Her buried talent came up shuddering, like a root pulled whole from clay, and the stone began to speak. [572]

"This is the shape of home," it read, "which is not a place on any map." [588] "Our town's name means: carried by whoever leaves it." [597] "Home goes where its language goes." [603]

Ilma wept without noise, which is the loudest way. [612] Outside, real thunder finally cleared its throat over the mountain, as if it had waited all evening in ambush. [631]

The girl touched the grooves and felt nothing but stone. [641] "Then it is dead," she whispered. [647] "No," Ilma said. [650] "A language is dead only when no hand holds it." [660] She took the girl's fingers, opened them gently, and laid them along the feather's edge, guiding them notch by notch through the first letter, seeding the script in a new hand the way her mother had seeded it in hers. [700] "Read," she said. [703] The fingertips moved; the lips moved; something older than both of them woke in the mirror room. [720]

That night, for the first time in eleven years, Ilma let the true memory come instead of the substitute: her brother's voice, the smoke, the words she should have answered. [750] It still hurt. [753] But under the hurt, like water under stone, lay the shape of home, and it fit inside her hands, which were no longer empty. [777] Far below, rain reached the mountain of books, and every corridor of the labyrinth drank. [792]
