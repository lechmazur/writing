The prep room hummed at four in the morning, formaldehyde and coffee, and Miriam counted her father's sins the way other people counted sheep. [24] He had been a forger of asylum papers, a seller of hope at crisis prices, and she had answered him by becoming the most honest creature in the death trade, a mortuary assistant who returned excess change even to vending machines. [65]

Tonight the body on her table belonged to Sami Rahimi, twenty-six, dead of a failing heart in a detention center three states from the border he had crossed on papers the government had declared forged. [100] His asylum claim had died with that declaration. [108] His heart had followed. [112]

The center returned his effects in a sealed plastic bag: a comb, a bus ticket, and a small brass ink pot, vintage, its lid engraved with a heron. [140] Miriam knew that pot. [144] She had ground ink in it as a child while her father bent over visas and residence permits, breathing false life into the dead metal of official stamps. [172] She unscrewed the lid and tilted it, and the ink inside was still liquid, black and moving, forty years past any reasonable expiry, swirling as if it had been waiting. [202] Her father used to boast, mid-sale, that good ink liquefies rigid thinking; she had assumed he meant the minds of border inspectors. [224] Watching it swirl now, she felt it working on her own. [235]

She set the pot beside the arterial machine and went to work, because waiting had never been passive for her. [255] Waiting actively was her whole craft: hands busy, eyes open, patience sharpened into a blade. [270] She eased the stiffness from Sami's hands while his file lay open beside her, whispering its story. [287] The forged visa reproduced in its pages carried a sheen she recognized the way children recognize a parent's handwriting. [306] Her father's ink. [309] Her father's fraud. [312] A family's last four thousand dollars traded for a document that had locked their son in a cell and broken his claim, and finally delivered him to her table. [341] Somewhere across an ocean, his sister's petition still crawled through the same system, built on the same tainted paper. [360]

Miriam had spent her life hunting the mathematics of a fair trade, believing fairness was arithmetic, every debt computable, every wrong repayable to the cent. [385] Four thousand dollars plus interest compounded by grief, and grief compounded at no rate she could name. [402] The ink pot gleamed under the surgical lamp, and in its dark curve she saw the shape of a different equation. [423] Rigid honesty had kept her clean and kept her useless, an honest number standing beside an unjust one and adding to nothing. [445]

At six, a county caseworker would come to view the body; at eight, the sister, flying in to claim him. [465] Two hours, and Miriam did the only thing a waiting woman can do actively: she prepared. [481]

First, the small breach. [485] She cut the seal on the effects bag, which regulations said must stay sealed until witnessed, and photographed everything: the comb, the ticket, the heron-lidded pot, the dried ink traces she lifted with a swab. [520] Then, on mortuary letterhead, she wrote a signed statement: this ink pot was the working tool of the forger Augustin Vey, her father, deceased; its ink matched the visa in file 4471; the fraud, if any, had been committed against the applicant, not by him. [565] Every word of it was true. [571] She had watched truth fail all her life for want of the right seal, so she dipped a pen in her father's ink and signed her father's trade backward, one true document where the lies had been. [608] The old ink flowed wet and alive, refusing to clot, refusing to be rigid. [622] When she slid the statement into Sami's file, the paper drank the ink and held. [637]

Around her the prep room pulsed with deathly vitality: the arterial machine keeping rhythm like a patient heart, steam rising, the pump of her own blood loud in her ears, everything dead and everything ferociously in motion. [674] She had thought fairness was a number. [681] It was a direction: the same ink that signed a lie could sign a truth, the same hand reversed, the trade made fair not by equal sums but by opposite ones. [712]

At six sharp the caseworker arrived, pink and yawning, and Miriam handed him the file with steady hands. [730] "Read the last page first," she said, and returned to her table, where Sami lay finished, his face at rest. [750] Beside him the ink pot stood open like a small dark well that had waited forty years for one honest act to drink from it. [775]
