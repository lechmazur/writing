On the last evening before the final transformation, Elowen the alchemist climbed the ridge above her tower, auburn hair bound back with copper wire, and stepped through the falling veil of the kaleidoscopic waterfall. [34] Behind it lay the hidden orchard, as it had lain for sixty years. [47] Quince and apple trees grew in crooked rows, planted by her teacher before Elowen was born, and the spray hung above them, splitting the low sun into slow wheels of amber and jade. [80] The air smelled ancient and fresh at once, of wet stone, fallen fruit, and blossom. [95] At dawn the work would enter its final transformation, if she did not ruin it as she had ruined it seven times before, and she had come here, as she always came, to think. [129] The work had a formal name in the old books, but she used her teacher's name for it: the Harmonic Opus, the joining of logic and intuition, proof and wonder, into one clear thing. [163] Forty years, and she had mastered only half. [171] Her scales were true to a seed's weight; her notebooks ran to ninety volumes; other alchemists spoke of her the way sailors speak of lighthouses. [196] No one guessed that the stern Elowen kept a locked cedar drawer beneath her workbench, or that inside it lived a broken thimble, a pressed quince petal, and a letter she could no longer read for crying. [233] Sentiment, she told her students, was contamination. [240] Tonight she had brought the drawer's chief treasure with her, though she would have called it something else if anyone asked: a frozen dew crystal, dew gathered at dawn on the morning her teacher died, held in alchemical cold for thirty years, as clear and useless as the day she sealed it. [292] Her teacher had died at dawn, mid-sentence, one hand out the window toward the light on the water, and the last words she ever finished were about the touch of wonder, the one reagent no balance ever built could weigh. [332] Elowen had spent thirty years proving her wrong. [340] Seven times the work had failed, and seven times the failure was flawless, every measurement exact, every result as dead as arithmetic. [362] Dusk came down through the spray in bands of rose and iron. [374] She cupped her palm, spoke the old syllable, and a small flame rose in it, alchemist's fire that burned without fuel, the same flame that would light the athanor at dawn. [405] By that light she unwrapped the crystal and held it up. [416] The flame passed through it and laid a small trembling rainbow across her wrist, weightless, worth nothing, thirty years old and still the first morning of the world. [444] Then the starlings came. [448] They rose from the reeds in their thousands, a soft dark rope of birds that knotted and unknotted above the orchard, and Elowen, who could not help herself, began to count. [479] Three rules governed every bird, and she knew them, she had written them out years ago: keep close, keep aligned, keep moving. [501] Out of three small laws the flock made its great breathing patterns, pillars, funnels, sudden silver turns, and no rule she knew predicted the shapes themselves. [527] The laws were true, and not enough; the pattern was real, and could not be measured in advance; both things were true at once, and the birds did not care about the contradiction. [560] Elowen stood very still, flame in one hand and crystal in the other, while the murmuration folded overhead into a shape like a wave standing up, and something in her chest unlocked with an almost audible click. [597] That had always been the failure. [603] She had brought logic to the work and left wonder locked in a drawer, and somehow expected the union of the two to come out of one of them alone. [633] The touch of wonder was not a contaminant; it was the other half of the equation, the half that could not be written down, only carried, the way the flock carried its laws. [666] She walked home beneath the wheeling birds and did not count them. [678] In the hour before dawn she opened the cedar drawer, set the thimble, the petal, and the letter on the bench beside her scales, openly, like ingredients, and weighed everything twice, because that part was true too. [715] Then, with steady hands, she carried the frozen dew crystal to the mouth of the great glass vessel. [733] It fell slowly, turning, a small cold star, and she reached for the flame she had carried home from the orchard. [754] Behind her, through the walls, she could almost hear ten thousand starlings, keeping close, keeping aligned, keeping moving. [772] Then she sealed the vessel, set it in the athanor, and let the flame in. [787]
