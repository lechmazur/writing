The arboretum perched on the seaside cliff like a handhold, its wind-bent pines planted by the last lighthouse keeper, who had gone down the hill for good the year the light learned to run itself. [35] That was forty years before Amira arrived with her suitcase and a job she had not told anyone she wanted. [55] She was quietly talented with living things, the kind of gift that never announces itself; cuttings rooted for her that refused other hands, and the director noticed within a month. [85] Nobody at the arboretum knew she had been a bride once, or that the word wedding still landed in her body before it reached her mind. [111]

The drone strike came four minutes into her marriage, just as the band began; she remembered the whine, then sugar-white dust, then nothing worth describing. [136] Thirty-one others, including the groom, remembered nothing at all. [145] In her coat pocket she carried a crumpled photograph, folded and refolded until the crease ran through her new husband's smile like a river on a map. [172] She never smoothed it flat. [177]

What she did instead, every morning in the keeper's old cottage where tools hung where the lamp oil used to stand, was teach. [200] Her students were others like her, sent by clinics and half out of other wars, and she began each session the same way. [223] Your body keeps score, she told them, through somatic markers: a tight throat, cold hands, breath gone shallow; these are not setbacks, they are instruments. [248] Learn to read them and the freeze stops being something that happens to you. [262] Inside the freeze, she said, there is a pearl of stillness, a small round quiet the panic cannot enter. [281] You go there not to hide but to choose. [290] She taught it because she had survived it herself first. [300]

The coastal survey drone found her on a Tuesday, low over the lupins, mapping erosion along the cliff. [318] The sound went through her like a hook. [326] Her jaw locked, her feet grew into the path, and the old animal freeze took her the way it always did, wholly and without permission. [351] But her hands were instruments now. [357] The cold blooming in her fingers was a marker, and the marker said: you are here, not there; the sky above you holds only a machine counting plants. [385] She dropped into the stillness the way she dropped into cold water, found the pearl, and stayed beside it. [404] From inside that small round quiet she watched her own body the way she watched seedlings: patiently, without demand. [423] Her breath returned on its own. [429] The drone passed over the lighthouse, now a white cylinder that flashed without anyone's love, and went south. [447] Amira unclenched her fists. [451]

Later, in the greenhouse, she laid the photograph on the potting bench and considered it the way she considered rootstock. [471] Her students sometimes asked why she planted oaks on a cliff she would never see shaded, and her answer never changed. [492] You anchor optimism in realism, she said, because optimism that ignores the odds is just another kind of freezing. [511] Plant in the frost pocket and know the frost pocket; calculate the wind; expect to lose a third of everything you set. [533] Then plant anyway, because two thirds is real, and real is what hope is made of when it grows up. [553] She had buried thirty-one people; she was not going to pretend the world was gentle. [568] Neither would she pretend it was finished with her. [577]

That evening she pressed the photograph flat under a pane of glass from the old lamp room, smoothed the crease once with her thumb, and set it on the sill facing the sea. [610] The fold would always show. [615] That was the realism; the pressing was the optimism; the thumb that did the work belonged to a survivor. [634] Below the window the automated light swept the water for no one, while in the seed beds the spring oaks put out their first true leaves, small and stubborn, gripping the cliff the way she had learned to.
