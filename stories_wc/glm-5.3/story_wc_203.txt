The city hired Marisol Voss, an elevator repair technician, to decommission the old freight elevator behind the abandoned Hollis Circus ticket office, a building dying quietly since the tents came down for good. [33] She was suited to the work because she was skillfully observant; she read hoistways like faces, every worn cable and scraped rail, the particular silence of a machine that has given up. [65] She kept one observation out of her reports, always. [74] She saw ghosts. [77] They drifted at the edges of machine rooms and dead lobbies, and carrying them was a weightless burden: they cost nothing to see and everything to ignore. [104] The ticket office still had power on a final circuit, and the ceiling fluorescents buzzed like trapped wasps while she worked. [125] She understood rooms through their dust, because dust accumulation patterns never lie. [137] Thirty years of grime lay even across the counter except for one clean oval, as if something round had sat there and been lifted away. [162] The stucco facade outside was cracked like drought soil, and inside the ceiling told the same story: a hairline fracture ran along the cornice above the ticket window, and the fracture had been caulked shut and painted over. [200] Hidden things, her grandmother used to say, always show through the cracks in the facade. [215] At 4:17, the overhead fluorescent buzz sputtered and died off. [225] In the sudden dark, he appeared: an old man in a horologist's apron, translucent, gazing straight up at the ceiling the way the dead gaze at what they can no longer reach. [257] She usually pretended not to see them. [264] Engaging meant owing them something, and she had debts enough. [274] But her whole trade, if she was honest, was to discover what ceilings hide, so she switched on her flashlight and spoke. [296] "Show me." [298] The ghost raised one hand toward the caulked crack, and she understood she was meant to go up. [316] She rode the elevator car top upward through the hoistway, flashlight clamped in her teeth, and stopped where an old freight opening gave onto the dim crawl space above the office ceiling. [348] The dust up there betrayed everything: fingertip drag marks along one joist, a clean rectangle in the grey shroud, and the dull gleam of brass half-buried where nothing should be. [378] Dust falls evenly on anything untouched; anything disturbed leaves a story. [389] It was wedged between joist and lath, a brass cylinder the size of a snail shell: a horologist's mainspring barrel, the drum that holds the coiled heart of a mechanical clock. [420] The caulked crack below was the only part of his secret that had ever shown. [435] Over the ticket window hung the office's regulator clock, hands stopped at 4:17 for thirty years, with a hollow in its works where a barrel exactly this size belonged. [464] The fluorescents had died at 4:17 too, and a cold feather walked down her spine. [479] Coiled inside the barrel with the spring itself was a curl of paper. [492] She eased it out with two fingers. [499] The note, in faded brown ink, was signed Albert Grum, the circus timekeeper. [512] "If the circus must end," it read, "let it end at the true hour." [526] So Grum had pulled the mainspring on the last day, stopped the clock at the exact minute the gates closed forever, ridden the freight hoist into the dark, and hidden the heart of the circus above the office ceiling. [565] Even a facade holds only until it cracks, and grief, she had learned, is the most patient water. [583] The ghost watched her, and his composure cracked as well, the calm of a man who had kept perfect time for forty years finally running down. [609] "You want it kept," she said. [615] "You want it to outlast the wrecking ball." [623] She engaged him fully then, the first time in eleven years she had let one of them see her seeing. [643] He nodded once, the way a man nods when the weight has lifted but the ache remains. [660] "I'll carry it out," she said. [666] "The clock's movement goes to the county museum with the salvage; the barrel belongs with it." [682] She pocketed the barrel and eased back down, and when she looked into the office the horologist was gone, though the air above the ticket window still held the shape of his looking. [715] She finished the decommission by flashlight, cut the power, and logged nothing of value recovered, which was a lie of about forty grams. [738] On the drive home the mainspring barrel rode in her toolbox beside the feeler gauges, almost too light to notice. [758] Some burdens weigh nothing at all, she thought, and still you wind them close and carry them home. [776]
