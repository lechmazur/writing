Mara gardened where no garden should live: a fire escape bolted to the side of the Chandler Building, four stories above a luminous harbor. [24] By moonlight the water below burned silver, and her pots of pale flowers caught it and threw it back, so that her little terrace glowed like a lamp with no cord. [55] That was the point, her father had always said. [64] He had been the lockmaster of the harbor, keeper of every gate, shed, and boathouse key from the breakwater to the fish market. [87] "Locks keep people out," he liked to say, "and you, little sprout, will let the light in." [104] So she planted only what was pale: white petunias, silver lamb's ear, moonflowers that opened like held breath. [122] Her motive was simple and stubborn: to reflect light into dark corners, the alley below first among them. [140] There, in a basement room with one window level with the alley, lived Theo, eleven years old and afraid of the dark water, ever since his father's boat came home without his father. [173]

The night the old rosebush dropped its final petal, Mara felt the season fold shut around her. [190] The petal came loose, wavered, and settled on the iron rail like a small surrender. [205] Afterward the whole harbor seemed to hold its breath, a quivering hush, as if the water itself were listening for what came next. [228] Mara stood in that hush, wistfully pragmatic as always: she grieved the rose for exactly one minute, then started planning its spring pruning. [251] Then she went down the stairs and knocked on the window that let into Theo's dark. [267] He surfaced like something unused to being seen, hair flattened, eyes wary. [279] "Final petal," she said through the glass, holding it up like a ticket. [292] "Boat time." [294]

He shook his head, and she watched him armor up, that careful blankness boys build around a hurt. [312] "I know," she said, gentler. [317] "I am not asking you to swim, only to row." [327] That distinction, for whatever reason, got him into her father's old dinghy with its oars worn smooth as candlesticks. [346] She had brought one thing from the apartment besides the petal: a small steel tooth from her father's bitting gauge, the tool that measured how deep each cut bit into a blank key. [379] It had snapped off years ago, and he had pressed it into her palm and said, "Everything that opens something wears down, sprout, so keep the tooth." [406] In the garden it served as a dibber, pricking planting holes no bigger than a lentil. [422] Tonight it rode in her coat pocket like a seed she meant to sow. [436]

They pushed off into the luminous harbor. [443] The moon had laid a road of light across the water, wide enough for two. [458] Theo rowed badly at first, oars slapping, and then, under her patient counting, found a stroke. [474] Dip, pull, breathe. [477] Dip, pull, breathe. [480] She timed her words to that beat, because lessons given in rhythm go deeper than lectures. [496] So, with the rhythm of oars in water, she began to instill in him what her father had instilled in her: that the dark was not a wall, only a lock, and that every lock, her father swore, had a key being cut for it somewhere. [542] "Stroke," she said, "and the water holds you." [550] "Stroke, and the moon comes with you." [557] "Stroke, and the dark is just the room before the light gets in." [570] By the middle of the harbor his fear was still there, but it rowed with him instead of steering. [589]

"Now look back," she said. [594] He looked. [596] The town had gone silver and blue, and four stories up, her garden blazed: white petals, silver leaves, and the strings of old keys her father had left her, hung to catch the moon and throw it down. [634] The light fell all the way to the alley, straight through the bars of the fire escape, and pooled outside his dark window like something waiting for him. [662] "That is for you," Mara said, "every night, all winter." [672] Theo's face did something complicated, and she looked away to spare him, guarding his dignity the way she guarded the roses from frost. [695]

When they reached the dock, she took the bitting gauge tooth from her pocket and closed his fingers around it. [715] "Everything that opens something wears down," she told him. [724] "So keep the tooth, keep rowing, and come spring you can help me prick the planting holes." [741] He nodded, not trusting his voice, and held the little tooth up so the moon could find it. [759] Behind them the harbor kept its quivering hush, and the last rose petal, folded in Mara's pocket, waited for whatever she would make of it next. [785]
