Mira had worked the 6:40 commuter ferry for eleven hundred crossings, and she still loved every one. [17] She loved the ritual of transients: the same faces, the same coffee, the harbor receding the same way each dawn. [37] Permanent people, she called them - permanently going somewhere. [45]

So when the Harbormoon vanished mid-crossing, forty-two commuters gripping their coffee in the fog, Mira refused to treat it as an ending. [67] The shoreline simply dissolved; the fog opened; and the ferry glided into a grotto no chart had ever admitted to. [87] Its walls were spectral, translucent as breath, and above the water hung stars - though they were underwater too, which the commuters politely decided not to question. [113]

The first sign of wrongness came from the galley clock: its hands had stopped circling and were ticking sideways, east and west, like a compass gone honest. [140] Every clock aboard did the same. [146] In the pilot house, Captain Odell held up his brass hourglass, forty years aboard, and it was empty. [164] "The sand left sideways," he said, tilting it. [172] "Poured right through the glass, out into the water." [181]

Mira held the hourglass to the grotto's starlight and saw it: fine granules drifting horizontally through the dark, thousands of glowing threads braiding between the walls. [207] Time here did not fall; it ran sideways, and the Harbormoon had been caught in the current. [224] Which meant, she realized, watching two versions of the same star blink out of phase, that the ferry had slipped into the seam between parallel crossings. [250] Somewhere a Harbormoon that had never vanished was docking on schedule, carrying a deckhand who was also, somehow, Mira. [269] Two timelines running side by side, and her commuters caught in the gap. [282]

Odell wanted to drop anchor and wait for physics to apologize. [293] Mira was ardent about many things, but mostly about the belief that hope was a discipline rather than a mood. [313] "Commuters have jobs to get to," she said, and went to the radio. [326]

Static answered her call, but static has a texture when you have listened to enough midnights. [342] Beneath the noise she could hear the other Harbormoon - its engines, its bell, the other Mira's voice calling the 6:40 stops. [363] The two worlds were not distant; they were interleaved, like the woven lanes of rush-hour traffic. [379] And if two identical ferries ran on two identical schedules, they could be tuned to each other - Mira had spent eleven hundred crossings memorizing how everything on the water rhymed: the interval between buoys, the wake's rhythm against the hull, the three-and-two bell code of departure. [425]

She keyed the microphone and transmitted the departure code, three bells and two, struck out crisp as a morning. [444] The reply came a half-second shy of perfect - the other timeline, slightly out of phase. [459] "This is the Harbormoon," Mira said. [465] "We are not a ghost crossing." [471] The other Mira answered, and hearing her own steadiness come back through the static was the strangest encouragement of her life. [492]

Together, via the interconnected patterns only two identical deckhands could share, they began to merge the timelines: bell code answered by bell code, the timetable recited in unison, wake timed to wake, until the two 6:40 crossings began to overlap like transparencies on a lightbox. [537] The grotto brightened. [540] Stars slid into the grooves between their own reflections. [549] The sideways granules hanging in the dark began to gather, threading back toward the boat. [564] Mira held the hourglass up, and the sand came home - not falling down, at first, but pouring in sideways through the glass until, with a sound like a held breath released, gravity remembered itself and the last grains dropped straight. [604] Every clock aboard shivered and resumed circling. [611]

The fog lifted, and the Harbormoon slid back into its ordinary channel, grotto dissolving behind it like a rumor. [630] Commuters checked their watches, found them merely ordinary again, and went back to their coffee, because that is what transients do. [651] Only one thing lingered: the faint doubling in the radio, a second set of footsteps on the deck that faded by the pier. [674] The other Mira's last transmission came through as they docked: "Same crossing tomorrow?" [687] "Every tomorrow," Mira answered, and signed off. [694]

Standing at the gangway as the permanent transients filed ashore, she decided the grotto had not been an interruption at all. [715] Every crossing vanished the moment it was made; every crossing was made again at 6:40 sharp. [731] The Harbormoon was proof of nothing, except that some things pass forever and never stop arriving. [747]
