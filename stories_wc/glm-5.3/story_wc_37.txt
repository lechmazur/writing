In the mirror world, nothing falls from the sky for free. [11] Rain is poured, stars are hung, and dawn is woven. [21] For four hundred years the weavers of the alchemical orchard greenhouse plaited each morning out of song, and the sunfruit trees drank that light and ripened it into amber hours that kept the whole realm warm. [57] Then, people said, the great mica window fogged over with a grey film no one could scrape away. [75] Within a year the last amber hour was spent, and the endless night settled like snow that would not melt. [95] The greenhouse still stood at the orchard's heart, panes black under a sky where no one had hung a star in years, its alembics cold, its grafting knives rusting in their racks. [127]

Wren had kept the orchard's memories for sixty of those dark years, though calling her one person was never quite right, for her mind was scattered across everyone else's. [156] When remembering daylight grew too painful, people gave their memories away, and she took every one: the baker's dead son laughing at burnt bread, the ferryman's first sight of fog lifting off the canal in gold. [192] A hundred dawns belonged to other people; not a single one was her own. [206] She could not tell you her mother's face, but she could sing the ferryman's mother's lullaby note for note. [225]

The town had shattered with the light. [232] In the long dark, each household hoarded its candles and its silence, and doors were bolted against neighbors who might be thieves of warmth. [256] Wren went among them anyway, because she alone remembered what they had been to each other. [272]

She had spent thirty years rebuilding the mica window one wafer-thin sheet at a time, pressing a memory of dawn into every sheet. [295] Mica holds whatever shines, the old alchemists taught, which is why the founders glazed their window with it. [313] Everything she did was for one purpose: to rekindle the dying realm before the last child forgot the word morning. [333] But a window only lets light through; it does not make light. [345] For that she needed the forgotten songs. [352] The founders had never borrowed their mornings from the true world; they had woven them, voice over voice, and the mica had only ever shown the weavers their own work doubled. [383] When the last weavers died and the songs went out of memory, the window had nothing left to double, and the fog everyone cursed was only grief. [410]

So Wren went door to door through the shuttered town, carrying the songs she had gathered along with the memories, and gave each household back its own voice. [438] The songs knitted the town back together, stitch by stitch, as nothing else could have. [453] The blacksmith wept at his grandfather's forge-hammering rhythm; the toll-keeper's wife remembered the harvest round every child once sang, and by the third verse two feuding families were holding the same melody like a rope between them. [490]

On the chosen night they came to the greenhouse: three hundred people, every soul she had ever carried. [508] They stood among the black skeletons of the sunfruit trees and began to sing. [522] As the forgotten songs rose, the memories pressed into the mica woke, and thread after thread of remembered light drew out of the window and fell across the branches like weft. [553] The bare trees became the loom. [559] Voice wove over voice, dawn-color over dawn-color, and on the grafting table a small woven morning took shape, no bigger than a sleeping child. [583] It was terribly fragile. [587] Newborn light dies of cold, the oldest song warned, unless it is swaddled. [600] So the town unbound its scarves, its shawls, its good dark curtains, and Wren wrapped the little dawn tight, snug as any infant, binding it against the cold while it gathered strength. [632] All night they sang over the swaddled bundle while it pulsed like a heart learning to beat. [649] Toward morning, a word they had almost lost, the bundle grew too bright to stay bound. [665] Wren, keeper of every dawn but her own, untied the knot herself. [677] The cloth fell away and light broke loose like water from a held breath, pouring through the mica window and doubling, and doubling again, until night could not find anywhere left to stand. [710] The sunfruit trees flowered before the first verse ended. [719] In the flood of it, the scattered keeper's burden lifted: the baker's boy laughed again, the ferryman's fog lifted, every borrowed memory floated home to its owner, and one mica sheet she had never dared to open warmed in her hand. [760] Inside was her mother's voice, kept for her all along by somebody, singing her name. [775] The realm rekindled around her, dawn woven and dawn released, and Wren, at last whole, wept and let go. [794]
