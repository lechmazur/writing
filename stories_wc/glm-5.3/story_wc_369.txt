Maren swept the labyrinth the way other people sweep kitchens, with the patience of someone who knows the floor will betray her again by morning. [25] Above her, the stormlit tower took another bruise of lightning, and the corridors shivered in their stone sleep. [43] As the labyrinth's maintenance worker, she replaced lamp glass, oiled hinges on doors that led nowhere twice as often as doors that led somewhere, and collected the things dreamers dropped. [73] The best thing anyone had ever dropped was the astronomer's broken sextant, found seventeen years ago in the corridor where he had last been seen. [98] Its arc was snapped, its mirror webbed with cracks, and Maren had carried it home anyway, the way you carry a wounded bird. [121]

The first miracle was small, which is how she preferred them. [132] Left on the windowsill during a break between storms, the sextant healed in moonlight, one hairline crack closing per clear night, like a bone knitting. [157] She never told anyone, partly because no one would believe her and mostly because miracles, like sourdough, spoil if handled too much. [179]

The second miracle was the sextant's new use. [187] It no longer measured the distance between stars; it measured sorrow, in neat minutes and seconds of arc, as if grief were a navigable sky. [212] Pointed at a widower sweeping his porch, it read eleven degrees. [223] Pointed at herself, it read higher, and she stopped pointing it at herself. [236] Sorrow, once quantified, became a substance she could work with, the way mortar is a substance, and Maren began to suspect the labyrinth was built of it. [263]

The proof was dreams. [267] People lost dreams in the labyrinth constantly, the way you lose a glove; they drifted off sleepwalkers and the grieving and settled in corners, glowing faintly, like spilled milk that refused to sour. [300] Collecting them was technically not her job. [307] She did it anyway, pinning each dream's location on a map she kept folded in her toolbox, until the map showed what no architect's drawing ever had: the labyrinth's true floor plan, written in longing. [342] Where the pins clustered, walls could be unmade. [350] This was how she learned to deconstruct the place she maintained: by mapping scattered dreams, reading their constellations, and unscrewing the grief-brick from wherever the sky inside a person had leaked out. [382] Each removed wall made the town a little lighter; the baker's bread rose better; the schoolchildren stopped crying at Tuesdays. [402] Mundane miracles, she called them, and kept a list. [411]

But one wall refused every map. [417] It stood at the labyrinth's center, behind the nine yew trees, and no dream had ever been lost near it, which frightened her more than any storm. [444] Trees witness everything, her grandmother had said; they simply keep minutes longer than we do. [459] Maren had spent seventeen years wanting to discover what trees witness, because whatever the yews had seen might tell her what the astronomer had been carrying, and why the wall he died behind was made of a sorrow so old no dreamer went near it. [504]

The night it happened, the storm quit without warning, the way guilt sometimes does. [518] The corridor went silent, wholly silent, and in that silence Maren heard a pin drop, hers, slipped from the map in her trembling hand, striking stone with the small bright ring of a bell in an empty church. [556] At the exact moment a pin is heard in a silent corridor, she understood, the world is obligingly quiet enough to hear itself change. [580] Moonlight poured through the tower's broken roof and down the corridor like water finding a drain. [596] She held up the sextant, and the last crack closed under her thumb, warm as skin. [612] Whole, it did not point at stars. [619] It pointed at the yews. [624] Maren walked to the center and read the dial: zero degrees, zero minutes, and beneath the needle, in the mirror the astronomer had once used for starlight, a dream she had never mapped, because it had never been lost, only kept. [665] It was an ordinary dream: a kitchen, rain on the window, someone laughing at a joke about bread. [683] That was what the trees had witnessed, all along: not the astronomer's death, but the modest happiness he had been carrying home to his wife the night the storm came down. [714] The wall, meeting such a small and stubborn joy, quietly came apart. [726] Maren swept up the dust, marked the spot on her map with the fallen pin, and went back to work. [746] Miracles, she had found, mostly look like maintenance from the outside. [757] In the morning the baker's bread rose a little higher, and nobody in town could say exactly why the walk to work felt shorter, or why the yews were humming. [787]
