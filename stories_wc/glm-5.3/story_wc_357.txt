Sorrel was an orchard pilot on Tarn, an orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches that hung above the valleys like the ribs of some drowned sky. [27] Every day at noon, for exactly one hour, gravity inverted. [37] Water leapt off the valley rivers and climbed the arches in silver ropes, and the pilots rode those rising streams, navigating by water flow alone, because no compass worked in a sky that changed its mind. [73] The trees survived it. [77] They bent, hung, and kept their fruit, and nobody had ever found the source of that resilience. [94]

Then the blight came. [98] Grove by grove, the leaves grayed, the roots let go of their stone, and arches that had flowered for nine generations began to fall. [122] The elders said the answer lay in the oldest arch, the Broken Bow, where the first orchard had rooted. [141] No pilot had returned from it. [147] The elders called Sorrel intrepid; the other pilots called her reckless, which was the same word with worse manners. [166] She went anyway, because her grandmother had told her to, and because of the thing her grandmother had left her: a broken arrow tip, black as wet bark, warm as a held hand. [199] Where its point touched soil, grass answered within minutes. [208] Sorrel had tested it once on a dying sapling and watched a season of green happen in an afternoon. [227] It was a piece of whatever kept Tarn alive, she was sure, and the Broken Bow was where it had been broken from. [250]

She flew out under a white sun, low over the Ashwater River, and waited. [264] At noon, the planet sighed, and gravity inverted. [272] The river lifted. [275] A whole silver body of water rose off its bed, unspooling upward through the arches, and Sorrel tucked her glider into the current and let it carry her, because water on Tarn always remembered its way home, and its home was high stone. [318] The world went whirling and quiet. [324] Falling leaves rose past her like snow played backward. [333] The only sound was her own breath, and beneath it, faintly, other breaths. [346]

The Broken Bow rose out of the haze, a ring of rock so wide its far side curved out of sight. [367] At its heart hung a hollow, and the rising water poured into that hollow and vanished, and Sorrel followed it in. [388] Inside was a gathering of echoes. [394] Every sound Tarn had ever made had come here during the inversions: wind from dead valleys, rain that fell a hundred years ago, the creak of roots, the laughter of pilots whose names were now only names. [431] They turned in the hollow like a slow cyclone of voices. [442] The whirling should have been loud, but it was not; every echo canceled another, and at the center of all that turning sat a silence so complete that Sorrel heard her own heart apologize for beating. [478]

The silence had a shape. [483] In the middle of the hollow, held in stillness while everything around it whirled, hung the first tree, no larger than a child, its bark scarred where a single arrowhead had once been cut from its heart. [520] Sorrel's grandmother's voice came out of the turning, patient and near. [531] "You found it," it said. [536] "Now discover what resilience is made of." [543]

Sorrel understood slowly, the way one understands in a silent place. [554] The tree did not endure the inversions by strength. [563] It endured by listening. [567] Every turning hour, all the world's sounds came here, and the tree gathered them, gave them a place to rest, and in exchange the echoes steadied it, the way a spinning top is steadied by its own motion. [605] Resilience was not hardness; it was the still point inside the whirling. [617] The blighted orchards were dying because no one listened to them anymore, not because they were weak. [634]

Sorrel unstrapped the broken arrow tip and pressed it into a crevice of the hollow's stone, point down, a gift returned. [655] Growth answered at once: roots ran like quicksilver, a trunk thickened, branches fanned out green, and before the hour ended a young tree stood beside the first one, wide enough to shelter a gathering of its own. [692] Sorrel took one seed from it and flew home along the falling water, carrying stillness in her pocket and a planet of voices turning softly behind her, keeping her steady. [722]
