For thirty winters, the Concord had banned paraphrase, and so Ilse had become a cloud-shadow poet. [16] She composed only while a cloud's shadow crossed her paper, lines that existed as long as the shade did, then faded with it. [39] The wardens could not confiscate what vanished. [46] She was, by nature, aesthetically driven; even in the years when beauty was contraband, she chose it over safety. [65]

Now, three days after the ban lifted, she walked the night road along the lake toward the atrium. [83] The lake lay frozen, white as a held breath, and on its shore stood the cobalt atrium, glass-blue even in the dark. [105] Inside were the sacred verses, and the humming quartz colonnade raised to cage every confiscated poem. [121] Under the ban, the Concord had not burned what it seized. [132] Burned words, the judges believed, became martyrs. [139] Instead, scribes sealed each offending poem in sand and fire until it fused into quartz, and the quartz was set in the atrium as pillars, holding the roof, holding the silence. [170] They hummed, those pillars, day and night. [177] Everyone said it was the wind off the frozen lake. [187] Ilse had always known better. [192] A hum is a word that has not been given permission to stop. [205]

Just inside the atrium door, a line of pale stones crossed the floor, and beyond it only ordained reciters could pass. [226] Sacred, the stones meant. [230] For thirty years she had stopped at that line and recited nothing. [242] She had come tonight to stride through boundaries once thought sacred, and she did not slow at the stones. [261] Her boots rang on the floor, once, twice, and the echo climbed into the blue vault. [277] The atrium held a lantern-moth hush, dim gold lamps burning low, moths drifting against them in slow spirals, wings ticking softly on the glass. [301] Under the lamps stood the colonnade, two rows of cloudy quartz veined with light, humming low enough to feel in her teeth. [323] She laid her palm flat against the nearest pillar. [332] The hum climbed into her wrist like something waking. [341] Inside the quartz she saw shapes, syllables gone milky and opaque, sealed poems suspended like insects in amber. [359]

At the far end stood the reciter's desk, and on it, open, the Verses, which no unordained voice had ever rendered aloud. [381] Under the old law, reciting them verbatim was worship, and paraphrasing them was heresy. [395] That was the whole of it: the words were sacred exactly as they stood, and to carry them differently was to break them. [418] Ilse had spent thirty years believing that if she ever stood at that desk, she would recite them perfectly, letter for letter. [440] Standing there now, she found she did not want to. [450] She wanted to carry them her own way, the way a cloud carries light. [464] Not defiance, exactly; her own sentence was simply more beautiful, and she had never once in her life chosen the less beautiful thing. [487] So she paraphrased. [490] Where the first Verse said, "In the beginning the lake was sealed with ice, and the word lay under the ice," she said, "Before anything, something waited under the ice, and it was patient, and it was ours." [528] Her voice cracked on the last word. [535]

Then the colonnade changed. [539] The hum rose, gathered, split into something almost like voices, and the quartz began to clear. [555] The seals had never been stone; they were the belief that meaning breaks when it is carried. [572] The sealed syllables stirred inside the pillars, warming, loosening, and through dissolving boundaries, word by word, the old poems passed back out of the stone. [597] They did not come out as sound. [604] They came out as light, drifting up from the columns like pale moths, joining the living ones at the lamps. [624] The atrium filled with a soft double glow, wing and word, word and wing. [638]

Ilse stood in the middle of it, shaking. [646] What she felt was a tremor of certainty, the first of her life, moving through her the way the hum moved through the stone. [670] Certainty that meaning is not a relic to be guarded but a light to be carried, and that it changes hands without dimming. [693] She paraphrased the second Verse, and the third, her voice steadying each time. [706] Each rendering freed another pillar's worth of light. [714] By the time she reached the last Verse, the colonnade was only quartz, clear and quiet, columns of ordinary beautiful stone. [735] The roof did not fall. [739]

She walked out past the pale stones without bowing to them. [750] Outside, the frozen lake held the last of the moonlight, and her shadow lay long across it, cloud-shadow poet no longer, just a woman with a voice, walking home over the ice. [782]
