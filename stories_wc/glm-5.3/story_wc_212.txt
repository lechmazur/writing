The egg hatched at moonset, and the icebound atrium filled with one small, hungry sound. [15] Hazel held the chick against her wrist and felt her whole life go furiously tranquil, still as a stored seed while her heart hammered out of rhythm. [42] Starlight came through the frosted panes in long silver bars, the only warmth the vault allowed. [58]

Seven generations of seed savers had stood on this flagstone, and every one of them had left her the same inheritance. [79] Not the cabinets of heirloom seed alone, labeled in fading copperplate, but the vortex, and the debt that came with it. [100] "Vortex inheritance," her mother had said, pressing the oak acorn cap into Hazel's palm the night the frost took her. [120] "Nothing comes down to us in straight lines, child. [129] Time is a spiral, and every heirloom seed rides it, inheriting summers that fell before you were born." [147] The cap was the eye of that spiral, a little oak hat off the family's first tree, kept three hundred years because it fit exactly one thing. [174] The last acorn. [177]

Gran had used the vortex each midwinter, before the freeze swallowed the map of the world, borrowing a week of July inside the dome so the seed library could be dried, tested, kept honest. [211] Every borrowed week was written in the ledger, because borrowed time is not free time. [226] When the debts were called in, the winters came to collect, and the atrium sealed itself like a held breath. [246] That was the year the last thrush egg came into the vault, cold-saved between packets of kale, and the year Hazel began carrying it against her ribs, lending it her own heat. [278]

Now the chick lay in her hand, gray as a bud, and its whole life was clearly borrowed: nothing that small survives an ice age on arithmetic alone. [306] It needed spring within days, and spring was a century south. [317] Hazel looked at the chick, then at the sealed heart of the atrium, where the vortex had once turned the air like slow water. [341] She had never wanted power over the seasons. [349] What she wanted, all her life, was to discover the texture of borrowed time, the way Gran had seemed to know it by touch, running a thumb along a tomato skin in January as if reading braille. [386] Now she had a reason and a deadline. [394]

One instruction stood on the ledger's first page, in Gran's slanted hand: you reopen the vortex by decreasing entropy, because order is the hinge of the world. [421] So Hazel began the work she had trained for since she could walk. [434] She opened every cabinet and sorted every drawer, aligning packets by family and decade, beans beside beans, letting each generation of seed rest against its own descendants. [461] She swept the flagstones, straightened the tools, folded the sacking, and set the chick in a nest of clean wool at the exact center of the dome. [488] A living thing, Gran always said, is a small argument against disorder, and the chick's furious breathing was an argument already winning. [510] Slowly the frost on the panes organized itself into crystals of perfect sixfold symmetry, and the air above the nest began, very quietly, to turn. [535] The vortex was waking. [539]

Hazel fetched the acorn from its box, unhooked the oak acorn cap from the chain at her neck, and carried both to the turning eye of the air. [567] She set the seed on the flagstone, and with both hands, like crowning something, she lowered the cap onto it. [587] It fit the way a key fits. [594] The vortex reopened. [597]

Summer came down through the dome in a slow spiral, gold threading the starlight, and the icebound atrium bloomed into a wild serenity: snow outside the glass, July inside it, both true at once. [631] The chick opened its eyes in a July that had been saved, like everything else in this vault, for someone who would need it. [655] She reached her hand into the turning light, and at last she learned what she had come for. [673] Borrowed time has texture. [677] It is ridged like a seed coat, warm as a chick's down, thin in places where the lender's fingers held it, and it hums faintly, like a jar of bees remembering a meadow. [710] No wonder seed savers could hold it: their whole trade is wrapping the future in a coat so it survives the trip. [732] She wrote the debt in the ledger, then planted the acorn at the eye of the spiral, so the borrowed week would root into something the winters could not collect. [762] By morning the oak had sprouted. [768] The cap she kept, for whoever hatched next. [776]
