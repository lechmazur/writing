Snow fell without sound onto the golden dock, and Nils Voss stood waiting, as he had waited thirty winters, for the fog to bring the fisherman home. [27] He was a collector of seeds, a philosopher of small beginnings, and he believed that every grain of the past deserved a future. [50] Fog rolled across the harbor in slow gray folds, swallowing the breakwater, the gulls, the world. [66] Out of that hush came the knock of oars, and then the fisherman, Brandt, easing his skiff alongside the pilings. [86] He was the last of the secret weavers, men who had never written a word, because words can burn, but knots hold. [108] For four hundred years they had woven their knowledge into rope: tide tables, storm warnings, planting calendars, the whole quiet law of the coast. [132] Every knot was a syllable; every length of cord, a sentence the sea had taught. [147]

Nils had come to coax one sentence out of him before the sentence was lost forever. [163] In his coat pocket slept a paper envelope of fog-barley seed, a strain no living farmer could name and no book had recorded. [186] The weavers' ropes alone remembered when to sow it, when to reap it, how to keep it alive. [204] From his satchel Nils drew a piece of fulgurite glass, a hollow tube of sand fused by lightning, dug from the dunes by his grandmother, a weaver's daughter. [232] It was the weavers' token, proof of lineage, glass made by heaven's own hand and therefore impossible to fake. [251] Brandt studied the fulgurite a long moment, then studied the snow piling on Nils's shoulders, and understood that the seed collector had been standing there for hours. [278]

"You are patient," the fisherman said. [284] "I am infinitely patient," Nils answered. [290] "Seeds teach you that; nothing you hurry ever germinates." [299]

Brandt grunted, reached into the skiff, and lifted out a coil of tarred rope, old as the harbor itself, its surface crowded with knots. [323] He laid it across the golden planks between them like a body falling open before a doctor. [340] "The barley cord," he said. [345] "Read it, if your line still has eyes." [353]

Nils drew off his gloves and began coaxing the meaning out of the rope knot by knot, the way he coaxed life out of husks. [378] A plain overhand meant the moon's first quarter, the week the fog-barley went into the ground. [394] A double fisherman's knot, cinched tight, meant a storm to be trusted and waited out. [409] A long spacer of bare cord meant silence, a month of leaving the field alone to listen. [426] He read with his fingertips, as the first weavers had, blind men who trusted touch over ink, and the fog thickened around the dock as if it too wanted to hear. [457] "Every knot is an argument against forgetting," Nils murmured. [466] "A seed is the same argument, made smaller." [474] Brandt said nothing, because the weavers never spoke while a cord was being read; that was their one law. [493]

Halfway down the rope the knots changed, growing clumsy, and Nils understood: Brandt's own hands had tied these, a young man's hands, learning. [516] The old smooth knots belonged to weavers long dead; the clumsy ones were a son's inheritance, tied beside his father's deathbed. [537] Nils read them just as tenderly. [543]

At the rope's end came a single knot unlike the rest, a stopper tied around something small and hard. [562] Nils unwound it and found three grains of fog-barley, dark as soil, saved by weavers ninety years ago against a famine that never came. [586] "They kept seeds too," he whispered. [592] "We are the same guild, you and I." [600]

Brandt finally spoke, his voice like water under ice. [609] "Then learn it and carry it," he said. [617] "My hands are the last that know it, and the fog is coming for them too." [633] He pushed the rope across the planks until it lay entirely in Nils's lap, an inheritance surrendering itself. [651]

Nils slid the three ancient grains into his fulgurite tube, where lightning's glass would keep out the damp, and sealed the ends with wax. [674] Then he took the rope's frayed end and tied a new knot, a plain overhand with a doubled turn, the sign, he hoped, for a beginning. [700] Brandt looked at it, nodded once, and pushed off into the fog. [712] The snow kept falling on the golden dock, hushed as ever, and Nils stood a while longer with a rope of centuries in his arms, listening to the harbor breathe. [742] In spring he would plant the barley, and in autumn he would tie what he learned, one patient knot at a time. [764]
