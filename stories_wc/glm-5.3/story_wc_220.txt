The scrapyard settlement sprawled across the cracked wasteland like a beached fleet, hulls of dead machines stacked into streets that nobody named. [22] After the last resource war, the wind did most of the talking. [34] It moved through carburetors and gutted buses, playing them like flutes, and people called that music the wild hush: the sound of a world holding its breath. [61]

Bracken lived at the settlement's edge, an old bloodhound retired from finding bodies. [74] For eleven years he had walked the wastes behind his handler, Iris, reading the ground for what the war left behind. [95] Where families went missing, Bracken's nose closed the distance, and he brought home the lost, the fallen, and the hidden, three hundred and some. [119] Each time, Iris spoke the same soft words over them. [129] Now the war was done, the missing were few, and Iris was gone. [142] She died in spring, of the sickness the old bombs left in the bone, and she left the settlement one thing besides her dog: a phonograph cylinder of dark wax she had made herself. [176] No machine in the scrapyard could play it; the players had been stripped for copper years ago. [193]

But there was Wren. [197] Wren was twelve, born after the war, unable to read printed words but famous for reading secret texts in the grooves of phonograph cylinders. [221] Hold a cylinder to the sun and she saw sound the way other folk saw weather coming. [238] She was intuitively descriptive; she could not tell you what a thing was, only what it was like, and every groove she read came to her tinted: silver rain, orange laughter, a low brown hum. [273]

On the evening Bracken carried her the cylinder, jaws careful around the wax, she held it up to the last light. [294] Inside the amber, sealed like a moth in resin, lay an autumn leaf preserved in wax, with the grooves spiraling around it. [316] Wren read the spiral, and what she read was a woman's voice in colors. [330] "Say it plain," said the scrapmaster, and half the settlement had gathered without anyone calling it. [346] Wren closed her eyes and gave them the riddle as it shone: "One leaf sleeps in me, and it never landed; its mother never left; find the mother, and dig, and I will be close again." [382]

A riddle, then, and the people chewed on it while the hush played overhead. [396] Bracken stood, ears lifting. [400] He knew that wax-smell; it was the smell of the last autumn, the smell of the maple. [417] The settlement had kept one tree through half the war, a maple growing out of a cracked engine block, until the final winter killed its courage. [443] Someone dragged the trunk to the commons and mounted the great salvaged foghorn on it, the brass horn the settlement rang at dusk. [466] The mother that never left. [471]

They dug beneath the horn at first light, Wren and the scrapmaster and Bracken with his stiff old paws. [490] Two feet down, the dog's nose found more wax: a crate sealed against the rust of the world. [508] Inside lay a hand-crank phonograph, whole and clean, and more cylinders than anyone could count, each labeled in grooves only Wren could read. [531] Wren wound the machine, and the small horn sang under the great silent one. [545] Iris's voice came out of the past, laughing a little. [555] "If you found this, then Bracken did his last find; good dog; sit, love; this one is the day the leaf came down; listen." [579] And the cylinder played an autumn: wind, the rattle of dry maple leaves, her humming, a dog sighing in warm grass. [600]

The people stood in the wild hush with open mouths, hearing a season none of them had met. [618] Every revelation arrived tinted, through amber wax and Wren's borrowed colors, and nobody minded that it was not pure, because truth poured through wax comes out changed and still true. [648] Bracken laid his head on the wooden box, where the vibration of her voice passed into his skull the way her footsteps once came up through the floorboards. [676] Distance was all he had ever worked with, the far dead made near, the lost made found, and now the farthest thing of all, a voice out of a closed year, lay down beside him. [711]

That evening the dusk horn rang, and after it the hush, and beneath both they played another cylinder, and another: Iris teaching the settlement how to keep bees, how to read sky, how to speak words over the found. [750] She had spent her last months translating distance into closeness, seasons into grooves, mornings into wax, herself into a voice that would wait beneath the horn, unrotting, like a leaf. [780]
