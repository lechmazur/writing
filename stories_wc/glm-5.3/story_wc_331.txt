The Library of Scientific Poetry keeps odd hours, and so does its librarian. [13] I met Edith Merrow at four in the morning; I was proofreading an astronomy supplement; she was reshelving sonnets about stellar collapse with her eyes closed. [39] A tattered silk scarf was wound at her throat, knotted until it had ruffled into permanent curls, like a question asked too many times to stay flat. [66]

The library itself is two floors of poems about tidal mathematics, odes to the periodic table, elegies for disproven theories. [86] Its patrons browse at strange hours: shift workers, insomniacs, a chemist who reads love poems to the noble gases. [105] Nobody finds any of this unusual, which tells you what kind of library it is. [120]

Edith is what I have come to call sanely eccentric. [130] Every odd practice of hers arrives with a footnote. [139] She shelves by the emotional temperature of a subject because, she says, grief recalled alphabetically is harder to find than grief recalled by degree. [163] She works the one-to-seven shift because the loneliest readers come then, and they need a librarian most. [180] The scarf, too, has a footnote. [186] Her mother was a mathematician who studied hyperbolic geometry and knotted the silk herself, adding stitches to every row until the fabric ruffled into a working model of negative curvature. [216] On a hyperbolic surface there is more room than flat space allows; the edge goes on forever, folding inward. [235] Edith wears infinity at her throat and will explain the mathematics to anyone who asks. [250]

What I could not explain, at first, was the back room. [261] It is called the Analog Memory Annex, and its rules are posted in Edith's careful hand. [277] Anyone may deposit a memory there, provided it is physical: a cassette labeled "Dad's laugh, 1987"; a recipe card stained with vanilla; a thimble, a braid of hair. [305] No digital files are accepted, and Edith's footnote for that is simple: a digital copy stays perfect, and perfection forgets. [325] Analog memories remember in their wear; every scratch on a record is a diary of the needle that loved it. [345]

For eleven years Edith had logged small anomalies in that room. [356] When a patron reads a poem aloud, the objects sometimes stir: the cassette turns a quarter inch, the recipe card smells fresh again, the braid of hair warms. [384] She charted every incident on graph paper, and for eleven years the dots made no sense on the page. [403] Then one October morning she laid the chart on her desk, the scarf beside it, and went very still. [422] Projected onto the ruffles of the silk, the anomalies resolved. [432] They clustered along the curves, denser toward the edge, exactly as points do on a hyperbolic plane. [449] Her mother's geometry had been a map all along, of a place with more room than the building could hold. [469]

Edith told me all of this at half past four, in the tone other people use for weather. [487] She had spent her career, she said, looking for the place where stories live. [501] Not the shelf, not the page; the actual residence. [510] The map said the place sat at the asymptote, the edge of the scarf's folds, where hyperbolic space goes on forever. [531] You could approach it forever and never arrive; that, she said, was why stories were always nearly gone. [549] She refused to close the library for the attempt. [558] Witnesses matter, she said, and so do readers; the anomalies only ever happened while patrons browsed. [574]

So at five, with the chemist murmuring to xenon and a nurse reading elegies, Edith carried the scarf into the Annex and draped it across the deposit shelf. [602] She asked me to read aloud, and handed me a poem about tides. [615] I read, and the browsing voices layered in around mine, and the room began, very slowly, to change. [633] The knots of the scarf shifted as though a patient hand were still adding stitches. [648] The cassette turned and played a father's laugh from 1987. [658] The recipe card breathed vanilla across the room. [666] Somewhere a thimble rolled the length of the shelf, keeping a rhythm. [678] The analog memories were reawakening, folding open along curves no flat shelf could contain. [692] It lasted as long as the reading did. [700] When the nurse closed her book, the objects settled like a tide, and Edith stood alone among them in the small silence after. [723]

The place where stories live, she said later, was never a location; it was the curvature itself, the extra room between a reader and a worn thing. [750] I still come at four in the morning, and Edith is still alone at her desk, the scarf at her throat like an answer now: crumpled, infinite at the edges, holding more room than it should. [786]
