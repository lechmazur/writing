The inscriptions had followed me all my life, glyphs cut into lintels and cornerstones, matching no grammar book I ever found. [21] I worked for the surveyor's office, recording buildings before demolition, and every condemned one whispered in the same dead alphabet. [41] My whole ambition was to learn the language of old buildings. [52] I wanted to read them before they fell. [60] Their glyphs felt familiar the way a lullaby does when you cannot remember who sang it. [76]

Then, on an evening I cannot place in any year, the woman from the top floor knocked and asked to borrow sugar. [98] I gave her a cupful. [103] She returned it the next Thursday, still full and capped with wax, together with an invitation to tea. [121] That is how it began: borrowed sugar that turned into weekly tea sessions in her rooftop garden, among rosemary pots and a rusted iron table. [146] She called herself Mrs. Vesper, and I never asked for more. [157]

On the third Thursday she noticed my notebook, opened it to a copied lintel, and read the line aloud as easily as a recipe. [181] "You have the ear," she said. [187] After that we stopped pretending the tea was the point. [197] Her hands were patiently nimble, tracing each glyph in spilled tea or warm wax so that speed looked like gentleness. [217] "Buildings speak slowly," she told me, "which is why only the patient hear them." [231] Under her, the language assembled itself: a word for weight, a word for shelter, a word for stay. [249]

There were things I did not say. [255] Her teacup never lowered. [259] Her shadow arrived late on winter afternoons, or not at all. [270] The borrowed sugar sat on her shelf, untouched and always full. [281] Once, in the archive, I found a photograph of this building's opening day, and she stood beside the cornerstone, unchanged. [301] I understood that we had agreed upon a lie: that she was an ordinary neighbor, I an ordinary student, and the lessons the only strangeness between us. [328] We both knew. [331] Neither of us said. [335] I think the old tongue survived on exactly those terms: the dead keep teaching it, and the living keep pouring. [355]

That spring, her building crossed my own desk in the demolition schedule. [367] I said nothing, and she did not need me to. [377] Our lessons turned urgent in the quietest way. [385]

On the night of the meteor shower she served tea as usual, but the tray carried one thing more: an antique desk seal, dark with handling, its silver handle worn smooth. [416] The sky began to fall in threads of old light. [426] "Some of that light left its star before this city had a name," she said. [441] "It arrives anyway." [444] Something in me broke its leash. [450] I interrogated her then, every held question at once: who are you, what are you, why me, why the sugar, why teach what the living gave up. [477] She did not flinch. [481] She warmed a puck of wax against the teapot, softened it with her thumb, and pressed the seal into it. [501] She lifted the stamp and turned the impression toward me. [511] It was a mason's mark, a half moon over a crossed line. [523] I knew it. [526] I had copied it from the cornerstone of this very building, seven floors beneath her rosemary. [542] "You wrote the walls," I said. [548] "Every stone of them," she said. [554] "The language went down with the last of us who built." [565] She folded my fingers around the seal, and it was warm, as if recently held. [580] "A mark is a promise that a hand was here," she said. [592] "Keep pressing it." [595] The last meteor fell, and the rooftop held only the garden, the cooling tea, the full cup of sugar, and me. [616]

In June the building came down. [622] I stood at the fence with the seal in my pocket and read the cornerstone aloud as the crane took the first wall: her word for weight, her word for shelter, her word for stay. [657] My dead language tutor, in both senses, had taught me to hear one building completely. [672] I carried her rosemary to a new balcony and kept the Thursday hours anyway. [686] I pour two cups, and we agree on the new lie, which is the oldest one: that no one ever really leaves. [708] We both know. [711] Neither of us says. [715]
