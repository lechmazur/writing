Nell had been many women that year. [7] In the bookshop downstairs she was a retired nurse; at the corner bakery, a widow of a diplomat; to herself, most nights, no one at all. [33] Reinventing her history was easier than carrying it. [41] The truth lived in the attic of Bellweather's Books, a shop of yellowed spines and damp-sweet air, where she catalogued donations and tried to forget she had ever trained courier pigeons. [72] Her sister Lark had been the singer, and the flock had been theirs together, until a March storm scattered the birds and Lark, chasing them through it, caught the fever that finished her. [105] Nell sold the survivors and kept only a small chip of Mars meteorite, Lark's last gift, bought from a museum sale with grocery money. [129] "Even rocks fall a long way," Lark had said, "and land somewhere that wants them." [144] Nell carried the chip everywhere and lied about everything else. [154]

The attic suited her. [158] It had once been a pigeon loft, generations back, when the building ran messages across the city; the shop's owner, Theo, liked to say the walls themselves were made of paper trails. [190] Nell catalogued ledgers, love letters, receipts pressed flat as moths. [200] When a donation overwhelmed her, she steadied herself the old way: she balanced the meteorite chip on the back of her wrist and, under the single skylight, mapped the swirl of dust motes, reading the invisible air the way she had once read wind before releasing birds. [247] Dust rose, hovered, sank; every mote a small bird; every pattern a flight she could still predict. [264]

Theo never corrected her stories, which is how she began, slowly, to trust him. [278] Across that year of learning to trust again, he brought her tea, then books he thought she'd like, then questions. [298] "You hold things like they might fly," he said once. [308] She almost told him the truth that day. [316] Instead she claimed she had been a librarian in another life, and he nodded as though librarians also watched the sky. [337]

In November, Theo pried open a cabinet warped shut since the building's loft days and found a tin box of message slips, thin as dragonfly wings, from the old courier service. [368] Some held shopping lists and apologies. [374] But eleven of them, in violet ink, held songs. [383] Each carried lyrics and melodies sketched in rising and falling strokes, a woman writing to her beloved across the city, a voice sent by wing. [408] Nell read one aloud before she could stop herself, and the tune arrived on its own, the way Lark's tunes always had. [430]

"We could transcribe them," Theo said. [436] "Museums keep letters," Nell said. [441] "Nobody keeps songs." [444] "We will," he said. [448] A song died twice, Nell thought, once in the throat and once in the silence after, and paper could hold the second death off. [472] So the attic became a workshop for preserving the echo of lost songs. [485] She copied the lyrics into a ledger; she hummed the melodies until the dust motes seemed to follow them; she pressed a sprig of the attic ivy between the finished pages, because each old slip still held a withered violet, a flower that had bloomed once and then withered into a way of staying. [539]

The night they finished the eleventh song, Nell set the meteorite chip on the ledger between them. [556] "This is what's left of what I really was," she said. [567] Then she told him everything: the loft on their roof, the fifty courier birds, the paper slips rolled into tiny tubes on their legs, Lark singing up into the dark while the birds murmured back, the storm, the fever, the selling, the lying since. [611] Theo listened the way she watched dust: without reaching for anything. [622] "The birds that lived," he asked, "where did they go?" [632] "To a racer two counties over." [638] "Then they're still flying," he said, "and so are the songs." [649]

In spring they printed the collection as a small chapbook, one song to a page, each page bearing a photocopied violet, and titled it 'Skylight.' [674] A woman from the historical society wept at the counter. [684] On the last page Nell wrote a note about her sister, true this time, and signed it with her real name and real trade: pigeon trainer, retired; keeper of songs, current. [715] The potted ivy climbing the skylight frame, winter-withered, budded that same week; new green unfurled over old brown, blooming and withering in one breath, which was, Nell decided, simply what staying alive looked like. [749] Some mornings she still balanced the chip on her wrist and mapped the motes, an old habit, a small religion. [769] But now, when the dust swirled, she hummed, and the attic answered: paper songs, patient stone, one skylight of moving light. [790]
