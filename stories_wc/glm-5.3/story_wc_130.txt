The orchard lived underground, its apple and pear trees drinking the slow rain of the stalactites. [16] By day the cavern rang with drips and leaf-rustle, but after the orchard grew silent beneath the moon, only Vessa moved among the rows. [40] She was a conjurer, secretive about it the way some people are secretive about praying. [55] Her sister Mara had kept the orchard before her, until the spring she walked toward the deep passage and did not walk back. [78] No body, no note, no draft in the tunnels to say she had gone out or gone under; only absence with no edges to it. [103] The town had held a funeral; Vessa had not attended, because a funeral requires a fact. [119]

Mara had been a taxidermist, the patient kind, and her masterwork was a pair of glass eyes so lifelike that clients swore they followed you. [144] Vessa carried them now in a chamois pouch, one amber, one grey, cool as river stones. [160] In the dark of the cavern they gathered what little light there was, and she had learned to read by them. [181]

Her conjuring was arithmetic: she was trying to derive the mathematics of emotion, and grief without a conclusion was the equation that would not simplify. [206] Every night she charted the drips from the stalactites, because sorrow, she suspected, had a rate, and hope had a rate, and somewhere the two lines crossed. [233] She was gently inventive about it, building her proofs out of apple blossoms, wax, and patience rather than blood or thunder. [254]

At the mouth of the deep passage lay a mosaic stepping stone Mara had set with her own hands, tesserae of green slate and white quartz. [280] Vessa had crossed it a thousand times before she noticed the chips were not damage. [295] Each tessera carried a small, deliberate nick, and the nicks repeated: three, then five, then eight, then thirteen. [313] Vessa knelt on the cold stone and felt her own pulse climb. [325] It was a Fibonacci sequence, the same spiral that curled a fern and shelled a snail, and Mara had chipped it where only the two of them would ever kneel. [355] Their grandfather had taught them both: growth that obeys a rule but never stops surprising you. [371]

Vessa's grief equation had been failing for three years, and now she saw why: it was one half of a join. [392] The other half had been under her boots all along, waiting in the chipped patterns on the mosaic stepping stone. [412] That night she took out her chalk and worked until the candle coughed, mapping her numbers onto Mara's, letting the two sequences dovetail. [435] Dovetail was the word her grandfather used for joints: two pieces cut so each holds the other, impossible to pull apart by pulling straight. [459] Her rates of drip, her counts of blossom, her sums of sleepless hours locked into the chips one by one, tooth into socket, and the equation closed like a hand. [489]

The answer it produced was not a body and not a goodbye. [501] It was a remainder, carried forward: grief that does not resolve, but can be carried without dropping. [518] And under the remainder, faint as a watermark, a second term: she went willingly. [532] Not dead, then, as far as arithmetic could swear; gone deeper on purpose, leaving the door mathematically open. [550]

Vessa set the taxidermist's glass eyes on the stepping stone, amber and grey, both facing the dark throat of the passage. [571] Mara had made them to give the dead a gaze; Vessa gave them a watch instead. [587] Let them gather the lantern light and hold the door for whoever came back up, or down. [604] Far down the passage, so faint she could have invented it, something dripped in an answering rhythm. [621]

She would keep the orchard, chart the drips, answer the moon's silence with small inventions, and one night the arithmetic might finally say look, and mean a direction. [649] Above her the stalactites went on measuring time into the roots, patient as sisters, drop by patient drop. [667] The equation did not end; it continued, and so did she. [678]
