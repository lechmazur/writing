The palace hangs above nothing. [5] Suspended from the cavern's giant stalactites by chains thick as towers, it was built to be a cradle, and like every cradle it was meant to hold something new. [34] That is why the founders called the machine at its heart the Genesis Engine. [48] The prince descended toward it now, bedraggled in a coat that had been royal when the surface still had weather, frayed now to gray thread at every hem. [76] He thought in hypotheses the way other men breathed. [85] Every corridor of the suspended palace posed him a question, and he answered each one twice, once in ink and once aloud, because a theory untested was only a rumor with better posture. [118] Why do the clockwork larks sing at the seventh bell and no other? [131] Because, he had hypothesized, the founders could not compose a true dawn, so they scheduled one. [147] That was the flaw at the root of everything, the crack beneath the cradle. [161] The Genesis Engine could imitate life but not summon it, and so the world it made was perfect and dead. [181] The prince had spent nine years hunting the one exception. [191] His purpose, unspoken even to his notebooks, was to reawaken beauty in a mechanical world that only ever ran. [210] His latest hypothesis ran like this: no copy without an original. [221] The engine had fabricated a thousand mechanical dawn-larks, therefore it must once have held one real bird, or at least the memory of one, sealed somewhere in its oldest chamber. [251] So he descended past the gardens of gear-flowers, past the ponds where silver fish swam on measured circuits, down the throat of the first stalactite, to the vault the maps refused to name. [284] The door opened to his bloodline, as he had predicted, and to nothing else, as he had feared. [302] Inside, in a nest of copper wire meant to look like straw, lay a single egg. [318] It was cream colored, freckled, cool to the touch, and utterly ordinary, which made it the most extraordinary object in the whole hollow world. [342] The last dawn-lark had died on the burning surface two centuries ago. [354] Here was its egg, unbroken, uncounted, waiting. [361] His hands shook as he lifted it, and he hated that they shook, and then he hypothesized that trembling was simply what joy did when it had been caged too long. [392] He named the next hours his proof. [399] In the engine's upper workshops he assembled a cradle of his own, scavenging a brass heat coil, a lens of polished stalactite quartz, a metronome governor, and the glass hood from a fallen clockwork lark. [434] Each part was machine, and he knew the argument that entailed. [445] Perhaps a mechanical world could only ever hatch a mechanical thing. [456] He placed the egg beneath the warm lens anyway, because the alternative hypothesis was despair, and he refused to publish that one. [478] For three days he barely slept. [484] He hypothesized about the shell, about the humidity of dead air, about whether warmth was a language an egg could still understand. [506] On the third midnight the shell answered with a sound like a hairline crack in a bell. [523] He knelt so fast his knees rang on the floor. [533] A black beak emerged, then a wet head, then wings unfurling like crumpled letters, and the prince felt the whole suspended palace seem to hold its chains still. [561] But the left wing opened with a tick. [569] Feathers gave way along its edge to fine pinions of brass, meshed and gleaming, unmistakably the engine's craft. [587] The founders had copied more than the bird's shape; they had threaded their flaw into the original itself, and the flaw had waited two hundred years to hatch. [615] A flawed genesis, the prince thought, still a genesis. [624] The hatchling opened its beak and sang, one small note, half breath and half chime, imperfect, alive, new. [642] Up through the palace floors the clockwork larks heard it, and one by one, off schedule, off script, they answered, adjusting their manufactured dawns toward that single uncertain sound. [671] The prince laughed and wept at once, quaking in the half light like a man waking from a dream he could not fully leave. [695] It was bitterly sweet, this victory: not the pure bird he had hunted, but beauty nonetheless, the first unscheduled thing in two centuries. [718] He cradled it against his ruined coat and began, already, his next hypothesis: that a world which could hatch one imperfect living thing might, given time and a mad prince, hatch another. [750] Above him the stalactites hummed. [755] Far above, or far below, the dead world waited, and for the first time it sounded like waiting rather than ending. [776]
