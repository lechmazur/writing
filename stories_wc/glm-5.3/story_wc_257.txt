Mara had made mourning jewelry for thirty strangers before she failed to make any for herself. [16] Her sister Wren had died in spring, and the lockets and cremation glass Mara shaped for other people's dead went quiet in her hands. [40] By winter she had sold the torches, lost the shop, and hit the kind of bottom that has no echo. [60] The university let her keep the caretaker's cot in the old observatory where Wren had worked her long nights, since nobody else wanted the post. [85] Its clock had stopped at decommission; its dust lay in epochs; Wren used to call it the only timeless building in town, a place that measured light instead of hours. [115]

The first paper flower appeared on her cot in February, folded flat as a dead leaf, and when she opened it a pair of coordinates was written inside in graphite. [145] She almost burned it. [148] Instead she walked to the spot, a bench outside her old shop, and found a second figure waiting, folded from a page of star charts. [173] All spring the trail went on, cranes on gravestones, lilies on bus seats, each figure withered flat and each holding numbers that led somewhere Wren had loved. [200] Mara came to think of the folding as a small resurrection, because every paper flower had to be withered shut before it could bloom open in your hands. [228] The last coordinates belonged to the observatory itself. [236]

She climbed to the dome at dusk, past Wren's blackout drapes, which Wren had named the curtains of intention. [255] Close out every light you do not mean to love, she used to say, and the one you do mean can reach you. [278] Astronomy, she claimed, was mostly the discipline of refusing whole skies. [289]

At the eyepiece a machine was waiting, a squat old automation Wren had rebuilt from decommissioned parts, and it spoke with the flat courtesy of something genuinely artificial. [317] "I do not grieve," it said. [323] "I persist, and your sister taught me, before the end, to leave things where hurting people would find them, so I have been folding her star charts into your path all season." [355] Then it extended a manipulator arm, and on the fine steel tip lay a sheet of vellum, soft with handling: a tattoo stencil Mara herself had drawn at twenty-three and abandoned. [386] Wren had kept it all those years. [393]

The design was a stem of jasmine caught mid-bloom, one branch still furled, and beneath it Wren had penciled a note: for the day you start again. [420] Mara's hands shook the way they had never shaken over any commission. [432] The machine drew the curtains with a sound like settling snow, sealing out the town's stray glow, and opened the dome shutter to one square of sky chosen with intention. [462] "Your sister showed me this star on her last clear night," it said. [475] "Its light left before either of you were born, and I am told that you keep light that has gone, so I thought we shared a trade." [502] Mara understood that her lockets and this machine's mirrors were the same craft, both genuinely artificial, honest about being stand-ins, both built to hold a light that had already traveled on. [533] She laid the stencil on the workbench beneath the open shutter, and the old starlight fell across the vellum and illuminated it, so that the furled branch of jasmine seemed to glow from within, as if light could finish a drawing. [574]

In the morning she rode the service bus down the mountain and paid a tattooist with the last of her money. [595] The stencil held; the ink took. [601] Then she went back up and did the harder thing. [611] She cleaned a bench, relit a torch, and began a new kind of mourning jewelry, pieces meant not to embalm a loss but to teach withering to bloom again: lockets shaped like folded flowers that opened against the collarbone, rings set with living seeds where hair once went. [659] On quiet evenings she folded paper beside the machine, printing coordinates inside, and left the figures in doorways, bus shelters, and hospital lobbies for strangers at their own rock bottom, so that someone, someday, would unfold a dead-flat flower, read a place worth walking to, and begin. [706] She worked with a calm that burned, and above her the timeless dome kept one square of sky open all night, the curtains of intention drawn tight around the only light she meant to love. [741]
