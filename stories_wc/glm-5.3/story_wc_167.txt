The mist came off Pearl Bay before dawn, and above it hung the last noctilucent clouds, silver-blue and ribbed like the underside of a shell. [25] Edric Hale had watched those clouds from his glasshouse for forty years and had named them long ago: noctilucent reckonings, the sky's account of the night just ended, read out in silver before the day could erase it. [63] This morning the account would close. [69] At noon the surveyors would arrive, and the glasshouse, the carp pond, and every crooked pane he had ever fitted would go under the new marina. [95] "Before everything shifts," his daughter had written, "I will come." [105] She had written nothing else, but Edric read between lines for a living: the wavering in old crown glass, the sentence a window wanted to say, and the silence after her promise said plenty. [139] Wren came up the shingle at first light, coat stiff with salt, the way her mother used to come off the ferries. [161] Neither of them had ever been good at the direct sentence. [172] So, being a man who thought twice about everything, he did what his old master had done at every parting: he led her to the carp pond and put a flat stone in her hand. [207] "We can commune like this," he said. [214] She threw, and the rings went out wide and unhurried, rings within rings, and where they crossed they built small cells of caught light. [238] His own stone fell near the reeds, throwing a tight, quick spiral. [250] They read each other the way he read glass: her wide rings said a grief that had finally made room; his quick ones said an anger long held in, thin as a flaw. [283] I thought your work was small, her rings had said for years, from cities with real light in them. [302] Then he took her through the mist to the prism atrium. [313] He had built it from what the trade threw away: bull's-eyes and mis-cut panes, chipped chandelier drops, forty years of leftovers hung on a frame of old spars, and the mist stood inside it like breath. [349] "This is why I stayed," he said. [356] The sun cleared the headland, and the discarded glass did what he had spent a life insisting it could do: it took plain gray light and split it into seven honest colors, throwing them across the mist, across her face, across the bare boards. [400] That had always been the whole of his creed, the reason a harbor glazier keeps cutting: to illuminate the value hidden in the mundane, in wrecked glass and carp ponds and ordinary dawns that nobody thinks to price. [438] Wren turned slowly inside the colors, and he watched her reckon it, the way the clouds above were reckoning the night. [459] "I'm tearing out half of what I make at the studio," she said at last. [474] "Nothing I build catches light like this." [481] "It isn't the glass," he said. [487] "It's the noticing." [490] Outside, the noctilucent clouds were fading as the bay turned pearl, their reckoning paid and entered. [506] In an hour they would be gone; in three, the atrium would come down with the rest. [523] But between the reckoning and the shift there was this, and Edric had learned to live inside such gaps. [542] He took down one small prism, flawed, a bubble caught at its heart, and pressed it into her palm. [561] "Waste glass," he said. [565] "Watch what it does at your window on a gray Tuesday." [576] She closed her hand around it and, for the first time in eleven years, hugged him, plainly, no lines to read between. [598] They walked back to the pond for the last throw. [608] Wren's stone struck the center, and the rings ran out perfect and wide, crossing and recrossing until the whole surface was one moving pattern, and Edric read it with a glazier's patience: stay, it said, and after it, fainter, stay. [648] He answered the only way left to him, one ring answering another: he threw his stone beside hers, so the patterns met and held, and for a moment the pond was a page both of them had written on. [687] Then the sun burned the mist off Pearl Bay, the day arrived on time, and everything began, at last, to shift. [708]
