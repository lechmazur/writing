The rain had followed the coach all the way down from the gloom-hill, ticking in the gutters like a small patient clock. [22] At the river the fog lay bound across the crossing, white and unbroken, and Harrow drew the horses to a stand. [43] Folks in the valley called him the gloom-hill coachman, and he had stopped correcting them years ago. [60] Forty years on this road had made him closely attentive, not to any one thing but to everything at once. [80] The river's murmur percolated through that ambient awareness of his, and what settled out was always a few plain grains of fact: water high, ferry late, nobody shouting. [108]

His single passenger that evening was a young woman named Ilsabet, bound for the far bank and a funeral that had already happened. [131] She had missed it by three days of bad roads and worse luck, and she sat now with her hands folded, as if grief were a parcel she was minding for somebody else. [164] "The ferryman crosses when the gutters stop ticking," Harrow told her, climbing down. [177] "Rain like this, the river pulls too hard on the rope." [188] So they waited inside the yellow ring of the crossing lantern while the fog moved past them like slow traffic. [208]

Harrow noticed the tear in her sleeve, a long split that grief had made her blind to. [225] He went to the box under the driver's seat and came back with a yarn needle of bone, worn smooth as a river stone. [249] "My wife's," he said. [253] "She could mend anything but a morning." [260] While the gutters ticked he stitched, and the quiet filled up with the small sounds of the fog-bound crossing: rope creak, water sucking at the pilings, a horse shifting its weight. [291]

At last she asked why he carried a dead woman's needle instead of leaving it in a drawer with the other keepsakes. [313] "Because of a promise," he said. [319] "Not the one you're thinking." [324] He told it plainly. [328]

Forty-one years ago his wife had taken the ferry across to attend a birth on the far bank. [346] "Keep the lantern lit," she had called from the water, "until I'm home." [359] She delivered the child clean and caught the fever after, and the far bank kept her. [375] For a year he kept the lantern lit for her return, which was absurd, and he knew it, and did it anyway. [397]

Then one night a traveler was about to miss the last ferry, and Harrow lifted the lantern down and swung it so the ferryman could find the water, and the promise changed under his hands without his permission. [435] "A palimpsest, the schoolmaster once called it," he said. [444] "You scrape the old writing off and write the new on top, but the old shows through." [461] Now the lantern read: I will keep it lit until you are across. [474] But beneath it, faint as a watermark, the older sentence still ghosted: until I'm home. [489]

The needle was the same. [494] It had promised to mend his wife's knitting, then his gloves, then tonight a stranger's sleeve, each vow written over the last. [516] "I've had forty years to make grief complicated," he said, snipping the thread with his teeth. [532] "My work now is to distill it back to essence. [542] A lit lantern, a mended sleeve, a person on the far bank who was waited for." [558]

Ilsabet looked at the fog, which was thinning into spindrift, fine spray lifted off the current and carried through the lantern light like slow snow. [583] Something of the crossing's serenity entered her then, quiet as a needle going through cloth. [598] The complicated thing she had carried three days, the missed funeral, the unsaid goodbye, the miles of bad road, percolated down through that stillness and left its essence at the bottom: she had loved him, and he had known it. [638] "Then it's enough," she said. [643]

The gutters ticked slower. [647] Tick. [648] Tick. [649] Then the rain quit all at once, the way rain does, as if it had only been holding the door. [669] The gutters stopped ticking, and out of the fog came the slow creak of the ferry rope tightening. [687] The boat slid into the lantern light, spindrift ghosting from its bow. [699]

Harrow handed Ilsabet aboard, then gave her the yarn needle with the last of the gray yarn wound around it. [719] "A promise is only thread," he said. [726] "It holds because you keep stitching it into whatever's torn." [736] She crossed. [738]

He watched the ferry become a blur, then a rumor, then nothing, the fine spray settling over the water with the calm of a thing ended exactly where it meant to. [769] Then he climbed up and turned the horses for the gloom-hill, the lantern still burning at his back. [787]
