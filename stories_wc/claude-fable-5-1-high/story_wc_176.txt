<story>
Ilse Varen had cast the sanctum bell at twenty-six, and it had split on its first winter night. [18] For eleven years she had told the story against herself: she had pulled the mold too early, hungry to hear it ring. [40] Now she climbed the ice steps with the story told out at last, the way a fever finally breaks. [59] She had forgiven herself somewhere on the road north, without ceremony, and only noticed afterward that her hands had stopped shaking. [80]

The sanctum was a hollow in the glacier, its dome an inverted bowl of ancient ice. [96] Above it the aurora moved in slow green folds, and the light came down through the dome as dim as milk. [117] The cracked bell hung mute where she had left it. [127] Beside it, stiff as a frozen serpent, hung the rope. [137]

She knew the rope only from the keepers' telling. [146] For six centuries, each time someone in the valley died, a keeper had braided the name into it, a knot language of over and under, three strands for a vowel, five for the rest. [180] The rope had been the register, the last keeper had said, and the bell was how you read it aloud. [200] Pull the rope, and the bell named everyone at once. [210]

The keepers were gone now, and no one living could read a braid. [223] No one except, perhaps, a dead organ grinder's daughter. [232]

Ilse unbuttoned her coat and took out the scrap. [241] It was a hand's length of punched card, brown and soft at the edges, torn from the roll her father had cranked through his street organ in a dozen market towns. [272] He had come to the sanctum once, when she was small, and the last keeper had paid him in dried fish to transcribe a length of the rope into holes, so the names could be played on a street corner far from the ice. [316] Her father had lost the rest of the roll to a flood. [328] This scrap he had kept in his boot, and she had kept it in hers. [343]

Lost-wax casting is, technically, a gentle craft. [350] Nothing is struck. [353] The wax gives up its place and the bronze accepts it, and the shape passes between them like a whispered word. [374] She worked on the rope the same way. [382] She breathed on the frozen braids until they softened, one span at a time, and she matched the pattern of holes in the scrap to the pattern of crossings in the hemp. [414] Three-over-two was a hole at the left edge. [422] Five-under was two holes close together. [428] Slowly the scrap taught her the grammar, and the grammar unlocked the rest. [441]

Then she began to loosen the braids. [448] Not to undo them, never that, but to open them, to spread the strands so that each name became a lattice with light between the fibers. [474] She worked from the bottom up, and when she had opened a span, she hung it across the space between the dome and the cracked bell's wax twin, the new model she had carried north in a padded crate. [513]

The aurora did the rest. [518] It came through the ice dome and through the opened braids and fell onto the wax in stripes and crossings, a shadow alphabet, green and gold and shifting. [546] Ilse followed the light with her graver. [553] Where the aurora lay bright on the wax she cut, and where the braid's shadow fell she left the surface whole, and name after name went into the model in a script that no one had written for a hundred years. [594]

Her fingers warmed as she worked. [600] Then her face. [603] Then something behind her ribs that had been frozen so long she had mistaken it for bone. [620] She had thought she was coming here to repair a mistake. [631] She was coming, she understood now, to give the dead their voices back, and she could only do it because she had stopped needing to be forgiven by anyone but herself. [662]

Near dawn she reached the last span, the newest braids, and found one she could read without the scrap. [681] Five strands, then three, then five, then three. [689] Her father's name. [692] The last keeper had braided in the organ grinder who had carried their names south. [707]

She cut it into the wax with the aurora fading above her. [719] In spring she would melt this wax out of its mold and pour the bronze slowly, patiently, and let it cool as long as it needed. [745] And when the new bell rang, every name in the rope would ring in it, her father's among them, in a sound like ice letting go of a river. [774]
