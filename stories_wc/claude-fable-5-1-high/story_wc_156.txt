[36] The sound wave garden had been built for exactly this trick. [79] Ines stood at the other, scalpel raised, and did not answer yet. [91] Paint freckled her forearms in cobalt and ochre, so that she looked like a detail of the wall that had stepped down to inspect the rest. [117] She was a mural restorer, and a good one, which meant she was paid to be certain. [134] The city's file was certain too: a 1961 mural, one sleeping woman in ochre and cobalt, restore to documented state. [154] She had approached the work the way she approached everything, methodically playful, labeling her solvent jars with riddles and her test squares with the names of chess openings, gridding the wall into a hundred small decisions. [222] Under the half-open eye was a third woman, sitting up. [232] Under that, a fourth, standing, one foot lifted as if she meant to walk out of the plaster entirely. [251] Four layers, four decades apart by the pigments, and the same painter's hand in every one. [267] He had come back again and again to put his waking woman to sleep, one layer at a time, until the city had photographed only the sleeper and called it the original. [299] And in the standing woman's raised palm, pressed into the wet plaster before it set, she had found a nickel. [319] Not a nickel, exactly. [349] Ines's grandfather had carried one like it through the Depression and had explained it to her once, turning it in his stiff fingers: you take five cents that everyone agrees on, and you carve it into a face nobody can price, and you hand it to a stranger, and the stranger owes you nothing but a question. [406] That was the trade, he said. [412] Certainty out, curiosity in.
