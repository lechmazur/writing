The grain elevator talks failed for the third night in a row, and Ines Radek sat on an overturned seed pail at the back of the scale house, listening to two languages break against each other. [36] On one side of the dusty table sat the co-op board, farmers whose grandfathers had raised the elevator in 1911, speaking a slow Plattdeutsch English. [61] On the other sat the elevator crew, most of them from Quetzaltenango by way of Kansas, whose Spanish the hired interpreter flattened into contract clauses. [86] The board wanted to sell to a conglomerate before the roof gave out. [99] The crew wanted shares, so the town they had made their home would not be hauled off in someone else's trucks. [120]

Nobody had asked Ines to be there. [127] She was only the choral librarian, the woman who kept the town choir's scores in the old clock tower and knew which alto would flat on a high E before the alto did. [160] But half of each side sang for her on Thursdays, and she had spent twenty years hearing what people meant underneath what they sang. [184] What she heard now was not greed on either side but the same fear in two tongues: that the place would go on without them. [209]

When old Henrik Voss slapped the table and the interpreter reached for his coat, Ines stood. [225] "Tomorrow, nine o'clock, the tower," she said, in the voice she used to cut off a rehearsal that had gone sour. [246] "Both sides. [248] Bring something heavy." [251]

The tower had been her great-grandfather's. [257] Emil Radek had come from Bohemia with a trunk of gears and built the town an astronomical clock: a blue dial ringed with the zodiac, a gilt sun and a silver moon that crept along their tracks, a star map turning once a day behind glass. [303] It had stopped in the drought year of 1988, when the council sold its two-hundred-pound driving weight for scrap and nobody could afford another. [327] The cable still hung in the shaft, empty. [335]

Ines climbed the stairs before anyone arrived, carrying the only weight she owned: the cast-iron pine cone from her grandmother's cuckoo clock, small enough to close her fist around. [364] She threw open the shutters. [369] The town's streetlights had been cut back to save money, so the only light in the room came down from the real sky and lay across the painted one, the gilt stars borrowing what shine they had from the true ones overhead. [411] She hooked the cuckoo weight to the cable. [419] The escapement did not move. [424]

By nine the room was full and split down the middle, boots on one side, boots on the other, everyone hushed by the strangeness of the starlit dial. [452] Ines did not explain. [456] She sang instead, one line from a Lutheran chorale the Voss family had sung at every funeral since the sod-house years, and Henrik's daughter answered it without meaning to. [485] Then Ines turned and gave the same melody a Spanish line from a Guatemalan song of leaving, and Marisol from the elevator crew caught it and carried it. [513] German, then Spanish, then a phrase of her great-grandfather's Czech, then plain English, one after another, never letting one language sit alone long enough to become a wall. [541] She did not translate the words. [547] She translated the fear, singing Henrik's dread of erasure in Marisol's language and Marisol's in his, until both of them heard their own hearts come back to them in a stranger's mouth. [579]

Then she pointed at the cable, the little pine cone hanging useless at its end. [594] "That is what one of us can lift alone," she said. [605] "The clock needs all of it." [611]

Henrik went first, because he was stubborn enough to hate being second. [623] He hung the brass scale weights from the elevator office beside her pine cone. [637] Marisol added a grain-leg wrench, then a boy from the crew a sack of ball bearings, then a Voss cousin the iron doorstop from the church. [663] The cable groaned, tightened, and somewhere above them a pallet dropped into a tooth and the escapement spoke, once, then again. [684] The silver moon shuddered forward a hair's width on its track. [695] The room made a sound that was not quite a cheer and not quite a hymn. [711]

The talks went on in the tower for six more nights, but they were different talks now, Ines feeding each side a line in the other's language whenever the room began to lean apart. [745] The crew took a third of the co-op. [753] The conglomerate went elsewhere. [757] And when they finally raised the money to rehabilitate the clock properly, Ines refused to take the cuckoo pine cone down, so it hangs there still, the smallest thing on the cable, holding up its share. [793]
