Mira Vell wrote fortunes for a living, and she wrote them badly on purpose. [14] Cryptic, her supervisor corrected, holding up a slip that read the ice still knows your name. [30] People wanted lucky numbers, he said. [36] Mira wanted to be understood by exactly one reader, and that reader was seven years old and thirty years gone. [56]

She was not homesick for a place. [63] The place was still there: the old glass spire on the north shore of Lake Harrow, a weather station abandoned before she was born, its panes gone milky with frost. [93] She was homesick for a year, for one January inside it, when her grandmother had carried her up the spiral stairs to watch the sun come up over the frozen lake. [124] You could not mail a fortune cookie to 1994. [133] She had tried anyway, one slip at a time, for eleven years. [145]

Now she climbed the spire herself, at four in the morning, with a thermos and a broken piece of nautilus shell in her coat pocket. [170] Her grandmother had given it to her that same January, a curved segment showing four chambers in cross-section, each one sealed and pearled and a little larger than the last. [200] The animal only lives in the newest room, her grandmother had said. [212] It closes the door behind it and keeps going. [221] Mira had spent her whole adult life trying to pry one of those doors back open. [237]

She had not slept in thirty-one hours. [244] That was deliberate too. [248] Exhaustion, when it finally hit, would knock her flat for a day, and she wanted to be finished before it arrived, because she knew from experience that what she was about to do could only be done in the thin, ringing clarity right before collapse. [293]

At the top, the room was a cold octagon of glass. [304] She set the shell on the sill facing east and took out her notebook. [318] The method was simple and had never worked. [326] One memory per chamber. [330] The smallest chamber: the smell of her grandmother's coat, wet wool and cloves. [343] The second: her own mitten dropped on the stairs, and the decision not to go back for it. [361] The third: the lake below, white and flat to the edge of the world. [375] The fourth chamber she always left blank, because the fourth was the thing she could not remember, the thing her grandmother had said at the moment the sun came up, and it was the only fortune Mira had ever actually wanted to read. [418]

Every cryptic slip she had written was a guess at it. [429] Eleven years of guesses, folded into cookies, scattered across the country to strangers who wanted lucky numbers. [446] She had thought of them, on her better nights, as a score. [458] Thousands of tiny instructions, opened in restaurants she would never enter, read aloud in voices she would never hear, a symphony performed everywhere and audible nowhere. [484] She had wanted, more than anything, to stand at the front of it and lift her hands. [501]

The dark outside began to thin. [507] Her hands were shaking now, and the letters in the notebook had started to drift, and she understood that she had perhaps twenty minutes before her body took the decision away from her. [540]

Then the sun cleared the far shore. [547] The lake had frozen glass-smooth this year, without snow, and it took the sky and gave it back whole. [566] Two suns rose, one climbing and one sinking to meet it, and the horizon between them was a seam of light so fine it looked like the edge of a folded page. [598]

Mira stopped breathing. [601] The fourth chamber opened. [605] Not the words; the words were still gone. [613] What came back was her grandmother's hand on the back of her neck, turning her head, and the sound of her own small voice asking which one is the real sun, and her grandmother laughing, softly, and not answering. [652]

That was all. [655] There had never been a fortune. [661] There had been a question, and a woman who loved her enough to leave it open. [677]

Mira sat down on the cold floor of the octagon and laughed the same way, once, quietly, and the sound went up into the glass and stayed there. [705] The hush that followed was not the hush of a sealed room. [717] It was the hush before a downbeat, the whole orchestra with its eyes lifted. [731]

She picked up the pen while she still could and wrote one last slip, not for 1994, not for anyone she knew. [753] Which one is the real sun? [759] Then, beneath it, for the supervisor: Lucky numbers 4, 7, 1994. [770]

Exhaustion arrived a minute later, gentle as a hand on the neck, and she let it turn her head toward the light. [792]
