Vesna Holt inked constellations for a living, but only at night. [11] By day she worked in the Laboratory of Lost Sounds, a basement of coiled cables and padded walls where technicians coaxed voices out of cracked wax and rotting tape. [40] The two jobs were the same job, she thought: fixing what had fallen into people, so they could look at it without flinching. [63]

The writing desk arrived on a Tuesday, donated by an estate that no longer wanted it. [79] Taped inside the top drawer was a baby monitor from the late nineties, cream plastic gone the color of old teeth. [100] A label on the back promised it translated baby cries into words: HUNGRY, COLD, HELD. [115] The donor's note said only that the firmware was haunted. [125]

Vesna's supervisor laughed and assigned it to her, because she was the one who never laughed. [141]

She powered it with a bench supply and tethered its output to the lab's spectral display, the way she tethered every unknown sound before trusting it. [167] There was no microphone attached. [172] There was no baby. [176] The screen filled with a cry anyway. [183] The little display blinked HELD, then unblinked it, then settled on a word the manual had never listed: BACK. [202]

Vesna sat very still. [206]

Twenty-two years earlier she had been nineteen, with a daughter three days old and nothing else. [222] She had signed the papers in a room that smelled of coffee, and she had tucked into the blanket the one thing she owned worth anything: a thumb of amber with a mosquito suspended inside, wings open, forever about to leave. [263] Then she had walked out and learned to put stars on strangers, because stars were the only things she trusted to stay where you left them. [289]

The guilt had never been loud. [295] It was a dissonance, two notes a hair apart, a hum under every quiet room she had ever entered. [314] The lab had been a mercy for that reason; here, noise was the point. [328]

She pulled the desk apart looking for a hidden speaker. [338] Instead the false bottom of the middle drawer gave way to a bundle of letters, handwritten, never mailed, all beginning the same way: To the woman with the stars on her wrists. [370]

The adoptive mother had written one each year. [378] She described colic, first steps, a fear of thunder, a child who fell asleep clutching a piece of amber and asked what the insect was waiting for. [405] The seventh letter said the monitor had begun to speak when no one was crying. [420] The twelfth said they had stopped being frightened of it. [430] The last, dated that spring, said the daughter was grown and gone to study the sky, and that the writer was dying, and that she had come to believe the monitor was not translating the child at all. [468] "I think it hears you," she wrote. [475] "I think it always has." [480]

Vesna looked at the spectral display. [486] The cry on the screen was not an infant's. [495] Slowed and stretched, it had the shape of a woman weeping in a room that smelled of coffee, twenty-two years ago, trying not to make a sound. [522]

Time did not pass in the laboratory so much as fold, old sounds pressed against new ones until the layers touched. [543] The firmware had caught her, the way the amber had caught the mosquito, and had been repeating her ever since to a house that had learned to love the noise. [573]

She understood then why the display said BACK. [581] It was not an accusation. [586] It was a translation. [590]

She put her hand flat on the monitor, and the hum she had carried for twenty-two years slid, for the first time, into tune. [614] Not silence; the lab did not deal in silence. [623] Something softer, like a meteor seen from a dark field: a brief bright line, and then a hush that is not emptiness but the sky closing gently over where it went. [654]

The last letter had an address on the back, an observatory three hours north. [668] Vesna copied it onto her wrist, between the stars, where she would not lose it. [683] Then she unplugged the monitor and let the old cry go quiet, because it had finished saying what it had waited twenty-two years to say, and because someone, somewhere, might finally be ready to hear the rest in person. [722]
