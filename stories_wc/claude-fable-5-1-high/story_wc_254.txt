The ban lifted on the first evening the ice began to speak. [12] Ilse heard the announcement the way she heard everything that winter, through the wall of her rented room, muffled, secondhand, already fading. [34] For eleven years no one had been permitted to paraphrase the Founding Verses. [47] You could recite them, exactly, in the exact order the quartz hummed them. [60] You could not say them otherwise. [66] So Ilse had become a cloud-shadow poet, because clouds were the one thing the Council had forgotten to make sacred. [86] She wrote about the gray shapes that crossed the frozen lake, how they slid over the ice without touching it, how they were the only things in the city allowed to move freely across a surface. [122] Everyone understood what she meant. [127] No one could prove it. [132]

Now she walked to the atrium with a lantern, though the ban was gone and she no longer needed the excuse of darkness. [155] The building stood at the lake's edge, a great cobalt shell of glass so deep in color that the frozen water beyond it looked bruised. [180] Inside, the colonnade waited. [184] Forty pillars of quartz, each as wide as a doorway, each humming its assigned line of the Verses in a tone just below hearing, so that the whole atrium vibrated faintly in the teeth. [218] For eleven years the faithful had walked around the pillars. [228] Never between them. [231] The gaps were sacred; the gaps were where the words lived. [242]

Ilse had never cared about the politics of it. [251] That was the part her friends in the movement never understood. [262] She did not want freedom in the abstract. [270] She wanted the Verses to be beautiful again, and they could not be beautiful while they were frozen. [288] A poem repeated exactly is a poem dying slowly, one recitation at a time, until only the shape is left and the meaning has drained through the floor. [316]

Moths had found the atrium before her. [323] They came up from the lake reeds every thaw, and now they circled her lantern in a soft brown hush, their wings ticking against the glass like a clock that had forgotten how to count. [358] She set the lantern down. [363] She stood before the first gap. [369]

The pillar on her left hummed the opening line: We came to the water and the water held us. [388] The pillar on her right hummed the second: We built our house upon the holding. [403] She had recited those lines ten thousand times, exactly, and they had come to mean nothing more than the taste of cold air. [426]

Ilse stepped between them and spoke. [432] "We arrived at the lake," she said, "and the ice did not break beneath us, so we mistook the ice for a promise." [455]

The hum faltered. [458] Not stopped, faltered, the way a singer falters when someone joins in a half step off, and then must decide whether to hold the note or bend toward the stranger. [488] The quartz bent. [491] She felt it in her feet first, a tremor running up through the cobalt floor, and she understood it was not the building shaking but her own certainty arriving, late and physical, like a train she had stopped waiting for years ago. [533]

She walked to the next gap. [539] "We put up walls," she said, "and called the walls a home, and called the home a law." [557]

The edges of the pillars began to soften in the lantern light, not melting but blurring, the way a word blurs when you say it too many times or, she thought, exactly the right number of times. [594] The hum of one line leaked into the hum of the next. [606] The boundary between the third pillar and the fourth dissolved into a chord. [619] Beyond the glass, the frozen lake answered with a long groan, the sound it made every year when the ice remembered it was only water holding a pose. [647]

Ilse went through all forty gaps. [653] She did not hurry. [657] At each one she spoke the line beside her in words that were not the Verses and were entirely the Verses, and at each one the quartz gave a little, and the moths followed her lantern from column to column in their hush, and the floor kept trembling with the thing she was finally sure of. [713] By the last gap the colonnade no longer hummed forty lines. [724] It hummed one long sound, unfinished, open at both ends, waiting for whoever came next to walk through it and say it differently. [747]

Ilse picked up her lantern. [752] Outside, the first cloud of the evening moved across the lake, and for once its shadow found no ice to slide over, only dark water, and so it simply sank in and was absorbed. [786] She would have to find something else to write about. [796]
