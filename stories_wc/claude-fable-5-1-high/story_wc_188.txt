Ilse Varga came to the fortress of Harrow Bend at dusk, chanting under her breath the way she always did when she walked. [23] "Three seconds lift, two seconds break, one second bloom." [32] The chant was not superstition. [37] It was fuse arithmetic, the timing of every shell she had ever built, and it kept her hands from shaking on roads where a fireworks designer with a cart of powder was welcomed or chased depending on the last thing that had burned. [80]

Harrow Bend had bloomed. [84] Nobody had pruned the walls in years, and the trumpet vine had taken the battlements, orange flowers spilling over stone meant to stop armies. [108] It was beautiful the way abandoned things are beautiful, which is to say it made her want to argue with it. [129]

The gate stood open. [133] Inside, people moved slowly through the courtyard with their eyes on the ground, carrying water, carrying bread, carrying nothing at all with the same bent care. [159] Ilse knew the posture. [163] She had seen it in a dozen towns after a dozen bad years: hopelessness has its own gravity, and it pulls the chin down first. [188]

"The harvest failed twice," said a woman filling a bucket, without being asked. [201] "Then the fever." [204] "Then the sky." [207] She nodded upward. [210] Clouds lay over Harrow Bend like a lid. [218] "Seven weeks, no stars." [222] "People stopped looking." [225]

Ilse did not look up either, but not for the same reason. [237] A designer of fireworks learns early that you cannot watch a sky by staring at one point in it. [256] You soften your eyes and let the whole field come to you, and you filter what matters out of everything else: the shell that hangs too long, the wind that shifts, the child in the crowd who is about to run. [297] She walked the courtyard that way now, chanting softly, letting the fortress arrive at the edges of her attention. [316]

Which is how she smelled the kerosene before she saw it. [327] Near the old well lay a knot of rag, tight and blackened, the kind a fire spinner ties onto the head of a staff and soaks before a show. [356] Someone had performed here once. [361] Someone had left this behind, still damp, still stinking of intention. [372]

She crouched, and the ground beneath the rag caught the last of the dusk. [386] The courtyard was tiled in mosaic, thousands of chips of glazed clay in browns and dull golds that everyone had walked across for a century without reading. [413] Stared at directly, it was pattern. [419] Taken sideways, softly, the way she took skies, it was a sentence. [431] Slightly paler tiles, scattered so no single step would reveal them, spelled across the whole courtyard: WHEN THE SKY GOES DARK, WE ARE THE SKY. [456]

Ilse laughed out loud, which made three people flinch. [465] Some long-dead mason had hidden a note where only the peripheral would find it, and some fire spinner, who knew what it meant to make light with your own arms, had found it and marked it with the tools of the trade, and then, for whatever reason, had gone. [514] The rag was a baton passed to nobody in particular. [524] She picked it up. [528]

"I need the well square cleared," she told the woman with the bucket. [541] "And I need everyone who has stopped looking up to stand somewhere they can fail to look up from." [560]

"You're mad." [562]

"Professionally," Ilse agreed. [565]

Night came down without a single star, the hush so total that the trumpet vines seemed to hold their breath. [585] To a fireworks designer, a starless night is not a curse. [596] It is a clean sheet. [601]

She set her mortars along the mosaic's hidden line, so that anyone who later found the note would know it had been answered. [624] She unknotted a strip of the spinner's rag, threaded it to the master fuse, and touched a coal to it. [644]

"Three seconds lift," she chanted, walking backward. [651] The first shell went up with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house. [667] "Two seconds break." [670] Chins lifted, against their own weight, against seven weeks of practice. [681] "One second bloom." [684]

Gold broke over Harrow Bend and hung there, longer than physics should have allowed, longer than anyone expected, and then a second shell, and a third, orange to match the flowers on the walls. [718] Ilse did not watch the sky. [724] She watched the courtyard at the edges of her eyes, filtering, and found what mattered: a hundred faces tipped back, a hundred mouths open, the woman with the bucket weeping and not bothering to hide it. [760]

Everything falls. [762] Every shell she had ever built came down as ash. [772] That was the joke, and it was a serious one: you fire anyway, and for one second the gravity loses. [792]
