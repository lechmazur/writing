Ines Farrow had spent twenty-two years correcting other people's rivers. [10] She had moved a delta three millimeters south, renamed a tributary that never agreed to its name, and once deleted an entire lake because a surveyor had been drunk in 1911. [41] "Cartography," she liked to say, "is the art of being confidently wrong at scale." [55] Nobody at the atlas house laughed, which she took as agreement. [66]

What nobody at the atlas house knew was the thing she had noticed in her ninth year of editing. [85] Trace any river on the northern plates backward, upstream, against its own arrows, and it did not scatter into a hundred polite springs the way the textbooks promised. [113] It bent. [115] Every one of them bent, in the end, toward the same blank oval at the top of sheet forty-one, a lake the old surveyors had labeled only with a question mark and a note: outpost, iron, abandoned. [152]

So she had come, with a borrowed coat and a dead man's phrasebook. [165]

The phrasebook had belonged to Teodor, the house polyglot, who could argue in eleven languages and apologize in nine. [184] He had dog-eared a single page before the fever took him, and the fold pointed to a phrase in the lake dialect that he had underlined twice: they do not begin here, they return. [218] Beneath it, in his cramped hand: ask after the falcon. [228]

The outpost was exactly as promised, iron gone the color of dried blood, a stove that coughed, one window. [247] The keeper was a woman named Sil who did not ask why an editor had walked eleven days into the cold, which Ines found either restful or alarming, and decided to feel both. [280]

"Falcon?" Ines asked, holding up the phrasebook. [287]

Sil glanced at the fold and nodded toward the white nothing of the lake. [301] "She hunts the far shore in winter," Sil said. [310] "When she comes back, the ice is done thinking." [319]

Ines waited four days. [323] She had never been good at waiting, so she edited instead, sketching the shoreline from the window and finding it did not match any version she had ever printed. [352] Of course it didn't. [356] She was subtly, almost embarrassingly confident about that. [364] Being wrong about a place while standing in it was a novelty she intended to enjoy. [380]

The lake made no sound at all. [387] That was the part that worked on her. [395] Snow should creak, ice should groan, wind should have opinions. [405] Instead there was a tranquility so complete that she began to hear her own pulse as an intrusion, and she found herself lowering her voice even to say good morning to the stove. [438]

On the fifth morning a shape came out of the flat light, low and fast, and landed on the rail outside the window. [461] The falcon. [463] She folded herself like a closed letter and looked at Ines with the flat patience of something that had already read the last page. [487]

"Now," Sil said, and did not point at the bird. [497] She pointed at the glass. [502]

The window had fogged overnight, as it always did, from the stove and two women breathing. [518] But the condensation was not sitting the way condensation sat. [528] It ran. [530] Dozens of fine beads slid inward from every edge of the pane, from the corners, from the top and bottom alike, ignoring gravity with a serenity Ines found deeply rude, and they gathered toward a single point just left of center. [571] Not a drip. [574] A convergence. [576]

She held her breath and the pattern did not change. [586] Sil breathed and the pattern did not change. [594] The glass was showing her a map, drawn in the one medium no atlas house would accept, and it was showing her the same thing sheet forty-one had been whispering for thirteen years. [627]

"They don't begin," Ines said slowly, tasting Teodor's phrase. [636] "They return." [638]

"Every river comes home before it leaves," Sil said, with the calm of someone repeating a weather report. [656] "The falcon heralds it." [660] "You have until the beads reach the middle." [668]

"To do what?" [671]

Sil shrugged. [673] "To see the lake open, or not to." [681]

Ines looked at the point where the water was gathering. [691] It was very small and very certain, and beneath it, through the thinning fog, the surface of the lake had begun, silently, to darken. [715]

She thought of every river she had ever moved with a pencil. [727] She thought that she had, perhaps, been correcting them in the wrong direction the whole time. [743]

"Well," she said, and reached for her coat, "I suppose the atlas will need a new sheet." [760]

Her hands were entirely steady. [765] That worried her more than anything. [771]
