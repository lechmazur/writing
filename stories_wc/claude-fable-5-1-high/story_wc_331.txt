The Halvorsen Library of Scientific Verse opened at four in the morning because its founder believed that equations and poems were both best read before the mind had finished waking. [30] Ilse Marrow kept those hours. [35] She arrived at half past three, unlocked the brass doors, and switched on the lamps one shelf at a time so the building would wake gently, the way a patient does. [66] The regulars called her eccentric, and she agreed, with the reservation that she was eccentric in a sane way: she talked to the books, but only when she had something to say. [98]

The library held no digital catalog. [104] Halvorsen had insisted on analog memory: card indexes typed on a manual machine, reel tapes of poets reading their work on the Krebs cycle and the tides, marginalia in pencil that faded exactly as fast as the hands that made it. [145] "A memory should wear," she had written in the founding charter. [156] "Otherwise how would you know you had used it?" [165]

Ilse had never met her. [170] What she had was the scarf. [176] It had come with the position, folded in the top drawer of the desk: gray silk, tattered at both ends, its hem worked into ruffles so dense they folded back on themselves like the gills of a mushroom. [214] A note pinned to it read: Where do stories live? [224]

For six years Ilse had taken the question as a riddle from a dead woman, the kind of thing one keeps in a drawer. [248] Then, on a Tuesday in November, a graduate student left a crocheted model of the hyperbolic plane on the reading table, and Ilse, clearing it away, saw that it was the scarf. [280] Not the same object, but the same shape: a surface that grew faster than the space around it could hold, so that its edge had nowhere to go but inward, into pleats. [312]

She sat down with the scarf across her knees while the morning patrons drifted in. [327] Nobody at the Halvorsen asked for help before seven. [336] They browsed the way people walk through a cemetery, reading whatever they passed, and Ilse was part of the furniture they passed. [358] A man in a wet coat glanced at her and away. [369] Two students whispered near the astronomy sonnets. [376] She was alone in the way one is alone in a room full of readers, which is the most complete solitude and the most watched. [401]

In hyperbolic geometry, she remembered from a poem shelved under Lobachevsky, parallel lines multiply. [415] Through any point outside a line run infinitely many lines that never meet it. [429] The plane cannot be flattened without tearing. [436] She looked at the tattered ends of the scarf and understood that Halvorsen had tried. [451] Someone had pressed this fabric flat, once, and it had split rather than lie. [465]

So Ilse did the opposite. [470] She let the pleats fall open, following each fold the way a finger follows a line of verse, and inside the seventh ruffle, sewn into the silk itself, she found writing. [501] Not ink. [503] Thread. [504] Tiny stitched letters, one poem per fold, each fold branching into two more, so that the scarf contained more poems than its length should have allowed. [530]

It was a catalog. [534] Halvorsen's own, the one she had never typed onto cards: which poems she had loved, in which order, and what she had been doing when she read them. [562] Reading Bohr in verse while her husband slept. [570] Reading a sonnet on entropy the week the diagnosis came. [580] The stitches were uneven, some of them pulled loose by years, and Ilse realized that this too was the point. [600] A memory should wear. [604]

Stories did not live in the books. [611] They lived in the space between a reader and a page, a space that expanded faster than any shelf could hold, that pleated back on itself, that could not be flattened into a database without tearing. [647] Halvorsen had known it and had sewn the proof into the one thing she wore against her throat, where her voice was. [669]

Ilse stood. [671] The man in the wet coat was gone; the students had moved to the geology section. [687] No one had watched her make the discovery, and she found that fitting, because the discovery was that no one could. [708] Analog memory could not be backed up, only reawakened, and only by hand. [721] She went to the reel tapes and threaded the oldest one, labeled only H., and turned the volume low. [740] A woman's voice, roughened by tape and time, began to read a poem about the curvature of space. [758] Ilse wound the scarf around her own neck, pleats and tears and all, and went back to the desk to wait for seven o'clock, when someone would finally ask her where to find something, and she would know. [796]
