The arbor at Hallam Spring had been an apple orchard once, before the water came up hot and heavy with silica and turned every trunk to quartz. [27] Now the trees stood white and branching in a mist that never lifted, and Ilse Varga read frost for the tourists who parked at its edge. [53] She would have told you she was a mystic, and she would have been lying, and it would also have been true. [75]

A car left overnight in the mist woke with its windows furred in ice, and Ilse asked the owner to lay a hand flat against the glass and think of what they had lost. [109] Then she communed with the frost. [115] What she actually read was the person: the tremor at the wrist, the way a jaw set before a name, the coat sleeve worn shiny from a habit of rubbing one thumb over a ring that was no longer there. [155] She was attentive the way a hawk is attentive. [164] Then she said what the frost said, and the frost was never wrong, because Ilse was never wrong. [182]

She did not think of it as fraud. [190] She thought of it as the scaffolding around a true building. [201] Ilse believed, with the whole of her, that objects absorbed emotion the way the orchard had absorbed the spring. [220] Grief soaked into a chair. [225] Joy stayed in a spoon. [230] She had spent twenty years unable to prove it, and so she had settled for the lie that got people close enough to feel it for themselves. [257]

The surveyor arrived in November. [262] His name was Dale Okafor, and he parked a mud-colored truck under the largest quartz tree and told her, before she could speak, that he did not believe in any of it. [294] He measured bedrock for a living. [300] He knew what held and what shifted, and he was here only because his daughter had begged. [317] Then he took a glass prism from his coat pocket and set it on the hood of the truck. [336]

"My wife hung it in the kitchen window," he said. [346] "Rainbows on the ceiling every morning. [352] Our girl thinks it still has her in it." [361] He said this the way a man reports a crack in a foundation. [374]

Ilse watched him. [377] Steady hands. [379] No ring, but no worn sleeve either, because he was not the kind to rub at things. [396] He had put his grief somewhere firmly grounded and built on top of it. [410] She already knew what she would tell him. [418]

She asked him to leave the truck overnight. [426] In the morning the windshield was white, and the mist hung between the quartz branches like breath that had forgotten to leave. [448] Ilse did something she had never done. [455] Instead of Dale's hand, she pressed the prism against the glass, held it there with her own palm, and asked him to say his wife's name. [481]

"Marisol," he said. [484]

She lifted the prism. [488] Beneath it the frost should have melted to a blank oval, the shape of warm glass. [504] Instead the ice had regrown, fast and fine, in a pattern of interlocking fans. [518] Ilse opened her mouth to name it: a garden, a gate, whatever he needed. [532]

Dale spoke first. [535] "That's the ceiling." [538] His voice had gone strange. [543] "That's what it threw on the ceiling. [550] The exact spread." [553]

The hush in the arbor shifted. [559] It had always been a heavy, wet silence, the mist pressing sound flat against the ground. [575] Now it thinned and rose, the way a room goes quiet when someone has just entered it. [592] Ilse felt the quartz trees listening. [598]

She had spent twenty years building a lie around a truth she could not reach, certain the lie was hers and the truth was theirs. [623] That assumption shattered cleanly, like ice off a wiper blade. [633] The frost had never needed her. [639] It had only needed something that had been loved long enough to remember the shape of it. [656]

Dale stood with both boots planted, as if the parking lot might tilt. [669] "Tell me how you did that," he said. [677] It was a man asking to be lied to, so he could keep his footing. [692]

Ilse looked at the fans of ice, already softening at the edges. [704] She could give him the trick. [710] Instead she told him the only thing she had ever wanted to say to anyone. [725]

"I didn't," she said. [729] "She did." [731]

He would decide later that it was a con. [740] She knew that; she read it in the set of his shoulders as he took the prism back and closed his fist around it, gently, the way you hold a hand. [771] But he would hang it in the window again. [780] And that, Ilse thought, was the truest lie she had ever told. [792]
