The observatory had lost its roof to a storm two winters back, and Ines decided this counted as an improvement. [20] Stars came in now without an appointment, and the wind off the cliff had started using the place as a throat. [41] She had come to clear out her brother's things and had gotten as far as his notebook. [58] It was open to the last few pages, and she had promised herself she would leave when she closed it. [78]

Ines busted myths for a living. [84] Twelve seasons of a show that proved lightning did not strike the wicked and that grandmothers could not smell rain. [104] Teo had watched every episode and called afterward to explain, gently, that she had missed the point. [121] The point, according to Teo, was that the universe was asking one question in a thousand accents, and every myth was a bad translation of it. [147] He had bought this dead observatory to listen for the original. [158]

The last pages of the notebook were mostly about the cello. [169] It stood in the corner where the telescope used to be, a body and a neck and two strings, the rest of it still a promise. [195] Balanced on its bridge sat a seashell painted in blue and gold, the paint chipped where a thumb had worried it. [216] Teo's handwriting called it the compass. [222] "It only points while the note lasts," he had written, "which is why nobody trusts it, which is why it is honest." [244]

Ines laughed, because the alternative was less useful. [252]

She had a habit she never mentioned on the show. [262] Whenever a conversation ended badly, she went back and rebuilt it, gave herself better lines, gave the other person the grace to hear them. [286] She had rebuilt her last call with Teo forty times. [296] In the real version she had said that a man who could tune a cello should be able to tune his own expectations, and hung up. [322] In the rebuilt versions she asked what the question was. [332] He never got to answer, because she was the one writing him, and she did not know. [349]

The notebook's instructions were simple and absurd, her favorite combination. [359] Bow the low string. [363] Hum the conversation you cannot fix into the body of the instrument. [375] Watch the shell. [378]

She picked up the bow, and the wind, as if cued, leaned in through the broken dome. [395] The string gave a note like a door opening in a house she had grown up in. [412] The half-finished body took it and made it larger, and Ines, feeling foolish and not minding, began to retell the call. [433] Not the rebuilt one. [437] The real one, hummed low against the ribs of the cello, her cruel line and his silence and the click. [457]

The shell began to shiver on the bridge. [465] It turned, the way a compass needle turns when it is deciding, and settled with its painted lip pointing not at the sea, not at the sky, but at her. [495] Then the note ran out of bow, and the shell was a shell again. [509]

She knew what a myth buster would say. [517] Resonance, a curved base, a tilted floor, a woman who had not slept properly since the funeral. [534] She said all of it aloud to the empty dome, and it sounded thin, and she was cheered by how thin it sounded. [557]

She bowed again and this time hummed a rebuilt version, the kind one, where she asked the question. [575] The shell turned toward the gap in the roof, toward a sky that had gotten in without knocking, and held there exactly as long as the hum did. [603]

Ines understood then, or decided to, which on a cliff at night amounts to the same thing. [620] The riddle was not a sentence waiting to be decoded. [630] It was a direction that existed only while someone was making a sound toward it, and the moment you stopped to check your answer, the needle went slack. [658] Teo had not been listening for the question. [666] He had been trying to stay inside it. [674] Bad news for a show about answers. [681] She thought that was very funny, and she cried a little, and neither activity got in the way of the other. [702]

On the last page Teo had written one line: "Finish it or don't, but keep humming." [718]

She put the painted shell in her coat pocket, where it would point at nothing until she needed it to. [738] She lifted the cello, which weighed about as much as an unfinished argument, and turned toward the stairs. [756] Then she closed the notebook, and the pages whispered shut, and the wind through the ruined dome hummed a note she was almost sure was pointing somewhere. [783]
