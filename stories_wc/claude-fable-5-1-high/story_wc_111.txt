The meters came to Hollin House the same spring the frost took Ysolde's last good vine. [16] Everyone wore one now, a brass dial on a cord, and the Ministry sent assessors to read the land the way a doctor reads a pulse. [42] Low land was sold. [46] Ysolde was seventy-eight and had the look of her own cordons in January, all knuckle and grey bark, and she did not believe in luck. [71] She believed in numbers, though, because numbers were what the Ministry believed in, and a pragmatic woman argues with the weather she has. [94]

The assessor's dial read the yew maze at four. [103] Four was condemnation. [106] So Ysolde offered to walk him through it. [114] She took the left turn where the hornbeam leaned, the right where the soil went sandy under her boots, and the young man's needle climbed with every corner: nine, fourteen, twenty-two. [145] At the heart of the maze, where the marbles were, it read thirty-one. [158] He wrote lucky on his form. [164] She let him. [167]

There were forty-three marbles pressed into the clay at the maze's center, and every one of them was cracked. [186] Ysolde did not do small victories quietly. [193] When the north-wall grape set fruit for the first time, she had cut a niche in the yew, lit a candle in a jar, read aloud the date and the weather and the name of the vine, and set a green marble in the ground with the ceremony other people reserve for christenings. [246] One for the winter the roses lived. [253] One for the year the blight stopped at the third row. [264] The frost heaved them every February and split them, and every March she dug them up, polished the glass with a rag and spit until the cracks shone like veins in a leaf, and pressed them back. [301] Forty years of that, and the center of the maze glittered like a broken sky. [316]

Maren, who owned Hollin now, came out with the form and a buyer's letter. [330] "Thirty-one," she said. [333] "A shipping firm wants lucky ground for its offices." [342] "They will pay for the whole estate on the strength of the maze." [355]

Ysolde had known this would come, and had spent three nights deciding what a pragmatic woman does with a lie that has grown legs. [379] "Walk in alone," she said. [384] "Take your dial." [387]

Maren went. [389] Ysolde sat on the bench by the entrance and listened to the estate's owner take wrong turns for forty minutes, her footsteps growing sharper, the yew swallowing her curses. [418] When Maren came out, scratched and furious, her needle rested at three. [430]

"The maze is not lucky," Ysolde said. [437] "I am not lucky either." [442] "The dials measure how often things go your way, and in there, things go my way, because I have spent forty years learning which way they go." [469] "The assessor read me, not the ground." [476] "Sell it as lucky and your shipping men will get lost in it until they tear it out." [494]

Maren stared at her. [498] "You lied to the Ministry." [503]

"I let a boy misread a dial." [510] "Now I am unletting it." [515] "Gardens know their gardeners, and this one does not know you yet." [527] "That is the only true thing on that form, and I would like to prove it before I go where the vine went." [550]

Maren was her mother's daughter, which meant she was angry for a full minute and then practical. [567] "Teach me, then." [570]

They went in together every morning of that summer. [579] Ysolde showed her the leaning hornbeam and the sandy patch, the place where the roots had lifted the path a finger's width, the corner where the light came green at ten and gold at four. [614] She taught her the marbles, one by one, each with its date and weather and name, until Maren could recite the forty-three victories like a rosary. [640]

Ysolde's hands gave out in August, and her breath in September, and by then Maren's dial read nineteen at the center, alone. [662] She sent the shipping firm a letter that said, honestly, that the maze was not lucky and was not for sale. [683]

In October the north-wall grape, dead by any sane reckoning, threw one green cane from the base of its grey stump. [704] Maren cut a niche in the yew. [711] She lit a candle in a jar, read aloud the date and the weather and the name of the vine, and polished a cracked amber marble with a rag and spit until its split shone. [746] Then she pressed it into the clay, the forty-fourth, and walked out of the maze without one wrong turn. [765]
