Mira had not slept through a night in eleven years, so she had stopped counting nights and started counting characters. [20] The rooftop greenhouse she rented for less than a parking space held forty-one panes of glass, and by the full moon she meant to have a word on every one. [50] Tonight the moon was nine days old, a lopsided lantern hung in the smog, and the smog turned its light the color of steeped saffron. [75] Mira was a painter by trade, so she noticed the color before anything else. [89] Below her the city coughed in its sleep. [97] Up here, above the exhaust line, it was quiet enough to hear the tomato vines drink. [113]

She unrolled the cloth case and took out her grandmother's brush. [124] It was a calligrapher's brush, goat hair bound to a bamboo handle, and its tip had been trimmed by some patient hand into a crescent, so that every stroke began with a small moon. [158] Her grandmother had written a script that no living person could read anymore, not even Mira. [174] It had belonged to the women of a river valley that was now a reservoir, and they had used it for one purpose only: notes folded around food and pressed into the hands of strangers during the hungry years. [213] Eat slowly. [215] You are not forgotten. [219] Walk another day. [222] Her grandmother had said the strangers never learned the words, but they kept the papers anyway, sometimes for decades, because a body remembers the shape of a kindness even when the mind cannot spell it. [257] Mira had nodded as a child and doubted as an adult. [268] Now she wanted proof, and she had decided to get it the way she did everything: one pane at a time. [289]

The trouble was that she had never been taught the script. [300] She had only watched it being made, at six and seven, half asleep on a stool while her grandmother's wrist rose and fell. [323] She could not rouse the memory by chasing it in daylight. [334] It came only like this, when she sat unsleeping under the moon and let her mind drift, and the saffron light on the glass began to move like a wrist. [364] Insomnia had taken her sleep, but it had left her the daydream, and the daydream was where the lost words lived. [385]

She wet the brush, tipped it in ink, and waited. [395] Somewhere between the ninth breath and the tenth, a character surfaced: three strokes leaning on a fourth, like people holding up a roof. [418] She did not know what it meant. [425] She painted it on pane thirty-two before it could sink again. [436] Then she wrote the date and the moon's age in her notebook, as she had every night, because hope without a ledger was only wishing. [461]

The stairwell door scraped. [465] Mr. Adjei from the sixth floor came out onto the roof in his slippers, carrying a bucket for the water tank, which he checked at three in the morning because he too had stopped sleeping. [500] They had nodded to each other for two years and never spoken. [512] He paused at the greenhouse and looked at the glass, where the moon lit thirty-two black words from behind. [531] He set down the bucket. [536] He walked in without asking, which he had never done, and stopped before pane thirty-two. [551]

"I had this," he said. [556] His finger hovered over the three strokes and the fourth. [566] "On a paper. [569] Around a rice cake. [573] I was eight, at the bus depot, and a woman I never saw again put it in my coat." [592] He traced the shape in the air, exactly, roof and all. [603] "I kept it until it fell apart. [610] I never knew what it said." [616]

Mira found that her hands were shaking, and she made herself pick up the notebook and write it down: night nine, pane thirty-two, one witness. [641] "Neither do I," she said. [646] "But I think it says something like, walk another day." [656]

Mr. Adjei was quiet for a while in the saffron light. [667] Then he asked if she needed the water tank checked, because the tomatoes looked dry, and she said yes, and he said he would come back tomorrow, when the moon was ten. [699]

Nine panes remained. [702] Mira wet the brush again, let the light pool on the glass, and waited for the next word to rise. [722] She had six nights, and now she had a reason to believe there would be more strangers, and that some of them, roused by nothing but a shape on a window, would remember. [755]
