After the story famine, Ilse copied music because music was the only thing left that no one had bothered to starve. [21] The Ministry had taken the novels first, then the folk tales, then the anecdotes people told at bus stops, until whole towns spoke only in weather and prices. [49] Symphonies had no plot, the inspectors decided, and so the orchestra was allowed to keep its parts, and Ilse was allowed to keep her pens. [74]

She was diligent in the way of people who have decided to be dangerous slowly. [89] Every viola part left her desk perfect, every rest counted twice, and every night she walked the copies out of town to the hillside where the old brick kilns had been. [120]

One kiln had never quite gone out. [127] Its mouth was an alcove now, knee-deep in ember that no one could explain and no one came to inspect, because the valley mist lay over it like a lid. [157] Inside, the warmth was constant and the light was orange and low, and against the back wall sat a shortwave transmitter she had assembled from three broken radios and a kettle. [188] The dial glowed a thin green when she turned it on, the only color in the alcove that was not fire. [209] She thought of it as the eye of someone listening. [219]

The broadcast went out at eleven, unlicensed, unannounced, on a frequency the Ministry had abandoned as noise. [236] Ilse did not read stories over the air. [244] Stories were what the detectors were tuned for: a name, a then, a therefore. [258] Instead she sang. [261] Her voice was ordinary, a copyist's voice, careful and unremarkable, and she used it to carry lyrics that never quite landed on meaning. [284] A girl with a lantern, a bridge, a coat left on a chair. [297] Nothing happened in any single verse. [303] But a listener who kept a notebook, who caught Tuesday's bridge and Friday's coat and the lantern from the week before, would find that the fragments locked together, and that the girl had crossed the bridge and left her coat because she was not coming back. [349] The story was cloaked in the song the way the alcove was cloaked in mist: present, warm, and invisible to anyone not already standing inside it. [375]

The kiln was where the Ministry had burned the libraries. [385] Ilse had watched from the road, years ago, and had understood then that the ember would not go out, because paper that has been read holds heat longer than paper that has not. [418] That was not science. [422] It was simply what she had decided to believe, and she was a woman who copied thirty-two bars of rest without skipping one, so her beliefs tended to hold. [451]

The ash on the alcove floor was fine and grey and, she had discovered, secretly luminous. [467] In daylight it was nothing. [472] Under the green of the dial, or any small light in a large dark, it gave back a faint answering shine, as if the dust remembered being pages and was waiting to be asked. [506]

So she asked. [509] Each night, before the broadcast, she stirred a pinch of it into her copying ink. [524] The orchestra parts went back to town with their notes exact in black ink and their margins lettered in ash, invisible, and in the pit, when the house lights fell and the stand lamps threw their small pools, the players began to see faint lines waking at the edges of the page. [576] A bridge. [578] A coat. [580] A lantern. [582] The same fragments the radio carried, so that a second violinist who had never owned a shortwave set found herself humming a tune she did not know and grieving a girl she had never been told about. [619]

Ilse did not want to be a hero. [627] Heroes had names and thens and therefores, and were easy to detect. [639] She wanted only to teach the dust to tell stories again, since it had been stories once, and since she suspected that anything which had been a story did not forget how. [671] It only needed a warm place, a little light, and someone patient enough to stir. [686]

There was a calm in the alcove that she did not trust entirely, a calm with thistles in it. [705] Every night the mist could thin. [711] Every night a detector van could decide that noise had a shape. [723] She sang anyway, sitting cross-legged in the ember light, the green dial watching, the ash on her fingers faintly answering. [743] Tonight's verse was about a door. [749] It did not say who opened it. [756] Somewhere in town, a notebook was finding out. [764]
