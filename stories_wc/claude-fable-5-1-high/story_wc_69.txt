Marit Halvorsen had taught choirs in eleven countries, and she had learned that the first rehearsal always sounds like an argument. [21] This one, in the reading room of the Lindqvist Library, sounded like a war that had only recently gone quiet. [41] The library had been closed for nine years, since the valley split into two towns that shared a river and nothing else. [63] Now the council wanted it reopened with a concert, and they wanted the choir drawn from both banks. [81] They had hired Marit because she was from nowhere near here. [92]

She arrived in October, when the maples along the road had gone the color of the library's chairs. [110] Everything inside was upholstered in a red velvet that had faded toward rust, and dust rose from it whenever anyone sat down, so that the room seemed to exhale each time it was disturbed. [144]

Twenty-two singers came. [147] Eleven sat on the east side of the aisle and eleven on the west, and nobody had assigned them. [166] Marit did not comment. [170] She had the manner of someone who had crossed too many borders to be surprised by one more, a stillness that was not peace so much as the residue of long travel. [202] Privately she expected the project to fail, and she had already forgiven it for failing. [217]

"Sing me one note," she said. [223] "Any note. [225] Just hold it." [228]

What came back was not a note but a weather system: twenty-two pitches, some of them chosen out of spite. [248]

"Good," Marit said. [251] "Now we have something to remove." [257]

That was her method, and it puzzled them. [265] She never asked anyone to add anything. [272] She asked them to take things away. [279] The scrape of a throat clearing, the breath sucked in too loudly, the little push of volume a singer uses to prove he is present: she named each one and asked for its absence. [313] "Harmony is what is left," she told them, "when the noise has been subtracted." [327]

In the third week a bass from the east bank named Teodor found the zither. [342] It lay in a glass case in the music alcove, strings slack, its soundhole covered by a carved wooden rosette so fine it looked like frost. [368] Nobody in the valley made them anymore. [375] The last carver had died before the split, and his apprentices had scattered, and the knowledge had gone the way such knowledge goes, quietly, without anyone deciding it should. [404]

"My grandmother had one like this," Teodor said. [412]

"So did mine," said a soprano from the west bank, and then looked as if she wished she hadn't. [431]

Marit lifted the case's lid and turned the instrument toward the window. [443] The rosette was not decoration only. [449] Carvers in this valley had once used the pattern to denote the maker's village, a lattice of leaves and knots that read like a signature to anyone who knew the alphabet. [480] Teodor knew it. [483] So did the soprano, whose name was Ilse. [491] They bent over the rosette together, arguing gently about which knot meant which hill, and Marit stepped back and let the argument run, because it was the first one in three weeks that had nothing to do with the river. [531]

The rosette's village, it turned out, no longer existed; it had stood at the bend where the river later became the boundary, and its people had gone to both banks. [561] Marit did not point this out. [567] She had learned, in eleven countries, that a lesson spoken aloud is a lesson refused. [582] Instead she restrung the zither with wire from the hardware shop and asked Teodor and Ilse to tune it between them, one tightening while the other listened. [609] Tuning, too, is subtraction: you remove the wrongness a hair at a time until only the note remains. [627]

By November the choir had stopped sitting by bank. [636] Nobody announced it; the seating simply eroded, the way hatred does when it is not fed, not in a moment but across a season of small unlearnings. [663] The velvet still exhaled dust when they sat, but they sat closer, and the dust rose as one cloud. [682]

On the night of the concert Marit stood before them with the zither in her hands. [698] She plucked a single string and let it ring through the rosette, and the sound came out changed, softened by the carved leaves of a village nobody lived in anymore. [728] Twenty-two voices found that pitch and held it. [736] Then, quietly, they began to divide it, not into sides but into parts, each strand distinct and none of them alone, braided the way the rosette was braided, so that the eye could follow any single line and still see the whole. [778]

Marit listened for noise and found none left to take. [788]
