The storm had knocked out the power at a quarter past nine, and now the only sound in the old mill was the brook behind it, running high and quiet over the stones. [33] Ines Calloway sat on the back step with a candle and the day's mail, because the escape room she had built inside the mill was dark, and a dark escape room is just a locked building. [69] The step was where she always ended up on bad nights; the brook had never once asked her for rent. [89]

Three envelopes. [91] The first was the developer's fourth offer, the number larger and the deadline shorter. [105] The second was the electric bill she could not pay. [115] The third was a padded packet with no return address, her name typed on a label, and inside it, wrapped in a twist of wax paper, a single seed the size of a walnut. [149]

She had read about this. [154] All summer, people across the county had been getting seeds they never ordered, and the agriculture office had put out a notice: do not plant them, do not throw them away, bring them in. [188] Most people were unsettled by it. [194] Ines was not most people. [199] Twelve years of designing rooms had made her notably receptive to anything that arrived without explanation, because that was exactly what a good puzzle was: an object that insisted on meaning before it revealed any. [234]

She turned the seed toward the candle. [241] Dark, ridged, heavy for its size. [247] She had seen one once, under glass at a conservatory in the city, beside a placard she still remembered: Amorphophallus titanum, the corpse flower, which waits seven to ten years to bloom, blooms for a single night, and smells of death so that the right insects will come. [295]

The brook kept talking to itself. [301] The trees dripped. [304] Nothing else in the valley made a sound, the way nothing does after a storm, when the air has been emptied and has not yet decided what to fill itself with. [335]

She asked the seed the questions she asked every locked box that came through her door. [351] Who benefits. [353] Who knows I would keep it instead of turning it in. [364] Who knows this mill has a greenhouse room I never finished because the money ran out. [380]

The label was typed on a machine with a dropped lowercase e. [392] She knew that machine. [396] Old Teodor Vance, who had run the conservatory's seed bank until it closed in the spring, had come through her hardest room three times and failed it three times, and had written her afterward, on that typewriter, to say that failing it was the most alive he had felt in a decade. [448] The seed bank's collection had been sold off or scattered. [458] Apparently not all of it. [463]

So the mystery was not who, but why now. [472] She thought about that the way she thought about everything, through orthogonal approaches, coming at the problem from directions that shared no edge. [495] Botanically: a corpse flower needs a decade of warmth, patience, and a room nobody else wants. [511] Financially: the developer wanted the mill by October, and a building with a protected rare plant growing in it is a building that is very hard to demolish. [539] As design: a puzzle that takes ten years to solve, whose final clue is a smell, whose players return every season to see if the answer has arrived yet. [568]

The three lines did not touch, and then they did, and she felt the thing she had not felt in a year of unpaid bills: ideas beginning to flow, one into the next, like the brook finding its channel again after the flood. [611]

Teodor had not sent her a plant. [618] He had sent her a reason to hold the building. [628] A seed bank is only a place that refuses to let the unfinished be thrown out, and he had lost his. [649] Her mill was the same kind of place, for a different kind of seed, and the developer's offer was a notice to bring it in and let it be destroyed. [679]

She would not. [682] Not because the room would ever make money, and it would not, not for years, but because somebody had to keep a few rooms in the world where a strange thing could sit in the dark and take its time. [722]

She blew out the candle, because she did not need it anymore, and sat a while longer in the dark beside the water. [745] The power would come back. [750] The developer would call. [754] For now there was only this hush, the seed warm in her closed hand, and the brook going on about its business, carrying the storm away downstream a little at a time. [786]
