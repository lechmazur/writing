In the mirror world, dawn had never risen; it had been woven. [12] Every morning for a thousand years the singers of Halver had gathered in the orchard greenhouse and threaded their voices through the mica window, and the light came out the other side as cloth, warm and gold, to swaddle the town until dusk. [55] Then the singers died, one by one, and no one had learned the songs, and the night simply stayed. [74]

Wren was the memory keeper, which in Halver meant she remembered for those who could not. [90] The trouble was that her own memory had gone scattered, the way mica goes when you pick at its edge: thin sheets sliding off, each one clear and useless alone. [120] She knew that a woman named Tam had once given her a pear. [133] She could not remember whether Tam was her mother, her lover, or a stranger. [147]

She walked to the greenhouse anyway, because the dark had made the town forget itself. [162] Neighbors passed each other on the lanes without nodding. [171] Children no longer knew which doors would open for them. [181] Without the woven dawn to hold them in one warm shape, the people of Halver were coming unraveled, and a realm that forgets it is a realm does not last. [211]

The greenhouse stood black against the black sky. [219] Inside, the alchemical orchard still lived, barely: rows of glass-barked trees whose fruit had once been small suns, now hanging dim as coals. [242] At the far end waited the mica window, a single pane the height of two men, layered a thousand sheets deep. [263] Each sheet, the old singers had said, held one dawn's song pressed flat inside it, the way a leaf is pressed in a book. [287]

Wren set her thumbnail against the edge and peeled. [296] The sheet came away with a sound like a held breath let go. [309] She raised it to her face, and through it the dead orchard flickered gold, and a woman's voice she did not know sang four notes in a tongue she did not know. [341] Then it was over, and the sheet was only glass. [351]

Four notes were not a dawn. [357] She peeled another, and another, until the floor glittered and her hands shook, and she had perhaps forty notes in forty voices, none of them hers, none of them fitting together. [388] The songs were forgotten because they had never belonged to one singer. [400] Every dawn had been a weave, warp from one throat and weft from another, and a single mouth could only hold a thread. [423]

Wren sat down among the mica and understood what a memory keeper was for. [437] Her mind was already in sheets. [443] She could slide the forty notes between her own scattered layers and let them lie flat there, each borrowed voice pressed against a piece of herself: Tam's pear beside a bass hum, the smell of her first winter beside a child's high call. [486] Her memories would be the binding. [492] She would not get them back. [498] Whatever she sang would be sung with them, and go out through the window as light, and she would be left holding the pane. [522]

She had been holding scattered things for a long time. [532] Letting them be woven felt, strangely, less like loss than like being finally allowed to set them down. [550]

She stood before the window and sang. [557] The first note was the unknown woman's, and under it Wren laid the pear. [571] The second was a boy's, and she gave it the winter. [582] Note by note the forgotten voices took her scattered self as their thread, and the weave held, and the mica began to glow from the far side, as if something on the other side of the mirror had turned its face toward her. [625] Light came through the pane as cloth, warm and gold, and poured down the rows of the orchard. [643] The coal-fruit kindled. [646] The dawn flowed out the greenhouse door and along the lanes, wrapping house against house, and in the streets people stopped and looked at each other and remembered whose child was whose. [678]

When the last note left her, Wren stood empty in the gold, holding nothing. [692] She did not know her name. [698] But she knew she had swaddled a town, and that somewhere in the warmth outside a woman named Tam was looking up at the woven sky with a memory of pears, and that the thread which had once been Wren was now the thing that held the whole cloth together. [748] Bound into the weave, she was free of the weight of carrying it. [761] She sat down in the light to learn, from the start, what the morning was. [776]
