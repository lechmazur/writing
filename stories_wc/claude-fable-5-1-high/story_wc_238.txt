Delphine Marr wrote the gossip column for the Halloway Courier, and she was also the only exorcist within sixty miles, which the town understood to be the same job. [29] The dead lingered on what went unsaid, and nothing in Halloway stayed unsaid once she printed it. [46] She came to the conservatory's sheet music archive an hour before dawn, on the morning the crews would cut down the Quill orchard. [69] The saws sat oiled on the flatbeds outside, and the trees held the particular hush of things waiting to be ended. [90] Inside, the archive smelled of rag paper and rust, and of something else, faint and floral, that had no business surviving forty years. [113] Hester Quill had died in this room, at the piano, with a score for her sister's wedding half inked. [132] The wedding had never happened, because a week earlier Hester had stood among the apple trees and told June she was marrying a man who only wanted the land. [161] June had thrown her perfume bottle at her sister's feet and walked out of the orchard, and Hester had never found the words to follow. [186] Now June was seventy-one, and selling. [192]

Delphine set the bottle on the piano lid. [200] It was cut glass, chipped at the lip, the atomizer bulb gone brittle as a wasp's nest, and June had handed it over without meeting her eyes. [227] "A grudge is a house you keep paying the light bill on," Delphine had told her. [243] "Nobody lives there, but you can't stand to see it dark." [254] June had said she talked like a fortune cookie, and given her the bottle anyway. [269]

Around the bottle's neck Delphine wound two hundred turns of copper wire stripped from a junked transformer, then ran the leads to the archive's old intercom, a brass grille that had once let the archivist call the house across the orchard. [310] The line had been cut decades ago. [317] She found both ends behind the shelving, green with age, and spliced them with her teeth and a pocketknife, kneeling in dust that had once been somebody's cantatas. [345] Ghosts were not spirit, in her experience, so much as weather: a moving charge, a pressure front that never broke. [365] Pass a charge through a coil and you get a current; give the current somewhere to go and you get a voice, of a kind. [390] At the far end, in a kitchen she was selling, June would be sitting by the other grille because Delphine had asked her to. [414]

"Hester," Delphine said to the room. [420] "The trees go at seven." [425] "After that there's no orchard to be sorry in." [434]

The floral smell thickened, and frost feathered the piano's black lacquer. [445] The copper coil began to hum, and through the brass grille came a sound like a hand dragged across the low strings, then a word, or the shape of one. [475] June. [476]

Delphine had performed enough of these to know what came next, and it was never what the living hoped. [495] Two hundred turns of wire cannot carry forty years. [504] The apology came through in pieces, static chewing the middle out of every sentence, so that what reached the kitchen across the orchard was wrong, and then a long crackle, and then the land, and then a single held note that might have been I or might have been the wind finding the splice. [558] Delphine, with her ear to the coil, heard all of it, which was the cruelty of her trade. [576] The frost burned off the lacquer. [582] The smell went out of the room like a held breath released, and the perfume bottle, when Delphine touched it, was only glass. [605]

That was the trouble with miracles. [611] They arrived like mail from a country with bad roads, torn open, half the pages missing, and you were expected to be grateful. [634]

She walked through the orchard as the light came up, past the crews drinking coffee off their tailgates, and found June on the porch with the intercom grille in her lap, its wire trailing back through the open door. [673] "I got three words," June said. [679] "One of them was my name." [685]

"A river doesn't have to reach you whole," Delphine said. [695] "You only have to know it started upstream." [703]

June looked at the trees, which she had not looked at in some time. [717] Then she stood, went down the steps, and told the foreman to wait, and the foreman, who had known her for forty years, put his coffee down. [744]

Delphine went home and wrote the column. [751] She printed the rest of it, the parts the wire had dropped, because that was the other half of the job: whatever the dead could not get across, the living would read over breakfast, and gossip would finish a sentence instead of starting one. [795]
