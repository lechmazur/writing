Ilse Marek measured things before she allowed herself to see them. [11] It was the habit of a woman who had spent nineteen years timing the spin-down of pulsars, stars so dense that a teaspoon of them would outweigh a mountain, and so regular that their slow decay could be tracked to the microsecond across decades. [55] Reality reached her preprocessed, sorted into signal and noise, and she had come to her mother's greenhouse three weeks after the flood with every intention of keeping it that way. [85]

Her brother Tomas wanted a gathering. [91] Candles, the old volunteers, someone reading a poem about wings. [101] Ilse had told him the structure needed to be assessed and either salvaged or demolished, and that all the rest was needless drama. [124] She had meant it kindly. [129] She always meant it kindly, and it always landed like a door closing. [142]

The greenhouse stood at the bottom of the property where the creek had jumped its banks. [158] The storm had taken a third of the roof panes and the flood had taken everything below knee height: the potting benches, the nectar feeders, the little painted sign that said Please Do Not Touch The Butterflies. [195] A tide line of silt ran along the glass. [204] The butterflies themselves were gone, of course; her mother had released the last generation in October, two months before the stroke. [225]

Ilse stepped through the twisted door frame with a clipboard and began to count. [239] Panes intact, panes cracked, panes missing. [245] Rot in the sills. [249] Load on the surviving ribs. [254] It was a decay problem, and she was good at decay problems. [266] You establish the rate, you extrapolate, you decide. [274]

It was the glass above the tide line that stopped her. [285] She had assumed the upper panes were merely dirty. [294] Closer, with the afternoon sun coming low through the gap in the roof, she saw that the dirt had a structure. [315] Pale ovals, dozens of them, clustered along the south wall at the height where the morning warmth first arrived. [334] Each oval shimmered faintly, blue at one edge and copper at the other. [347]

Wing dust. [349] The scales that butterflies shed every time they land, a few hundred at a touch, too small to notice and too light to fall far. [374] Her mother had never wiped the upper glass; she said it dimmed the light in a way the morphos liked. [394] So for twenty-two years the butterflies had rested in the same warm places, and each had left behind a breath of color, and the breaths had accumulated. [421]

Ilse put the clipboard down. [426] She knew this shape of knowledge. [432] No single pulse from a neutron star told you anything; it was only the stacking of millions of them, year over year, that revealed the star was slowing, that it was losing something it would never get back, that it was changing in a way no living observer would see finished. [483] She had built a career on patience with the enormous. [493] She had never once extended it to the small. [502]

The ovals thinned toward the east, where a pane had cracked years ago and the draft had discouraged landing. [521] They thickened near the corner where her mother had kept her chair. [533] It was a record, as honest as any timing residual, of two decades of a woman sitting still enough that butterflies came to rest near her hands. [560] Ilse had called that sitting a waste. [567] She had said it kindly. [572]

She touched one oval with a fingertip and the color came away, a smear of blue on her skin so slight she had to tilt it to the light to be sure it was there. [607] It had taken thousands of landings to make that mark and one careless second to unmake a corner of it. [627] She stood very still for a while, the way her mother had. [639]

The structural verdict did not change. [645] The ribs were compromised; the greenhouse would come down. [654] But that evening she called Tomas and asked whether the old volunteers might want to help remove the south panes whole, before the demolition, and carry them somewhere dry. [683] He was quiet long enough that she wondered if the line had dropped. [696] "You want to keep the dirty glass," he said. [705] "It isn't dirt," she said, and then, because she was trying to learn a new way of arriving at things, she did not explain what it was. [732] "Come and see it in the morning light." [740] "I'll bring people," Tomas said carefully. [746] "Bring them," Ilse said. [750] "Somebody can read the poem." [755]
