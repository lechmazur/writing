Ines Marrec had painted the same quay for fifty-one years. [10] Every morning she set her easel between the fish scales and the bollards and gave the tourists what they wanted: blue hulls, white gulls, a light that had left the harbor decades ago. [43] The fishermen called her by name and she called them by theirs, and knew their boats to the last flake of rust, and had never once let any of them past her door. [76]

On the morning the mirror cracked, she did not go to the quay. [89] It was a small oval hand mirror, Marguerite's, left behind in 1973 along with everything else Marguerite had left behind, and it split across the middle with a sound like ice while Ines was tying her hair. [126] Nobody had touched it. [130] Her grandmother would have said seven years of bad luck. [140] Ines was seventy and did not have seven years to spare on omens. [153] She took the cracked glass as something simpler: a deadline. [163]

At nineteen she had made a promise to no one but herself, and she had told no one, not even Marguerite, who was asleep beside her in the grotto when she made it. [196] She had painted a seashell that afternoon, a whelk the size of her palm, and on its curve she had painted Marguerite's face in four colors, the best thing she had ever done. [229] She set it on a ledge above the pool and promised herself she would come back for it only when she had painted something better. [254] She never had. [257] So she had never gone back, and the hulls and gulls piled up in other people's hallways, and the promise sat in the dark, keeping. [282]

Low tide came at eleven. [287] She walked the shingle below the headland with a torch and a stick and entered the caves where the cliff opened like a mouth. [311] The network ran for a kilometer under the fields, chamber into chamber, and the fishermen's children still dared each other into it and still, sometimes, had to be fetched out by boat. [343] She had rehearsed the route for fifty years: second chamber, keep the water on the right, the fork, go right. [363]

She went right. [366] The passage narrowed, climbed, and ended in a wall of fallen stone that had not been there in 1973. [385] She stood a long while with her hand on it. [395] Then she went back to the fork and, because there was nothing else to do, went left. [412]

Halfway down the left passage she began to laugh, quietly, because her knees remembered before her head did: they had gone left the first time as well. [439] Marguerite had been leading and Marguerite had been wrong, and Ines had tidied the memory for half a century until she had tidied the mistake right out of it. [468] The passage dipped, wet to the ankle, then opened. [477]

The grotto was as it had been. [484] A crack in the roof let down a blade of daylight; a spring came out of the rock and fed a pool so still it might have been poured glass; ferns grew in the light, impossibly green, a garden a hundred meters inside a cliff. [529] The shell was on its ledge. [535] She lifted it and turned it into the light. [544]

The four colors had gone to two, then nearly to none; the damp had eaten Marguerite down to a ghost, an outline of cheekbone and the dark hint of a mouth. [575] And yet the fading revealed what the paint had covered: the shell's own spiral running under the face, turning and turning inward, the thing Ines had painted over at nineteen because she had thought a face was more important. [614] She knelt at the pool to rinse the grit from it and saw, in the water, a face that was not cracked. [636] Seventy, and lined like a tide chart, and whole. [645]

She understood then that she had kept the promise after all, only backwards. [658] She had not come back because she had painted something better; she had come back to learn what better was. [678]

The pool had held its stillness for fifty-one years and would hold it for fifty-one more, and the tide would close the passage in forty minutes. [704] Both were true, and she sat between them with the shell in her lap, in a forever that had a clock in it, and let it be enough. [732]

When the water began to talk in the passage she put the shell back on its ledge, face down this time, spiral up. [755] The next morning she set her easel at the far end of the quay, facing the cliff, and when Pol from the Marie-Ange asked what she was painting, she told him. [786]
