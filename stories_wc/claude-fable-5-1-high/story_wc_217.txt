The rain had stopped an hour ago, and the lagoon lay cobalt and still, holding the sky so carefully it seemed afraid to spill it. [25] Ilse walked the shoreline the way she always did after weather, slowly, saying nothing, her notebook buttoned inside her coat. [45] People in the village called her the poet, though few had read a line she had written, and she preferred it that way. [68] In her pocket she carried a drake's tail feather, the curled black one a mallard grows for show, and she had trimmed its shaft to a nib. [95] With it she had written three hundred and forty dawns, one poem for each morning she had stood here, a sequence she called Woven Dawns because each poem borrowed a thread from the one before. [130] She had meant to finish at a year. [138] Then, in the spring, her hands had begun to shake, and the line she wrote on the three hundred and forty-first morning had wandered off the page like a bird leaving a wire. [171] She had not written since. [176]

Now she came only to look, and to tend the small things the tide left undone: righting a beached crab, freeing a strand of net from the reeds. [204] Above the far bank a kite hung in the washed air, red against the clearing gray, and a boy stood beneath it with both fists around the string. [232] Ilse watched the way she watched everything, warmly attentive, as if the world were a guest who might need something. [252]

The wind shifted off the water. [258] The kite lunged, the boy leaned back, and the string snapped with a sound so small she felt it more than heard it. [281] For a moment the kite rose free, as though it had been waiting for permission, and then it tilted, spun, and came down into the shallows twenty paces from where she stood. [313]

The boy came running around the lagoon, and by the time he reached her he was making the ragged, hiccuping noise children make when they are trying not to cry and failing. [345] Ilse had already waded in. [350] The water was cold enough to make her ankles ring, and the kite lay face down in it, its tail torn away, one spar cracked, the paper gone soft and dark. [381]

"It's ruined," the boy said. [386]

"It's wet," she said. [390]

She carried it to a flat stone and knelt, and the boy knelt too, still hiccuping, and the wind went on whining through the wet reeds in a pitch that did not agree with anything. [425] Three sounds, she thought: the boy, the reeds, the slow drip from the kite's edge. [440] None of them fit. [444] She began to hum, very low, finding the note between the boy's breath and the reeds' complaint, and holding it there until the two seemed to lean toward it. [473] You could not silence a discord, but you could offer it a third voice and let it become a chord. [493] The boy's hiccups slowed. [497] Without thinking about it, he started humming too, off-key, and she moved her own note to meet him. [515] Meanwhile her hands, which shook, were splinting the cracked spar with a reed and a length of the broken string. [535]

"It needs a tail," the boy said, "or it just spins." [546]

Ilse reached into her pocket and took out the drake's feather. [557] She looked at the trimmed nib for a while. [566] Then she tied it to the kite's bottom corner with the last of the string, the black curl bobbing against the red like a signature. [591]

"That's not a real tail," the boy said, though he was smiling now. [604]

"No," she agreed. [607] "It won't fly the way it did." [614] "It will fly some other way." [620]

He held it up, and the wind, still faintly out of tune, took the paper and the feather together and lifted them a little, testing. [645] The lagoon had gone from cobalt to something with gold in it, the low sun burnishing every ripple, and the whole shore held that particular hush that comes when weather has passed and nothing has yet decided to replace it. [685]

Ilse unbuttoned her coat and took out the notebook. [694] She had no pen now. [699] She wet her finger in the lagoon and drew, beneath the wandering line on the three hundred and forty-first page, a second crooked one that would dry to nothing by morning. [730] It was the first dawn she had written that was not a dawn, and the first she had let stay unfinished on purpose. [753] The weave, she understood, had never needed a last thread. [763] It only needed the next one, and a hand willing to shake while it held it. [779]

Behind her the boy laughed, and the feathered kite climbed. [789]
