The last fish was caught on a Tuesday, and by Friday the bell had fallen. [15] Nobody in Halven connected the two events except Piet, which was typical of Piet. [29] He was the town's only mage, and not a good one. [40] Where other mages summoned fire, Piet could raise the temperature of a thing to roughly that of a resting hand, and no further. [63] He warmed bathwater for the elderly and took soup off the boil. [75] The fishermen called him the lukewarm mage, and he had never once heard the unkindness in it. [92]

The bell had hung for two hundred years on the sapphire viaduct, the great blue-stoned span that carried mountain water over the harbor and out to the sea. [120] It had come off a wreck, and every dawn a fisherman climbed the arches and rang it once, because the fish came up to the sound. [146] Everyone knew this the way everyone knows a thing they have stopped examining. [159] Then the nets came up empty, and emptier, and finally old Marren hauled in a single grey mullet and wept over it, and that was the last fish. [187] Three days later nobody climbed the viaduct to ring for nothing, and on the fourth the rusted bracket, unrung and unoiled, gave way. [210] The bell struck the stone and split from lip to crown. [221]

Piet found it that evening, lying in a puddle beneath the arches, and stood over it a long time. [240] The fishermen had it backwards, he decided. [247] The bell had not stopped because the fish were gone; the fish were gone because the bell had been rung by men who no longer meant it. [274] Somewhere a ledger had gone out of balance, and the sea, being fair, had closed its accounts. [291]

He could not smelt bronze. [296] He could barely warm it. [301] But the viaduct leaked. [305] Every arch wept a slow blue drip where the water seeped through the stone, and where the drips landed there were knobs of hard turquoise mineral, built up over centuries a film at a time. [340]

Piet dragged the bell beneath the fastest drip and began to count. [352] One drop every nine seconds. [357] Each drop left a skin of mineral thinner than breath, and that skin took nearly an hour to dry on cold bronze. [379] On bronze held at the temperature of a resting hand, it dried in a quarter of that. [396] He worked it out on the back of a fish-tally with a stub of charcoal: the width of the crack, the depth, the drops per hour, the films per day. [426] Nineteen days, if he never let it cool. [434]

So he did not let it cool. [441] He sat beneath the arch with both palms on the bronze through nineteen nights of autumn, turning the bell a finger's width each hour so the drip would find every part of the seam. [475] Fishermen passed him on their way to sell boats. [484] Marren brought him bread and did not stay. [492] "It won't ring true," she said. [498] "Stone in a bell." [502]

"It only has to ring once," Piet said, and believed it completely. [514]

On the twentieth dawn he carried it up. [522] The viaduct at that hour was the color of the inside of a wave, blue light coming up through the stone as the sun cleared the headland, and the water in the channel above him ran loud with melt. [561] The mended seam showed as a vein of turquoise down the bronze, bright and unmistakable and almost certainly weaker than the metal around it. [585] He hung it from the new bracket he had hammered himself, badly. [597] Below, along the harbor wall, a few figures had stopped to look up, not because they expected anything but because a man was doing something on the viaduct, and there was nothing else to watch. [632]

Piet took the rope and pulled. [638]

The note was wrong. [642] It was dull and flat and had a grit in it, the sound of a bell with a stone spine, and he felt the seam give under the stroke, a hairline opening where the mineral could not flex. [680] It would not ring twice. [685]

It rang once. [688]

The sound went out over the harbor in the blue light and the water below the viaduct did something the water had not done in a month: it moved against the tide, a long silver shrug just under the surface, as though a great many small things had turned at once to see who was calling. [744]

On the harbor wall, Marren was already running for her nets. [755]

Piet looked at the split bell and did the arithmetic again. [766] Nineteen days, if the drip held. [772] He could do that. [776]
