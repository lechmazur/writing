On the third night after friction disappeared, Ilse climbed the tidal bluff on her knees, because knees were the only part of her that still knew how to hold. [29] Below her the village slid. [34] Boats drifted off their moorings without a sound; a cart rolled downhill forever; a man who had fallen at noon was still skating gently toward the sea, arms out, calling for a rope no one could grip. [71]

Ilse had seen all of it when she was nine. [81] That was the curse: she saw what would come and never once what it meant. [96] She had seen her grandmother's boat overturn eleven years before it did, had seen the black grain fall from the sky and bury itself in the bluff, had seen the aurora that followed it, green and copper, spilling over the water like a dawn borrowed from some other planet. [145] She had not seen why. [150]

The grain was still there, no larger than a lentil, sunk into the rock at the bluff's crown. [168] Collapsed star dust, the schoolteacher had called it before he slid away, a fleck of something so dense it should have fallen through the world. [193] Instead it had stopped here and taken the world's grip with it, as if friction were a debt and the star had come to collect. [218]

Ilse was an eel herder, which in the village meant she was half a rumor. [233] She came in at low tide and left before anyone could ask her anything, and she spent her nights in the channels below the bluff, guiding the silver runs with lantern light and lines of painted stones. [270] Her grandmother had taught her the stones: white for one, white for one, red for two, blue for three, yellow for five, on and on, each number the sum of the two before it. [304] "The eels know this count," the old woman had said. [314] "It is older than us. [319] It is how the sea remembers how to turn." [328]

Ilse had laid the stones every season since and had never understood them. [341] They worked; the eels came. [346] But knowing a thing works is not the same as knowing it, and the not-knowing had sat in her like a swallowed hook. [369]

On the night the boat overturned, her grandmother had gripped her wrist with cold fingers and said, "When the world lets go, finish the spiral on the bluff. [397] Promise me." [399] Ilse had promised, and the promise had lingered for eleven years like the ache of a tooth already pulled. [418] Now the world had let go. [424]

The trouble was that stones would not stay where they were set. [436] On a frictionless slope even a pebble crept toward the tide. [447] So Ilse did the only thing that made sense to a woman who had spent her life handling creatures that could not be held. [471] She did not fight the slipping. [477] She harnessed it. [480]

She scraped a breath of dust from the black grain with a fishbone, mixed it into her paints, and felt each pot grow heavy as a drowned man. [508] A stone painted with star dust did not slide; it sank a finger's width into the rock and stayed, pinned by its own impossible weight. [533] She laid them in the old count, one, one, two, three, five, eight, thirteen, twenty-one, curving out from the grain in a spiral that widened as it climbed. [561]

The aurora sharpened overhead as if the sky were leaning in to read. [574] She had seen this too, she realized, kneeling with the last yellow stone in her hand, but she had always seen it from outside, a small figure on a bluff under strange light. [607] She had never seen it from inside her own hands. [617]

She set the stone. [621]

Below, the eels came. [625] They came up the channels in the count, one and one and two and three, silver threads following the spiral's shadow on the water, and where they turned, the water turned, and where the water turned, the sliding village slowed, and the drifting man's heel caught at last against a tuft of grass. [678]

Ilse understood then, all at once, the way one understands a face. [690] The pattern was not a trick for calling eels. [699] It was the shape of holding on without gripping, the sacred geometry of things that stay together by turning toward each other. [721] The star had not stolen friction; it had come to see whether anyone still knew the older way. [739] Her grandmother had. [742] Her grandmother had promised the sea that someone would. [751]

The aurora paled toward a real dawn, the borrowed light given back, and the black grain sat quiet at the center of the spiral, no longer taking anything. [779] Ilse stood up, on her feet this time, and did not fall. [791]
