Wren kept her prophecies in chalk because chalk was honest about forgetting. [12] She ran the junkyard at the mouth of the estuary, forty acres of drowned Buicks and stacked refrigerators, and people drove out from town to ask her what was coming. [42] She always knew. [45] The trouble was that she knew the way other people remembered, and the knowing wore away before the thing arrived, so that by the time a fisherman's boat actually sank she had forgotten ever seeing it sink. [82] So she wrote each vision on the nearest hood or door panel with a stub of plain white chalk, and the fog came up the estuary at night and read them and wiped them clean. [117] This suited her. [120] Nothing in the yard was meant to last, least of all what she saw. [134]

Inside the trailer, though, one shelf held things she would not let the fog have: nineteen videotapes in cardboard sleeves, one for every year since Ines had left, each mailed without a note. [167] She had never watched them. [172] She told herself a tape could only be played new once, and she was saving that. [188] The truth was closer to the tide: she wanted her daughter near, and the tapes were the nearest she could stand. [209] So the shelf hushed at her every evening, nineteen small black silences, and she sat among them with a cassette recorder running, trying to catch a sound she had never heard. [240] Other people, she supposed, heard themselves grow older: a knee that spoke, a voice that dropped. [256] Wren lived facing the wrong way, remembering forward and losing it, and the years went by behind her without a sound. [277] She wanted, just once, to record it. [284]

On a morning thick as wool she found her own handwriting on the hood of a Skylark: SHE COMES THROUGH THE GARDEN. MARK THE WAY. [309] She did not remember writing it. [315] That was the only proof it was true. [323]

The garden was up on the headland, the last work of a sculptor who had built nothing solid there: only steel frames and vents that drew sea fog off the water and shaped it, so that on gray days figures stood in the grass and breathed and slowly changed. [372] Walkers lost themselves in it every season. [379] Wren loaded her truck with driftwood from the tideline, pale and light as bone, and drove up. [396] She worked from the road inward, laying each branch with its thick end toward the junkyard, an arrow anyone could follow home. [418] The fog figures leaned over her as she knelt. [427] One had a woman's shoulders; one held out an arm that dissolved and re-formed; one was small, a child's height, and Wren stayed beside it longer than she meant to. [457] The recorder ran in her coat pocket, gathering wind. [466]

She set the last marker at the garden's far edge, where the ground fell toward the beach, and straightened, and heard footsteps that were not fog. [492] The woman who came up the slope had gray at her temples and Wren's own jaw. [508]

"You wrote to me," Ines said. [514] "A postcard. [516] In chalk, of all things. [521] It said you'd be lost up here by the time I came, and to look for the wood." [539]

Wren had no memory of a postcard. [546] She looked back the way she had come and found she could not say which of the pale figures she had passed, or where the road lay. [573] The markers had been for her. [579] She had known that once, and forgotten, and done it anyway. [590]

They followed the driftwood down together, branch to branch, the thick ends pointing them out of the breathing garden and toward the sea-smell of wrecked cars. [616] Ines did not ask why her mother had never called. [626] Wren did not ask what was on the tapes. [635]

In the trailer Ines saw the shelf and understood it in a glance, the way you understand a tide chart. [655] She pulled the first one and fed it to the old player without asking. [669] Static, then a kitchen, then a nineteen-year-old saying, "Mom, I don't know if you'll watch this." [685] Wren set the recorder on the table and pressed the red button. [697] Ines fed in the second tape, and the third, and the voice in the room deepened by degrees, lost a lisp, gained a laugh, went hoarse the year of some grief Wren would never be told. [733] There it was, the sound she had been hunting in the wind: not in any one tape but in the gaps between them, the small drop from one year's voice to the next. [766] She would forget this evening, she knew; she was already forgetting it. [778] But the cassette was turning, and for once the echo would outlast her. [791]
