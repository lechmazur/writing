Ilse Varga had spent forty years as a hands-on folklorist, learning that lore is not a thing you read; it is a thing you do. [25] She had dug for hag stones, boiled nettle cures, slept in barrows to test whether the dead really did complain. [45] None of it had given her back the summer she was nine. [57]

So, decades later, she came back to Sallow Cove, where the land had slipped into the sea and taken her grandmother's orchard with it. [81] At low tide the greenhouse still stood, its iron ribs green with weed, its glass panes clouded like old eyes. [101] Around it lay the wrecks the cove was famous for: a herring drifter, a coal barge, the ribs of something older, all sunk in a rough circle as if the orchard had been the thing they came to see. [140] Inside, the apple trees had drowned standing up, and between their trunks the mud kept a memory of paths. [159]

She had been a curious child, chronically so, the kind who licked batteries and asked the vicar what God ate. [179] Her grandmother had told her never to step inside a ring of mushrooms, and so of course she had. [198] She remembered the pale caps in the greenhouse loam, the smell of warm glass and windfall fruit, and then nothing until October, when she woke in her own bed with a whole season gone and her grandmother refusing to say what had happened. [241] Stolen, the old woman said once, and would not be drawn further. [253]

What the fair folk take, they hide behind the sky, and they pay for it with stars. [270] A ring that has swallowed something leaves a constellation broken above it: a missing point in the Plough, a gap in the Swan's wing. [294] Find the break, stand beneath it, and the ring will show itself where the mud is honest. [311]

So each night she waded out with a lantern and a star chart gone soft at the folds, and lay on her back on the greenhouse floor. [338] The glass overhead was a ruin of salt and cracks, and through it the stars arrived already damaged, so that she had to learn which absences were the roof's and which were the sky's. [372] She kept a ledger. [376] Pane by pane, night by night, she subtracted the greenhouse from the heavens until only the true breaks remained. [395]

On the twentieth night she detected it: Cassiopeia with her chair kicked out from under her, one star simply not there. [416] She sat up too fast and the lantern went over. [426]

That was the moment, though she did not know it yet. [437] Every night for twenty nights she had set the lantern on the same brick, chalked the same mark on the same drowned trunk, waded home along the same line between the barge and the drifter. [472] Her grandmother had always said the fair folk are careful people; they cannot enter a house where the routines are whole. [493] They come when habits break. [498]

In the dark, without her light, without her mark, Ilse went the wrong way between the trees, and her boot came down on something soft and springy that was not mud. [529] She knelt and felt them with her fingertips, the way she had learned to trust: caps like coins, a perfect circle of them, fairy ring mushrooms fruiting in salt loam where nothing should fruit at all. [565] Directly above, through a clean pane, Cassiopeia sat crooked and waiting. [576]

Ilse stood in the ring, and she was nine. [585] Not remembering it; being it. [590] The glass was whole and hot with August. [598] Wasps worked the split pears. [603] Her grandmother stood at the far end with a basket, singing something in the old tongue, and there was a boy beside her, thin and freckled, laughing at Ilse for having stepped where she was told not to. [641]

Tomas. [642] Her brother. [644]

The summer came back not as a story but as a weight, all at once: his hand in hers on the cliff path, the storm in September, the boat, the search, the silence afterward that her grandmother had chosen for her because a child cannot carry that and a child cannot be made to. [698] Stolen, yes. [700] But not by anything with wings. [706] The ring had only kept what it was given. [715]

When she came back to herself the tide was turning, sliding cold around the drowned trunks, and the mushrooms were already softening into the loam as though they had done their one job. [748] Ilse found her lantern by feel, did not light it, and waded out a new way, past the older wreck whose name she now remembered, and did not look up to see whether the queen's chair had been mended.
