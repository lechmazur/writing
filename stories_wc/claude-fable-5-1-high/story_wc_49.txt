Pell Ambrose was the sort of tinkerer who fixed clocks so they ran on birdsong and mended kettles so they hummed lullabies, and the village of Harrow Vale loved him like a favorite uncle who never quite grew up. [39] He spun golden stories at the tavern, tales of moth-powered lanterns and rivers that remembered names, and nobody believed a word, which suited him fine. [64] But the arbor was dying, and that was no story. [74]

For three hundred years the great vines on the hill had given Harrow Vale its wine and its reason for gathering, and for three hundred years the elders had sung to them beneath the harvest moon. [110] Then came the hard decades, the war and the fever, and the singing stopped because the singers were gone. [129] Nobody wrote the songs down. [134] Now the leaves came in yellow and the grapes shriveled before August, and the young council spoke of pulling the roots and planting barley. [158]

Pell found the remedy the way he found most things, by accident and stubbornness. [172] He was repairing a music box for the widow Serra when he noticed that its brass cylinder, pinned with tiny notes, looked exactly like a vine tendril coiled around a trellis wire. [204] The thought arrived whole and ridiculous: what if the vines had listened? [216] What if every tendril had curled itself around the last melody it heard, the way a sleeping hand curls around a warm cup, and kept the shape for sixty years? [246]

He climbed the hill at midnight with a lantern and a pair of shears and came down with a basket of the oldest, woodiest tendrils he could find. [274] For six weeks he built. [279] The machine that emerged from his workshop was part barrel organ and part spinning wheel, with a cradle where a tendril could be clamped and turned against a comb of steel teeth, each tooth tuned to a note. [317] The first tendril produced a noise like a cat in a rain barrel. [330] The fortieth produced seven clear notes that made the widow Serra, who had come to collect her music box, sit down very suddenly on his workbench. [356]

"My mother sang that," she whispered. [362] "At the pressing. [365] I was six." [368]

Pell asked her when the singing had always happened, exactly, and she told him what he had half-remembered from his own boyhood: not under the harvest moon itself, but at the dawn after it, when the moon sank into the western notch of the hills at the very moment the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and the whole arbor stood lit from both sides at once. [434] "When everything aligns," she said. [439]

The harvest moon was eleven days away. [446] He ran every tendril in the basket, sorted the fragments, and stitched them into something that might have been three songs or one long one, full of gaps he filled with guesses and the widow's humming. [482] He told no golden stories at the tavern that week. [492] People noticed. [494]

On the morning after the harvest moon, he hauled the machine up the hill in the blue dark, and he was not alone. [517] The widow came, and her grandchildren, and then the baker, and then, grudgingly, two members of the council with barley seed still on their sleeves. [542] Nobody spoke. [544] A hush gathered under the green vaults of the arbor, thick as the mist that lay along the rows, and even the children felt it and went still. [572]

The moon slid, orange and enormous, toward the western notch. [582] The eastern ridge went pale, then pink, then white. [591] Pell set his hand on the crank and waited, and for one held breath the moon touched the hills and the sun broke free and the yellowing leaves caught fire from both directions. [624] He turned the crank. [628]

The forgotten song came out thin and strange, steel teeth plucking the memory of wood, and it should have been absurd. [649] Then the widow Serra began to sing along, and her voice found the gaps he had guessed at and corrected them, and the baker knew a verse, and one of the councilmen, to his own visible astonishment, knew the refrain. [689] The song had never been lost. [695] It had only been scattered, a line here and a line there, waiting for a reason to gather. [713]

Whether the vines recovered because of the singing or because of the wet spring that followed, Pell never claimed to know. [734] But the council put away the barley, and the next year Harrow Vale climbed the hill again at dawn after the harvest moon, and nobody needed the machine. [762] Pell kept it anyway, and when children asked what it was for, he spun them a golden story about vines that listened, and this time every word was true. [791]
