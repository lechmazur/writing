Ines Varga wrote obituaries for the living, and she had never stayed anywhere long enough to be written about herself. [20] Her clients paid to read, while they still breathed, what the world would say of them once they were gone. [40] Most wept. [42] A few laughed. [45] All of them asked how she did it, and she told them the truth: she sat with them until she could feel them in the room after they had left it, and then she wrote to that presence. [83] What she did not tell them was why she traveled at all. [95] She was looking for the place where grief ended. [104] Her brother had died six years ago in a city she had since crossed off her maps, and she had come to believe that healing must have a geography, some coastline or valley where the ache thinned like air at altitude. [145]

The island of Maruz was not on any list of candidates. [156] She arrived on the last ship of the season and then, standing on the quay with her satchel of pens, watched it sail again without her. [182] She could not have said why. [188] The harbor emptied of sound behind it. [195]

Above the town, the old palace stood with its shutters closed, and beneath it, on the terraced slope, lay a garden so shadowed by cypress and wall that autumn seemed to have settled there first and stayed. [232] Inside the gate, leaves lay on the paths in drifts of copper and bruised plum, the fountains held rainwater and nothing else, and gold leaf still clung to the pergola posts in flakes small as fingernails. [267] It was the loveliest abandoned place she had ever seen. [277]

"You missed the ship," a voice said. [284]

An old man sat on a stone bench beneath a fig tree, so still she had taken him for a statue. [305] His name was Teodor, and he had been the palace weaver when there was still a palace to weave for. [325] He did not ask what she did. [332] Instead, over the following days, he walked her through the garden and explained its absences. [347]

"The princess sat here," he said of an empty bench, "every morning, with her back to the sea so she would not see the ships and want one." [375] "The gardener stood at this pond and fed the carp by hand; he left in the third year of the emptying." [396] "My wife pruned these roses with a knife because she distrusted shears." [408]

He did not say when his wife had gone, and Ines did not ask. [422] She recognized what he was doing because it was what she did. [434] He spoke of each vanished person so exactly, the angle of a wrist, the smell of a coat, the way a laugh caught on a bad tooth, that they stood in the garden beside him, more present than the leaves. [474] He had kept a whole court alive by describing it. [484]

On the ninth evening he asked her to write his obituary. [495] So he knew after all. [500] They sat close on the princess's bench, his shoulder against hers for warmth, the light going amber and then gray over the empty harbor, and he told her about his hands. [531] From his pocket he drew a single thread, crimson, no longer than her forearm. [545]

"Weft," he said. [548] "From the great carpet in the throne room, the last row I ever wove." [562] "The warp is the part that stays fixed on the loom." [573] "The weft is the part that travels, back and across, back and across, and it is the traveling that makes a carpet out of string." [598]

He pressed it into her palm and closed her fingers over it. [610]

"You are looking for a place," he said. [618] "I looked too, after Liesl." [623] "There is no place." [627] "There is only the crossing, and what you carry across." [637]

Ines wrote his obituary that night by candlelight in the weaver's cottage, and it was the best thing she had ever written, because she did not have to summon him; he was asleep in the next room, breathing. [675] Then, for the first time in six years, she turned the page and wrote her brother's. [691] She projected him into the small room the way Teodor projected his court into the garden: the crooked front tooth, the way he hummed when he was lying, the coat he refused to replace. [725] He came. [727] He sat in the chair by the window, and he was not gone, only absent, and absence could be explained. [747]

In the morning she walked down to the empty quay with the crimson thread wound around her wrist and looked out at the gray water where no ship would come until spring. [779] For the first time, staying did not feel like being stranded. [790]
