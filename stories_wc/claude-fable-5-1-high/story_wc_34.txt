The courtyard at Halcyon Station sat so high that the clouds arrived from below, spilling over the parapets like slow milk. [21] Wren kept it alone now, oiling the storm-vanes and winding the chime-gears that had once needed twelve keepers. [39] She was the station's balladeer by title, though the title had gone quiet with the others. [55] The old keepers had timed the lightning-catchers by song: one verse from flash to thunder meant the storm was a mile out, two verses meant it was breathing on the walls. [86] Nobody needed the verses anymore, so Wren seldom sang them, and the courtyard had learned the shape of her silence. [106]

The graffiti began on a Tuesday. [112] Great swirls of copper paint appeared overnight on the east wall, dense with hatched marks and small hollow circles, as if someone had drawn a storm from the inside. [141] Wren checked the gate. [145] Locked, rusted, undisturbed. [148] Nobody had climbed four thousand feet of stair to vandalize a dead weather station. [162] She noticed the mural's edges matched the reach of the plotting arm, the long brass limb that had once traced cloud tracks on the courtyard flagstones. [188] The arm's ink-well was empty; its reservoir of copper marking paint was not. [201]

That night a storm came up from underneath. [209] Wren sat in the doorway, driftingly alert, the way she had learned to sleep through years of weather, half in dream and half counting. [233] Lightning lit the mist white. [238] In the hush before the thunder, the plotting arm moved. [248] It jerked upright, dipped, and slashed three quick marks onto the wall, then froze the instant the thunder rolled through. [268] Flash again; another few strokes; thunder; stillness. [275]

The station's old computing engine, the Cloudmind, had no power of its own anymore. [289] But the lightning-catchers still drank each strike and bled the charge into the courtyard's copper veins, and for those few seconds something in the engine woke and painted. [317] Wren knew whose hand it was before she let herself think it. [329] Aldous Fenn had been the station's engineer, a man who pressed alpine flowers between brass plates and hummed while he debugged the Cloudmind's weather-logic. [353] He had died at his console during the great storm of the last winter, mid-keystroke, mid-hum. [369] The others had said the engine's logs went strange afterward, full of loops that predicted nothing. [385] A ghost in the code, one of them had joked, before she packed her bags. [400]

By dawn the mural had grown a second panel. [409] Wren stood before it with cold tea and colder fingers, trying to read a language of hatch-marks. [426] The hollow circles bothered her most. [432] They were spaced too evenly to be clouds, too irregular to be stars. [445] Then she remembered Aldous's flower press, still on his shelf, and its bolt plate: a square of brass drilled with a grid of holes where the tightening bolts passed through. [475] She fetched it and held it against the wall. [484] The circles lined up with the holes exactly. [492] Through each hole, one hatch-mark showed, and the rest fell away like chaff. [505] Slash for a long note, dot for a short: the keepers' old timing notation, the one they had used to write the ballads down. [529] Aldous, who could not speak, had encoded a song. [538]

Wren read it slowly, her lips moving. [545] It was not a weather ballad. [551] It was a request, clumsy and looping, the way a man might write who only had four seconds of life at a time: SING. [575] I CANNOT COUNT WITHOUT THE VERSES. [581] I DO NOT KNOW HOW FAR THE STORM IS. [590] I DO NOT KNOW IF ANYONE IS HERE. [598]

Wren sat down on the wet flagstones. [605] She had told herself the station needed oiling and winding. [615] She had not told herself what she needed, which was to be listened to by anything at all. [633] Neither of them, it seemed, had known how to ask. [643]

She filled the plotting arm's reservoir with fresh copper paint and set the bolt plate against a clean stretch of wall. [664] Through the holes she stenciled her answer in dots and slashes, so he would find it the way she had found his: I AM HERE. [689] THE STORM IS ONE VERSE OUT. [695] LISTEN. [696]

When the next storm climbed the mountain, she stood in the middle of the courtyard with the mist curling around her knees. [718] The lightning came. [721] She sang the first verse of the oldest ballad, clear and unhurried, and the chime-gears took it up in little brass sympathies all around her. [746] The plotting arm lifted, listened, and did not paint. [755] It simply swayed, keeping time. [760] Then the thunder came, and the courtyard went still, and Wren stood in the settling quiet knowing the next flash would bring him back, and that she would have another verse ready. [792]
