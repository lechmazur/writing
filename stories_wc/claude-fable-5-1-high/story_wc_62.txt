The monarchs came to the ancient greenhouse the same week the ferry went down, and Ines Varga did not think this was a coincidence. [24] She had stopped believing in coincidence the year her grandfather died and kept arriving anyway. [39]

The greenhouse stood at the edge of the cemetery she had served for eleven years, its iron ribs green with age, half its panes missing, the rest clouded like old eyes. [70] Every autumn the butterflies roosted there on their way south, folding themselves against the glass until the whole structure seemed to breathe orange at dusk. [95] The town council had given her the commission: one stone for the nine who drowned, because nine separate stones would empty the memorial fund and, one councilman admitted quietly, because the families could not agree on anything. [132]

She was a thoughtful carver, slow, the kind who spent a week deciding on a serif. [148] But she had never carved for nine at once. [157] Grief for one person had a shape she knew: a name, two dates, a line of scripture or a line of verse. [179] Grief for nine felt shapeless, a weather rather than a wound. [190]

So she went to the greenhouse at dusk, because that was where Teodor waited. [204]

He had carved stones in this town for fifty years before her, and he had been dead for six, and every September, when the monarchs came, he stood among them with his sleeves rolled and said very little. [242] She had never told anyone. [247] Some things you keep the way you keep a chisel edge, out of the weather. [262]

"Nine," she told him. [266] "One stone." [268]

He nodded toward the potting bench where his woven ribbon roll lay, the same linen measuring ribbon he had used all his life, knotted at every inch by his own hands because he trusted no manufactured tape. [305] She had buried it with him. [311] It was here anyway. [315]

"Retrace," he said. [318]

So each dusk across the migration she unrolled the ribbon along his old letters on the older stones, laying it in the grooves of names he had cut before she was born, feeling how the cloth bent where his hand had hesitated, where it had not. [364] She did this in a silence so complete it had weight, the fierce meditative quiet of a person listening for something under the floor. [388] Teodor watched with the patience of a man who had all the time that remained to anyone. [405]

It taught her his hand, which she already knew, and not the shape of nine. [420]

On the ninth evening she brought two plates to the greenhouse and set them on the bench, one in front of the empty air where he stood. [447] In the Varga house you traded poems at dinner; you did not get dessert until you had given one and received one, and this had been true since before her grandfather's grandfather. [479] She recited Dickinson to him. [484] He gave her, in the voice she remembered, four lines of Attila Jozsef about a river. [500]

Then he said, "Ask them." [505]

She understood, and the understanding arrived like thunder heard from far off, enormous and unhurried. [520]

Over the next three weeks, while the monarchs thickened on the glass, she invited each of the nine families to dinner, one household a night, and told them the custom. [550] Give a poem, receive one. [555] The Okafors gave a hymn their son had sung badly and loudly. [567] Old Mrs. Lindqvist gave a nursery rhyme and could not finish it. [579] The Reyes brothers gave the same Neruda poem without knowing the other would, and laughed, and then did not laugh. [599] Ines gave back what her grandfather had given her, fifty years of dinner poems, and wrote nothing down until they left. [620]

At night she laid the ribbon along each poem as if it were a line of letters, measuring the length of what each family had chosen to say. [648] Some were four lines. [652] Some were forty. [655] None were the same length, and she saw at last that this was the answer: the shape of collective grief was not one line but many lengths laid side by side, uneven, and a single stone that pretended otherwise would be a lie cut in granite. [701]

She carved nine lines down the face of the stone, each the measured length of a family's poem, each unlabeled, so that any mourner could stand before it and find their own and see it lying beside the others. [740] It looked like a ribbon roll unspooled, or like the paths of butterflies drawn on a map, all leaving together but never in step. [764]

The morning the monarchs lifted from the glass, Teodor was not on the bench. [778] The ribbon was. [781]
