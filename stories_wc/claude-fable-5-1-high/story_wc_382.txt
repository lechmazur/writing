The first lie was the sugar. [6] Wren had a full canister in her own kitchen, but she stood at the door across the landing anyway, holding an empty cup like a passport. [32] The woman who answered was small, silver-haired, and unsurprised. [41] "Nobody borrows sugar anymore," she said, "so you must want something else." [53]

Her name was Ilse Marrow, and she had tutored dead languages for forty years: Latin, Old Norse, a little Akkadian for the desperate. [76] Wren worked for the city's preservation office and had been sent to assess the Corvane Building for demolition. [94] She had rented the empty flat on the top floor, and the building had begun, quietly, to defeat her. [113] Every lintel carried carved Latin she could not read. [122] Every keystone bore a mason's mark, a small crowned bee, repeated like a signature on a letter she was not meant to open. [145] She wanted to learn the language of old buildings before she signed the paper that would silence this one. [164] She did not say any of that. [171] She said, "Sugar," and Ilse said, "Tea, then," and that was the second lie, agreed upon by both. [189]

The tea moved to the rooftop garden by the second week. [200] Ilse kept it herself: thyme in cracked urns, a fig tree that should not have survived this far north, iron chairs older than the streetlights. [225] Each Thursday she laid out the tray with patiently nimble hands, fingers quick but never hurried, as if the cups were declensions she had set a thousand times. [253] She never asked why Wren wanted Latin. [260] Wren never asked why Ilse always knew how she took her tea, or why the flat across the landing felt like a room from a forgotten dream. [287] On the tray, holding down the napkins against the wind, sat an antique desk seal: brass handle worn to gold, a matrix of dark steel. [312] Wren had turned it over once while Ilse fetched the kettle. [323] Cut into the steel, reversed and waiting for wax, was a small crowned bee. [337] She set it down exactly where it had been and said nothing, and Ilse, returning, glanced at it and also said nothing, and the silence became the third lie. [366]

The lessons were real, at least. [372] Wren learned the ablative, learned to see a carved word as a shape a chisel had chosen. [389] She began reading the building on her way up the stairs: the dates, the pieties, a stair-rail dedication to someone's mother. [410] Only the main lintel resisted her, four words worn nearly smooth, and she suspected Ilse steered her around them the way you steer a guest around a locked door. [439] Meanwhile the report sat on her desk, finished, unsigned. [448]

The night of the meteor shower, Ilse brought no lesson, only the tray, the seal, a candle, and a stick of red wax. [471] The sky began to fall in thin bright threads over the fig tree. [484] Wren decided she was done being polite. [491] She interrogated Ilse the way she would have interrogated a witness: no preamble, questions laid down like tiles. [509] Whose mark is the bee? [514] Why is it on your seal? [520] What do the four words say? [526] Why did you never ask what I am here to do? [537]

Ilse let a meteor finish before she answered. [545] "My grandfather cut every stone in this building," she said. [555] "The bee was his mark, and the seal was his, and the lintel was his joke." [571] She wrote the four words on a napkin without looking down. [582] Wren translated them herself, slowly, and felt the roof tilt: What we agree upon stands. [597]

"Every building is a lie people consent to," Ilse said. [607] "Stone says forever and means a hundred years. [615] A door says home and means whoever is paying rent. [625] He knew that, and he built it anyway, and he wanted someone to be able to read the confession." [644]

"You knew about the report from the first day." [653]

"From the sugar." [656] Ilse smiled, and the smile was another thing Wren almost recognized. [667] "I could not stop you signing it. [674] I could only make sure the building had someone left who spoke its language when it argued for itself." [693]

She softened the wax over the candle and pressed the crowned bee into it, then slid the seal across the table into Wren's hand. [717] It was warm, and it fit her palm as if her palm had been measured for it. [734] Above them the sky kept spending itself, each streak a brief bright falsehood that it would last. [751]

Wren went downstairs, read the lintel aloud in the dark, and tore the report in half. [767] It was not the truth, exactly. [773] But it was something she was willing to agree to. [783]
