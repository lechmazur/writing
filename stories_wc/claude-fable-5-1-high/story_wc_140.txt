The paper said she died on a Tuesday, and in a port city paper outranks breath. [16] A clerk at the harbor hospital had held two charts and one pen. [29] The woman in the next bed, the one who had swallowed lye, was wheeled to the morgue under a stranger's name, and the stranger who walked out three days later found her bank account closed, her lease voided, her face useless at every counter. [73] She kept one thing from that ward: the label peeled from the bottle the other woman had drunk, a skull faded to a thumbprint, the word CAUSTIC still legible. [102] It was the only document that agreed with her. [111] Somebody else had drunk this. [116] Somebody else was dead. [120]

Now she lives among the containers at Pier Nine, where stacked steel makes a labyrinth six high and a mile deep, and nobody is required to have a name. [149] The head injury that put her in the hospital took something with it. [162] She does not filter the world the way she once did. [173] Facts slide off her; voices catch. [179] A tide table is meaningless, but a stevedore saying "my old man used to check the tide with his thumb" stays in her for weeks, textured as rope. [207] She cannot remember her own mother's kitchen. [214] She can remember, precisely, a Filipino deckhand describing his mother's kitchen, the smell of vinegar and burnt sugar, the radio that only got one station. [239]

So she collects. [242] The port keeps time by water. [248] At high tide the ships come alongside and the yard fills with men on break, leaning against corrugated walls, talking the way people talk when they think the steel is empty. [279] She moves through the alleys of containers as the water rises, and she listens. [293] A crane operator whose brother drowned in the Baltic. [302] Two customs women arguing about whether a grandmother had really hidden gold in a piano. [317] A night watchman who says his whole village was renamed after the war and now appears on no map at all. [338] She takes only fragments, never whole stories; whole stories belong to someone. [350] Fragments are driftwood. [353]

At low tide the yard empties, the water pulls back to show the black pilings, and she climbs to the container she has claimed on the fourth tier, its manifest torn away, its doors stenciled with a company that no longer exists. [395] There she layers. [398] The deckhand's kitchen goes under the crane operator's Baltic, which goes under the watchman's vanished village. [414] She presses them together the way silt presses into stone, one tide's worth over another, and she waits for them to set. [436]

She is not trying to build a life. [444] She is trying to learn what a memory feels like from the inside, so that if one of her own ever surfaces she will recognize it by its grain. [473] Does a real memory have weight? [479] Does it have temperature? [483] The watchman's village, when she turns it over, is cold and slightly damp. [496] The kitchen is warm and sticks to her fingers. [505] She suspects her own past, if it exists, is neither, and that worries her. [519]

One evening, at the slack water between tides, she overhears a longshoreman telling a new hire about a woman who drank lye in the harbor hospital and how the family got a call two weeks later from a collections agency, because according to the county she was still alive and behind on rent. [572] "They buried her under a name nobody knew," he says. [582] "Nobody could find the other one." [588] The new hire laughs. [592] The longshoreman does not. [596] "She was my cousin," he says. [602]

She stands very still in the alley between two containers. [612] This is the first fragment that has ever fit her own shape. [624] She takes the label out of her jacket and holds it against the story, and for one moment the paper has a texture she recognizes: not warm, not cold, but exactly the weight of a thing that happened to her. [664] So this is what it feels like. [671] She does not step out. [676] She does not say a name, because she does not have one to give, and his cousin's name belongs to his cousin. [698] She climbs to the fourth tier as the water begins to rise again, and she lays the label down first, at the bottom, the way you lay a foundation. [727] Then she layers the longshoreman's voice over it, and the kitchen over that, and the village, and the Baltic, and she sits with her hands flat on the pile like a woman warming herself at something that has finally started to burn.
