Everyone in Halvern called Odile Marsh the booth lady, and nobody meant it kindly. [14] She was seventy-one, wore a raincoat in August, and spent her afternoons polishing the glass of telephone booths that had not carried a call in thirty years. [41] Coastal Telephonic had folded when she was forty, and the city had kept its booths as heritage ornaments, sealed and painted, like the shells of something extinct. [68] What nobody knew was that Odile still held the master key. [79] It hung on a bootlace under her collar, a brass thing stamped with a dead company's name, and it opened every booth from the rail yards to the lagoon. [108]

The Ministry of Record had issued its ninth revision that spring. [119] Textbooks were recalled, plaques were quietly swapped, and the Auric Lagoon, where forty dredge workers had drowned the night the company scuttled their boats, became a recreational basin, golden since the founding, a lovely place for the Founding Night fireworks. [159] Nobody marched. [161] That was the part that frightened Odile most: not the lie, but the shrug. [175] A tyrant who beat you at least admitted you were there. [186] Indifference simply revised you out. [191]

Her cell had a plan for Founding Night, and by four o'clock the plan was in pieces. [208] The printer's van was seized at the bridge with two thousand copies of the original harbor ledger. [225] The courier carrying the flyers was arrested at his mother's house. [236] By five, the messages on Odile's phone said only: line is down, stay home. [250] She read them twice, typed back hold please, and put on the raincoat. [263] Thirty years ago she had been the night operator at the Coastal exchange, and she knew something the Ministry had never bothered to learn, because it had never bothered to think of her at all. [298] The copper was still in the ground. [305] The booths were still wired. [310] And the pier at the lagoon still had its old tide horns, which had once been connected to the exchange so that an operator could warn the dredge crews of weather. [341] For six years she had spent her pension on tape, splicing the testimony of widows and sons onto reels in the abandoned exchange, waiting for a night when the city would stand at the water. [376]

The trouble was reaching it. [381] Checkpoints sat on every avenue to the lagoon, and the cell's route map had been in the printer's van. [400] At dusk Odile stood at the corner of Wren Street and looked down instead of ahead. [416] Where Coastal had buried its cables, the pavement had settled over the decades into shallow troughs, and the rain had found them. [438] The puddles ran in a broken line down the alley, each holding a rectangle of amber sky, and in the amber she could see the reflected roof of the next booth, and beyond it the next. [474] The city had drawn her a map out of its own forgetting. [486] She followed the water. [490] The puddles led through service lanes and a shuttered laundry yard, under a rail arch, never touching an avenue, because Coastal's engineers had always preferred the cheap ground behind things. [520] Twice a patrol passed the mouth of a lane and saw an old woman in a raincoat picking her way around puddles. [542] Nobody looked twice. [545]

The lagoon opened before her at full dark. [553] Its water was gold even at night, stained by a century of copper tailings, and the stars lay on it like coins dropped in a fountain. [579] Thousands stood along the shore waiting for fireworks, holding paper cups, holding children, holding nothing in particular. [596] The last booth stood at the foot of the pier, painted the Ministry's cheerful blue. [611] Odile drew the key from under her collar. [619] The lock had not turned in three decades; she had oiled it, a little at a time, for six years, and it turned now without a sound. [646] Inside, the receiver was cold and heavy, the way it had been when the whole city passed through her fingers. [666] She dialed the exchange. [670] Across the lagoon, in a building everyone believed was empty, a relay closed and a reel began to turn. [689] The tide horns coughed, cleared their throats after thirty years, and spoke. [701] A woman's voice, cracked with age and tape, said the name of her husband and the number of his boat. [721] Then another voice, and another, forty names reappearing over the golden water one by one, the way stars do when your eyes adjust. [744] Along the shore the paper cups stopped moving. [752] Odile leaned against the glass she had polished for six years and kept the receiver pressed to her ear, because a line stays open only as long as someone holds it. [783]
