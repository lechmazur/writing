The grotto at Saltmarrow gave up a new floor every morning, scrubbed to glass by the retreating tide. [18] Ines Vardy arrived with the low water, carrying a canvas satchel, a coil of string, and her father's altitude gauge. [38] The needle had frozen at two thousand one hundred and forty meters on the day his balloon went out over the water and did not come back. [65] She was not the sort to weep at a needle. [75] She was the sort to ask what a needle knew. [85]

"A gauge is a witness," she told the grotto, unpacking her balloons. [97] They were small, no larger than a lung, each fitted with a paper tag and a return address. [115] Trial balloons, her father would have called them: cheap questions you send up before you risk the expensive one. [134] She released the first at the grotto mouth and watched it climb, lucidly watchful, her pencil already moving. [152] Wind from the southwest, eleven knots, drift toward the headland. [162] The second went north. [166] The third hung above the cliff as if deciding, then vanished inland. [178]

Ines had trained as a mathematician, which people mistook for a cure against dreaming. [192] It was not; it was a discipline for dreaming accurately. [202] Her thesis had been on branching systems, the way a single condition splinters into a fan of outcomes, each one as real to its own observers as this one was to her. [234] Somewhere along that fan, she reasoned, a wind had turned two degrees. [246] Somewhere a fishing boat had been looking up instead of down. [257] She could not visit those timelines, but she could count them. [268] That was the equation she had come to the grotto to build: not where is he, which the sea had answered, but in how many worlds did he land. [297] An equation for hope, if such a thing could be made rigorous. [309]

The tags came back over the following weeks, some in envelopes, some tucked under stones at the grotto mouth by villagers who had heard what she was doing and did not quite know how to say they approved. [347] Found in a hedge at Corran. [353] Found on a trawler deck, forty miles out. [361] Found by my daughter, who wants to know if you are the balloon lady. [375]

Ines did not file the tags. [381] She pinned them to the grotto walls with limpet shells and strips of ribbon, one column for each day's wind, each row for the distance flown. [407] Green ribbon for landfall. [411] Blue for open water. [415] Red for the ones never returned. [421] Decoration, a stranger might have said, seeing the shells glint in patterns down the polished stone. [437] But a pattern is only decoration until you learn to read it, and Ines could read this one from across the grotto: a fan, opening. [462] Green outnumbered blue by a margin that held steady week after week, no matter how the weather changed. [480]

Every high tide took the lowest rows, of course. [489] She would come back to find the bottom of her chart wiped clean, the stone shining and blank, and she would rebuild it from her notebook while the water was out. [520] It should have felt like loss. [526] Instead it felt like the sea checking her work. [535]

On the last morning of spring she came down with a single balloon, larger than the others, and the gauge. [555] She had cleaned its mechanism the night before, oiled the pivot, freed the needle from its long testimony. [573] It rested at zero now, revitalized, waiting to be asked a question. [585] She tied it beneath the balloon with the good knot he had taught her. [599] The tag read: This instrument once stopped at 2,140 meters. [609] Please write down how high it goes this time, and where it comes down. [623]

Behind her the grotto walls glittered with shells and ribbon, hundreds of small journeys pinned in rows, most of them green. [644] The tide was already turning; by noon the lowest rows would be gone again, and that was all right. [663] The proof did not live on the wall. [671] It lived in the ratio, and the ratio had held. [681]

She let go. [684]

The balloon rose past the cliff edge with the gauge swinging gently beneath it, its needle lifting off zero for the first time in two years, climbing through numbers her father had once passed through on his way to a place she could not follow. [729] Ines watched it until it was a fleck, then nothing. [739] Somewhere along the fan, a child was already running toward a hedge. [751] Somewhere a trawler crew was looking up. [758] In most of the worlds she could count, the gauge would come down on land, and someone would write to tell her so. [781] That was the equation. [785] That was enough. [788]
