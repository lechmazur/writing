Ilse wove nets on the wharf until the wharf was gone. [11] The surge took the pilings, the fish-houses, and nineteen people, and left behind a beach of splintered cedar and a town that no longer slept properly. [37] She had always talked to her shuttle in half-rhymes, and the fishermen had called her the poet of knots, but there were no more knots to tie. [64] So she watched instead. [68]

Every night, after the tide pools emptied and the moon dragged the water off the flats, the survivors rose from their borrowed beds and walked. [93] They walked barefoot up the wet sand to the mouth of the canyon, where the red walls opened like a wound that had healed into a door. [120] Inside was the labyrinth, carved into the stone centuries before anyone kept records, its passages cut chest-high and deep. [139] The precedent was older than the town: only the dead went in. [151] Bodies were carried to the first turning and left, and no living foot crossed the threshold, and no one had ever drawn a map. [175] Now the living went in every night, asleep, and came out at dawn with red dust to their knees and no memory of it. [199]

The priest called it grief. [204] Ilse called it a pattern looking for a loom. [213]

She had one thing of value left, dug from the mud where her grandmother's print shop had stood: a copper illustration plate from a storybook, the one every child in town had been read to sleep with. [250] The engraving showed the labyrinth from above, a thing no one alive had ever seen, and in its heart a single flower. [272] The story said the flower belonged to the dead and the maze would swallow anyone who came for it. [291] Ilse had stopped believing that the night she saw the baker's widow walk the third passage smiling. [308]

She salvaged sailcloth from the wreck of the wharf, scrubbed it, and inked the plate the way her grandmother had taught her, pressing the image onto the canvas with the flat of a broken oar. [343] The print came out reversed, of course, as prints do. [353] That was one precedent broken; then she broke the older one and followed the sleepwalkers in. [369]

She carried the cloth and a needle and a spool of red net-twine, and each night she stitched one path onto the printed maze: the widow's route in a running stitch, the harbormaster's in a backstitch, the twins' in a line that doubled on itself where they walked hand in hand. [420] No single sleeper went far. [425] Each turned back at some wall, some dead end, and drifted home. [437] But by the twelfth night the cloth was thick with thread, and where the paths overlapped they made something none of the walkers had made alone. [463] One route, unbroken, stitched by two dozen sleeping hands. [472]

It ran against the printed lines, not with them, and Ilse understood: the book had been printed from the plate, so every dreamer in town had learned the maze backward, and their sleeping feet had spent twelve nights correcting it against real stone. [515] She turned the cloth over. [520] On the back, where the ink bled through faint as a memory, the red stitches lay down the passages exactly. [540]

The thirteenth night she did not follow anyone. [548] She walked the sewn line with the cloth held before her like a lantern, turning where the twine turned, until the walls widened and the ceiling of stars widened with them. [579] At the center, in a crack where seep-water darkened the red stone, an orchid grew. [594] Just one, pale violet, its throat the color of the hour before the tide returns. [609] Around it the dust was untouched; no one had come this far, though all of them together had. [627]

She thought of the dead left at the first turning, generation after generation, so close and so far from this. [647] She thought of the nineteen, who had no turning at all, only the sea. [661]

In the morning she strung the cloth across the canyon mouth where everyone would pass. [676] The priest said it was sacrilege. [682] Ilse said the old rules had been made by people who had never been this sad, and that sadness was allowed to make new ones. [707]

That night, after the tide pools emptied, the town walked into the labyrinth awake. [721] They carried nineteen names written on scraps of sail, and they followed the red thread, and where the walls opened they knelt in the dust around a single flower and began, for the first time, to grieve out loud. [760] Ilse stood at the back, twine in her pocket, composing something in half-rhymes about a maze that had never been meant to keep people out, only to teach them the long way in. [793]
