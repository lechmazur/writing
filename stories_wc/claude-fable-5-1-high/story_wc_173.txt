The Silver Court kept its courtesans the way it kept its snow: pristine, expected, and swept into place each dusk by a song. [23] Mother Verane sang it from the gallery, and the whole house hummed along without deciding to. [39] Ilse alone kept her mouth closed. [45] She had noticed, in her first winter, what the humming took. [56] A guest would open his lips to join the lullaby, and something faint would leave him with the note, a thin bright thread that drifted across the courtyard and settled on the prism at its center. [92] Verane called the prism a sanctuary. [98] It stood taller than a man, a spire of cut glass in the silver courtyard, and under starlight it drank whatever the song loosened: the argument a merchant had swallowed, the question a daughter never asked, the refusal a young courtesan almost gave. [141] By morning the threads had wound themselves around it in a tight, glittering cocoon, and the guests woke agreeable. [160] Verane called that the harvest. [165] Ilse called it nothing, because naming it would have required her to sing. [178]

Her silence had made her the most expensive woman in the house. [190] Men paid to sit across from her while she did nothing but move. [203] The turn of a wrist was a line of verse; the slow lowering of her lashes was a stanza's break; she could compose an elegy with the angle of her spine and never let a hand touch her. [241] Chaste, the other girls said, half mocking, half afraid. [250] What they meant was that she had given the house nothing it could wind around the glass. [267]

On the night of the deep snow, the lullaby ended and the gallery emptied, and Ilse walked out into the courtyard barefoot. [289] The cold did not hurry her. [295] Snow lay on the flagstones like a held breath, and the stars came down hard and white, and the prism stood in its cocoon of stolen voices, faintly singing to itself. [326] There was no lock on it. [332] The threads were the lock. [337] She knelt, and began to untangle them, which was the only way to unlock it. [352]

It was slow work, and she was good at slow work. [363] Each thread she loosened gave up its echo as it came free: a man's voice saying I do not want this, a child's voice asking why, a woman's voice, very young, whispering no into a pillow years ago. [401] The echoes rose and did not scatter. [408] They hung in the starlight over the courtyard, patient, waiting to go home. [421] Ilse's hands moved the way they moved for patrons, deliberate and unhurried, each gesture a phrase, and she understood that this was the same art. [446] The lullaby asked everyone to be one smooth voice. [455] Her body had only ever answered in its own. [464]

She did not hear Verane come out. [471] She felt her, the way one feels a window open in another room. [484] The old woman stood at the courtyard's edge in her nightdress, snow gathering on her shoulders, and looked at the half-bared prism and the crowd of echoes shining above it. [514] Verane sighed. [516]

Ilse kept working. [519] Between that sigh and whatever came after it, there was a space, and she meant to fill it. [537] Three threads. [539] Two. [540] The last was her own, she realized, a single grey strand the house had managed to take from her after all, some night when she had been too tired to keep her lips entirely still, and its echo was her own voice saying, very quietly, I am afraid I will become like them. [593] She pulled it free. [597]

The prism stood naked under the stars and went dark, an ordinary column of glass, and the echoes above the courtyard broke like a flock and streamed toward the sleeping windows of the house. [631] Nothing crashed. [633] No one screamed. [636] The snow went on falling with perfect composure, and inside the walls, one by one, people who had agreed to everything for years opened their eyes in the dark and remembered what they had wanted to say. [673]

Ilse stood, brushed the snow from her knees, and turned to face the woman who owned her. [690] Verane's face was calm. [694] Ilse's was calmer. [697] Tomorrow there would be no lullaby, or there would be one that no one hummed, and the house would have to learn what it sounded like full of separate voices. [727] It would be a terrible noise. [733] She found she was looking forward to it. [741]

Then, because the space she had opened was closing, and because she had spent her whole life speaking with everything but her mouth, Ilse let her body finish the poem. [771] She smiled. [773]
