The hydraulics failed on a Tuesday, and by Friday the lower town belonged to the sea. [16] Everyone went inland, in trucks and borrowed cars, the way birds go when the cold decides for them. [34] Mara went the other way. [39] She climbed the headland road to the signal station, where for a century a keeper had hoisted lamps to tell barges when the gates were open. [65] There were no gates now, only the stump of the barrage and the water moving through it as it pleased. [85]

She had been a navigator on the estuary barges for twenty years, reading sandbars by the color of the water. [105] Her coat was sewn from Tomas's shirts, flannel squares and one blue stripe, stitched the winter after he drowned in the lock chamber when the pumps first stuttered, and the crews had called her the patchwork navigator ever since. [144] She had not minded. [148] A navigator is only ever pieces of other people's knowledge held together with attention. [162]

The station had no power, but the old landline hummed, fed by some exchange nobody had thought to switch off. [182] The first call came at midnight on her third night. [192] A woman's voice, thin as thread, said, "The bakery step, the one Lena cracked her chin on," and hung up. [212] Mara sat in the dark with the receiver against her cheek for a long time. [227] Then she understood. [230] Everyone in town had known the station number; it was painted on the harbor wall, the number you called to ask if the gates would open in time. [258] Now, scattered across inland rooms, sleepless, they were calling it still. [269]

The calls came every night after that, always at midnight, never with names. [282] "Third pew from the back, the seat with the knot shaped like a fish." [296] "Forty paces from the chandlery to where the dog is buried." [307] "The window my mother watched from." [313] Nobody explained, and Mara did not ask, because she recognized the grammar. [325] It was how barge pilots talked, bearings and landmarks, the shorthand of people who needed to find something again in the dark. [347]

So she began to build. [352] She unrolled the station's old tide charts across the floor, blank on the back, and started drawing the town from what the callers gave her. [377] Every map needs a datum, a fixed point from which all distances are true. [391] Hers was a bronze censer foot, green with patina, that had washed up below the headland the week the church went under. [413] Tomas had swung that censer at Easter for thirty years; she knew the foot by the dent where he had dropped it once, laughing, on the vestry stone. [441] She set it on the chart where the altar had stood and measured everything from there. [457]

The line was not steady. [462] It fluctuated with the moon; at the new and full moons, when the spring tides drove furthest up the drowned streets, the voices came clear and close, as if the water itself carried them. [496] In the quarter moons they dissolved into static and she caught only fragments, "the gull weathervane," "her blue door," and drew those in pencil, to be confirmed. [523]

Some nights she said things back. [529] "The chamber ladder, seventh rung, where the rust bit his palm." [540] "The kitchen chair he never fixed." [546] There was always a silence on the other end, and then a breath that meant someone had written it down. [566]

By the first full moon of autumn the chart covered the whole floor. [579] It was not the town as it had been; it was the town as it was missed, every street bent toward the places where something had happened to somebody. [608]

That night the phone rang and a man's voice she almost knew said, "Are you going to light it?" [627] She looked up through the station's glass at the stars, the only lamps the headland had shown for months. [646] Below, the tide was running out through the broken barrage, back toward the sea, and against it, low over the water, a line of geese was flying north, the wrong way for the season, or the right way for something she did not have a word for yet. [694]

"Yes," she said. [697] She filled the signal lamp with the last of the oil, trimmed the wick, and hoisted it up the mast the way keepers had for a century, one steady light meaning: the way is open, come through. [734] Somewhere inland, in a dozen dark kitchens, she imagined them standing at windows, facing the coast, unable to see it and seeing it anyway. [758] The censer foot sat on the altar of the chart, cold and green, and she kept her hand on it until morning. [780]
