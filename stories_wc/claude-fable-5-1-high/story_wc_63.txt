The pendulum bob of the Saltreach clock is a brass disc the width of a cartwheel, and Ilse Varn has watched it cross the tower's dark for thirty-one years. [29] She winds the weights each dawn, precise to the quarter-turn, because the tower stands on the obsidian ridge and its bell is the only warning the town has before the tide closes the crossing. [63] Twice a day the sea peels back and the ridge emerges, black glass steaming, and twice a day it drowns again. [84] Between those two drownings, the bell must ring true. [93] The town calls her the most exacting winder it has ever kept. [105] No one knows why. [109]

Beside the winding drum, wrapped in oilcloth, lies half a rake handle, ash wood, broken clean at the ferrule. [128] Along its length, in letters no taller than a grain of barley, run sixteen hundred words, one for every week since the night the clock stopped. [154] She carves the newest one tonight, kneeling with a needle-file while the bob swings above her. [170] The word is Maren. [174] It is the last one the wood has room for. [184]

The pendulum reaches the top of its arc and, as it does every second, stops. [199] Ilse has always lived in that pause. [206] To anyone else it is nothing, a held breath between ticks. [217] To her it opens like a furnace door. [225]

She is nineteen again and late. [231] The tower door has swollen shut in the wet, and Tobiah's rake is leaning where he always leaves it after clearing weed from the ridge steps so the fishers' children can cross without slipping. [265] She jams the handle into the gap and levers. [274] The ash snaps like a bone. [280] She gets in, but the weights have run down; the bell misses the evening tide by nine minutes. [298] Nine minutes is the difference between Tobiah walking the steps with his lantern to see why the tower is silent, and Tobiah at his own table. [324] They found the lantern wedged in the obsidian. [332] They found the broken rake head and said the old man had been careless, had gone out without his tool to steady him. [355] Ilse said nothing. [358] She had the handle under her coat. [365]

She could not bear a single confession; it would have been a stone dropped, a splash, and then the town going on around the ripples. [390] So she chose the slower arithmetic. [396] She took the winder's post when it fell vacant and kept the bell to the second for thirty-one years, so that no one else would ever be nine minutes late. [426] And each week she carved one true word into the wood that had failed him, building the account she owed a syllable at a time: the hour, the swollen door, the sound of the ash breaking, the nine minutes, her own name. [468] She told herself the debt was paid out in increments, that each word bought back a grain of the balance she had knocked from the town. [494] She knew, too, that this was a lie she carved to keep herself carving. [508] Absolution taken in private is only patience wearing a better coat. [519]

The pause holds. [522]

Below the tower, Maren rakes the ridge steps now, her father's work, with a new-handled tool. [538] She has done it since she was twelve, believing what she was told. [551] Every low tide she walks past the tower and lifts a hand to the woman who keeps the bell so faithfully, and every low tide Ilse lifts hers back, and the black glass between them shines like something cooling that will never quite cool. [595] That is the balance still owed. [601] Not Ilse's guilt against Tobiah's death, which no ledger closes, but the town's story against the truth of it, Maren's careless father against the careful man he was. [629] The words have been building toward one reader all along. [639] The wood has simply run out of room for her to pretend otherwise. [652]

The bob falls back through its arc. [659] Tick. [660]

Ilse wraps the handle, checks the weights out of thirty-one years of habit, and finds them exactly where they should be. [681] The bell will ring the morning tide without her. [690] She goes down the stair and out onto the ridge while the sea is still drawn back, the obsidian slick and steaming under the first light, and she does not slip, because Maren's work is good. [726] At the far end of the steps a woman with a rake looks up. [740] Ilse holds out the oilcloth bundle with both hands, the way one hands over something heavy. [756]

"It begins at the broken end," she says. [764] "Read it there first. [768] "It will take you a while. [774] "I have nowhere I need to be." [781]

Behind her, right on the second, the bell begins. [790]
