On the third night of the forest elections, when the pines below Halden had closed every trail to cast their slow root-votes, Ines climbed the lavender bluff because it was the only ground still open to people. [37] The aurora was already up, green and violet, sagging over the valley like a curtain that had forgotten which window it belonged to. [60] She glowed a little herself, the way people her age did, a faint blue seeping through the skin of her forearms from the lumen implants everyone had gotten at twelve and nobody made anymore. [94] It made her easy to find in the dark and hard to take seriously, which suited a skeptic fine. [113]

Ines did not believe that some people carried weather with them. [124] She believed in microclimate drones, in humidity vests, in the human hunger to be exceptional. [139] But she had seen the man at the tram stop who stood in his own drizzle while the pavement around him stayed dry, and she had come up here to find out how he did it. [175]

On her smallest finger she wore her grandmother's tin compass ring, a cereal prize from a century that still printed things on boxes. [198] The needle had never pointed north in her lifetime. [207] Her grandmother used to say that was the point. [216]

The bluff was crowded with the displaced: hikers, foragers, a few election observers with their listening rods, all of them waiting for the woods to finish deciding. [243] Ines walked the rows of lavender and did what she always did when she wanted the truth: she paid attention to her mouth. [266] She had learned young that lies tasted like pennies and that fear tasted like the inside of a battery, and she trusted her tongue more than any instrument the university had ever handed her. [300]

Near the eastern edge the lavender turned to ozone on her palate, sharp and sweet, and she stopped. [318] A woman sat there with frost on her shoulders in the middle of a warm night, breathing steam. [336] Ines checked her for vents, for hidden coolant, for anything that would let her go home satisfied. [353] There was nothing, only the taste of a storm that had not arrived. [366]

Then the drizzle man came up the path, and the taste changed to wet stone. [381] A boy with heat shimmer rising off his scalp arrived from the west, and her mouth went dry and dusty. [401] None of them had planned to meet. [408] They were simply converging, drifting toward one another across the bluff the way water finds the low place in a field. [429]

Ines looked down at the tin ring. [436] The needle was pointing at the spot where their paths would cross. [448] She turned her hand, and the needle held. [456]

Her first thought was magnetism, some local anomaly, but tin is not magnetic and neither is a cereal prize. [475] Her second thought was harder to dismiss: the ring had never been the compass. [489] The compass was the whole slope, the frost and the drizzle and the heat, the forests below humming their ballots up through the roots, the aurora above spilling charged particles down, and somewhere in the middle, small enough to miss, the taste on one skeptic's tongue. [535] The direction emerged from all of it at once, the way a flock has a shape no single bird decided. [555]

The frost woman looked up at her and smiled without surprise. [566] "You taste it," she said. [571] "Most of us hear it, or feel it in the teeth." [582]

Ines wanted to say that she was not one of them, that she was here to explain them, but her mouth was full of rain now, cold and clean, and it was not coming from the man or the sky. [622] "Why," she managed. [625] "Why do you carry it?" [630]

The woman considered the aurora, then the dark voting woods, then the blue light leaking from Ines's arms. [648] "We don't carry it," she said. [654] "We're just where the ground and the sky keep meeting." [664] "Everyone used to be, before people stopped listening for it." [674] "Your grandmother didn't stop, did she?" [680]

Ines remembered her grandmother's kitchen, the way the windows fogged on clear days when the old woman was sad, and how nobody in the family had ever called that weather. [710]

Below them, the forests finished their count, and a long sigh of pollen went up through the aurora. [728] The tin needle swung, loose again, satisfied. [735] Ines stood in the lavender with rain on her lips and her arms glowing a soft, obsolete blue, a small terrestrially cosmic thing, embarrassed and finally paying attention. [763] She still did not believe in much. [770] But she believed in her mouth, and her mouth said the storm had been hers all along. [787]
