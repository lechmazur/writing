Ilse Varga had kept seeds for sixty-one years, and she had kept the atrium for the last nine of them, alone. [21] The glass dome had frozen from the inside after the heating failed, so the ferns and the fig trees stood locked in their own breath, and at night the stars came through the ice in smeared, patient constellations. [59] She did not mind the cold. [65] Cold was how seeds waited. [70]

In the center of the atrium, where the old fountain had been, the inheritance sat closed. [86] Her grandmother had called it the vortex, though when it was shut it looked like nothing, a disc of black ice the size of a millstone, perfectly still. [114] Open, it turned. [117] Open, it ran things backward. [122] A rotted bean dropped into it came out hard and glossy and ready to plant. [137] That was the whole family trade, five generations of it: they saved heirloom seeds, and when a line failed, when weevils or damp or war took the last of some ancient pea, the keeper carried its husk to the vortex and let it decrease the entropy of the world by one seed. [189]

Nothing is free, her grandmother said. [195] Order here is paid for with disorder somewhere close. [204] It only reopens when something nearby is spending itself. [213]

Ilse had the wren's egg inside her coat. [221] She had found it in December, whole and absurd, in a nest frozen into the dead ferns, and she had carried it against her breastbone for nineteen days because she had nothing else warm to offer it. [258] In her other pocket she carried the oak acorn cap. [268]

It was the last thing her grandmother had put into her hand: a small rough cup, empty, its nut long gone to squirrels or rot. [293] The oak it came from had been the first tree the family saved, in a country that no longer had that name, and no acorn from it existed anywhere on earth. [324] Only the cap. [327] Only the shape of where it had been. [335]

Her grandmother had never dared. [340] To pull a whole acorn back out of an empty cap would take more time than a seed's worth, and the vortex did not make time, it borrowed it. [369] Ilse was seventy-eight. [372] She had decided she wanted to know what borrowed time felt like before she ran out of the ordinary kind. [392]

Near midnight the egg moved. [397] She felt it as a tap, then a scrape, and she knelt on the frost beside the black disc and opened her coat so the starlight fell on the shell. [427] A crack ran around it like a thought. [435] Then a beak, then a wet gray head, then the whole ugly furious scrap of a bird, steaming in the cold, burning its little store of yolk as fast as any fire. [467] That was the payment. [471] That was disorder, spent gladly, close at hand. [479]

The disc began to turn. [484] It did not creak or grind; it simply was moving, and then moving faster, a black spiral under the stars with a wind coming off it that smelled of wet loam and August. [517] The rim of it screamed. [522] The center of it did not move at all. [531] Furiously tranquil, her grandmother had said, and Ilse had not understood the phrase for fifty years, and now she did. [551]

She held the wren in her left hand, against her heartbeat, and with her right she set the acorn cap into the still eye of the vortex. [578] She kept her hand there. [583] This was the part no keeper had done. [591]

The texture of borrowed time, it turned out, was grain. [601] It ran through her fingers like the grain in a plank, straight and tight, and it ran the wrong way, from the tip of the finger toward the wrist, so that the ache in her knuckles loosened and the burn scar on her thumb went pink and then went away. [651] Under her palm the cap filled. [657] She felt it swell against her skin, smooth and hard and slightly warm, an acorn assembling itself out of the scattered dark, entropy running downhill toward a single perfect thing. [687]

The wren in her other hand shivered and went on burning. [698] She could feel the two of them balanced through her body, the bird spending and the acorn saving, and herself the wire between. [721] It was not peaceful, and it was not violent. [730] It was both at once, the way a river is, and she knelt inside it under the smeared stars and laughed out loud in the frozen atrium for the first time in nine years. [764]

When the wren stopped shivering and began, instead, to cheep for food, the vortex slowed and sealed itself to black glass. [785] She opened her hands: one whole acorn, one hungry bird. [795]
