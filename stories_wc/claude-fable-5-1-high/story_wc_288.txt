The cloud tide went out at dawn, uncovering the lower stacks one shelf at a time, and Ilse woke the way she had woken every morning since the memory arrived: brittle, as if her bones had been fired too hot and set out to cool in the wrong body. [49] For eleven months she had kept the tidal library alone. [59] For three days she had also kept a second life, one that had surfaced whole while she slept: a girl on a black rock in a cold sea, watching men in a rowboat lift the last two great auks by their necks. [101] She had been their contemporary. [106] She had breathed the same salt air as a species, and then she had outlived it, and then she had died and forgotten, and now she remembered. [133] The remembering was the easy part. [139] The waking was what cracked. [144] Each morning she had to decide, in the first grey second, which life to stand up into, and each morning the choice left a hairline fracture somewhere she could not point to. [176]

She climbed to the terrace because the sundial needed her, and because needing was a thing she could still answer. [196] The brass sundial had never told time. [203] Some keeper generations before her had polished its gnomon past brightness, past mirror, down to a surface so atomically reflective that it refused a shadow altogether. [229] Instead it threw the sun back as a single needle of light, and the keeper's task was to angle the dial, hour by hour, so that the needle fell through the gap in the cloud and struck the ancient stone circle on the moor a mile below. [276] Struck, the stones hummed. [280] The hum rose through the cloud and held the library up. [291] That was all anyone knew of the mechanism, and it was enough: light down, hum up, shelves aloft, tide in and out on the breath of the stones. [319]

Ilse set her palms on the cold brass and turned it a degree. [332] Far below, the hum shifted pitch, and she felt the fracture in her chest go quiet, the way a glass stops ringing when you touch it. [358] This was the discovery of the third day. [366] The hum did not only carry the library. [374] It carried her too, both of her, the keeper and the girl on the rock, and it did not ask them to become one thing. [399] In the shelter of that sound she could let the two lives coexist, side by side, the way the auks had once stood shoulder to shoulder on their ledge without needing to be the same bird. [435]

She thought of the letters. [440] Eleven of them, one a month, sent down on the cloud tide to the guild, each asking when her relief would come. [462] None had been answered. [466] She had spent the year waiting the way the girl on the rock had spent her life waiting: for the boats to turn back, for someone with authority to arrive and say stop. [499] Nobody had. [501] The auks were gone and the girl had grown old on the rock and died still waiting, and that, Ilse understood now, was the true shape of the memory: not the birds, but the waiting. [536] She would not be given that lesson twice. [544] Being alone had been done to her. [551] Being independent was something she could do back. [559]

That afternoon, when the tide came in and drowned the terrace in white, she did not go down to write a twelfth letter. [582] She went instead to the catalog room and found the entry for Pinguinus impennis, which read, in another keeper's hand, extinct, no living witness. [606] She struck through the last three words and wrote: one, remembering. [617] Then she went back up through the fog by feel, found the brass by its cold, and turned it the degree the hour required without waiting for the cloud to part and show her whether she had done it right. [657] The hum answered anyway. [661]

The next morning she woke brittle again; the memory had not changed, and she did not expect it to. [680] But she stood up into both lives at once, and nothing cracked, because she had stopped asking the morning to decide for her. [703] Below the receding cloud, the stones were already humming, waiting for the light she would send them. [720] It was, she thought, the only kind of waiting worth doing: the kind where you are the one who arrives. [740]
