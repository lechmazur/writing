The shell had been a city for four hundred years before Wren stopped sleeping in it. [16] Nobody knew what creature had built the thing, only that it lay along the dead shoreline like a fallen moon, its spiral wide enough to hold nine thousand people and the markets, chapels, and cisterns they had ensconced in its chambers. [57] The sea that made it had withdrawn generations ago. [66] The coral had not. [70] It crept inward through the shell's pearl walls, pink and bone and violet, a slow tide of stone that sealed doorways and swallowed stairs, and every spring the burning crews went out with lye and torches to push it back. [110]

Wren had been on a crew once. [117] She had heard the polyps hiss as they died, and after that the sleep simply left her, the way a cat leaves a house where something has been done wrong. [147] So she wrote instead. [151] Poems, mostly, scratched on whatever came to hand, because a poem was the only thing she knew that could take the raw ache of three in the morning and turn it, leaf-like, toward some kind of light. [188] Photosynthesis, she called it, half joking. [194] Pain in, beauty out. [198]

Her favorite writing surface was a galaxy postcard she had found wedged in the coral as a child: a glossy spray of stars on one side, and on the other, in a hand faded to ghost-brown, the words I will come back when it blooms. [243] No date. [245] No name. [247] She had filled the margins with tiny verses for twenty years and never once written over that sentence. [265]

It was the wind that finally told her what the coral wanted. [277] The shell was a horn, after all. [284] On restless nights the offshore wind pushed up the spiral and came out the far chambers as sound, always a little wrong, a little flat, like someone humming a tune they had learned from a parent who had learned it from a grave. [327] Everyone else slept through it. [332] Wren, awake, listened, and one night she realized the coral was listening too. [345]

It grew toward the sound. [350] Not toward the water it had lost, not toward the sun, but toward those half-sung lullabies, leaning into the off-key drafts the way a sleeper leans toward a voice. [379] The coral was not invading. [384] It was lonely. [387] It had been lonely since the ocean left, growing through centuries of longing toward the only thing that still sang to it. [409]

Wren was not a singer. [414] She was, in fact, terrible, which turned out not to matter, because the wind was terrible too. [431] She began carrying her poems to the mouth of the spiral at night and singing them badly into the draft, letting the shell carry her voice up through the chambers where the coral pressed against the walls. [468] She felt absurd. [471] She kept going anyway, shyly unstoppable, the way she had kept going on every sleepless night of her life. [490]

By the third week the coral had stopped advancing. [499] By the fifth, it began to open. [506] Polyps the size of fists unfurled in the doorways it had once threatened, and where the burning crews would have found stone they found flowers, translucent and trembling, each one turned toward the sound of a bad poem sung worse. [546]

The council called it the blossom armistice, because councils need names for things. [559] The terms were simple. [563] The coral would grow only where it was sung to, and no one would burn it again, and every night someone who could not sleep would go to the mouth of the shell and give it a voice. [601] There were, it turned out, more insomniacs in the city than anyone had admitted. [615]

Wren went to the deepest chamber on the first night of the truce, where the old coral was, the coral that had been growing since the sea left. [643] It had bloomed too, enormous and pale, and the light coming through its petals lay across the floor in shifting blue and green, like the shadow of a reef seen from below by something that had finally learned to breathe. [683]

She took out the galaxy postcard. [689] She read the faded sentence one more time, then tucked it gently into the heart of the oldest blossom, ensconcing it there where the coral had kept it for who knew how long before a child pried it loose. [728] Whoever had written it had been singing to this coral too, in their way. [742] It had bloomed. [745] They had come back, or something of them had. [754]

Wren sat down against the warm stone and sang, off-key, a lullaby she did not know all the words to. [774] Somewhere in the second verse, for the first time in years, she fell asleep. [788]
