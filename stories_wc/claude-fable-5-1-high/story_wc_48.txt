Ines Varga had written eleven novels, and in every one of them she had hidden a stranger. [17] A ferry cook in chapter nine of the second book, stirring soup for two paragraphs and then gone. [35] A girl selling wet almonds outside a station in the fifth, who looked up once and said the trains were late because the sea was breathing. [61] Critics called these cameos a tic. [67] They were not a tic. [72] They were her collection. [76]

Each stranger had been real, and each had told her, when asked, what they hoped for, and she had kept the shape of it: the cook wanted a daughter to call; the almond girl wanted a room with a window that opened. [118] Not the hopes themselves, which were private, but their shapes, the way a shell keeps the shape of a creature that has left. [141]

Now the twelfth book was almost finished, and she could not end it. [154] Her editor wanted the protagonist to reach the city, to win, to be carried forward on the current the whole book had built. [177] Ines wanted him to turn around. [183] She did not know why, and not knowing frightened her more than any deadline. [197]

So she went, at four in the morning, to the estuary. [208]

It was the place she had first begun collecting, twenty years ago, where the river met the sea and neither agreed to yield. [231] Mist lay over the mudflats in slow coils. [239] Reeds stood up through it like the masts of drowned boats, and somewhere a heron lifted with the sound of a coat being shaken out. [264] Nothing here had been tended. [269] The channels changed their minds each season; the salt crept up the willows and killed them from the roots; the tide came in against the river twice a day and pushed the fresh water backward up its own bed. [308] It was a wild knot of meetings, and it belonged to no one. [321]

She sat on the flat stone she always sat on and took the shell fragment from her coat pocket. [340] It was a piece of something that had once been whole, a whelk perhaps, broken so that only the inner spiral remained, a staircase to nowhere the size of her thumb. [371] She had found it on this stone the morning she decided to become a writer instead of something sensible. [390] She had thought then that it looked like a plot: everything curving inward toward a center you could never quite see. [411]

She watched. [413] Not dreaming; she had never trusted dreaming. [420] She watched the way she had taught herself to watch on train platforms and in ferry galleys, lucid, alert to every small motion, letting the mist decide what to show her. [451]

The tide was turning. [455] She could hear it before she saw it, a low argument beginning at the mouth of the channel, and then the surface of the river wrinkled and began, very slowly, to move upstream. [488]

A figure stood in the reeds where no figure had been. [499] A man in a white apron, stirring nothing, looking toward the hills. [511] Then the mist closed and he was a heron, or a post, or nothing. [525] Farther along the bank, a girl crouched with a paper cone in her hands, and lifted her face, and was gone. [546]

Ines did not believe in ghosts. [552] She believed in mist, and in a mind that had spent twenty years arranging strangers into rows. [569] But she understood, sitting very still with the shell warm in her fist, what the closing pages needed. [587]

Her protagonist would turn back, yes. [593] He would walk against the whole forward pull of the book, and as he walked, he would pass them: the cook, the almond girl, all eleven strangers from all eleven novels, each appearing for a sentence or two and then dissolving. [634] Not to explain anything. [638] To be witnessed. [641] The ending would not be a victory but a rite, a way to solemnize every hope she had ever carried out of the mist in the shape of a shell. [671]

The editor would call it indulgent. [677] Readers who wanted the city would feel cheated. [685] She found she could hold that, the way the estuary held two waters that refused each other and made, out of the refusal, something neither could make alone. [713]

The tide reached her stone and touched the toe of her boot. [725] The mist, which had been everywhere, thinned into ribbons and began to lift, turning slowly in the first gray light like something stirred from below. [750] Ines stood and put the fragment back in her pocket, where it had been for twenty years, and would be for the few hours it took to write the last six pages. [782] Then she walked home along the river, upstream, the same direction the sea was going. [797]
