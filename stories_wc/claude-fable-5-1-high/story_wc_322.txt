Mireille Osk designed fever dreams for a living, and she did it the way other architects designed bridges: with load calculations. [21] The Institute called her practice rational enchantment, which meant every impossible thing she built had to obey at least one rule. [42] A staircase could climb forever, but it had to climb. [52] A door could open onto the sea, but the sea had to be wet. [66] Patients healed faster inside dreams that made sense, or almost made sense, and that "almost" was where Mireille earned her fee. [87]

The commission that autumn was a man named Teodor Vance, forty-one, admitted with a fever that would not break and a wife who visited every day and was never remembered. [117] "His memories are altering," the physician said, sliding the chart across. [128] "Each time the fever peaks, they rearrange. [135] We need something that holds." [140]

Mireille chose a gallery. [144] Galleries were honest about being looked at, and optical illusions were honest about lying, which made them the most rational enchantment she knew. [167] She wove it from sinew thread, as she wove everything, because sinew was the only material that behaved the same in a body and in a dream. [194] By weaving patterns tight enough, she could make a wall that was realistically imaginary: a thing you knew was not there and still would not walk through. [221]

She had a private reason for the commission, one she did not put in the proposal. [237] Twenty years of building rooms for strangers to lie in, and she had never learned what connection actually felt like under the hand. [260] Not the idea of it. [265] The texture. [267] Teodor forgot his wife every night and met her again every morning, and Mireille wanted to know what was holding, in the dark, when the memory was gone. [295]

She went in at the third fever. [302]

The gallery was quiet in the way of places that have been waiting. [315] The floor was a checkerboard that tilted when she did not look at it. [329] On the walls hung the illusions she had woven: two lines of equal length that refused to agree, a vase that was two faces, a corridor painted so that its far end sat closer than its near one. [367] Teodor stood before the last one with his hands in his pockets. [379]

"I keep thinking someone should be at the end of that," he said. [392]

"Who?" [393]

"I don't know. [396] That's the trouble. [399] The shape of her is right there, but the face keeps changing." [411]

Mireille felt the fever climb. [416] It came through the floor first, a warmth in the sinew, the whole gallery breathing out. [432] As his memories altered, the pictures altered with them. [441] The two faces in the vase became one face, then a face she recognized from the visitor's log, then a stranger. [462] She had built for this. [467] Every pattern was woven with slack in the right places, so the walls could shift without tearing. [484]

What she had not built for was the corridor. [493]

Teodor took a step toward it, and the painted far end came forward to meet him, and the sinew under the paint began to pull. [518] Not tear. [520] Pull, the way a tendon pulls when a hand closes. [530] The wall erupted, not outward but inward, thread unspooling from the picture into the room and reaching for him, and Mireille understood, quite calmly, that she had never been the one weaving. [562] She had only ever supplied the loom. [569]

The thread wrapped his wrist. [574] It was warm and slightly damp and it had a pulse that was not his. [589] He did not flinch. [593] "There she is," he said, though the end of the corridor was still empty and would be empty tomorrow when he woke and asked the nurse who the woman in the chair was. [626]

Mireille reached out and laid two fingers on the thread where it crossed his skin. [641] This was what she had come for. [648] She had expected something like silk, or something like a rope. [659] It was neither. [662] It was the texture of a thing that had been torn and had knitted itself, over and over, without ever once being asked whether it wanted to. [689] Scar tissue, she thought. [693] Connection is scar tissue that has learned to hold. [702]

The fever broke at four in the morning. [710] The gallery went dark room by room, in order, as she had designed it to. [725] Mireille filed her report and wrote that the structure had held, which was true. [739] She did not write that when she took her fingers from the thread, it had taken a moment to let her go. [761] Nor that she went home and sat in her own kitchen and pressed two fingers to the inside of her wrist, and waited to see if anything there would pull back. [792]
