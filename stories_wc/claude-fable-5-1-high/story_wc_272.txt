Maren had stopped painting faces the day her grandmother's face refused to hold still in her memory. [17] Every portrait since had come out in pieces: an eye a fraction too high, a mouth that belonged to a stranger, as if the world had been shattered and reassembled by someone working from a rumor. [53] The last client had wept, and not with gratitude. [62] So she came to the lighthouse, to the library nobody remembered, because the letter said her grandmother had left her something there. [84]

The library climbed the tower in a spiral. [92] Shelves had been built into the curve of the wall so that the books themselves seemed to lean inward, listening. [112] The higher Maren climbed, the closer the walls pressed, until she could touch both sides with her elbows and the spines brushed her shoulders like hands. [138] On the topmost landing, beneath the dead lamp, sat the box. [149]

Her grandmother's tarot box, cedar, hinged with brass, now split down the middle as though it had been dropped from a height. [171] Inside, the cards were not cards anymore. [178] Her grandmother had never trusted printed decks; she had embroidered her own on linen squares, each figure stitched in colored silk. [199] Now the silk had come loose, threads pulled free of their patterns and knotted into a single furious tangle, the Tower snarled with the Moon, the Star's rays wound through the Hanged Man's rope. [233] Fragments of faces looked up at her from the wreckage, and Maren thought: yes, that is exactly how everything has looked since you died. [257]

Tucked under the tangle was a note in her grandmother's slanting hand. [269] Who could ever mend such a thing? [276]

Most people would have read that as a sigh. [285] Maren had never been able to leave a rhetorical question alone; it was the one thing about her that had made her grandmother laugh out loud. [311] When a teacher asked who could possibly care about the shape of a stranger's jaw, Maren had raised her hand. [331] When a mourner at the funeral murmured who would have thought she would go so soon, Maren had said, quietly, the doctor, in March, and been asked to leave the room. [362] A question was a door, and she had never understood why anyone would paint one on a wall and then refuse to open it. [386]

Who could ever mend such a thing, she read again, and answered aloud: someone with better light. [403]

But the lamp above her was cold, and the fog had swallowed the windows, and the walls had closed in so tightly that her shadow had nowhere to fall. [432] She could not see the colors of the threads. [441] Red and violet were the same grey. [448] The grandmother who had stitched these cards had done her finest work, Maren remembered, in the winter when her eyes were failing, working the needle by feel in the dark room at the base of the tower where the oil had once been kept. [492]

Maren went down. [495]

Each turn of the stair widened the walls and thickened the dark until, at the bottom, there was no light at all, only the smell of old oil and salt and the sound of the sea working at the rock. [535] She sat on the stone floor with the broken box in her lap and put her hands into the tangle. [555]

At first it was nothing, a mess of wire-fine strands. [565] Then her fingers began to distinguish: the slick heavy twist of the gold, the fine dry silk of the blue, a rougher thread she guessed was the brown of the Hermit's cloak. [597] She followed one strand to its end, freed it, laid it across her knee. [611] She stopped trying to see the cards as they had been. [622] Down here, without her eyes, the fragments did not have to fit their old pictures; they only had to be true to her hands. [646] She began to lay the threads in new arrangements, redesigning as she untangled: a Tower that was also a lighthouse, a Star whose rays reached down instead of up, a face that she built line by line from the memory in her fingertips rather than the memory in her eyes. [696] She knew the cheekbone before she knew whose it was. [706]

When she came back up the spiral, morning had thinned the fog to a pale gold that leaked through the windows and lit the silk in her hands like filament. [736] The walls did not seem so close. [743] She looked at the rough face she had laid out in silk and did not weep, though something in her chest glowed and hurt in equal measure. [770] Who could ever mend such a thing? [777] Someone, she said, and this time the answer was a name she could say without breaking. [793]
