The bookstore had eaten the mountain from the inside, and Ilse had been climbing through its shelves for three days. [20] Corridors of spines wound upward in tightening spirals, lit by lamps that someone refilled while she slept. [37] Far below, weather moved inside the rock, a low grinding she felt in her teeth more than heard. [55]

She carried one thing that was not a book: a stone the size of a heel, cut with a script no living person could read. [80] It had come from her village before the water took it. [91] For years she had remembered the village on purpose, the way one presses a bruise. [106] Then remembering had become the bruise itself, and she had stopped, and now she could not say whether the streets had run in circles or lines, whether the well stood at the center or the edge. [142] She wanted the shape back. [147] Not the grief; the shape. [152]

The clerks on the lower levels had told her the seer kept the summit, and that the seer was made of mercury glass. [175] Ilse had assumed this was a manner of speaking. [184] It was not. [187]

The seer sat in a reading room where the shelves finally ran out, a woman-shaped vessel of silvered glass, hollow, the mirror inside her gone mottled and dim as old spoons. [218] Ilse saw herself in the seer's face, but wrong, warped toward the edges. [231]

"You have brought me something unreadable," the seer said. [240]

"They said you could read anything." [246]

"I cannot read at all." [251] The voice came from the glass, a chime with a crack in it. [264] "I substitute. [266] Each night I take the world as it is and set another in its place, and in the morning the mountain puts it back. [290] That is the only trick I have." [297]

Ilse set the stone on the table between them. [306] "Then substitute my village. [310] I need to see its shape." [316]

"I can only build from what you give me. [325] You have given me a stone you cannot read and a village you will not remember. [341] That is nothing." [344]

The grinding in the rock deepened. [350] Ilse felt it climb her spine. [356]

The seer turned her mottled face toward the sound, then reached into the hollow of her own chest and drew out a feather, gray and unremarkable, the kind a pigeon drops. [387] "Close your eyes. [390] Your eyes are the part of you that gave up." [400]

Ilse closed them, and the seer laid the feather in her hand and guided it across the stone. [418] The barbs caught in the carved grooves and dragged, each cut pulling the feather differently, a stutter here, a long smooth glide there, a snag that bent the vane and let it spring. [451]

Ilse's hand knew this. [455] Her hand had done this before the rest of her was old enough to be told anything was impossible. [474] Her grandmother, blind by forty, had taught the script this way, feather to stone at the kitchen table, because the letters were not meant for eyes. [500] They were meant for the drag and release in the fingers. [511] Ilse had buried the talent under a decade of reading like everyone else, and it had waited, the way seeds wait under snow. [534]

The first line came up through her palm: the well at the center. [547] Then: nine houses turning around it, sunwise. [554] Then, in the smaller cuts near the edge, the line she had never gotten far enough to read as a child: When the water comes, carry me to high ground and plant me. [587]

"It is not a record," Ilse said. [594] "It is a seed." [598]

"Then you have given me something after all," the seer said. [609]

The lamps guttered. [612] The thunder in the rock did not break; it never did, the clerks had told her, it only turned over and over like a sleeper. [637]

That night, the seer did her only trick. [645] The reading room went out like a candle and Ilse stood in a ring of nine houses around a well, sunwise, the air smelling of wet slate, and she walked it, counting doors, until the shape was hers again and not the seer's. [688] It did not hurt. [692] It was only a shape, and shapes could be carried. [702]

In the morning the mountain put the room back, as promised. [713] Ilse did not go down. [718] She climbed the last stair above the reading room, where the shelves gave way to bare stone and a thin skin of soil the wind had left, and knelt, and pressed the stone into the ground the way you press a bruise, on purpose, to feel that it is still there. [769]

The feather she kept. [773]

Below her the thunder turned over once, and was quiet, and did not break. [787]
