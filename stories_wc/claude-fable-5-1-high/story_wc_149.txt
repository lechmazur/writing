The greenhouse stood alone over the hush of winter-cracked fields, its glass ribs black against a sky that had not admitted dawn in eleven years. [25] Ilse Marrow had bargained the sun away herself, one sealed retort at a time, because the orchard inside could only wake in darkness. [48] Forty trees, grafted from quicksilver and quince, stood dormant along the aisles with mercury beading on their bark like sweat. [68] Not one had ever blossomed. [73]

She had almost stopped believing they would when the bride first appeared. [85] It was a cameo only, a face caught in the curve of an alembic, veiled in something like ash that never fell. [107] Ilse turned, and the aisle was empty, but the nearest tree shivered, and a single bud split open with a sound like a struck bell. [132] The blossom was white and it smelled of bitter almonds. [142]

She named the phenomenon in her ledger with an alchemist's caution: resonant awakenings, cause unknown. [157] The cause did not stay unknown. [163]

The bride ghosted through the greenhouse all that winter, never lingering, never arriving in the same way twice. [181] She was a grey hem vanishing behind the athanor, a reflection in the frost that bloomed and burned off the panes, a shape of steam rising from a crucible in the exact posture of a woman waiting at an altar. [221] Each appearance lasted less than a breath, and each time a tree woke. [234] Ilse learned to hear it coming, a low hum in the glass, as though the whole greenhouse were a bead rolled between enormous fingers. [258]

On the night the twentieth tree flowered, she found the bead itself on the workbench. [273] It was carved stone, dark green, no larger than a plum pit, worked with a pattern of quince leaves so fine she needed her lens to see them. [301] Her hands knew it before her mind did. [309] Her thumb found the flaw on the underside, a slip of the knife she had never made, in a life she had never lived, and she sat down hard on the cold floor. [342]

The memory came the way the blossoms did, splitting open with a bell's clarity. [356] A young carver, three centuries gone, kneeling in an orchard at true noon, pressing this bead into the palm of a girl whose family had promised her to a richer house. [387] The girl had worn the bead beneath her wedding dress anyway. [398] She had also worn, in a locket, enough quince-seed distillate to make certain the marriage would never be consummated. [417] She had drunk it at the altar, smiling at the carver in the back pew, ash from the censers settling on her veil. [440] It had been the most loving thing she knew how to do, and the most venomous, and she had never been able to tell the two apart. [467] The carver had lived another forty years and never carved again. [478]

Ilse understood, then, why the sun had had to go. [488] The bride could only cross in darkness, and she had been trying to cross for a very long time, waiting for a life in which the carver's hands would build a door. [520] Ilse had thought she was making an orchard. [528] She had been making a way in. [535]

"You don't owe me anything," she said to the empty aisles. [546]

The hum rose in the glass. [552] The bride appeared for the last time not in a reflection but whole, standing between the rows, her veil grey with three centuries of ash that never landed. [580] She lifted the veil. [584] Ilse had expected beauty, and found instead something plainer and more terrible: a woman who had chosen poison over a cage and had spent every lifetime since trying to pay for the grief it cost the one person she had done it for. [627]

"Forty trees," the bride said, and her voice was the hum made words. [640] "One for each year you did not carve." [648]

She stepped forward and pressed her mouth to Ilse's, and it burned like almonds, like quince seed, like a locket opened at an altar, and it was the sweetest thing Ilse had ever tasted. [682] Then the remaining twenty trees woke all at once. [691]

The sound was not a bell but a peal, forty tones striking together, and the glass could not hold it. [711] The panes did not shatter; they simply forgot to be dark. [722] For the first time in eleven years, dawn came over the winter-cracked fields and poured through the greenhouse onto forty trees in full white flower and one woman kneeling among them with a carved stone bead in her fist. [761]

The bride was gone, and would not return, and the debt was paid. [774] Ilse wept, and laughed, and could not have said which one she meant. [787]
