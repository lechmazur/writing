Nell Okafor had learned early that ghosts and dust obeyed the same rule: both settled where nothing moved. [18] It was why she had stayed in elevator repair for twenty-two years. [30] A shaft that moved every day stayed clean of both. [40]

The ticket office of the Halloran Brothers Winter Grounds had not moved in forty years. [55] The developer wanted the little cash lift behind the counter certified dead before the wrecking crew came, and Nell was the only technician in the county who would take a job in a building with no power except one fluorescent tube wired to a generator outside. [101]

The facade outside still grinned. [106] Painted clowns, a strongman, a ringmaster with his arm flung toward a tent that had been sold for canvas in 1983. [127] Up close the paint had split along the plywood seams, and the cracks ran straight through every smile. [145]

Inside, she did what she always did first: she read the dust. [157] It lay on the counter in an even grey felt, undisturbed by anything with feet. [172] It thinned toward the back wall in a fan shape, which meant air moved there, which meant a gap. [191] And in the open cash drawer, among furred coins and a rotted ledger, sat one object with no dust on it at all: a brass cylinder the size of a tuna can, toothed around its rim. [227] A mainspring barrel, the kind a horologist would pull from a large wall clock. [241]

Nell did not touch it. [246] A clean thing in a dirty room, with no footprints leading to it, was a thing that got handled by someone who left no prints. [271]

She looked up instead. [275] Above the counter, a clock face without hands hung crooked, and beside it the ceiling plaster had a hairline running to the corner where the cash lift's hoistway went through. [305] The dust on the ceiling was thinnest along that crack. [315]

Nell had never been able to explain to anyone why she liked ceilings. [328] Every building hid its real face above the drop tiles, in the wiring and the shafts and the things people had shoved up out of sight and called finished. [357] She had gone into elevators to find out what ceilings kept. [368]

The fluorescent tube overhead buzzed, ballast failing, the sound she waited for on every job. [383] Then it died. [386]

The silence came down like a dropped sheet, and in it, he was there. [400] An old man in a ticket-taker's vest, sleeves gartered, standing behind the counter with both hands cupped around nothing, at the height of the open drawer. [426] He was looking at the ceiling too. [433]

"Evening," Nell said. [436]

Ghosts did not weigh anything, she had found, but they did not leave either. [450] They were like a wound spring: no heavier for the force stored in them, and no lighter until something let the force out. [473] She carried a dozen from old jobs and had never once set one down. [487]

"You pulled the barrel so the clock would stop," she said. [498] "Why?" [499]

He lifted his chin toward the crack. [506]

Nell picked up the barrel, felt the coiled tension humming inside it, and climbed onto the counter. [523] The lift car was a wooden box on a hand-cranked drum, stalled a foot below the ceiling hatch. [541] She found the crank, engaged the pawl with her thumb, and turned. [553] The box rose, the hatch lifted, and the dust that had been breathing through the crack for forty years came down in a slow grey curtain. [579]

Inside the hoistway, wedged between the guide rails, lay the clock movement, and beneath it a canvas bank bag stenciled HALLORAN BROS. [601] The last night's gate. [605] Not stolen, as the brothers had told the crew when the old man was found dead at the foot of the ladder, but hidden in the ceiling by a man who had seen the ledger and the payroll and known which one the brothers meant to honor. [652] He had stopped the clock so no show would start until the crew was paid. [667] Then his heart had quit on the ladder, and the brothers had found a story easier to paint over than the truth. Nell set the barrel back into the movement. [697] It seated with a click she felt in her teeth. [707] The movement began to tick, forty years late, counting toward a show that would never open. [723]

When she climbed down, the ticket-taker was gone, and the counter dust where he had stood was as smooth as ever. [744] She wrote up the lift as functional, not dead, and put the bank bag in her toolbox for the crew's grandchildren, if any could be found. [770]

Overhead the tube buzzed back to life, and outside the ringmaster went on pointing at nothing, his smile cracked clean through. [791]
