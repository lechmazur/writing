The lake froze on the ninth night of the dry cold, and Ilse marked it on the basalt wall with the tip of a nail. [25] She was the sanctuary's horizon mapper, paid by the Company to watch the four edges of the world and report what crossed them: weather, wolves, riders, rain. [52] Rain mattered most. [55]

Below the black tower, the magnetic viaduct ran out over the narrows on pillars of ore-veined stone, and when rain wet it, the iron in the beams woke and pulled. [85] The haulers felt it first in their yokes. [93] Twenty-two of them wore the iron collars, hinged at the throat, clasps that would not open within reach of the viaduct's hum. [115] After rain the hum reached farther, across the wet shingle and up the sanctuary stairs, and a hauler who strayed was drawn back like a filing to a blade. [144] Dry cold shrank it. [148] Ice, Teodor said, could carry them beyond it; the lake itself would unyoke them. [162]

Teodor was the oldest hauler, and the revolution was his, though he refused the word. [177] "A revolution is a thing that turns," he told her. [187] "Ours will not turn." [191] "It will wait, and then it will walk." [199]

Ilse hated waiting. [202] She had been born wanting to run at whatever she saw on the horizon, and the Company had hired her precisely because she never looked away from it. [230] Vigilance, they called it. [234] She knew it was hunger with its teeth clenched. [243]

So she gave the hunger a task. [250] Each night after the lake froze she climbed to the tower's east window and tracked the moon's path, scratching its arc into the basalt: where it rose, how it slid, what it lit on the ice and what it left dark. [291] Moonlight on thick ice came back white; on thin ice it came back gray, like breath on glass. [309] Night by night the gray shrank. [315] Night by night the moon crept toward the ridge that would, on one particular evening, throw the sanctuary's shadow clear across the crossing and blind the Company watch on the far shore. [347]

She counted the scratches. [351] Fourteen. [352] Nineteen. [353] Each one was a night she had not run, and she began to think of them as something carved rather than endured, a shape made out of the very thing that wanted to break her. [388]

On the twenty-third night it rained. [394]

Ilse stood at the window with her fists shut and listened to the viaduct wake, a low iron note rising through the floor. [417] Below, she heard the haulers shift as their collars leaned toward it. [429] The moon was one night from the ridge. [437] Every part of her said now, before the beams dry, before the Company sends the thaw crews, now. [455]

She went down to Teodor instead. [461] "The pull will hold until the stone dries," she said. [471] "Two nights at least." [475] "The moon will have passed the ridge." [482]

"Then it passes," he said. [487] "And comes again." [490]

She wanted to strike him. [495] Instead she climbed back up and measured the rain. [504] She measured how it ran off the black pillars, how long the ore-veins stayed dark and wet, how the hum changed as the cold took hold of the water and made it into something that could not conduct. [542] Frozen rain was not rain. [547] Ice on iron was only ice. [553]

By the second midnight the viaduct stood glazed and silent, its pull drawn back to the beams like a tide gone out, and the moon hung one finger past the ridge, its path a hair off her chart. [591] The shadow fell short of the far shore by forty paces. [602] Forty paces of lit ice. [607]

She went down and told them the truth, and Teodor nodded as if she had brought good news. [625] "Forty paces of standing still," he said. [632] "We know how to do that." [638]

They went out in single file across the white, Ilse first with her chart of gray and white in her head, softly purposeful, setting each foot where the moon had told her the lake would hold. [674] At three hundred paces the first clasp clicked. [682] Then another, then all of them, a sound like a pocketful of coins let go, and twenty-two yokes lay dark on the ice behind twenty-two people who kept walking. [711]

At the edge of the shadow they stopped, and stood, and let the moon go by. [727] Ilse felt the old wildness climb her throat, the thing that wanted to sprint the last forty paces howling. [746] She held it the way she had held it for twenty-five nights, not by killing it but by giving it something to watch. [769] The horizon. [771] The slow slide of the light. [777]

When the dark came over the last of the ice, she was the one who said walk. [794]
