The wrong seeds came up anyway. [6] Ines had meant to plant basil in the station's single tray of soil, but the packet had been mislabeled somewhere between Earth and the Lens, and what pushed through the dirt was mustard, yellow and stubborn, leaning toward the viewport as if it knew where the light bent. [54] She had decided to let it grow. [61] It was, she told herself, data. [67]

Outside the viewport the Lens did what it always did: nothing visible, everything felt. [81] A gravitational anomaly the size of a small moon, mass without matter, it sat between the station and home and folded every signal that passed through it. [108] Messages arrived bent, stretched, layered over themselves, a word from Tuesday tucked inside a word from last spring. [126] Someone had to unbend them. [131] That was Ines: celestial signal interpreter, the only human posting with a job title that sounded like a prayer. [150]

The morning the mustard bloomed, a transmission from her sister came through in eleven fragments. [165] The Lens had shuffled them. [170] Some were seconds old; one carried a timestamp from three years back, a message Ines had already received and answered and grieved. [192] The Lens did that sometimes, returned things you thought were finished. [203]

She laid the fragments out on the console. [211] The ink of her work was probability. [218] Fragment four, "she is sleeping through now," could not precede fragment nine, "we are trying a name." [235] Fragment two mentioned rain; fragment seven mentioned the drought ending. [245] Nothing stood on its own. [250] Every piece meant something only because of what it leaned against, and Ines built the message the way she had learned to build everything out here, through contingent relationships, one dependency at a time. [284] If this, then that. [288] If that, then not this. [293]

She was, in her reports, confidently uncertain. [300] She wrote "probable order" and "likely intent" and assigned each reconstruction a number between zero and one, and the station's procurement office had once complained that she never sent a single message home marked certain. [335] She had written back: neither does the sky. [343]

On the shelf above the console lay her grandmother's fountain pen. [354] The nib was a strange thing, cut from a sliver of meteoric iron by a craftsman in Lisbon who had believed fallen metal remembered its trajectory. [380] The reservoir had dried years ago; ink did not travel well through the Lens either. [395] She kept the pen because it was the last object that had touched a hand that had touched her. [414]

Fragment eleven would not settle. [419] It was the old one, the three-year echo: "I don't think I can do this without you." [436] When it had first arrived, before the drought, before the baby, it had meant one thing, and Ines had answered it in the only way distance allowed, badly and late. [466] Now it lay beside "we are trying a name," and the sentence had changed without a single letter moving. [485]

This was the part of her job no one on Earth understood. [497] She did not just receive history; she edited it. [506] Wherever she placed fragment eleven, that was where it had happened. [517] Her sister would never know the pieces had come apart, only that a reply arrived, and the reply would answer whatever order Ines chose. [541]

She picked up the dry pen. [547] She pressed the iron nib to the back of a printout, no ink, only the groove it cut, and wrote fragment eleven at the end. [572] Not as a plea. [576] As a thing said to a sleeping child, by a woman who had learned that "without you" could hold love as easily as fear. [600] Probability: point six. [603] She would not pretend to more. [609]

The letters had no color, but tilted against the light of the viewport they threw small shadows, legible. [627] A message written in relief, which was the only kind the Lens had ever let through. [643]

She thought of the mustard, planted by mistake, blooming on schedule. [654] Of the pen with nothing in it that still left a mark. [666] Of a sentence three years late that had arrived, she was fairly sure, exactly on time. [682] This was why she had taken the post, though she had told the review board otherwise: to thresh meaning from experience, to stand where the universe shook every message loose and learn which grains were worth keeping. [719]

She keyed her reply. [723] It would bend going out, as everything did. [731] Somewhere on the other side, someone would have to unbend it, and they would probably get it a little wrong, and the wrongness might be the truest part. [759] She found she did not mind. [765] Out here, that was what comfort looked like: strange, unlabeled, and growing anyway. [778]
