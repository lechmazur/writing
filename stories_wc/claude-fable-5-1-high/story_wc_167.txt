Marit Solveig had replaced every window on Pearl Bay's waterfront at least once, and she had learned that glass tells you where a building is honest. [26] A pane that cracks along the corner means the frame is lying about its level. [41] She had taken the habit ashore with her, into the letters people handed her and the pauses they left in conversation. [62] The harbor authority's notice, pinned to the fish shed a week ago, had said "consolidation of moorings" and "efficiency review." [82] Marit read the space between those phrases and saw a sale. [93] The vote was at noon. [98]

So she was on the seawall at dawn, before everything shifted, with a stone in her pocket and the mist so thick it had weight. [123] Behind the wall lay the tide pond, a flat oval of trapped seawater that the bay refilled twice a day and otherwise ignored. [146] Her father had brought her here as a girl. [155] "Throw," he would say, "and then shut up and watch." [165] The rings spread, and where they wavered or broke early, something lay under the surface: a snagged net, a stone, a shelf of mud. [189] He called it communing, half joking, and she had never found a better word. [203] The pond answered questions you had not learned to ask yet. [214]

Last night the sky had done its rare trick: long after sunset, high clouds had caught light from beneath the horizon and glowed silver-blue, noctilucent, over a harbor gone dark. [244] The fishermen had sat under it on upturned crates, doing their reckonings out loud: what the boats would fetch, what the debts would take, what was left. [271] Nobody had mentioned the atrium. [276]

Marit had built it over eleven years at the end of the old pier, out of every offcut and rejected pane the harbor had handed her. [302] Beveled edges, thick wheelhouse glass, a ship's lantern lens the size of a dinner plate. [317] It was a room with no purpose that the town could name, and so the town called it Marit's folly and walked past. [340] She had never argued. [344] You could not explain to a man counting debts that the thing he walked past every day was the point. [364]

She took the stone out and threw it. [372]

The rings went out clean through the mist, one, two, five, and then the seventh ring buckled on the eastern side and folded back on itself. [398] She waited, the way he had taught her. [406] A second set of rings arrived from the far edge, where the sluice let the bay in, and crossed the first. [427] Where they met, the water did not cancel; it made a lattice, brief and exact, a pattern neither ripple carried alone. [448]

Marit understood she had been reading the notice wrong. [457] Not wrong about the sale; she was right about the sale. [468] Wrong about what was under the surface. [475] She had assumed the hidden thing was the buyer's intent. [485] But the ring had buckled on the east side, toward the pier, toward the atrium, and the lattice had formed only where the harbor's own tide met her thrown stone. [515] The thing nobody was saying was not the sale. [524] It was that nobody had ever stood inside the folly at dawn. [536]

She walked the seawall to the pier as the mist began to thin. [549] The sun came over the headland at the low, ruthless angle it kept for ten minutes each morning, and it struck the atrium broadside. [573] Every prism in the structure took the light and broke it, and the mist inside and around the glass caught the pieces. [595] Color stood in the air like something you could lean on. [606] Not the harbor transformed; the harbor as it had always been, with the ordinary light finally taken apart so people could count its parts. [630]

Down the pier, Halvard Brekke stopped coiling a line. [639] Two of the Aune brothers came off their deck. [648] They did not speak, which Marit read as the first honest thing the town had said all week. [666]

She propped the atrium door open with her toolbox. [675] "Come in before it goes," she said. [682] "It only does this at dawn, and only in mist, and it has done it every such morning for eleven years." [703]

Halvard came in first and stood with a stripe of violet across his hands. [717] "What is it worth?" he asked, because that was the question the night had left in everyone's mouth. [735]

"Nothing you can sell," Marit said. [741] "Which is the only kind of worth they cannot take at noon." [753]

The sun climbed, the angle went, and the colors slid off the walls and back into plain glass, plain water, plain morning. [775] Nobody left. [777]
