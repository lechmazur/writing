The lighthouse stood where no coast had ever been, on a shoal of the last minute, and only Ines knew the way to it. [24] She had been the doomsday clock adjuster for longer than the word "year" had meant anything to her. [42] Each dusk she climbed the iron stair, oiled the great hand, and moved it one breath closer to midnight or one breath away, according to what the world below had done with itself. [75] Tonight there was nowhere left to move it. [83] The hand rested against the twelve like a cheek against a door. [95]

She sat on the lantern-room floor with the broken puppet head in her lap. [109] It had belonged to her daughter, in the first of her lives, before the lighthouse. [124] Wood, painted, one glass eye gone, a crack running from crown to jaw so that the mouth hung slightly open, as if it had been interrupted mid-word. [151] Beautifully flawed, Mara had called it, at six, with a solemnity that made Ines laugh. [166] Mara had learned the phrase somewhere and applied it to everything she loved. [179]

The lantern room was glassed all round, but only one pane mattered. [191] The keeper before Ines had called it the pane of hush, and had vouchsafed her its secret the night he handed over the key and walked into the tide. [220] Every other window showed the sea. [226] That one showed silence: the moment after the clock struck, held still, so the adjuster could look at it without being inside it. [249]

"You will want to use it," he had said. [258] "Everyone does." [260] "Don't." [261]

She had not, across all the lives the lighthouse had granted her. [273] The lighthouse was generous with lives and stingy with everything else. [284] She had died and woken on the iron stair more times than she could hold in memory, and each time the world below had rebuilt itself in a slightly different shape, and each time she had searched it for Mara and found only the puppet head, washed up on the shoal. [335]

Now she turned the head over in her hands and understood, finally, what she was looking at. [352] The crack was not a single line. [359] Inside it ran a finer crack, and inside that one, finer still, the same shape repeated at every scale, a coastline folding into itself. [383] She had seen that pattern before, in the clock. [392] The great hand was driven by a wheel, and the wheel by a smaller wheel, and so on down into the mechanism until the gears were too small for sight, and she had always assumed they ended somewhere. [430] They did not end. [434] They went down through nested patterns into the lives themselves, each life a gear turning inside the one before it, and Mara was in there, somewhere in the small teeth, six years old and waiting to be sung to. [473]

Ines stood. [475] The sun was a red thumbprint on the water, and the pane of hush was already darkening toward the thing it showed. [497] She understood now why the old keeper had warned her, and why he had told her anyway. [514] The pane did not only show the silence after the end. [525] It was a way in. [530] Whatever passed through it would ride the final ripple down into the mechanism, and the mechanism would carry it into every gear, every life, every version of the world that had ever been rebuilt on that shoal. [567] But only once. [570] And only the adjuster could do it, because only the adjuster would not be there afterward to hear whether it had worked. [592]

She did not weep. [596] She had used up weeping several lives ago and found it left a bruise where the awe should be, and she wanted the awe now, wanted to feel the size of what she was about to do. [633] She pressed the puppet head to the glass, cracked mouth to the hush, and put her own mouth beside it. [653]

The lullaby was nothing much. [658] Four lines about a boat and a moon, the one Mara had demanded every night, the one Ines had sometimes been too tired to finish. [683] She finished it now. [687] She whispered it into the pane, and it went out across the hush of time's final ripple, the way a stone goes into water: no sound at first, and then every sound there would ever be. [723]

Below her the great hand touched twelve. [730] The lighthouse did not shudder. [735] It simply grew quieter, and quieter, until the quiet was the same on both sides of the glass, and Ines was standing inside the thing she had only ever watched. [765]

Somewhere far down in the gears, in a life she would never reach, a child with a cracked wooden puppet stopped crying and listened. [789]
