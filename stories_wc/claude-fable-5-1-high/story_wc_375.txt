Odile Marsh had drawn eleven thousand faces and forgotten none of them. [12] That was the trouble. [16] Every morning she set her stool at the mouth of the Vaelen Crystal Cave, opened her tin of charcoal, and waited for the tour buses to exhale their passengers. [45] Four minutes per portrait, three coins each. [52] Her fingers stayed black to the second knuckle from April to October, and the tourists took this as proof of authenticity. [73]

She could have drawn them with her eyes shut. [82] A face, once seen, filed itself in her like a card in a drawer, and she could pull it decades later with the mole in the right place. [110] The guides called it a gift. [116] Her father had called it a living, which was why she was still here, twelve years after she had stopped painting. [137]

The last tour of the day came up at six. [147] A boy of perhaps seven sat for her, squirming, and when she showed him the sketch he laughed, a bright unguarded sound that ran down the throat of the cave behind her and came back doubled, then tripled, then thin. [187] He did not know she would keep him. [195] She waited until after the last echo of laughter faded from the passage. [208] Then she lit a lamp and went down. [216]

The show cave ended at a rope; the true cave began past it. [229] Two hundred steps below the tourist rail the walls turned to quartz, whole seams of it, and the lamp broke against the crystal into colors that had no business underground: rose, pale green, a violet like the last minute before dawn. [270] She had painted here once. [275] Only here. [277] Aboveground the light had never been strange enough. [285]

Her father had said the cave paintings were a hobby, and the portraits were a trade, and one paid for the other only if you stopped the one. [313] She had not argued. [317] Instead she had carried her single finished canvas down to the old guide station, hung it on the driest wall, and behind it, in the niche it covered, set a steel box with a combination lock. [353] Brushes, pigments, a knife. [357] She had spun the dial to a number she picked without looking and told herself she would forget it. [376]

She had not forgotten it. [381] That was the cruelty of her particular mind. [389] Twenty-six, four, seventeen. [392] It surfaced every morning with her first cup of coffee, uninvited, like a name from a face in a crowd. [412] Twelve years of mornings. [416] Whatever she had tried to bury had refused, politely and daily, to stay buried. [430]

The painting was where she had left it. [438] She saw at once that it was wrong. [446] She had painted the crystals grey. [452] She had been grieving something that day, though she had not known what, and she had made the whole cave the color of ash. [476] It looked like a portrait of a dead place. [485]

She lifted it down and set it against the wall, face inward. [497] The lock was there, furred with pale mineral crust. [506] Twenty-six, four, seventeen. [509] Her black fingers turned the dial from memory. [517] Nothing. [518] She tried again, slower, and felt the wheels grind and slip inside, corroded by twelve years of cave breath. [537] The steel had forgotten what she could not. [545]

Odile sat down on the cold floor and laughed once, and it came back to her off the quartz, small and wrong. [567] Then she picked up a loose spar of crystal the length of her forearm and struck the shackle until it sheared. [588]

The brushes had stiffened into bundles of quills. [596] The pigment cakes had cracked. [601] It did not matter. [605] She dipped a cake of madder in the seep pool by the wall and it bled red into the water, alive after all. [628]

She turned the canvas around. [633] With charcoal, with her thumb, with the ruined brushes and cave water, she began to rectify it: rose over grey, green through the ash, the violet of the hour before sunrise laid into the highest seam of stone. [671] Her hand knew where every facet fell because she had seen them once and could not unsee them. [689] The gift that had held her in the tourist chair had also held this room for her, perfectly, all these years. [710]

She would be back at the stool by nine. [719] The buses would come. [723] Faces would sit for her and she would keep them all, and none of that was going to change. [742] But there was a second thing she could not put down, and she had finally stopped pretending the lock had ever been the thing holding it shut. [769]

The lamp guttered toward a dawn she could not see, and the crystals kept their colors a little longer. [788]
