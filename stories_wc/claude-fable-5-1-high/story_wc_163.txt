Tamsin Vell read the land the way her grandmother had read the sky, and she had never once found her own name written in either. [25] A landscape astrologer charts ridgelines against constellations and tells farmers where a well belongs, tells widows where a house will hold, and then moves on before the ground learns her weight. [56] That was why she came to Halloway Reservoir in the driest June in forty years, when the water had sunk low enough to bare the rusted crown of the Meridian Deep Station. [88] The station had been built to study the drowned town beneath the dam, a research facility bolted to a church steeple sixty feet under, and then the funding had gone the way of the town. [123] A landscape with no sky over it, she reasoned, could not lie to her about where anything belonged. [141] She climbed down the access ladder at dusk with a lantern and a notebook and the intention to stay until sunrise, when the dam's pumps would cycle and the water rise again. [173]

The corridors smelled of iron and old lake. [181] Light did not so much enter the station as leak through its seams, thin bright threads stitched along the welds where her lantern caught wet metal, and every shadow had an edge that glowed. [215] On the third landing she found a burnt feather laid across a console like a bookmark. [231] Cormorant, black to begin with and blacker now, its vane charred to lace along one side. [247] It was warm. [250]

"You'll want to hold that up to the vent," said a voice from the dark, "if you're wondering whether the air down here is moving." [275]

Her name was Ilse Marrow, the station's last technician when the last paycheck failed to arrive, and she had simply not left. [297] She was softly ingenious in the way of people who have had to be: the generator ran on filtered diesel she siphoned from the dam's maintenance shed, the dehumidifiers were wired from aquarium pumps, and the feathers were her instruments. [337] "Singe the edge and it curls," Ilse said, showing her. [347] "A curled feather turns in a breath of air a candle flame would miss." [361] "The cormorants roost on the tower when it's exposed, and they leave me what I need." [377]

Tamsin asked her why she had stayed. [384] Ilse did not answer directly. [389] She led her instead down to the observation gallery, where the great curved window looked out on the drowned town, and killed the lantern. [413] For a long moment there was nothing. [420] Then Halloway assembled itself out of the black: the steeple, the schoolhouse roof, the main street running downhill into silt, all of it faintly lit from within by colonies of something pale that had learned to live on the algae of the windows. [463]

"That's the town's own light," Ilse said. [470] "It comes on about an hour after full dark and goes off before dawn." [484] "I timed it for three years before I understood I wasn't studying it anymore." [498] "I was keeping it company." [503]

Tamsin opened her notebook and found she had nothing to chart. [514] There was no ridgeline here to lay against a constellation, no sky to argue with, only a town that had gone on glowing in the dark for decades with no one to see it, and a woman who had decided that someone should. [557] They stayed in the gallery through the small hours, the drowned light below them a kind of luminous respite from every place Tamsin had ever measured and left. [585]

Toward four the town began to dim, and Ilse checked her watch and said it was time. [602] They climbed the tower together, up through the hatch into a night gone gray at its eastern hem, and stood on the wet rust with the reservoir black around them and the far shore a line of reeds. [640] Ilse held up a burnt feather and it did not stir. [651] "Hold your breath," she said, and Tamsin, not knowing why, obeyed. [662] The water lay still. [666] The reeds lay still. [670] Then the two of them let go at once, a shared exhale that curled the feather in Ilse's fingers, and in the same instant the first fireflies of summer rose out of the reeds along the shore, one and then a dozen and then a scattered constellation, blinking in a rhythm Tamsin could feel in her own chest. [728]

It captivated her entirely. [732] Not the light, which she had seen in a hundred meadows, but the timing: the town going dark below as the shore lit up above, breath held and released, the place passing its glow from one hand to the other. [772] Belonging, she understood, was not a place you found; it was a rhythm you kept. [787]
