Marit had not slept a full night since the reservoirs were drained, and she had stopped apologizing for it. [19] Insomnia, she liked to say, was simply the body refusing to waste good darkness. [33] So when the rain came to Holm Island at three in the morning, she was already up, already mixing pigment, and she set down the brush and went out to follow it. [65]

After the water wars, everyone on the coast learned to read rain the way old sailors read sky. [83] The wells were brackish and the desalination barges had been shot to ribbons a decade ago, so fresh water arrived only from above, and where it ran mattered. [111] Marit followed the rain trails down the granite with a lantern hooded low, watching silver threads braid and split across the rock, memorizing each channel so she could set her barrels where the threads thickened. [146] It was also, she admitted, the only subject she had painted in four years. [160] Water going somewhere. [163] She had a wry theory that if she painted enough of it she would eventually understand what the wars had been for. [185] She did not believe the theory, but she was earnest about the painting, and she had learned that those two things could live in the same body without arguing. [214]

Tonight the trails did something new. [220] Three of them, from three faces of the island, bent toward a hollow on the seaward slope she had always taken for bare stone, and they met there and vanished. [250] Water does not vanish. [254] She knelt in the wet and dug with her hands, and under a skin of turf and grit she found iron: a hatch, round, its rim stamped with the crest of the coastal authority that had not existed since she was nineteen. [296] A cistern. [298] Someone had built a catchment here before the wars and sealed it, and the rain had been quietly finding it every storm for twenty years while she slept badly a hundred meters uphill. [331]

"Well," she said to the hatch, "you might have mentioned it." [342] Then she sat back on her heels, soaked through, shaking, and laughed until it frightened her. [358]

By the time the tide dragged itself off the causeway at dawn, she had pried the hatch and lowered a lantern into eight meters of dark, clear, drinkable water, more than the whole island had seen since the treaty. [397] That was when she saw the man walking the causeway toward her, ankle-deep, hurrying against the sea's return. [415] He was old, salt-cracked, carrying nothing but a canteen and something clenched in his fist. [430]

"I heard the mainland pumps failed," he said, when he reached her, and opened his hand. [446] An amber brooch lay on his palm, honey-dark, with a small winged thing suspended inside it that had been dead for forty million years. [470] "My wife's," he said. [474] "For one canteen. [477] I know it isn't a fair exchange." [484]

Marit looked at the brooch for a long moment, and then at the hatch behind her, still breathing cold air from below. [506] Amber, she knew, was only resin that had run downhill once, following the grain of a tree the way rain followed granite, until it stopped and held what it touched. [536] It was the oldest painting she had ever seen. [545]

"You're right, it isn't fair," she said, and filled his canteen from the cistern, and then filled the two jerrycans she had brought for herself and gave him those as well. [576] "Keep the brooch. [579] Bring people. [581] The tide turns in five hours, so tell them to walk fast." [593]

He did not keep the brooch. [599] He pressed it into her wet hand with the stubbornness of a man who had already decided what his grief was worth, and walked back across the causeway with water sloshing at his hips, and she let him win because she was too tired to be gracious about it. [648]

Marit did not sleep that day either. [655] She sat at the easel with the brooch pinned to her collar and painted the three rain trails meeting at the hollow, and for the first time she understood that she had never been trying to paint what the wars were for. [697] Nothing was for anything. [701] She had been trying to paint the way things ran downhill toward each other regardless: rain toward a buried tank, a man toward an island, a dead insect toward a stranger's collar across forty million years. [737] That was the meaning, or it was close enough to hold. [748] She threshed it out of the night the way you thresh grain, with tired arms, keeping what stayed. [766]

Outside, the tide came back over the causeway, and the rain, which had never once asked her permission, began again. [786]
