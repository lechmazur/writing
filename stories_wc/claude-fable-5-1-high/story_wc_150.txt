Teodora Vayle came back to the island with her grandmother's ashes in a tin that had once held almond biscuits. [20] Forty years earlier the two of them had left together, and the old woman had spent every one of those years promising to return. [44] She had died in a rented room above a laundromat, halfway through packing. [57]

The excavation had begun without anyone asking the village. [66] A university team was opening the old tomb on the hill, the one the villagers called the Orchard, where for six centuries the dead had been laid in tunnels beneath the fig and quince trees. [101] Roots had found the bones long ago. [108] They snaked between ribs and wound through the eye sockets of people whose names had rubbed off the stones, and the trees above bore fruit anyway, sweet and heavy every autumn. [139] Her grandmother had believed that the dead fed the living on purpose. [151] Teodora had stopped believing anything of the kind somewhere around her twentieth winter abroad. [165]

The lead archaeologist, a tired man named Halvard, let her walk the site because she spoke the dialect and the workers did not trust him. [190] So she walked. [193] Not idly; she had a list in her head, things her grandmother had described so often that they felt like memories of her own. [217] The third fig from the gate, with a knot shaped like a sleeping dog. [231] The low door where the tunnels began. [238] And the sundial. [241] It had stood at the center of the Orchard, her grandmother said, so that the dead would know what hour it was, because a journey should not be made blind. [271]

Teodora found the base on the fourth day, tipped into a hollow between two quince roots, half swallowed. [289] It was a round stone drum, waist high, with a socket in the top where the dial plate had sat and a rim of worn lettering she could not read. [319] The plate and gnomon were gone. [325] Halvard glanced at it and said it was post-medieval and unimportant. [336] She knelt beside it a long while anyway, running her thumb around the socket, warm with the odd curiosity that had been waking in her since the ferry. [364] She did not believe the dead needed to know the hour. [375] But she wanted to know why someone once had. [384]

Over the next two weeks, while the team lifted the tomb's lid stone by stone, she wandered the hillside with a purpose she did not name aloud. [411] She found a wedge of bronze in the roots of the dog-knot fig, green as a leaf. [428] She found a curved shard of slate with a numeral cut into it, being used by an old woman in the village to prop open a chicken coop. [456] The old woman remembered Teodora's grandmother and gave up the slate with a laugh and a question about whether the city had been kind. [480] Teodora said it had been a city. [487]

Down in the tunnels, where the workers let her follow with a lamp, she moved carefully, stepping over roots that had lifted femurs into slow arches. [513] The air smelled of fruit and clay. [520] In a niche near the tomb's threshold she found the last piece, a second slate shard, laid deliberately across a skull like a hat. [544] Someone had carried it there. [549] Someone, long ago, had also been interrupted. [556] She did not touch the skull. [562] She took the slate and said thank you, quietly, in the dialect, and felt foolish, and did not stop. [581]

The day the tomb came open, everyone gathered at its mouth. [592] Teodora was not there. [596] She was at the center of the Orchard, on her knees, fitting the two slates together with mortar Halvard had grudgingly given her, setting the bronze wedge upright in the socket where it had once thrown its shadow. [634] It was not the whole dial. [640] Half the numerals were missing; the gnomon leaned. [648] But when the sun cleared the quince branches, a shadow fell across the slate and touched a number, and the number was, as far as she could tell, correct. [677]

She sat back on her heels and let herself be a little frightened by how much that mattered. [695] Then she opened the biscuit tin. [701] She did not scatter the ashes; that seemed too much like letting go. [714] She dug a small hole where a fig root broke the surface, and poured her grandmother in among the others, where the roots would find her. [740]

Behind her, at the tomb, someone shouted at what they had found, and the shout was full of wonder. [759] Teodora did not turn around yet. [765] She watched the shadow move, a hair's width, toward the next hour. [777] Whatever she believed now, it was at least this: the journey had not ended in a room above a laundromat. [797]
