Ilse came from Echo Harbor, where the cliffs gave every sound back twice, and so she had grown up knowing that nothing said there was ever truly finished. [28] She was a violinist, and the harbor had taught her ears more than any teacher. [43] She could hear the size of a room with her eyes shut, hear where a hull had rotted by the way it swallowed her footsteps. [68] Then the rainforest upriver burned. [73] For six weeks ash fell on the harbor like grey snow, and when it stopped, the birds did not come back. [94] Neither did the frogs, nor the insects that had once made the evenings tick. [108] People spoke less, as if noise were an insult to something grieving. [120] The hush became chronic, a condition the town lived inside the way one lives inside a low fever. [138] Ilse stopped playing. [141] She wrapped a cord around her bow and her violin case together and told herself it was respect. [159] It was fear. [162]

Her family had one possession older than the harbor itself: a mendicant's bowl, dented brass, carried by an ancestor who had wandered the coast begging not for food but for stories. [193] Every midwinter the family built a bonfire on the flats and passed the bowl around it. [209] Whoever held the bowl told part of a tale, and the rule was absolute: no one finished. [226] You spoke until the fire needed feeding, said the words and then, and passed the bowl. [242] Her grandmother said a finished story was a dead one, and the bowl had never held anything dead. [260] This midwinter there was no family left in the harbor to pass it to. [274] Some had gone inland to fight the fires and had not written. [286] Some had simply gone. [290] Ilse took the bowl and the bound violin and walked out at low tide to the sunken greenhouse. [308]

A merchant had built it on the sandspit a century ago, an orchard under glass, and the spit had settled beneath it before the last panes were set. [336] Now it stood to its waist in the mud, half roofed, its iron ribs open to the sky at one end like an unfinished sentence. [361] Around it lay the wrecks the harbor had collected over the years, hulls on their sides, keels pointing at nothing, a ring of them like old men around a table. [391] Inside the glass, the trees still lived. [398] The ash had not reached them. [404] They had blossomed every spring for a hundred years and never once set fruit, because whatever they needed to finish had never come. [427]

Ilse built the bonfire on the mud between the greenhouse and the nearest hull, out of driftwood and the broken wood of the wrecks. [451] When it caught, she sat with the bowl in her lap and listened. [464] The fire spoke first, as fire does, and the hulls answered it. [476] Each wreck had its own voice: the long schooner gave a low hum back, the fishing boat a dry clatter, the greenhouse a bright ringing of glass. [503] The ring of them was a room, and she knew the size of it exactly. [518] So she began the story alone. [524] She told of a beggar who walked the coast with an empty bowl, and how the bowl was never filled, and how that was the point. [550] The schooner hummed her words back to her a half-beat late. [561] She told of a forest that burned, and the fishing boat clattered, and she told of a town that forgot how to make a sound. [586] The fire needed feeding. [590] And then, she said, and stopped, because there was no one to take the bowl. [605]

The greenhouse rang softly with the last of her voice, glass answering glass, and the sound did not die so much as go somewhere she could not follow. [633] She understood then that the rule had never been about waiting for another mouth. [647] The story was unfinished because the world was, and the beauty was in the and then, in the open ribs of the roof, in the trees that flowered without fruit and did not apologize. [681] She set the bowl on the mud and unbound the cord from her violin. [695] She had tied it well. [700] Then she stood in the center of the ring of wrecks and played the next part of the story, the part that had no words yet. [726] The schooner took the low notes and the greenhouse took the high ones, and the fire took the rest. [745] It was the first music the harbor had heard since the burning, and the hulls gave it back doubled, the way the cliffs had when she was small. [773] She did not finish it. [778] She let the last note hang over the empty bowl, and left it there for whoever came next. [796]
