The orchard went silent an hour after moonrise, when the last bats spilled up through the sinkhole and the limestone finished cooling. [22] Only then did Aurel come down the rope ladder, because only then could he hear the drips. [39] Forty stalactites hung over forty quince trees, and every one of them fed its tree a slow, mineral rain that had taken Marit eleven years to learn how to trust. [69] She had planted the orchard. [74] He had only married into it. [80]

Three years ago she had taken a lamp into the lower galleries to look for a fox she swore was living down there, and her footprints had ended at the edge of the black pool where the searchers stopped. [119] No body, no lamp, no fox. [125] People in town said drowned, and Aurel nodded, because a conjurer learns early that agreeing is the cheapest way to keep a thing hidden. [149]

What he kept hidden was the ledger. [156] He had spent his working life refusing to explain how a coin left one hand and arrived in another, and he saw no reason to explain this either: that every night since the pool, he had come down here and assigned his grief a number. [201] Not a poet's number. [205] A measured one. [208] He had built a pendulum from a plumb line and a quince pit to time how long he could stand at the pool before his breath changed. [235] He had rigged a tin cup under the loudest stalactite and counted drips until her name stopped arriving with them. [255] None of it was cruel to himself; he was gentle about it, the way he was gentle with card stock and doves. [277] He simply believed that if sorrow had a shape, it had an end, and if it had an end, the mathematics would tell him the night he could finally stop leaving a lamp lit at the mouth of the galleries. [317]

Tonight he carried something new in his coat pocket: a pair of glass eyes, amber with slit pupils, that Marit had ordered for the fox she never caught. [345] She had been a taxidermist before she was an orchardist, and the eyes had sat in their paper twist on her workbench for three years, waiting for a head. [374]

He crossed to the stepping stone at the orchard's threshold. [384] Marit had laid it herself, a mosaic of an owl in river tile, and it sat exactly beneath the most patient stalactite in the cavern. [409] For three years the drip had been chipping it. [418] Aurel had recorded every chip, the same way he recorded every number in the ledger, and it was only last week, laying the two columns side by side, that he saw they dovetailed. [451] The stone had lost tile fast at first, then slower, then slower still, each loss smaller than the last but never quite nothing. [474] His grief had done the same. [480] The chipped pattern, read as a curve, bent toward a floor it would never touch. [495] He had wanted an ending, and the stone had handed him an asymptote. [508]

He knelt in the moonlight that fell through the sinkhole and studied the owl. [522] The two deepest chips were where its eyes had been. [532] He had known that for months and pretended not to, which was another conjurer's habit. [547] He unwrapped the fox eyes and set them into the sockets. [558] They fit as though the water had been measuring them the whole time. [571]

The owl looked up at the moon through a fox's eyes, and Aurel felt the equation finish itself in a place below arithmetic. [594] The same water that took the tile fed the trees. [604] It had never been two processes. [610] Loss and nourishment were one drip, counted from different cups. [620] He would never reach the night the lamp could go out, because the curve did not have such a night in it. [642] But the slope was flattening. [647] Each night cost less than the one before, and the quince, which did not know the difference between erosion and rain, kept setting fruit. [671]

Aurel wrote the night's number in the ledger. [679] It was the smallest yet. [684] Then he walked to the mouth of the galleries, trimmed the lamp's wick a fraction shorter than the night before, and left it burning. [708] Somewhere below, a fox might be learning to see by it. [719]
