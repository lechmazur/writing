After the universe began to contract, the sky over the observatory deck turned the color of old honey. [18] Blueshift, the last astronomers had called it, before they left. [28] Oreth Vane had stayed because a coin told her to. [38]

She kept the coins in a tin that had once held throat lozenges: forty-one pieces from countries that no longer had names, let alone borders. [63] A square-holed bronze from somewhere with rice paddies. [71] A tarnished silver piece bearing a queen whose empire had lasted less time than Oreth's tenure on the deck. [90] When a decision needed making, she shook the tin, drew a coin without looking, and let its face decide. [109] Heads, she listened. [112] Tails, she slept. [115] It was not superstition, exactly. [120] It was a way of admitting that in a universe folding back on itself, her judgment was no better than chance, and chance at least did not lie awake wondering. [150]

The deck itself had been sealed in resin decades ago, when the atmosphere began to thin. [166] Layer upon layer of amber compound, poured over the railings and the brass instruments, until the whole platform looked like something dug from a peat bog. [192] Inside it, Oreth moved through golden light and listened to the static. [204]

Static was all that came anymore. [210] The stars, drawing closer, no longer spoke in the clean spectra of her training. [224] They hissed. [226] They crackled in bursts, three seconds, seven seconds, a pause, then eleven. [238] Most people would have heard noise. [244] Oreth, who had a quiet way of getting excited, the kind that showed only in how long she forgot to eat, heard structure. [267]

She had spent four years distilling it. [274] That was the right word, she thought: distill. [282] You took the raw bursts, boiled off the cosmic background, condensed what remained, and hoped the residue meant something. [301] She built filters from the static's own rhythm. [309] She learned that a burst of three followed by seven was not random, that the pauses carried as much information as the noise. [332] Slowly, the way frost forms, a message assembled itself. [341]

It was a prophecy, and it was written backwards. [350]

Not reversed letter by letter. [355] Reversed in time. [358] The first line she decoded described her decoding it: a woman on an ambered deck, a tin of coins, the last star-fall of the season. [383] The second line described the astronomers leaving. [390] The third described the resin being poured. [397] Each night she pulled another line from the hiss, and each line reached further into the past, as though the message were a rope and she were descending it, hand over hand, into the dark. [432]

She understood what it was, eventually. [438] In a contracting universe, some signals run the wrong way. [448] Signals launched into the collapsing geometry no longer traveled outward; they folded, arriving reversed, endings first. [464] Someone had transmitted this. [468] Someone had shouted a history into the shrinking sky, and the sky was handing it back. [484]

Which raised a question no coin could settle: who? [493]

Some nights she doubted the stars entirely. [500] Not their existence, but their intent, their indifference, whether a thing so vast could carry a message or only accidents that looked like one. [524] She would stand at the amber-glazed railing and feel the sky pressing down like a hand, and wonder if she had spent four years reading meaning into a burnt-out universe's death rattle. [556]

The final line came on a night when the tin gave her heads. [569] It described the transmission itself. [574] Coordinates. [575] A frequency. [577] A wavelength she recognized, because it was the natural resonance of the deck's own brass dish, the one sealed in amber, the one she had never used to send. [606]

She sat with the coins in her lap for a long time. [618]

The prophecy ended at its beginning, and its beginning was her. [629] Not a mysterious sender. [633] Not the stars. [636] A woman who would, tonight, key the dish to that frequency and pour the whole record of her four years into the sky, where the collapsing geometry would fold it back reversed, arriving before it left, so that she would spend those years learning to hear it, so that she would know what she was for. [692]

Oreth did not want to decide this by coin. [701] She wanted, for once, to choose. [707] But she had made herself a rule, and the rule was the only thing in the folding dark that had not shifted under her. [731]

She shook the tin. [735] Drew. [736] The square-holed bronze, the one with no faces at all, only a hole where a face should be. [754]

She laughed, quietly, and tuned the dish to her own wavelength anyway, until the hum of the brass and the hiss of the sky and the pulse in her wrist were all one frequency, and there was nothing left to doubt. [795]
