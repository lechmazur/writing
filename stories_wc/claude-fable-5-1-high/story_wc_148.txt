They called him the wolf-boy's son long after his father was in the ground, and the name followed him out past the harbor to the lighthouse prison on Skerrin Reef. [30] His father had cried wolf as a child, three times, and the village had never let the family forget the fourth time, the true time, when nobody came. [58] So when a warehouse burned and Tomas Vell swore he had been asleep in his own bed, the magistrate had only smiled. [80] A liar's blood, the magistrate said, and sent him to the reef. [92]

The tower stood where no tower should have stood, on black rock that the sea chewed at every hour of every day, and the old keeper used to say the reef sustained impossibilities the way a widow sustains a grudge. [132] The keeper was dead now. [137] So were the two guards and the four other men, all of them taken in eleven days by the fever that came in on the autumn supply boat, and Tomas had carried each of them down to the cold room beneath the cistern and laid them out in a row. [187]

The relief boat would come in spring. [194] It would find seven bodies and one living man, and that man would be the wolf-boy's son. [211] He had heard his father try to explain the fourth wolf to men who had already decided, had watched the words pile up and go nowhere, a man drowning in his own voice. [244] Tomas had learned the opposite lesson. [250] All his life he had been chasing hush, the kind of quiet that a man could hide the truth inside like a coin in a loaf. [276] Now he had the whole winter to find it. [285]

The prison had a chapel, or had once. [293] The chaplain had rowed away decades before Tomas arrived, and the confessional stood in the corner with its narrow door left ajar ever since, because the latch had rusted open and no one had cared enough to close it. [332] The lower half of that door was a history of knees and boot toes, scuffs layered on scuffs, generations of men who had knelt to say what they had done. [362]

Tomas could not read or write. [368] But he could read that door. [374] He spent the first weeks of winter on his stomach with a lamp, learning the marks: the short hard gouges of angry men, the long pale drags of the tired, the little half-moons where someone had rocked on his heels for an hour before speaking. [419] Every man who had knelt there had left his story without a word, and every story had been believed, because the door could not lie. [444] That was the hush he wanted. [450]

He began to add his own. [456] With the heel of his boot and the edge of a spoon he worked his winter into the wood, mark by mark, through the scuffs of the men before him: eleven small gouges for eleven days of fever, seven long drags for seven trips down the stairs with a body across his shoulders, a half-moon for each night he had sat with the keeper and held the cup to his mouth. [527] The prison was a labyrinth of spiral stairs and cells that doubled back on themselves, and he threaded it with the same alphabet, kneeling at each threshold he had crossed with the dead, leaving the door's marks behind him like a trail of bread. [571] Anyone who followed them would arrive at the cold room already knowing. [583]

Last of all he unscrewed the lattice from the confessional, the carved screen the chaplain had whispered through, and carried it up the tower to the lamp. [610] He fixed it inside the great copper reflector, so that when the flame was lit the light went out over the reef in a hovering grid, a net of gold squares that trembled on the black water without ever quite settling. [651] The relief boat would see a light like a confessional grille, and its crew would come ashore already softened toward the shape of penance, whether they knew it or not. [681]

Then there was nothing left but to tend the flame and wait. [693] He sat in the lamp room through the long amber evenings with the sea folding itself over the reef below, in a copperlight repose he had never known in the village, where every silence had been an accusation. [731] Here the silence was his, and it was full. [740] His father had shouted the truth once and died for lack of an ear. [754] Tomas had knelt his truth into the wood and hung it in the light, and if they found the bodies and did not look, the door would go on telling it anyway, ajar, for whoever knelt there next. [792]
