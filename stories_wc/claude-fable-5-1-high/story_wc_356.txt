Wren came to Halloway-in-the-Boughs with one seed and more ambition than the rope bridges could comfortably hold. [17] The village hung in the canopy of a drowned forest, its platforms lashed to trunks that stood knee-deep in an estuary, and twice a day the whole place leaned as the tide pushed through the roots below. [54] Nobody would explain how the trees had grown so tall or why the salt had not killed them. [72] Wren had not come for that. [78] She had come because the seed in her pocket was a corpse flower, and every book agreed it would not germinate anywhere that moved. [102] The books also agreed that Halloway held, once each tide, a moment the villagers called the turning of the tide's thought, when the water below stopped deciding and the whole canopy went still as held breath. [138] She meant to plant in that moment. [145] She meant to be the herbalist who bloomed the worst-smelling flower on earth and stood beside it without flinching. [164]

This mattered more than it should have, because Wren could smell everything. [176] Rain three valleys off. [180] Fear on a stranger's collar. [185] The exact hour milk would turn. [191] It had made her an herbalist, since no one else could tell nettle from its poisonous cousin in the dark, and it had made her lonely, since most of what people carried on them she wished she did not know. [231] A corpse flower was the one scent she had never faced. [242] She wanted to master it the way climbers want the peak that has killed the most people. [259]

The villagers gave her a platform on the seaward edge and a pot of black soil and said nothing about her plans, which she took for indifference. [286] Each dusk she waited for the turning. [293] She could smell it coming before anyone could see it: the brine went flat, the mud breathed out, and for the space of perhaps forty heartbeats the boards under her stopped their long slow sway. [328] On the third evening she pressed the seed in. [337]

Then she did what her family had always done. [346] The Marrows had sold remedies wrapped in brown paper for six generations, and every packet went out with the inside of the wrapping doodled over in pencil: the plant, the dose, a small joke, the weather the day it was picked. [387] Her grandmother called it journalizing, and swore a record you had to unfold was a record you would actually read. [407] So Wren kept no notebook. [412] She kept the papers her rations came in, smoothed them flat, and drew the pot each night on the plain side: the soil line, the seed's position, the angle of the platform, a scribbled note on smell. [449]

For eleven days the notes said the same thing. [458] Damp. [459] Nothing. [460]

On the twelfth she smelled the seed dying. [468] It was faint, a sweetish flatness under the salt, but she knew rot the way other people knew their own names. [489] She sat on the platform with the folded papers in her lap and did not cry, because crying had a smell too. [511]

An old woman named Ilse, who mended nets on the next platform, came across the bridge without asking and sat down beside her. [534] "You planted at the turning," Ilse said. [541] "Yes." [542] "Because it was still." [546] "Yes." [547]

Ilse spread the doodled papers across the boards in order, and Wren saw what she had drawn without meaning to: eleven pots, each tilted at a slightly different angle, the pencil horizon rocking behind them like a slow pulse. [586]

"The turning is not stillness," Ilse said. [593] "It is the tide making up its mind." [601] "Under it, the water never stops." [607] "That is what feeds the trees." [613] "That is why the salt does not kill them." [622] "They root in the moving part." [628]

Wren looked at the rocking horizons she had drawn and understood that her hands had known before she did. [647] The seed had not failed because the village moved. [656] It had failed because she had planted it hoping the village would stop. [669] She wanted stillness to stand beside the terrible bloom, and stillness was the one thing this place did not grow. [689]

She had a second seed. [694] Ambitious people always carry a second seed. [701]

That night, with the full tide running loud beneath the roots and the platform swaying like a cradle, she planted it. [722] Then she unfolded a fresh scrap of wrapping and drew the pot leaning, the horizon leaning, herself leaning with them, and wrote at the bottom in her grandmother's small square hand: Moving. [754] Fine. [755]

The corpse flower would take seven years to bloom, if it bloomed at all, and when it did it would smell like every death she had ever caught on the wind. [786] She found she could wait for it, rocking. [794]
