Wren had made mourning jewelry for eleven years before she stopped being able to touch hair. [16] She had braided the dead of strangers into brooches and rings and tiny willows under glass, and then her brother drowned, and the lock the coroner gave her sat in its envelope until she sold the shop. [53] Rock bottom, it turned out, had a floor of cold concrete and a ceiling of riveted copper: the abandoned observatory on Fallow Hill, where she slept because no one charged rent for a building the town had forgotten. [91] The clock drive that once turned the telescope with the sky had seized decades ago, so the great tube pointed forever at one patch of dark, and the wall clock had stopped at 3:12, and Wren found this restful. [130] Nothing in the dome asked her what came next. [139]

The first origami crane appeared on the eyepiece in October. [149] She unfolded it with the care she had once given hairwork and found two numbers inside in a slanted hand: a right ascension and a declination. [175] It took her three nights to work the frozen gears loose enough to aim the tube by hand, and what she found was a star that had shed its skin, a green shell of gas with a white ember at the center. [217] A planetary nebula, said the mildewed almanac on the shelf. [227] A dying thing that had learned to look like a flower. [238]

More figures came: a frog, a lily, a boat, each folded from vellum rather than paper, each carrying coordinates, each pointing her at another star coming apart in slow, luminous petals. [269] She did not know who was leaving them, and she noticed she had started to care. [285]

The last figure was not folded at all. [293] It was a flat sheet of vellum laid across the eyepiece, purple ink bleeding faintly through: a tattoo stencil of a chrysanthemum, its petals drawn as strands of braided hair, and beneath it the same slanted hand had written the coordinates of the observatory itself. [338] Wren knew the design. [342] She had drawn it herself, four years ago, for a tattooist named Dov whose wife had died, so he could carry the hairwork pattern on his forearm as well as in the ring she made him. [378]

He was sitting on the dome's iron stair when she turned around, sleeves pushed up, the chrysanthemum faded to the blue of old veins. [402]

"I heard you closed," he said. [408] "I heard about your brother." [413]

She held up the stencil. [418] "You wasted good vellum on me." [424]

"Stencils are meant to be temporary." [430] He shrugged. [432] "They exist to be pressed onto skin and then wiped away, once the intention is set." [448] He looked up at the dome's shutter, sealed since before either of them was born. [463] "You told me once that mourning jewelry is genuinely artificial." [473] "Real hair, false flowers, honest grief." [479] "You said the artifice is what lets people bear to look." [490]

"I was selling rings." [494]

"You were teaching me." [498] He nodded at the telescope. [503] "Those stars you've been finding aren't dying." [510] "Withering is just the part before the bloom, seen from too close." [522] "The gas doesn't know it's supposed to be a tragedy, so it goes and becomes a flower instead." [540]

Wren wanted to say that gas was not a person and a nebula did not have a brother, but the shutter above them had begun to matter in a way she resented. [572]

"You think grief is a curtain," she said. [580]

"I think intention is." [584] "Grief just happens, like weather." [589] "But you choose what you draw across it, and you chose copper, and you chose a clock that says 3:12 forever." [610]

The shutter crank had rusted into a fist. [618] It took both of them, shoulders against the wheel, breath loud in the cold, before the copper groaned and a seam of sky split the ceiling and widened. [646] Starlight came down the tube and out the eyepiece and drew a coin of white on the concrete, and then, as the slot opened fully, the whole floor was illuminated with the soft, indifferent silver of a night that had gone on the entire time she was not looking. [695]

Wren took the envelope from her coat. [702] She had carried it through every move and never once opened it, and her brother's hair was darker than she remembered, and it caught the light like a thing still alive. [733]

"I don't have tools," she said. [739]

"I have a needle," Dov said, and turned his arm so the faded chrysanthemum showed. [754] "And I've never once mixed hair into ink." [762] "Show me how it braids." [767]

She sat on the cold floor, in the light, and began to teach him. [781]
