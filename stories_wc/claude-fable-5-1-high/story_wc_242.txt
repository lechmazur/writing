Under the Fulton Street junction, where the utility tunnel bends away from the live tracks, Ines Marrow kept the last working phone booth in the city. [26] It was not on any map, and the transit authority had stopped billing for the line in 1994, so the calls that reached it arrived by accident. [53] That was the point. [57]

Ines worked only between the hum of streetlamps, the two thin hours at dusk and again at dawn when the grid shifted load and the old copper picked up stray connections from above. [90] During those hours, a misdialed digit anywhere in the borough could loop down through the dead exchange and ring the booth's black receiver. [113] She answered every one. [117]

"You've reached the wrong number," she would say, "but I have time if you do." [132]

Beside the receiver, on a shelf of cable spool, sat a single glass harmonica disc, the size of a saucer, ground thin at the rim. [157] Her brother Teo had played the instrument in a touring ensemble before his lungs failed, and the disc was the one piece that had not been sold to pay for the hospital. [189] When the express passed overhead, the tunnel shook and the glass sang a G-sharp on its own, melodically haunted, as if the note were still looking for the hand that used to draw it out. [224] Callers heard it and asked what it was. [232] Ines never told them. [236]

She had come below deliberately. [241] After Teo, she found that every relationship she kept was simply an ending she had not yet been handed, and she could not bear to wait for them anymore. [270] So she cultivated estrangement the way other people cultivated gardens: patiently, by design. [283] She let her lease lapse, let her friends' calls ring out, and moved her life into a booth where no one could find her on purpose. [309] The wrong numbers were the only friendships she permitted, because they were built to end. [324] A woman trying to reach her pharmacy talked to Ines for forty minutes about her dead husband's handwriting. [342] A boy who had meant to dial his father read her his chemistry homework instead. [357] A night baker called every Thursday for a month by pressing the wrong speed-dial, and when he finally fixed it, he did not call again. [382]

That was what she wanted, or what she told herself she wanted. [394] But the endings kept arriving badly, the same as they always had, cut off mid-sentence, unresolved, a hang-up and a dial tone. [416] The disc rang its lonely G-sharp after each one. [425]

It was the baker's silence that changed her method. [434] On the fifth Thursday, when he did not call, she realized she had been running the same loop as everyone above ground, waiting for the other person to close the door. [465] She thought of Teo, who had ended every concert not with a final chord but with the first three notes of the next piece, played once and left hanging, so the audience walked out carrying a beginning instead of an ending. [506] He called it teaching endings how to begin. [514] She had thought it was a showman's trick. [522] Now she understood it was the only decent way to leave a room. [535]

So she changed what she said. [541] When a call began to wind down, when she heard the breath that meant goodbye, Ines would lift the glass disc from its shelf, wet her fingertip from the cup beside it, and draw one slow circle around the rim. [581] The note bloomed into the receiver, clear and unearthly. [590] "That's my brother's," she would say. [596] "Next time you dial wrong, I'll tell you about him." [606] Then she hung up first. [611]

It was a small thing, and it should not have worked. [622] Yet the callers started coming back. [628] Not by accident; they misdialed on purpose, learning the exact wrong sequence that dropped them through the dead exchange between the hum of streetlamps. [652] The pharmacy woman brought a story about her husband's hands each time and asked for one about Teo in return. [672] The boy passed chemistry and called to tell her. [681] The baker rang back on a Thursday and admitted he had been afraid to interrupt whoever else she was talking to. [702]

Ines noticed that she had stopped counting the calls as endings. [713] Each one closed on the same three-second note, and each one opened the next. [727] She understood, finally, what her brother had known: an ending was only a beginning nobody had bothered to finish. [746]

She still lived in the tunnel. [752] She still called it estrangement, out of habit. [760] But the disc no longer sang on its own when the express passed overhead. [774] She had gotten there first. [779]
