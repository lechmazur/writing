Ilse Marrow had not summoned anything in eleven years, and the valley remembered her for it. [16] She lived alone where the brook bent under the alders, in a low stone house that the villagers called the haven because no one who slept there ever dreamed. [45] That was her doing, though she had never told them. [55]

The whispered secrets began at dusk. [61] They rose from the reeds in fragments, a syllable here, a pause there, and anyone passing on the road heard only water. [83] Ilse heard the code. [87] Eleven years ago, the last circle of summoners in the valley had called something down that was not a spirit and not a star but some terrestrially cosmic thing, root and constellation at once, and it had spoken a prophecy in a cipher of pauses. [132] The words meant nothing. [136] The silences between them meant everything. [142] The circle had scattered in terror, and the thing had sunk into the brook, and the brook had been repeating it ever since. [165]

Now scholars were coming. [169] A man from the university had taken rooms in the village and sat by the water each evening with a notebook, counting gaps. [192] He was close. [195] Ilse knew what he did not: a prophecy in code is harmless until it is read, and this one, once read, would become the shape of what came next. [224] The thing in the water had not predicted a future. [234] It had offered one, and a reader would be the one to accept. [247]

So she would hush it. [252]

She did not work by binding. [258] She worked by mapping silence. [263] Every night for a season she had sat beside the brook with a lantern turned low and a sheet of thin mulberry paper, and each time the whispering paused she marked the paper: here, a gap of two heartbeats; here, a gap of five. [307] The marks made a pattern like a shoreline. [315] The pauses were the prophecy's spine, and she was drawing its skeleton so she could fold it into something that could not speak. [338]

Tonight the map was complete. [343]

The lantern burned steady and small, a tranquil blaze that did not flicker even when the wind came down through the alders. [365] Ilse smoothed the paper across her knee. [372] She began to fold. [376]

An origami crane, because a crane is made almost entirely of what is tucked away. [391] Each crease followed a line of silence on her map, so that every pause the thing had ever uttered was now bent inward and pressed against another pause, gap to gap, until they cancelled. [425] The whispers in the reeds grew thinner as she worked. [435] By the time she shaped the neck they had dropped to a hiss. [448] By the time she pulled the wings out flat they had stopped altogether. [461]

She held it up. [465] One wing had a flaw, a crease she had made against the grain where the map showed a silence eleven heartbeats long, the longest pause of all, the one that had frightened the circle into scattering. [501] That wing would not lie smooth. [507] It trembled in her fingers as if something still breathed inside it. [519]

She could have left it. [524] The scholar would have found nothing tomorrow but water. [533] But a folded pause is only postponed, and Ilse had spent eleven years learning that solitude is not the same as safety. [555]

She set the crane on the surface of the brook. [565] The current took it, slowly, the way it took everything, and she watched the paper turn once in an eddy under the alders, the flawed wing lifting. [592] Then she tipped the lantern. [597] A single tongue of flame leaned out and touched the crane, and the crane lit without haste, a small even fire riding the flow downstream, brightening the water and the reeds and the underside of the leaves. [634] It looked less like burning than like a lamp being carried home. [646]

The flawed wing went last. [651] When it caught, Ilse felt the eleven-heartbeat silence release, not as sound but as a kind of smoothing, the way a sheet settles after being shaken, and then there was only the brook, and the brook said nothing. [689] It flowed. [691]

In the morning the scholar sat with his notebook and counted nothing for an hour, then shut the book, puzzled, and walked back toward the village. [717] Ilse watched him go from her doorway. [724] She had summoned nothing. [728] She had called something back into its own quiet, and the hush she had made was so smooth that even she, listening, could not find the seam. [751]
