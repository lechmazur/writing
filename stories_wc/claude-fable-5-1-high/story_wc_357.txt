Every forty days on Cassel, gravity forgot itself for one hour, and the rivers climbed into the sky. [18] Wren Ashby had flown the orchard skiffs for eleven years, threading the floating rock arches with crates of graft stock, and she had never once flown during the inversion. [47] No one did. [50] The arches drifted when the pull reversed, groaning against their anchor chains, and a pilot who misjudged one would be a smear on the underside of a stone the size of a cathedral. [83]

But the Ninefold Arch was killing her with questions. [92] Every other orchard on Cassel had lost half its trees to the gray blight that spring. [108] The Ninefold had lost none. [113] Its pears set fruit in six weeks instead of twelve, its bark healed over saw cuts before the sap dried, and the agronomists in the lowland station had shrugged and written soil variance in their reports. [149] Wren trusted her grandmother instead, who had surveyed these arches before there were skiffs, firing cord-trailing arrows from stone to stone to measure the drift, and who had said one thing about the Ninefold before she died: the water knows where it goes. [192]

So she was aloft when the hour came. [200] The change began as a lightness in her stomach, then the skiff's altimeter spun into nonsense, and the river below her lifted from its bed like a rope being pulled from above. [232] Every instrument she owned depended on knowing which way was down. [243] The water did not need to know. [250] It simply went. [253] Wren cut her engine and followed it. [260]

The silence was the strangest part. [266] Wind tore past the canopy hard enough to whirl loose leaves in tight spirals, yet none of it made sound; the inversion did something to the air, thickened it, so that the whole sky seemed to spin around her in a held breath. [309] She rode the upward river the way she would have ridden a thermal, banking where it bent around the drifting arches, trusting its silver line over her own eyes. [338]

The river did not scatter into the sky as she had assumed. [350] It gathered. [352] The nine arches of the Ninefold, drifting on their chains, swung inward during the inversion until their undersides closed like the petals of a stone flower, and the water poured up into the hollow they made. [388] Wren slipped the skiff through the last narrowing gap and set it down on what had been, an hour ago, the ceiling. [410]

Inside, the silence broke. [414] Into echoes. [416] The water struck the enclosed stone and the sound came back from nine curved walls at once, and each return struck the others, and the chamber filled with a layered hum that Wren felt in her teeth before she heard it. [457] It sounded like every inversion the arches had ever held, still ringing. [469]

And the roots were listening. [474] The Ninefold's pear trees grew on the tops of the arches, but their roots went all the way through, and here in the hollow they hung exposed in the upside-down rain, pale and thick as her wrist. [511] While she watched, a hair root beside her boot lengthened by the width of her hand, feeling along the wet stone, then another. [534] The hum was doing it. [539] Or the water was, or the two together; she was a pilot, not an agronomist, and she did not need the mechanism to recognize the result. [565] Once every forty days, for one hour, the Ninefold's trees were fed and hurried and made whole. [582]

Then she saw the arrow. [587] It was only the tip, a finger of black glass snapped off at the shaft, driven into a seam of the rock at the chamber's mouth. [613] A root had grown through the break and wound itself around the obsidian, the way a tree swallows a fence wire. [634] Wren knelt and put her thumb against the notch ground into its base, the double groove her grandmother had cut into every survey point so the cord would not slip. [664]

She had been here. [668] Forty years ago, alone, in this same held breath, she had shot a line across the hollow to measure it, and something had broken the arrow, and she had gone home and said nothing but a riddle. [705] Wren thought she understood why. [710] If the station knew, they would chain every arch inward, force a hundred hollows, and the echoes would thin to nothing, the way any voice does when made to fill too large a room. [744]

The hum was already softening. [749] The arches were beginning to swing apart. [756] She left the arrow tip where it was, in the root that had made it part of the tree, and flew home following the water back down as it remembered where to go. [789]
