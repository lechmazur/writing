Wren could only open locks while the moon was being eaten. [11] The rest of the year her hands were ordinary, good for carrying and bad for anything delicate, and so she had learned to live between eclipses the way driftwood lives between tides. [43] Tonight the shadow would take nearly two hours to cross, and she meant to spend every minute of it in Aurelio's library. [65]

The stair wound up through the cloud layer, and the cloud was cold enough to sting. [81] Above it the library stood frozen in the open sky, its shelves furred white, its books swollen and stiff, its windows glazed with ice that had not moved in the seven years since the old clockmaker stopped climbing. [119] He had kept his workshop here because the air was thin and honest, he said, and because the reading hall was lined with mirrors. [143] Long ago scholars had angled them to gather moonlight onto the tables so they could read without candles. [161] Aurelio had used them to watch the tiny gears he worked by night, a hundred reflections of one steady pair of hands. [183]

Wren stepped into the hall as the first bite left the moon. [195] The mirrors caught the reddening light and threw it back and forth until the whole room glowed hauntingly bright, copper and rust and blood-orange, as if the frost itself were remembering fire. [227] A hundred Wrens looked back at her, and every one of them had hands that could open anything and mend nothing. [248]

That was the trouble. [252] A locksmith who only opens is just a polite thief. [262] Aurelio had told her that the last time she saw him, wiping oil from his fingers, and then he had said that repair had a rhythm and that he would teach it to her next eclipse. [298] There had been no next eclipse for him. [306]

She went first to his bench, where the tin sat exactly where she remembered, its lid sealed under a skin of ice. [328] Her hands, awake now, found the seam without looking, and she uncapped it with a sound like a small breath. [348] Inside lay his gear assortment, dozens of brass wheels sorted by tooth count into little paper cups, each one worn smooth at the edges from years of being lifted and set down and lifted again. [383] She knew how to take them out. [390] She did not know how to put anything back. [399]

The mirrors showed her the corner before she thought to look there: a low shelf where the frost had cracked, and behind it a wedge of paper, folded and refolded until the creases had gone soft as cloth. [437] Aurelio had never believed in prophecy, but he had collected it the way other men collect shells, scraps torn from almanacs and margins, and he had hidden them here where only the eclipse light reached. [472] She read them by the red glow. [479]

The girl who opens will learn to close when the moon closes. [491]

Count the shadow, not the hand. [497]

Every tooth waits for its partner; the mending is the waiting. [508]

In his cramped script beneath the last one he had added: This is not prophecy, Wren, it is just how it works, but you never listened unless it sounded like fate. [539]

She laughed, and the hundred mirrored Wrens laughed with her, and the sound came back smaller each time until it was gone. [561]

The great library clock had stopped the winter he died, its hands frozen at some hour nobody remembered. [579] Wren carried the tin to its open case and did what the scraps said. [593] She watched the shadow crawl across the moon in the mirrors and let its slowness set the pace in her wrists. [614] She did not force the first wheel. [621] She held it near its neighbor and waited, and when the teeth found each other on their own she understood, finally, that opening was quick and closing was patient, and that the difference was the whole craft. [658]

Gear by gear the movement filled. [664] The moon began to return. [669] The mirrors dimmed from copper to silver to plain frost-white, and her hands began, as they always did, to forget. [689] But the rhythm did not live in the hands. [698] It lived in the waiting, and she could carry that down the stair with her. [713]

Under the soft hush of a distant morning, far below the clouds and hours from arriving, the library clock took one tick, then another, ordinary as a heartbeat. [741] Wren folded the scraps back into their corner for whoever came next, capped the tin, and left the mirrors to keep the time. [764]
