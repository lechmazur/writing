Ines Okoro's title at Halcyon was corporate memory keeper, which meant she decided what the company was allowed to forget. [20] Most of what it wanted to forget was Murmur, the social network it had bought for its patents and then let die. [42] Forty million dormant profiles sat in the archive, and her job was to keep what the law required and purge the rest. [64] She was good at it because she was brightly attentive in a way that made the engineers uneasy; she noticed things that were not supposed to be noticeable. [92]

She noticed Dana Vell on a Tuesday. [99] The profile was flagged for a legal hold that had expired: a missing persons case, opened in 2011, closed without result in 2016. [122] Dana had been twenty-six. [126] Her last post was a photograph of an embroidered patch, frayed at one edge: a stylized ship over a red planet, the words AURELIAN 7 stitched around the rim. [155] Ines knew the mission without looking it up; everyone her age did. [167] Aurelian 7 had broken apart on reentry at 7:14 in the morning, and the crew's names had been read aloud in schools. [189] One of them had been Commander Ruth Vell. [197] The caption said only: found this in Mom's drawer. [206]

Ines should have queued the profile for purge. [214] Instead she opened the activity log, and that was when the numbers started. [227]

Dana had posted a song every day for three years, and the timestamps clustered around 7:14, morning and evening, as if she had set an alarm to grieve. [255] The songs themselves were gone; the streaming service that hosted them had folded, and every link resolved to nothing. [274] Only the titles remained, and the durations, and Ines began reading them at night the way other people read poetry. [294] Seven of the songs ran exactly seven minutes and fourteen seconds. [305] Dana's user ID ended in 714. [311] Ines told herself this was pattern-hunger, the ordinary madness of archivists, and then she found that the account had deleted 714 items in its final week. [337]

Halcyon's public position was that deleted meant deleted. [345] Ines had written that sentence herself, for a compliance report, and she had never quite believed it. [362] Nothing at Halcyon was ever destroyed; it was moved somewhere the lawyers could not see, and the lawyers were paid not to ask where. [386] She had wanted, for years, to know where the deleted files went. [398] Now she had a reason that felt less like curiosity and more like debt. [412]

So she lied. [415] She told Priyam in infrastructure that legal needed a chain-of-custody audit on cold storage, and she said it with the flat confidence of someone who had signed a hundred such requests. [446] Priyam did not check. [450] He gave her a path to a tape library in a building she had never visited, and a login that expired in seventy-two hours. [474]

The deleted files were there. [479] All of them, everyone's, a decade of second thoughts stacked in the dark. [492] Dana's 714 were mostly drafts, unsent messages to a father who had stopped answering, photographs of the patch from different angles as though she were trying to see behind it. [522] The last deleted item was a note, never posted, written at 7:14 in the morning on the day she disappeared. [542] It said: I keep finding her number everywhere and I think it is her way of telling me where she went. [563] I am going to go look. [569]

Ines sat in the humming room for a long time. [579] She understood that she had just done what Dana had done, followed a number into a place records were not supposed to go, and that the number had led her only to the shape of a person and not the person. [620] The archive was full of Dana. [626] It could tell her what Dana listened to at dawn for three years and could not tell her whether Dana was alive. [648] That was what an archive was, Ines thought: the outline left when something is lifted away. [664]

She copied the 714 files to a drive she would not log. [676] Then she went back to her desk and marked the profile for indefinite retention, citing a hold that did not exist, her second lie of the week and the easier one. [707]

That night she looked up the song titles one by one. [718] Most of the bands had vanished too, and the few recordings she found were wrong versions, covers, live sets with the crowd talking over the quiet parts. [745] She played them anyway, at 7:14, and listened for whatever Dana had been listening for. [760] She did not hear it. [765] But she kept the hour, the way you keep a chair empty at a table, and found that the absence had a sound of its own. [791]
