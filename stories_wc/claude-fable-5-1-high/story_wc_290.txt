Ines Marrow hated ceilings, which made her a poor kind of explorer and a careful one. [16] She went into ruins the way a swimmer enters cold water: knowing exactly where the shore was. [33] The fallow period was the only time she could reach Sallow Cross at all. [47] In summer the fields around the abandoned village grew wheat to the church steps, and the farmer would not have her trampling them. [70] In the dead weeks after harvest, the ground lay bare and rested, and nobody cared who walked across it under the stars. [92]

Her grandmother had spoken of the church only once. [101] "It did not stay where Havel drew it," she said, and would say nothing more. [116] Havel was the architect who had planned the whole village from one sheet, every lane a radius, the church crossing at the center where his compass pin had bitten the paper. [147] Ines had found the blueprint in a county archive, and pressed into its fold, a small steel spike: the drafting compass point, the needle itself, rusted to the color of dried blood. [179] She had taken it. [183]

They called it the mutiny. [188] Walls that leaned away from their drawn lines. [196] A crypt door that appeared on the north side one winter and the south side the next. [213] Lanes that curled instead of running straight to the center as Havel intended. [226] Space itself, the old people said, had rebelled against the man who tried to fasten it with a pin. [245] When the villagers left, they took the key to the crypt with them, or lost it, or buried it; the accounts disagreed. [267] Whatever lay below the crossing had lain there for a century, and Ines's grandmother had died wanting to know what it was. [289]

So Ines came at night, prudently, with a rope, a headlamp, chalk, and a rule she never broke: never enter a space she had not first watched from outside. [318] The tower had fallen long ago, and the crossing lay open to the sky. [332] Starlight came down through the wound in the roof and pooled at the meeting of nave and transept, a shadowed nexus where four dark arms of the church reached toward a center none of them quite touched. [369] Because that was the thing: the walls did not meet. [379] The nave pulled a hand's width east. [386] It was not ruin. [390] Ruin falls inward. [393] This had pulled away, deliberately, like a crowd stepping back from a man they no longer trusted. [410]

She stood at the edge of the starlight and could not make herself step under the dark arms. [428] Then the wind changed. [432] Somewhere in the south transept the organ still had pipes, and the wind found them, and a single low note came out of the dark and did not stop. [461] It reverberated through the stone floor, up through her boots, into her teeth. [474] It transfixed her, not with fear but with the strange loneliness of a machine still singing to a congregation that had walked away. [497] She stood pinned in it, and while she stood, she watched the note travel: it did not echo evenly. [516] It rang from the flagstones toward a single point at the crossing's heart and died there, swallowed, as sound is swallowed by a hollow. [540]

She knelt in the starlight. [545] There, in the center flag, a hole no wider than a nail, worn smooth. [559] The pin's mark. [562] She understood then what her grandmother had meant and what the village had done. [576] Havel had built his church to be locked from the center, a single key for a single master. [594] And the people had not stolen the key; they had simply moved the walls, inch by year, until no measurement from any wall would find the center. [621] A quiet, decades-long mutiny of space against ownership. [629] The key was never lost. [634] It had been lying in a county drawer, mistaken for a draftsman's scrap. [647]

She took the compass point from her pocket and set it in the hole, and it fit the way a tooth fits its socket. [671] Below her, stone shifted. [675] The organ note broke off. [680] A slab beside the north pillar sank a finger's depth, and cold air breathed up from a stair that had not been there a moment before. [706]

Ines looked at the narrow black mouth of it and felt the old panic rise, the certainty that stone wanted to close. [728] Then she thought of a hundred hands leaning on a hundred walls for a hundred years, refusing to be fixed. [748] She tied off her rope, chalked the first step, and went down with the key in her hand, one breath at a time, into the dark the village had kept for someone who would come afraid and come anyway.
