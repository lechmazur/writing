Ilse had spent thirty-one years in the pit, in the space between the stage and the seats, where nobody ever looked. [21] The river had come through the doors of the Lindqvist Opera House on Tuesday, and by Thursday the stalls were a black lake. [44] She waded in anyway, a lantern hissing in her fist, because her viola was still down there and because, for three weeks, she had been waking with music in her head. [75] Not other people's music, which was all she had ever played, but her own. [89] It arrived in fragments, always stopping at the same place, like a chord raised and never struck. [106]

The water reached her thighs by the time she found the pit rail. [119] Other lanterns hung where the stagehands had left them, guttering on hooks along the boxes, so that the whole drowned house flickered like something breathing. [144] Her case floated against the timpani, sealed and dry. [153] Beside it, spread open on the surface like a lily, was a program from the house's first night, October 1911, which had lived for a century behind glass in the lobby. [184] The glass was gone. [188]

Ilse lifted the program and held it to the lantern. [198] The water had done something to the ink. [206] The printed names had bled and run together, and where they pooled, the stains had shaped themselves into pictures: a woman at a rail, a raised hand, five thin lines with marks along them. [240] A staff. [242]

Everyone in the pit knew the story of Anna Voss. [252] Second viola, opening night, when the old river had risen for the first time and the performance stopped in the second act with the orchestra's bows lifted and the downbeat never given. [284] Everyone else got out. [288] Anna's music stand was found upright the next morning, her chair beneath it, and Anna beneath the chair. [306] Players said she was still down there, in the hush before the conductor's arm fell, in the half-second that belonged to no one. [329] Ilse had never believed it, but she had never once played the first note of anything without feeling the silence lean toward her. [352]

She turned the page. [356] More stains, more staves, the marks on them too deliberate to be accident. [369] She read them the way she read anything, hearing before understanding, and her breath caught, because it was her melody. [389] The one she had been waking with. [396] The one that stopped. [400] Only here it did not stop. [406] Here it went on for four more bars and ended on a chord she had never been able to find. [426]

It had not been inspiration, then. [432] It had been dictation. [436] Someone had been humming to her for three weeks from a place with no voice in it, and this was the rest of the sentence. [461]

The lanterns dimmed together, then steadied. [467] Ilse understood it as a question. [473]

She wedged the lantern on the conductor's podium, opened her case, and tuned in the dark, the water shifting cold around her legs. [496] For a long moment she held the bow above the strings and did nothing, and the house held its breath with her. [518] This was where Anna lived. [523] A century of unstruck chords stacked one on the next, a whole opera of beginnings that nobody had ever disrupted. [543] Ilse thought she finally understood the code, and it was not a message so much as a plea: break it. [563]

She brought the bow down. [568]

The first phrase went out across the water and came back off the boxes doubled, and she played the answer to it before the echo faded, so that for a few bars there were two violas in the room. [607] Neither of them said a word. [613] The stains on the program went on ahead of her, and she followed them through the four new bars, and the lanterns leaned with each phrase like heads nodding. [642] Then the final chord, the one she could never find alone. [653] She struck it. [656]

It was not beautiful, exactly. [661] It was resolved, which is different, and the sound of it went down through the water to the floor of the pit and did not come back. [688] The silence afterward was only silence. [694] Nothing leaned toward her. [698]

One by one the lanterns along the boxes burned down to blue and went out, unhurried, the way a hall empties after the last curtain. [723] Ilse stood in the dark with the program soft as cloth in her hand. [737] Her own lantern alone still burned, and by it the images were gone, the ink run to plain grey, the paper saying nothing at all. [762] She had the melody whole, and she waded back toward the doors humming it, out of the between-place and into the ordinary world, where chords get struck and things are allowed to end. [795]
