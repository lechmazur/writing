The drought ended on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the water had found Dell Okafor in the maintenance tunnel under Ninth Street. [22] Ines Varga read the preliminary report twice before Halvorsen called. [32] "Act of weather," he said, in the voice that had trained her for eleven years. [47] "Four years dry, then six inches in a night. [56] Nobody could have seen it." [61]

She was the transit authority's preventable death investigator, and her job was to decide whether he was right. [79] Her job, in practice, had become confirming that he was. [89]

She went down anyway. [93]

The tunnel still smelled of the flood: river silt, diesel, something older. [105] Her boots rang on the catwalk and the sound reverberated ahead of her, down the long throat of brick, and came back thinned. [128] She kept her face watchfully calm, the way Halvorsen had taught her, because a calm face was a wall nobody thought to climb. [151]

The pump station sat where the modern tunnel met a wall of older, hand-laid brick. [166] On a hook beside the control panel hung a tin lantern, dented, its glass smoked nearly black. [183] The crews called it Harriet. [188] Ines knew the story because Dell had told it to her, grinning, three years ago: the old wall was the back of a church cellar, and when the city cut the tunnel through in 1911, workers found the lantern wedged in a hidden alcove, left by someone who had guided people north through the dark. [243] Every crew since had kept it lit. [250]

It was dark now. [254]

She noticed that first, and noticed herself noticing, and then noticed Halvorsen's voice arriving to explain it away: the flood put it out, obviously. [278] That was the trouble. [282] Eleven years of his explanations had grown into her own recollection until she could not tell which memories were hers. [302] She remembered the Larkin derailment as a signal fault, but she also remembered standing on that track and thinking the rail looked wrong before he ever said signal. [330] The two memories lay tangled in her like wet rope, and she had stopped trying to pull them apart. [349]

So she did the only thing she trusted, which was to look. [361] She measured. [363]

The high-water line on the brick sat at one hundred forty-one centimeters. [375] The pump's intake grate sat at thirty. [382] The grate was packed solid with four years of dry silt, baked hard as tile, and no flood had done that; the drought had, one dustless day at a time, while the inspection log beside the panel showed the same initials, month after month, in the same unhurried hand. [431] Halvorsen's initials. [433] He had signed off the pump without descending, and Dell had gone down on the night the rain came to clear it by hand. [457]

Ines stood very still. [461] Her radio crackled with his voice, asking what she saw. [471] She did not answer yet. [476]

She took the lantern down instead. [482] It was lighter than she expected, and inside, under a century of soot, the wick was dry. [499] Not drowned. [501] Dry. [502] Dell had let it go out before the water ever came, or someone had, or it had simply starved in the drought like everything else, and she would never know, and that not-knowing was hers, unnarrated, a small clean fact with no one's voice around it. [548]

She thought about whoever had carried this lantern last, before the alcove. [560] They had walked people through cellars and culverts with nothing but what the light showed them, no one behind them saying what the dark meant. [585] They had trusted their eyes because the alternative was to stay owned. [597]

Her hands were shaking. [601] She let them. [604] The calm on her face stayed where it was, but it had become something else, less a wall than a door she was choosing not to open yet. [632]

She keyed the radio. [636] "Intake grate obstructed prior to the event," she said. [645] "High-water line one forty-one, grate at thirty. [652] Log shows monthly sign-off, no physical inspection." [659]

Silence, then his voice, gentler, the voice that always came before he rewrote her. [673] "Ines, come up and we'll talk about what you saw." [683]

"I know what I saw," she said. [690] "I wrote it down before you called." [697]

She clipped the radio to her belt and did not wait for the answer. [711] The report would name him, and she would lose the job, or he would, and either way she would spend months learning which of her memories were her own. [740] That was fine. [743] She had a lantern, a dry wick, and matches in her kit, because she had checked. [759]

She lit it, and the small flame steadied, and she walked back up the tunnel with her own light going ahead of her and the sound of her boots coming back only as itself. [793]
