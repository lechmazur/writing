Ilse Varga told the bookseller she had been born on a ferry between two countries that no longer existed. [19] She told the woman at the bakery she had grown up in a lighthouse. [33] Neither was true, but both were easier than the truth, which was that she had once trained three hundred courier pigeons and now trained one. [58]

The one was a grey hen named Kestrel, and she lived with Ilse in the attic above Halloran's Antiquarian Books, where the rent was paid in shelving. [85] Kestrel had stopped coming home the winter the loft burned. [95] She would fly, and circle, and land on any roof but Ilse's shoulder. [108] A bird that will not return is not a courier; it is only a bird. [123]

The attic had a single skylight, and every afternoon the light came through it in a slanted column thick with dust. [144] Ilse had learned, in the loft years, that a pigeon reads the air before it reads the hand. [162] So she sat beneath the skylight with a pencil and a ledger page torn from one of Halloran's ruined stock books and drew where the motes went. [189] They rose along the east rafter, hung, and fell in a slow spiral toward the chimney wall. [206] Day by day the map thickened, a bloom of pencil that showed her the attic's one calm pocket, the place where a nervous bird might choose to stop. [234] She moved the perch there. [239]

Halloran came up the ladder with tea and found the pages pinned to the beams. [254] He was a man who lived by paper trails, who could tell you which of his books had passed through a customs house in 1911 by the color of a stamp, and he studied her dust maps as if they were provenance. [296] The ferry, he said, was that also a map? [305] She did not answer, and he did not press, and that was the first thing she trusted about him. [324]

The truth was a farmhouse loft and a mother who sang while she banded the birds, in a dialect that had died with the last four people who spoke it. [354] Ilse had never written the songs down; you do not archive what you assume will always be in the next room. [375] Then the room burned, and the singer with it, and now the songs existed only as the shape her own throat made when she was too tired to stop it. [405] Every year they withered a little more. [412]

The only other thing that had come out of the fire was a chip of meteorite, black and pitted, no larger than a fingernail. [436] Her mother had bought it from a traveling dealer, with a certificate declaring it Martian, and had laughed and said it was the only thing in the house that came with its own paperwork. [470] The certificate burned. [473] The chip did not. [477] Ilse kept it because a stone from another world was the one story she owned that she had not invented. [497]

In the spring Kestrel landed on the perch. [505] Ilse did not move. [509] The dust turned in the light exactly as the map said it would, and the bird settled into the calm pocket and closed one eye. [534]

That night she borrowed Halloran's jeweler's scale, the brass one he used for weighing gilt. [549] A courier's message capsule can carry two grams before the bird begins to list. [563] She set the meteorite chip in one pan; it weighed, she knew from her mother's certificate, a little under two. [583] In the other pan she laid a strip of onionskin and began to write the first song, phonetically, letter by letter, adding paper until the pans hung level. [611] The stone that had survived the fire would tell her exactly how much of her mother a bird could bear. [631]

Halloran watched from the ladder. [636] Where will it go, he asked. [642] To you, she said, and for the first time gave him the farmhouse, the loft, the four dead speakers, the fire, all of it, the true paper trail of a woman who had been scattering false ones for a decade. [682] A song is not preserved by being kept, she told him. [693] It is preserved by being sent, and received, and filed by someone who will remember the color of the stamp. [713]

In the morning she carried Kestrel to the roof, the capsule balanced on her leg, and let her go. [732] The bird rose, and circled, and came down through the skylight into the column of dust, into the calm pocket, into Halloran's waiting hands. [756] He unrolled the onionskin and read the first line aloud, badly, in a language nobody living spoke. [773] It bloomed in the attic for a moment and faded, as songs do. [786] But it was written now, and weighed, and delivered. [795]
