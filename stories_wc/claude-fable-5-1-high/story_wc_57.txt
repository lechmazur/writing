Ilse had spent thirty years believing that a seed was patience made small. [13] It waited in the dark without complaint, and it never argued with the season. [27] Colleagues called her infinitely patient, though she thought the phrase belonged to seeds, not to her. [43] She tried to live the same way, which was why she had come to the harbor every morning for eleven days, standing on the golden dock while the snow settled on her shoulders. [76] The dock had earned its name from paint, not sunlight. [86] Someone long ago had coated every plank in ochre varnish, and the old color still showed through where boots had worn the snow away. [110] Nobody in the village remembered why. [116] Ilse suspected she did. [120]

Anselm arrived as he always did, as fog rolled across the harbor and swallowed the boats one by one. [139] He was the last fisherman who still worked from this dock, and the last man who owned a rope tied by his grandmother. [162] He set his coffee on a bollard and looked at Ilse with the weary tolerance of a man who had decided she was harmless. [186] "Still here," he said. [190] "Still here," she agreed. [194]

She had never asked to see the rope. [202] That was the point. [206] You did not pull a seedling to check its roots; you waited, and you made the ground hospitable. [224] So she talked instead about eelgrass, the underwater meadows that had once filled this harbor and held the shoreline in place. [245] She told him how the meadows had thinned to nothing, how her institute had tried to replant them with machines and failed, and how she had found, in a nineteenth-century ledger, a single line about the women of this coast who had sown the grass by hand and kept the method to themselves. [298] The ledger called them the secret weavers. [305]

On the twelfth morning Anselm brought the rope. [313] He did not hand it over so much as let it fall across the bollard between them, a coil of tarred hemp gone gray with salt, knotted along its length in a pattern too regular to be accidental. [351] "She used to tie these while she waited for the boats," he said. [364] "I thought it was nerves." [369]

Ilse knelt in the snow and did not touch it yet. [380] The fog pressed in, and the only sound was water moving under the planks, a hush so total it seemed the sea itself was holding its breath. [407] She had studied knots for three years for this moment. [417] Overhand knots, she saw, came in clusters of one to six. [428] Between them ran plain lengths, and every so often a figure-eight broke the sequence like a comma. [445] She counted aloud, softly, and Anselm leaned closer despite himself. [455]

"Clusters are days after the new moon," she said. [464] "The plain lengths are depth, measured against the rope itself." [474] "The figure-eights are where the pattern repeats." [481] "It's a calendar." [484] "It's a planting calendar for the harbor floor." [492]

Anselm said nothing for a long while. [499] Then he said, "There's a knot at the end that isn't like the others." [513]

It was a monkey's fist, a tight sphere of rope wrapped around something hard. [527] Ilse worked it loose with the slowness she had practiced her whole life, coaxing each turn free without forcing the fibers, the way one coaxes a stubborn husk from a kernel. [558] Inside lay a finger of fulgurite glass, a hollow tube of sand fused by lightning, rough on the outside and smooth as a throat within. [583] She understood it at once. [588] The weavers had threaded eelgrass seeds through it into the knots of biodegradable cord, so that the seeds sank anchored and stayed put until they rooted, while the cord rotted away. [619] The lightning glass had been their needle, and the golden dock, painted to be seen through fog, had been the mark from which they measured every length. [646]

"She never told anyone," Anselm said. [652] "They were paid to keep the harbor," Ilse said. [661] "When the fishing companies dredged the meadows, the payments stopped, and the women kept the knowledge in the only place no one would think to read." [687]

He picked up the fulgurite and turned it in the fog-light. [698] "You'll take it to your institute." [704] "No," she said. [707] "I'll bring the seeds here." [712] "You'll tie the cord." [716] "Your hands already know the knots, even if you don't." [726]

The snow kept falling onto the ochre planks, and the fog moved slowly out toward the open water, and somewhere beneath them the harbor floor waited, dark and patient, for the first new moon of spring. [762]
