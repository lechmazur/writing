The foghorn stopped at eleven minutes past four, and Ismene Vail knew it would not start again. [17] For sixty-one years it had sounded across the harbor every fog-heavy night, and for sixty-one years she had listened to it from the orchard on the roof of the Calloway Tower, where the oaths of the city were sworn and set down. [59] The orchard was older than the tower. [66] When the founders raised the building, they had lifted the trees up with it floor by floor, twelve grafted apples and one bitter quince, so that promises would be made beneath branches that had already outlived every liar who planted them. [107]

Ismene was the last oath scribe. [113] She took the vows in the old way: the speaker knelt under the quince and repeated the words in a tongue no living person used for buying bread, Akkadian or Oscan or the court dialect of a kingdom that had drowned. [154] Dead languages could not be lied in, the founders believed, because no one had loved or bargained in them for centuries; they had gone clean. [179] Ismene spoke fourteen of them. [184]

But the words were never what she kept. [192] The ledgers held the words. [197] What she kept, on wax cylinders in the phonograph her predecessor had left her, was everything around the words: the catch before "always," the small dry swallow after "until death," the way a merchant's voice went up half a tone when he swore fidelity to a partner he had already decided to ruin. [250] She had spent her life archiving these patterns, certain that feeling repeated itself more faithfully than any vow, and that someone, someday, would need the catalogue. [276]

Now the harbor was closed, the last ferry gone, and the tower below her sixty floors of dark. [294] The silence where the horn had been was not empty; it had texture, like velvet pulled over a mouth. [313]

She went into the shed among the apple trees and lit the lamp. [326] On the shelf above the phonograph stood forty jars of shavings, the pale curls of wax that came off a cylinder when she planed it smooth to record again. [355] She could never throw them out. [361] Each curl had once held a voice, and she had a superstition, never spoken aloud in any language, that the feeling stayed in the wax after the sound was gone. [391]

One jar was labeled only with a year, forty years back, and a single word: children. [407] She unscrewed it, took out the longest shaving, and lowered it into a bowl of warm water to soften, the way she had always done when she wanted to press a curl flat and read the grooves under a glass. [447] The wax uncurled slowly, and the memory came up with it. [458]

A third-grade class, the only one ever permitted on the roof, herded under the trees by a teacher who kept counting them. [480] Most of them had wanted to know if you could see the sea. [493] One boy, small, with a gap in his teeth, had wanted to know what the horn on the machine was for, and whether he could talk into it. [521] She had let him, because a cylinder was cheap and a curious child was not. [536] He had leaned into the brass and recited a rhyme he said he made up, a skipping-rope nonsense of hard syllables, and the teacher had laughed, and Ismene had gone very still. [568]

Because it was not nonsense. [573] It was Hittite, badly stressed but whole, and it said: when the horn in the water goes quiet, the keeper of promises will be the only promise left. [601]

She had told herself it was chance, that a child could stumble into any sentence if you gave him enough languages to stumble in. [625] She had shaved the cylinder that same week and reused it for a wedding. [639] But she had kept the curl. [645]

Now she lifted it from the water, pressed it flat on the glass, and bent over it with the lamp, and she did not need the grooves. [672] She could hear him perfectly, the gap-toothed cadence, the pleased little breath at the end. [687] Sixty-one years of catalogued feeling, and she had never found a pattern for that: a child prophesying by accident, with no fear in his voice at all. [714]

The quince rattled outside in a wind that came off a harbor no ship would cross again. [731] Ismene set a fresh cylinder on the spindle, knelt on the shed floor because her knees would not let her reach the tree, and spoke into the brass in the oldest language she had. [765] She swore to stay. [769] Then she listened back, once, to hear what her own voice did around the words. [784]
