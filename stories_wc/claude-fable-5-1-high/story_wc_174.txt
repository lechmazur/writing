The radar dome had not listened to anything for thirty years, so it had become very good at holding silence. [20] Ines Varga felt that silence settle over her the moment Dov slammed out through the hatch, his footsteps ringing down the steel stair and dying against the bluff. [48] The argument had lasted forty minutes. [54] Its residue lasted longer: the fire in the drum at the center of the floor had burned low, the puppets lay face down on the workbench like exhausted drunks, and the story stick had rolled under a folding chair where Marisol had dropped it. [98]

Marisol herself sat with her boots on the drum's rim, not looking at anyone. [112] "Well," she said. [115]

Ines did not answer yet. [120] She had spent twenty years lettering wedding invitations and gallery catalogs in a city that paid well for elegance, and she had learned there that the most useful thing a person could bring to a room was calm. [158] People mistook her uncommon serenity for indifference. [165] It was closer to attention. [170]

She bent, retrieved the stick, and turned it in the ember light. [182] It was driftwood, salt-cured, a little longer than her forearm, and along its length she had inked, in a script she'd once sold for a hundred dollars a line, the troupe's single rule: whoever holds this speaks as if the absent were listening. [225] They passed it around the fire every Thursday, building the next show one voice at a time, then carried the result down to the Anchor Room and the Blue Eel and the other bars along the harbor where nobody checked licenses and everybody liked to watch the powerful get made of felt. [277]

The current show was about Teodor Hask, the harbor master, who had taken money to look away from certain boats. [297] That much was true, and the bars roared at it. [307] But Dov had added a scene in which the Hask puppet sold his own daughter for a crate of lamp oil, and the daughter was real, and she tended bar at the Blue Eel three nights a week. [345]

"It's satire," Dov had shouted. [350] "It's a lie that rhymes," Marisol had shouted back. [359] Ines had said almost nothing, which was perhaps why the argument had gone on so long; there had been no one to fill the pauses. [384]

Now she looked up. [388] The dome's fiberglass panels had cracked in a dozen places over the decades, and through the cracks the night came in thin as thread. [412] A meteor went across one of them, a brief bright stitch, gone before she could decide she had seen it. [432] Then another. [434] The Perseids, she remembered. [438] Half the sky lit for half a second, then dark again, and in that flicker she understood something she had not been able to say at full volume. [466]

The stick always went clockwise. [471] Every Thursday, each hand that took it added a sharper edge to what the last hand had said, because that was how you got a laugh in a bar at midnight: you escalated. [504] The daughter was not a scene anyone had chosen. [513] She was where the direction of the stick had carried them. [524]

The hatch groaned. [527] Dov came back in smelling of cold air and stood at the edge of the light, not sitting, not leaving. [547]

Ines held the stick up so the inked rule caught the embers. [559] "We're going to run it backward," she said. [567]

"The show?" [569]

"The stick first." [572] She handed it to Marisol on her left, against the old direction, and Marisol, surprised, took it. [589] "Start with the last scene and tell it toward the first. [600] Hask at the end, alone on the pier, and then each of us walks him back a step. [618] The bribe. [620] The year before the bribe. [625] The day he got the job." [631]

"That's not funny," Dov said. [636]

"Not at first." [639] Another meteor crossed the crack above the workbench. [647] "But you'll find out why he did it, because you'll have to invent it, and whatever you invent will be kinder than what you'd invent going forward. [674] And the daughter stays out of it, because by the time we reach her she's a child, and no one in the Blue Eel wants to laugh at a child." [704]

Dov stood a long moment. [709] Then he crossed the floor, sat, and held out his hand for the stick, though it was not yet his turn and would not be, going this way, for two more voices. [741] He put the hand back in his lap and waited. [751]

Marisol began quietly, a man alone on a pier, and Ines fed the fire one small board and watched the dome fill, by pieces, with a story arriving in the only order that could hold it. [787]
