Mireille sold ancestors. [3] For twenty years she had built family trees for people who could pay, grafting bakers onto barons and foundlings onto shipping dynasties, and she was good at it because she never stopped at paper. [37] A forged baptismal record was cheap; what her clients truly bought was the recipe card in a grandmother's hand, the tarnished spoon, the story told so well that they remembered being told it as children. [72] She manufactured false memories, and she had made peace with most of them. [85] Not with Tobias Rann. [89] Eleven years ago he had come to her wanting his father, and she had found him: not the lost naval officer Tobias hoped for, but a watchmaker named Aurel Lisk who had walked out on a pregnant woman and gone to carve driftwood on a shore nobody visited anymore. [138] Tobias had paid for a hero. [144] She gave him one, a lieutenant drowned in a storm, complete with a commendation she typed herself. [161] The one true thing she found among his mother's effects, a single brass watch hand wrapped in tissue, she kept. [181] It was in her pocket now. [187]

The guesthouse terrace was tiled in cobalt, chipped and salt-dulled, and at dusk it held the last light the way a bowl holds water. [211] Below it the forgotten shore curved away into dune grass, and the quiet there was not empty but sieved, every sound strained through sand until only the fine grains of it reached her: a gull, a hinge of wind, the tide breathing. [253] She knew that kind of hush. [259] She had sold it to Tobias, a silence with the truth strained out. [272]

His letter had arrived in March, three lines in a shaking hand: he was ill, he was grateful, and he wanted to be buried with his father's commendation. [300] She had not answered. [304] For weeks she had lived under the weight of unspoken words, drafting confessions and burning them, until she understood that she could not judge whether to speak without first learning how much the truth actually weighed. [340] Aurel Lisk had died four winters back. [347] The carvings had not. [351]

She went down while there was still light. [359] They lay where he had left them along the high tideline, wedged into rock clefts and half-buried in sand, dozens of figures gone silver with weather. [385] She sifted through them on her knees, brushing grit from faces, turning each one to the fading sky. [403] Birds, mostly. [405] A woman with her hair tied back, carved again and again, always turned slightly away. [420] Then a child. [423] Then a boy a little older, and another older still, and she felt her breath go thin as she understood what she was kneeling in. [448] He had carved his son every year. [455] Guessing at him, aging him by imagination, a boy he had never seen grow. [469]

Each figure had a small hole bored in the base, empty, the wood around it stained faintly green. [487] Brass. [488] She took the watch hand from her pocket. [496] It slid into the first carving, the infant, and seated itself with a click she felt in her teeth. [515] He had sent them, then. [520] One hand from one watch, a year at a time, marking hours he would never share, and the mother had kept only the first and let the rest go, or returned them, or the sea had. [556] Mireille would never know which, and she noticed that the not-knowing hurt. [568]

So that was the weight. [573] Not a coward who left, which Tobias could have carried, but a coward who kept counting, which was heavier, and which she had decided on his behalf he could not. [603] She had told herself for eleven years that she was protecting him. [615] Kneeling among the boys Aurel Lisk had imagined, she could no longer quell the plainer explanation: a hero was easier to invoice. [637]

She did not weep. [641] She had spent too long counterfeiting other people's feelings to trust a large one of her own. [658] Instead she chose two: the infant with its brass hand restored, and the last figure, a stooped man carved the winter before Aurel died. [682] She wrapped them in her scarf. [688] Tobias would not get his hero. [694] He would get a father who had been ashamed for forty years and had never once put the knife down, and he would have to decide for himself what that was worth, which was the one thing she had never let a client do. [738]

She climbed back to the cobalt terrace as the blue drained out of the tiles. [753] The hush down on the shore went on sieving itself, patient and fine, keeping nothing back that was not simply too large to pass. [777] She would take the morning train. [783]
