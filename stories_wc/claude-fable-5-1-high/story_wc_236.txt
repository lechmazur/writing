The fog came in before the gulls did, and Ilse carried the clock through it like a sleeping child. [19] It was a water clock, her mother's, a brass basin with a float and a needle that had crept faithfully around its dial for forty years and then, three weeks ago, stopped. [51] The same week her mother had. [57]

Aurel's shop sat at the end of the canal lane, its window sweating with the morning. [73] She did not go in at first. [80] She stood on the wet stones and watched him through the clouded glass, a shape bending over a bench, and she read him the way she had learned to read everyone this past year: not by scent, because there was no scent anymore, but by what the fog left visible. [130] A slow shoulder. [133] A hand that paused before it touched. [140] Patience, she decided, and some old grief he had made room for. [152]

For fifteen years she had been an aromatherapist, and her gift had never really been the oils. [169] It had been the way a client's fear smelled before the client admitted it, the sourness of a marriage, the green note of someone about to leave a job. [198] She had mapped emotional terrain by nose, and then a fever had taken the map away and left her with a face that could still smile at lavender and feel nothing. [229] She had closed the practice. [234]

The bell was a real bell, not a chime. [243] "You are early," Aurel said, without looking up, and it was not a complaint. [257]

"I couldn't sleep with it silent." [263]

He took the clock the way she imagined he took all of them, as if it might be injured. [282] Around them, a dozen other water clocks dripped at a dozen slightly different tempos, so that the shop seemed to breathe unevenly, like something recovering. [307] He lifted the float chamber, tilted it toward the grey window, and squinted through the murk of the old water. [327]

"There," he said. [330] He fished with tweezers and came up with something no longer than her fingernail: a brass screw, threaded fine, with a flattened wing for turning by hand. [357] "That is not from a clock," he said. [365] "It jammed the outflow." [369]

Ilse knew it before she touched it. [376] Her mother had been a midwife for forty years, and the old birthing clamps, the ones from before the plastic ones, had a screw exactly like this to set the jaw. [407] She had watched her mother clean them at the kitchen sink a thousand mornings, foggy ones like this, saying nothing, all brisk hands. [430] Her mother had never been a woman who explained. [439]

"It didn't fall in," Ilse said. [445] "The chamber has a lid." [450]

Aurel said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. [460]

She turned the screw over. [465] Three weeks ago her mother, already failing, had asked to be left alone with the clock for an hour. [484] Ilse had thought it was sentiment. [490] Now she read between the lines of that hour the way she had read Aurel through the glass. [508] A woman who had clamped and cut two thousand cords, who had stood at the exact seam where one life became two, had chosen to stop time with the smallest part of the tool that did it. [545] Not a note, not a speech. [551] A screw in the water, for her daughter to find, saying: this is what I was for, and it is finished, and you will have to start it again yourself. [581]

Ilse's hand shook, and then it did not. [589]

"She was hard to know," she said. [596]

"Everyone is," Aurel said, "until you find the thing they left in the works." [610]

He refilled the chamber from a pitcher. [617] The float rose, the needle shivered, and the first drop fell into the basin with a sound so small that Ilse understood, finally, that she had come for it. [646] Not to fix the clock. [651] To see that water, given a chance, still found its level; that a stopped thing could be persuaded; that a woman made of hands could be made human by one deliberate, wordless act. [684] Ordinary miracles, all of them, the kind her mother had delivered before breakfast and never mentioned. [700]

Outside, the fog had not lifted, and Ilse was glad. [710] She had lost one way of seeing and was learning she could stand behind clouded glass and see almost everything that mattered. [732] She would reopen the practice. [737] She would watch her clients come up the lane through the murk before they knocked, read the slow shoulder and the pausing hand, and keep the screw in her pocket to remind her that people say the most in what they leave unsaid. [780]

Behind her, the clock dripped, and dripped, and kept its trembling time. [792]
