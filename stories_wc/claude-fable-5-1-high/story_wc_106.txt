Vela stole only at golden hour, because that was the only time a portrait would let go of its wall. [20] In Caldmoor, painters worked at sunset for a reason: the light did not just color a face, it caught the moment inside it, and the canvas held that moment the way amber holds a wing. [55] Such paintings could not be pried loose by daylight or lamplight; they came free only when the same gold returned to the room. [78] So Vela worked in a window of twenty minutes, and she was quietly savvy about every one of them. [97]

She never spoke to clients. [102] Her commissions arrived as flowers. [107] A florist named Odile kept a stall beneath the green glass vault of Emerald Station, and anyone who wanted Vela's hands left a bouquet there with no card. [135] Vela read them the way other people read letters. [144] Ivy for the district, foxglove for the house, the count of stems for the hour. [159]

The Tuesday bouquet was wrong from the first glance. [168] Ivy, yes, and seven stems of foxglove, which meant Hollowmere Row at seven. [181] But at the center sat a moonflower, its white trumpet shut tight against the afternoon, and beneath the blooms, wedged where the water should have been, a small glass jar that glowed faintly blue. [215]

"Someone paid a great deal for that," Odile murmured, not looking up from her ribbons. [230] "Moonbeams, captured on the solstice, or so the man claimed." [240]

Vela turned the jar in her palm and felt the cold of it seep through her glove. [257] Moonlight had no place in her trade. [264] Moonlight was the opposite of what she did; it showed nothing warm, held nothing still. [279] In her code, a moonflower had never meant anything, because she had never needed it to. [295] She had to reinterpret the whole arrangement around it, and the only reading that made sense was this: take the portrait at sunset, but do not look at it until the moon is up. [329]

A client who knew her code well enough to extend it was either a friend she did not have or a trap she could not see. [355] She went anyway, which was the part of herself she trusted least. [367]

Hollowmere Row was a street of tall shuttered houses, and the seventh had a study facing west. [384] The sunset came fast and low, a spill of gold across the floorboards that she knew would last no longer than a held breath. [408] The portrait hung above a cold hearth: a young woman at a window, unfinished around the edges, the light on her cheek so fresh it looked wet. [435] When the sun found the frame, the canvas loosened with the small sigh they always made, and Vela lifted it free, wrapped it in linen, and did not look. [464] She did not look on the stairs, or in the alley, or on the long walk back through streets going violet. [485]

Emerald Station after the last train was a cathedral of green glass and silence. [499] Moonlight came down through the vault and turned the platforms the color of deep water. [514] Vela sat on a bench, unwrapped the linen, and unscrewed the jar. [526]

The moonbeams rose like breath in winter and settled over the canvas. [538] The gold in the painting did not fade; it moved. [548] Where sunset had held a single moment fixed, the cold light let it run forward, the way a river runs once the ice is lifted. [573] The young woman at the window turned from it. [582] She aged a year in a heartbeat, then five, then twenty, the unfinished edges filling in with a life Vela had never lived: a house, a garden, a florist's stall, a shop of her own with sunlight for sale. [621] Then the woman in the painting looked out at her, and Vela finally recognized the face, because it was the one she had avoided in mirrors for years. [649]

She sat in the green dark and laughed once, softly, the way you laugh when the impossible turns out to be simple. [671] Someone, somewhere, had sat for this portrait in a light she had not yet stood in. [687] Someone had sent it back down the years, wrapped in flowers she alone could read, so that she could press her ear against destiny's door and hear, on the other side, her own footsteps approaching. [722]

She had spent her life stealing other people's illuminated moments and selling them to strangers. [737] No one had ever told her she was allowed to keep one. [749]

When the moonbeams thinned and the portrait stilled into gold again, the woman at the window was smiling, and Vela, who did not believe in any of this, found that she was smiling back. [783]
