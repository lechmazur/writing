The rain had beaded on the skylight for three days, and Wren had stopped waiting for it to clear. [19] The loft had been her great-aunt Ilse's for forty years, and now it was hers, along with a kettle, a wardrobe of black coats, and a wooden box that nobody in the family had ever been allowed to open. [58] Ilse had been a cartomancer, though she never used the word. [69] She drew her own decks in onyx ink and read them for the neighborhood, and the neighborhood paid her in cash right up until it stopped having any. [97]

The crash had come in March. [103] Wren had watched it from the analytics floor of an exchange that no longer existed, tracing the collapse in real time until someone cut the power to her monitor. [132] Ilse had died in April. [137] Wren did not believe the two events were connected, but she had been raised to notice sequences. [154]

The box held the deck and a tailor's measuring tape, soft yellow cloth marked in the same black ink. [173] The cards were not tarot. [178] There were no cups or towers, only places: the pawnshop on Vell Street, the bakery with the green awning, the bus shelter where teenagers sold phone chargers, the cafe that had put a coin logo in its window two summers ago. [219] Seventy-one cards, seventy-one corners of a twelve-block radius, each drawn with the patience of someone who had walked past them ten thousand times. [242]

Wren was not mystical, but she was steadily curious, which her mother had always said was the same disease with a better reputation. [265] She treated the deck the way she had treated undocumented code: as a system someone had built for a reason, and she began to reverse engineer it. [292]

The tape came first. [296] Its marks were not inches. [301] When she unrolled it beside a street map, the marks landed on intersections, and the spaces between them matched walking distances, block for block. [325] Ilse had laid her cards along the tape. [333] Every reading was a route. [338]

On the fourth day of rain, someone knocked. [346] Dario owned the cafe with the coin logo, or had, until the landlord stopped accepting apologies. [362] He stood dripping under the skylight and did not ask who Wren was. [375] He asked if the cards were still here. [383] Then, because grief and bankruptcy loosen the same hinge, he confided everything: the savings converted to tokens, the ovens he never bought, the reading Ilse had given him three years ago that he had chosen to hear as encouragement. [422] She laid out the cafe, then the pawnshop, then the bakery, he said. [435] I thought she meant I'd be rich enough to buy them both. [447]

Wren said nothing, but she felt the system click into place the way a bad function suddenly reveals its intent. [467] She asked him to sit, and she dealt. [475] Not one route this time. [480] She stretched the tape three ways from his cafe, then five, then eight, multiplying the paths until the floor under the skylight was a black-inked web with his door at the center. [512] Every path crossed the pawnshop before it reached anywhere that sold bread. [524] Not because the cards predicted it, but because Ilse had walked this neighborhood for forty years and knew exactly which corners people passed when money was leaving and which they passed when it came back. [559]

The deck was not fortune. [564] It was a map of habits, a chart of how fear and hope moved through twelve blocks, drawn one card at a time by a woman who had been mapping cultural patterns long before anyone paid her to call it prophecy. [605]

Wren told Dario this, gently, and watched him decide whether it was worse or better than magic. [622] Then she traced the one path that did not end at the pawnshop. [635] It ran past the bus shelter, where teenagers had lately started selling coffee from a thermos to the line of people waiting for buses that came less often now. [664] Ilse had drawn that card last; the ink was barely a month old. [677]

Dario left with a direction, which is not the same as a promise, but he seemed to understand the difference now. [698]

Wren gathered the cards and found one she had not counted, stuck to the bottom of the box. [716] Seventy-two. [717] It showed a skylight, rain-beaded, and beneath it a figure kneeling over a web of yellow tape. [734] The figure had Wren's haircut. [739]

She sat with that for a long while, listening to the rain, and decided it was not a prediction either. [759] Ilse had simply known the family, and known which of them would be curious enough to open the box, and patient enough to learn what the tape was for. [788] That was pattern, too. [792]
