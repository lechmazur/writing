The alchemist's market hangs above the city on ropes as thick as a man's waist, and Ione climbs into it at dusk the way she has climbed since she was nine. [31] Below, the streets speak in permits and ledgers; up here, among stalls lashed between chimneys, the alchemists speak in pigment, smoke, and glass. [54] Ione carries meaning between the two, and is paid by whichever world needs the other to understand it. [72] Tonight the city has hired her. [78] Old Teodor the painter died on Sunday, and his stall is to be cut down, his canvases catalogued and sold to furnish offices below. [102] The clerk who walks behind her carries a ledger and will not look over the edge. [118]

In Ione's pocket rides a wedge of prism glass no bigger than her thumb. [132] She took it from the rubble of the harbor lighthouse the morning the wreckers finished, when she was nine and her grandmother, the last keeper, stood beside her saying nothing. [162] No record names that woman. [167] The lens fragment is elusively tangible: cool and definite in her fist, yet when she holds it to the light its edges soften, as though part of it still stands in the tower that no longer exists. [204] Translators need anchors. [207] When two worlds insist on contradictory truths, intuition alone drifts; it must be tied to something that stays. [225] The glass is hers. [229]

Teodor's stall sways under her boots, a platform of salvaged doors slung between two chimney stacks, canvases stacked along the rail. [250] "Harbor views," the clerk says, already writing. [257] "Twelve, various sizes." [260]

They are harbor views, and they are not. [268] Ione knows Teodor's varnish; he mixed it himself from resin and ground shell, and it goes cloudy under lamplight. [287] She takes out the fragment, half from habit, and turns the last of the sun through it onto the nearest canvas. [308] The cloudiness parts. [311] Beneath the painted water, in strokes finer than eyelashes, stand figures: a woman on a gallery with a lamp, rope-walkers hauling the first cables of this market between roofs, salt-rakers bent on the flats the city drained for warehouses. [350] Under each figure, a name. [355] Ione moves the glass and the figures vanish; moves it back and they return, waiting. [370]

Teodor painted for lighthouse glass. [375] He must have known she carried it; he must have known that one day the city would send a translator, and he had chosen what she would be able to read. [406] The gallery woman holds her lamp exactly the way Ione's grandmother did. [418] Below her, in letters the size of grains of sand, is the name no record kept. [434]

Something in the evening stops. [439] The market goes on around her, bellows sighing, a kettle of blue glass singing two stalls over, but Ione is nine on the rubble with the glass warm in her palm and she is thirty-one on a swaying platform with the same glass warm in her palm, and the two moments have no distance between them at all. [497] Everything between childhood and now folds flat, and she understands that she has never been climbing toward anything; she has been standing in this one instant the whole time, learning the words for it. [531]

"Twelve harbor views," the clerk repeats. [537] "Is that correct?" [540]

A translation is a choice about what a thing is allowed to mean in the other world. [557] Ione kneels and begins to unfasten the first canvas from its frame, working the tacks loose with the edge of the lens. [579]

"What are you doing?" [583]

"Translating," she says. [586] "The frames are harbor views; you may have them." [595] "The canvases are a census." [600]

She holds the glass steady over the gallery woman and reads the tiny name aloud, slowly, the way one reads to someone taking dictation. [624] The clerk hesitates, pen lifted, caught between the world that pays him and the woman who speaks both. [642] Then he writes it down. [647] She reads the next, and the next: the rope-walkers, the salt-rakers, a boy who carried water up the ladders before there were ladders. [670] The ledger is an official document; whatever enters it, the city below must keep. [684] Teodor understood that too. [688] He could not carry the names down himself, so he hid them where only her anchor would find them, and trusted the instant to arrive. [713]

When the last canvas is rolled and the last name written, the sun is gone and the market lights its lamps, hundreds of them, strung between the roofs like a harbor turned upside down. [747] Ione closes her hand around the fragment. [754] It is still cool, still not quite all here, still the one thing that has not moved since she was nine. [775] She starts down the ladder, carrying the census, and does not feel that she is leaving. [791]
