The station rode its four moorings above the drowned atoll, and Ines had learned to feel the difference between the sea lifting it and the sea letting it down. [29] Twenty-two years after the fossil fuel ban, the water no longer hurried, but it had not agreed to stop. [48] Her job was to write down how much it had not stopped. [60] She did this with the gauge and the satellite link and the pressure sensor on the seabed, and she did it with her fingers, tapping. [85] Two short, one long, on the rail while the kettle heated. [96] Three quick on the gauge housing when the reading came in. [107] The habit was older than the station. [114] It came from the reef, from the years when she and Teo had dived it with steel tanks and a language of knuckles: tap once to look, twice to follow, three times to come back. [149]

She had been there the summer the reef went white. [159] Not gray, not dead-looking, but white the way bone is white when it has been cleaned, a color so bright it hurt at depth. [183] The fish had still been there that summer, moving through the branching corridors as if the walls were only sleeping. [203] Teo had tapped twice on his tank, follow, and swum into the channel they called the Needle, and she had followed him between the white walls, and they had come out the other side into open water, laughing through their regulators. [244]

Above the desk she kept a page torn from an atlas, the atoll drawn in blue and cream, the Needle marked as a fine dark thread between the reef flats. [274] Teo had torn it out himself, the morning of his last dive, and left it on the kitchen table with a mug set on the corner so it would not blow away. [306] The divers who went after him found the Needle closed, a shoulder of sand and rubble where the thread should have been, and Teo on the far side of it, facing the way he had come. [342]

She had taken the station posting because it was moored over that water, and because she wanted to know why some paths disappear. [365] Not the general answer, which everyone had. [372] The particular one: how a channel that had been open for ten thousand years could shut in the space of one man's breath. [395]

So at night, when the satellite window closed, she assembled things. [406] The station's own record, eleven years of tide. [414] Older survey soundings pulled from archives. [420] Sediment cores. [422] Her own dive logs, the depths of the sill written in Teo's hand and then in hers. [439] She laid them out along the desk in the order of time and tapped her way across them, one knuckle for each reading, so she could hear the shape of the numbers instead of only seeing them. [476]

The tide made a sound she knew, a rhythm that came around and came around and never quite repeated, periodically eternal, the same moon pulling on a slightly different sea. [506] The sill made a different sound. [512] It rose. [514] Slowly, then faster, beginning the year the coral went white, because the living walls of the Needle had been what held the sand back, and the dead ones crumbled and became the sand. [547] By the year Teo dove it, the channel had been shut for at least three seasons. [563]

She sat with that a long time, the station lifting and settling on its lines. [578] It had not happened in the space of his breath. [588] It had happened while they both went on living inland and telling each other the reef was as they remembered it. [609]

Then, because she had never once done it in twelve years, she took the atlas page down from the wall and turned it over. [633] On the back, in the small type of the publisher's margin, was the edition date. [648] The atlas had been printed four years after the bleaching, from surveys made forty years before it. [665] The Needle on the page had never been a path. [675] It was a memory of one, reprinted faithfully, edition after edition, while the sea it described went somewhere else. [694] Teo had tapped twice and followed a map. [702]

And she understood, finally, that the mug on the corner had not been to hold the page down for himself. [722] He had the route in his head; he had left it so she would know where he had gone, and she had spent twelve years reading it as a map when it was a note. [757]

She went out onto the deck. [763] The water was black and the moorings hummed and the station drifted the little it was allowed to drift. [782] She tapped three times on the rail, come back, and then, after a while, once. [797]
