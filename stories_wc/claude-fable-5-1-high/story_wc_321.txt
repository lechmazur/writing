The 6:40 ferry from Harrow Point vanished every night, and Ines Calloway was the only deckhand who kept signing up for it. [22] The others called it bad luck. [28] She called it a route nobody else had learned to read yet. [40] Every evening the boat pushed off with forty commuters, crossed the strait under ordinary dusk, and somewhere past the second buoy the clocks in the wheelhouse began to run sideways. [70] Not backward, not forward: the hands slid along the face like beads on a wire, and the minute they did, the shore lights went out and the ferry slipped into the grotto. [102]

Ines loved the grotto with an ardor the union rep found alarming. [114] It had no business existing, a cavern the size of a cathedral opening out of open water, its ceiling torn away so that starlight fell straight down onto the deck. [144] The walls were pale and half-transparent, and through them she could see the other ferry. [159] Same hull, same forty passengers, same deckhand in the same orange vest, gliding in the opposite direction toward a Harrow Point where the 6:40 had never gone missing at all. [189] Two timelines, one departure. [193]

The passengers on her side never seemed to notice; they dozed, scrolled dead phones, arrived at a dock that looked right and went home to houses that were almost theirs. [223] Almost was the part that kept her up. [231] A woman named Dell had told her once, quietly, that her daughter's name was spelled differently now. [248]

So Ines had a project. [253] In her locker she kept an hourglass the old captain had left behind, its sand long gone, both bulbs empty. [273] He had said it was the only clock that told the truth in the grotto: nothing fell, nothing was measured, the interval simply was. [297] She had taken that as a dare. [304]

Each crossing she carried the hourglass up to the wheelhouse, set it beside the radio, and waited for the hands to go sideways. [327] Then she keyed the mic. [332] The radio in the grotto did not carry voices, only static, but the static had structure. [348] She had spent months logging it and had found that the bursts matched the wake pattern off the stern, which matched the shimmer moving through the grotto walls, which matched, when she finally laid the sheets side by side, the printed timetable of the 6:40 itself. [394] Everything in the grotto was the same shape repeated. [403] If she could send that shape back, tapping it out in the static, she thought the two ferries might recognize each other and fold together. [428] One boat, one dock, one spelling of a daughter's name. [438]

Tonight the pattern came in clean. [444] Ines tapped it back, long and short, watching the empty hourglass because there was nothing else to watch, and on the fourth repetition the other ferry turned. [471] It came through the wall the way a reflection comes through a window when you lean in close, and for a moment she was looking at herself across six feet of starlit water. [504] The other Ines was holding a radio mic too. [513] The other Ines was grinning. [518] Of course she was. [522]

The hulls touched and did not stop; they slid into one another, seat into seat, passenger into passenger, Dell into Dell. [543] The clocks on both wheelhouse walls jerked upright and began, hesitantly, to run forward. [557] But the grotto did not release the ferry until the merge finished, and a merge needs one thing left over to carry the remainder. [581] Ines understood it before the other Ines did, which was fair, since she had read the pattern longer. [599] Two deckhands could not become one; the boat only needed one, and the grotto would keep the other. [617]

She picked up the hourglass, walked to the rail, and stepped off onto the pale shelf of rock where the wall had been. [640] "Tell Dell to check the spelling," she called. [648] The other Ines, now the only Ines aboard, lifted the mic in a salute that was also a question. [667] "I'm fine," Ines said, and meant it, because she always had. [678]

The ferry shrank down the channel, its lights steady, and did not vanish. [691] Ines sat on the rock under the open sky and turned the hourglass over. [705] Nothing fell, because there was nothing to fall, and she found she liked that. [719] A commuter is someone always between two places. [727] She had just made it official. [733] Somewhere above, the stars kept their patterns, and she began, out of habit, to log them. [749]
