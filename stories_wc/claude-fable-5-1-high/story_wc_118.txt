The silo had held grain once, then nothing, then books. [10] Now it held Ilse Marrow, who had been called an odd historian even when there were enough people left to call anyone anything. [33] She lived on the third gallery of the reclaimed tower, where shelves curved with the concrete and the light came down the central shaft the color of old bronze. [62] The quiet up there was not empty. [69] It was the quiet of metal cooling after a long day, of a bell that had already rung. [87]

Nobody dreamed anymore. [90] The last human dream had been dreamt eleven years ago, by a child sheltering in the feed office at the silo's base, and Ilse had written down what the child said on waking because no one else thought to. [129] A door in the sea, the child had said. [138] Then the child grew up and slept like the rest of them, clean and black and unlit, and the world went on turning around its displaced axis, seven degrees off from where the old charts put it, with winters arriving in months that used to be spring. [185] People said the tilt had shaken the dreams loose. [194] Ilse did not argue. [198] She only thought that a world could lose its center in more ways than one. [213]

She had come to believe that walls remembered what their inhabitants forgot. [225] This was her work: to collect the stories walls had heard. [236] She pressed her palm to plaster in abandoned farmhouses and waited. [247] She listened to stairwells. [251] It embarrassed the settlement, which is why they let her keep the silo; a strange woman is easier to house at a distance. [274]

The fireplace in that same feed office had given her the diaries. [286] Someone, before the tilt, had tried to burn them and lost their nerve or their kindling halfway. [303] Ilse worked the ash with a bone spoon, probing gently, the way she might have felt for a splinter under skin. [324] She lifted out a spine, then a second, then a hand-stitched book whose pages were scorched to a brown scallop along the outer edge, leaving the inner half of every line intact. [356] Half-sentences. [357] A diary that could only be read by someone willing to supply the rest. [371]

She was that kind of reader. [377] She had always been sensitively perceptive to the point of discomfort, noticing which chair a visitor avoided, which word a mother stopped short of. [401] Now she sat cross-legged on the gallery floor with the burned book on her knee and let the surviving halves tell her what the missing halves had been. [429]

...we dug beneath the north footing and found... [437] ...older than the silo, older than the farm, wrapped in oilcloth... [448] ...she says the dragons are not dragons but the shape the sky makes when... [462]

Between the last two pages, pressed flat, lay the thing itself. [473] A fragment of tapestry no larger than her two hands, wool gone the color of tea, the weave medieval, unmistakably so; she had handled enough museum scraps in the old life to know the twist of the thread. [511] Two dragons coiled around a single point, tails knotted, jaws open toward a star picked out in thread that had once been gold. [534] Around them, faint, the tiny stitched figures of sleepers, each with a line running from their foreheads to that star. [554]

Ilse understood before she wanted to. [560] The medieval weavers had known Draco, the dragon that curls around the pole; they had known the pole star drifts across centuries, that the axis of heaven itself wanders. [589] They had woven the sleepers tethered to it anyway. [598] Dreams hung from the fixed point of the sky. [607] Move the point, and the lines went slack. [615]

The diary's owner had guessed this, and been frightened enough to try to burn the guess. [631] Ilse was not frightened. [635] She laid her palm flat on the silo wall beside her, concrete cool and grained, and felt what she always felt: a patience like the inside of a bell. [664] This wall had heard grain pour, and men swear, and a child describe a door in the sea. [682] Now it had heard this. [687]

She put the tapestry fragment in her ledger between the child's dream and a description of a farmhouse pantry that smelled of dill. [710] She did not know whether the axis could be pulled back or whether people would ever dream again. [728] She knew only that someone had to keep the tether visible, a scrap of gold thread, so that if the sky ever settled the sleepers would know where to look. [758] Above her, the bronze light went down the shaft and the silo held its quiet, and Ilse, who did not dream, sat in it and listened. [783]
