Fennick woke at dawn's earliest whistle, the sound the wind made through the one slat he had never nailed shut. [20] He left it open on purpose. [26] A man who sleeps beneath the dunes needs something to tell him the sky still exists. [42] The loft had once belonged to a lifeboat station, before the sand came up the walls like slow water and swallowed the ground floor whole. [67] Now it was feral: marram grass rooted in the rafters, a fox denned somewhere under the floorboards, and the only door was a skylight he climbed through on a rope. [97] Along the beams stood his jars. [103] Forty-one glass jars, lids screwed tight, each labeled in pencil: GULLS, FIRST STORM OF OCTOBER, A CHILD LAUGHING AT NOTHING, MY OWN COUGH, THE TIDE GOING OUT WITHOUT ME. [132] He caught sounds the way other men caught fish, holding the jar open to the air until the moment felt full, then capping it fast. [157] Whether anything stayed inside, he could not prove. [165] But when he unscrewed one at night and held it to his ear, he heard enough to write by. [184]

That morning he went down to the tideline to glean. [194] The night's storm had left its usual leavings, rope and kelp and a boot, and among them something small and square that did not belong to the sea. [222] A music box. [225] Rosewood gone grey, hinge rusted, the little brass comb inside clotted with sand. [238] He worked at it with a fishbone and spit until the cylinder turned, and it played eleven notes, positively wistful, of something he had never heard and somehow already missed. [268] Someone had forgotten this. [272] Someone had once needed it very much. [279] He put it in his coat and went to work. [289]

Work was the pier. [293] A crate, a cardboard sign, POEMS ONE PENNY, and a pencil worn to the length of his thumb. [311] Tourists paid him for his strangeness. [317] Locals paid him because he had once written something true about a dead husband and nobody had forgotten it. [336]

The girl came at nine, as she had every Saturday for a month, with a penny and a problem. [355] Her name was Ilse, she was ten, and her grandmother had stopped making sense. [369] "She talks about a boat with a green lamp," Ilse said. [380] "She hums the same thing over and over." [388] "Mum says it's the illness and to nod." [396] "I don't want to nod." [401] "I want to know what she's saying." [408]

Fennick had a daughter once, grown now, somewhere inland. [417] He had spent her childhood speaking in poems when she wanted plain words, and she had spent her adulthood not calling. [438] No single act could repay that. [444] But he had come to believe redemption was not a harvest; it was gleaned, stalk by dropped stalk, from other people's fields. [466] If he could not be understood by his own blood, he could at least stand between someone else's old and young and carry the words across. [492]

"Hum it for me," he said. [498] She did, badly, and his hand went to his coat. [508] Eleven notes. [510] He wound the box and let it answer her, and Ilse's face did what faces do when the world turns out to be stitched together after all. [537]

They went to the grandmother's house together. [544] He brought two empty jars. [549] The old woman sat by a window facing the wrong way to see the sea, and when the music box played she went very still, and then she talked. [578] Not sense, exactly. [581] Fragments: a green lamp, her father's boat, a brother who wound the box for her when the fog came in and she was frightened, a lullaby with words she could no longer find. [614] Fennick held one jar open to her voice and one to the box, and capped them both. [631]

That night, in the loft, with the fox shifting under the boards and the dunes breathing against the walls, he listened to the jars and wrote. [657] He did not invent. [661] He translated. [663] He took the broken pieces of what the old woman meant and sweetened them only as much as a lullaby needs, so a ten-year-old could hold them without cutting her hands. [694] Eleven lines for eleven notes. [699]

At dawn's earliest whistle he climbed down the rope and walked to town, and when Ilse read the poem to her grandmother, the old woman sang the words back, all of them, as if she had only been waiting for someone to say them first. [744] Fennick kept no penny for it. [750] He walked back along the empty beach, an old man in a salt-stiff coat, and the morning was so wide and gold and lonesome it hurt. [776] That evening he opened a fresh jar, held it to the wind, and labeled it: SOMETHING CARRIED ACROSS. [794]
