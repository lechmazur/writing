The Vale Gardens closed for the last time on a Tuesday in October, and Marisol locked the gift shop with a key that had belonged to her grandmother. [28] The bank would take the hedge maze, the ticket booth, the orchard garden hidden at its heart, and whatever else was left. [50] She should have gone home. [55] Instead she walked into the maze with the last coin from the register in her fist, a hand-painted tarot coin her grandmother had sold as a souvenir, the Wheel of Fortune worn to a smear of gold and blue. [94]

Marisol had always heard colors. [99] Her grandmother called it a blessing; the school nurse had called it a concern. [113] The hedges hummed a dull olive as she turned the corners she knew by heart, and the gravel underfoot spoke in small gray syllables. [137]

Then the maze opened, and the orchard was wrong. [146] Apple trees stood in blossom and in fruit at once, petals falling onto ripe windfalls, and the little wooden stage at the center wore three seasons of paint in overlapping coats. [177] The air tasted like applause. [182]

"You came back," said a woman on the stage, and her voice was the deep violet Marisol had heard in the maze every closing night of her childhood without ever finding its source. [215] The woman wore a beaded gown from a century gone and flickered like a film with missing frames. [233]

"Ondine Vale," Marisol said, because the portrait in the gift shop had that same tilt of the chin. [251] "You built this place." [255]

"I built the stage." [259] "The maze was to make them work for it." [268] Ondine smiled, and the smile broke into a dozen smiles from a dozen nights, laid one over another. [286] "I died in the second act of the last show, in 1929." [298] "Never got my curtain call." [303] "Every night after the gates lock, I try to earn one." [314]

She lifted a hand, and the orchard answered. [322] An echo of laughter rolled out from 1929, warm and brass-colored. [333] Another followed from some later summer, thinner, a child's giggle in pale yellow, and Marisol knew it was her own, age six, on her grandmother's lap. [359] The echoes came faster, out of order, and the timeline of the orchard split along them like ice. [377]

"That's how you do it," Marisol whispered. [384] "You've been replaying the past so hard it cracked." [393]

"I have been keeping it," Ondine said, and for a moment she was not a performer but a tired woman standing in a broken garden. [418] "Your grandmother stood where you stand, the night the bank came the first time." [432] "She was ready to burn it all down." [440] "I gave her an echo of a full house, and she heard something in it she could still want." [459] "She stayed forty years." [463] "Now you are the one at the edge." [471]

Marisol opened her fist. [475] The tarot coin lay there, and she remembered her grandmother's paintbrush, the tiny wheel, the words she had murmured while the gold dried: nothing that turns is ever finished. [504]

"Your echoes don't fit together," Marisol said. [511] "That's why no one claps." [516] "You're playing every night at once, and it comes out as noise." [528] "But I can hear the colors of them." [536] "Let me arrange the set." [541]

Ondine went very still, then bowed, and the gesture was a hundred bows collapsed into one. [557]

Marisol closed her eyes and listened. [563] She found the brass laugh of 1929 and set it first, then the yellow of her own small voice, then a rust-red cough from a wartime winter, then the silver hush of her grandmother saying her name across the stage. [603] She braided them, color by color, letting each echo intertwine with the next until the fractured years lay against one another like strands of a single rope. [630]

The blossoms stopped falling. [634] The windfalls stopped rotting. [638] The stage settled into one coat of paint, the one her grandmother had chosen, a green Marisol could taste. [657]

Ondine stepped forward into the light of a moon that finally agreed on its own date and spoke the last line of her last show. [682] Marisol did not know the play. [688] She clapped anyway, alone, and the sound went out through the orchard and returned from every year it had ever touched, a full house of one woman's hands. [716]

Ondine curtsied, and this time there was only one of her, and then there was none. [732]

Marisol stood in the ordinary orchard, in ordinary October, with the coin warm in her palm. [748] The bank could have the buildings. [754] She knew where a stage was, and how to make people work for it, and what the wheel had said. [774]
