After the numbers changed meaning, Wren kept the clock anyway. [10] Nobody asked her to. [14] The council had ruled, in the last year anyone bothered counting years, that seven now meant enough and twelve meant too late, and after that no one trusted a dial that insisted on three. [48]

She climbed the emerald silo every morning regardless. [56] The grain co-op had painted it green the summer before the harvests stopped, and the paint had held better than anything else in town. [80] Inside, a clock mechanism someone had bolted to the ladder platform still ticked, and Wren wound it because she had a heart that was not hers and wanted to know what she was spending. [114]

The surgeon had said ten years, back when ten was a quantity. [126] Now ten meant a kind of weather. [133] Borrowed time, the donor's mother had called it at the hospital, and Wren had spent every day since trying to feel its texture, the way you might run a thumb along cloth in the dark to learn whether it was silk or sackcloth. [176] The ticking helped. [179]

The flood in spring helped more. [185] Water undermined the silo's east footing and, when it drained, left a rusted steel box canted in the mud like a tooth working loose. [209] A plate on the lid read TO BE OPENED IN, followed by a number that no longer said anything. [228] The seal had failed. [232]

Inside, the co-op's time capsule had gone to pulp: photographs fused into a single gray brick, a cassette tape unspooled like intestine, seed packets sprouting and dying in the same dark. [263] Only one thing had kept its shape. [270] A curved shard of glazed ceramic, blue outside, chalk-white in, with a fine gray residue clinging to the inner face. [290] Wren knew a cremation urn when she saw one, and she knew ash. [303]

Someone had put a person in the capsule. [311] Not all of them, just a piece, sealed toward a future that was supposed to arrive on schedule. [329] The schedule had been cancelled. [334] The fragment was, quite suddenly, timeless, a message with its date torn off, and Wren could not stand the thought of holding it alone. [358]

There was one way left to speak to the whole town at once. [371] Since prices had become nonsense, garage sales ran on descriptions, and the Saturday flyer stapled to the feed store door was the only thing everyone still read. [398] Wren listed a chipped blue vase, "recovered from the green silo, holds a little of someone, will trade for the rest of the story." [422]

She wound the clock and waited, and waiting had a texture, she discovered: coarse, then finer, then almost smooth. [441]

The next flyer answered her. [446] "Box of a man's winter shirts, never worn after the co-op closed, free to whoever found the blue thing. [465] He wanted to be opened later. [471] Ask for Dell." [474]

Wren answered with a listing for a wristwatch, "stopped, but not broken, would like to meet the shirts." [492]

Dell was the widow of the man who had installed the silo clock. [505] She climbed the ladder slowly, one hand pressed to her hip, and sat on the platform while the mechanism ticked between them. [527]

"He said the future should have to shake his hand," Dell said. [539] "So I broke off a piece of the urn and put it in with the photographs. [555] The rest of him is in my kitchen. [563] I never expected to rediscover the piece I gave away. [573] I thought I would be gone before anyone opened it." [583]

"The flood opened it early," Wren said. [590] "Or late. [592] I can't tell anymore." [596]

Dell took the shard and turned it so the light from the hatch fell on the ash. [613] "That's the trouble with waiting for a date. [621] You miss what the waiting felt like." [628]

Wren put her hand flat against her sternum, where the donor's heart kept a count nobody could read. [646] She had been afraid of the number the surgeon gave her, and then the number had dissolved, and she had expected relief. [668] Instead she had felt the years go featureless, like a road with the mile markers pulled up. [685] This was different. [688] The shard, the shirts, the flyer on the feed store door: each one was a grain she could feel pass under her thumb. [711]

"Keep it," Dell said, and pressed the fragment back into her palm. [723] "Put it somewhere. [726] Write it down in next week's flyer so someone has to come looking." [739]

That Saturday, Wren listed a small blue shard, "buried again at the foot of the green silo, no date, find it when you find it." [764] Then she climbed the ladder and wound the clock, not to count what was left, but to hear it go. [784]
