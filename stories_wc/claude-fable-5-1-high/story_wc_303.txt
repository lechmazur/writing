The Halden family paid Ines Marroquin to prove that things belonged to them. [13] She was a provenance researcher, which meant she followed objects backward through bills of sale and wills and wars until she could say, with a signature, that no one else had a claim. [46] On midsummer night they brought her to the velvet observatory, an old dome the Haldens had lined in dark cloth so that sound died there and light did not leak. [76] The violin waited on a pedestal under the open slit of the roof. [89] It was transparent, carved from ice crystal, and it glowed faintly from within, the way a promise does when it is new. [111] It did not melt. [115]

"The luthier, Abel Orsk, swore it would last until midsummer dawn," said Theodora Halden, who owned shipping lines and three museums and the word "empire" whenever journalists wrote about her. [145] "Then it stops glowing and becomes ordinary ice, and ordinary ice can be frozen forever." [160] "I need the chain of title clean before that." [169] She handed Ines a folder and a cup of coffee, black, and left her alone with the stars. [187]

The documents were perforated. [191] Not torn, not burned: punched through in neat rows, so that every deed of gift and every receipt had a lace of holes where the names of the recipients should have been. [223] Orsk had done it himself, she was sure. [231] A man does not build a violin out of ice unless he wants it to belong to no one. [250]

Ines drank the coffee because that was how she saw. [260] Her grandmother had read the future in the stains left in the bottom of a cup, and Ines had inherited the habit the way other people inherit bad knees. [289] She tipped the cup and looked. [295] The stain showed a vault, a steel door, the violin dull and dead behind glass with a Halden label beneath it. [316] But the stain, too, had holes in it, dry pale islands where the coffee had refused to settle. [334] She had never seen a future with gaps in it before. [345]

Then the mulberry tree spoke to her. [352] It grew in the courtyard just outside the dome, and its branches had pushed through the roof slit years ago, so that its leaves hung above the violin like a second, softer ceiling. [385] Ines had never asked to hear plants. [392] It had begun in childhood, after a fever, and it was less a voice than a pressure behind the eyes, green and slow and impossible to argue with. [420] The mulberry remembered Orsk. [424] He had planted it as a cutting from his mother's tree, and he had carved the violin from the ice of the well beneath its roots, and he had promised the tree that at midsummer dawn the violin would be played once and then allowed to melt back into the soil that made it. [478] The glow was the promise. [483] When it faded, the promise would be broken, and the tree would stop fruiting, and the song frozen in the crystal would never propagate into anything living. [510] The tree did not say please. [516] Trees do not. [519]

Ines sat with the folder in her lap and felt the ache she always felt at midsummer, a dark ripe bruise of a feeling, the color of the berries staining the courtyard stones. [552] On one side was an empire that had paid her fee and would pay the next one. [569] On the other was a dead man's dream, punched full of holes so no one could own it, and a tree that had waited twenty years to be kept a promise. [600] She understood the perforations now. [605] Orsk had not erased the future; he had left doors in it. [617] The stain in her cup had holes because destiny had holes, and a person could step through one if she was willing to lose her fee and her name. [646]

The sky in the slit was turning the gray of old paper. [658] The glow in the violin had begun to thin. [667] Ines lifted it. [670] It was so cold it burned, and so light it seemed to be made of breath. [686] She had played badly as a child and not at all since, but the bow was there on the velvet, and she drew it across the strings. [713] The note was not beautiful. [718] It did not need to be. [724] The velvet drank it, the tree took it, and the violin began, gratefully, to weep. [739] She carried it out to the courtyard and set it in the roots as the sun came up, and she watched it go. [762]

Before she left, she cut a green switch from the mulberry and wrapped it in the perforated deeds to keep it damp. [784] Provenance, she wrote on the last page, ends here. [793]
