Marisol played the sky-bridge because it was the only place in Tollhaven where nobody told her to stop. [19] Two hundred feet above the pistons, her violin was just another noise in a city of noises, and the wind took most of it before it reached anyone. [47] Below her, wedged between the foundries like something the engineers had forgotten to pave, lay the orchard. [64] The trees still fruited, and the fruit still glowed at dusk, though the glow had gone the color of old brass. [85] Nobody entered. [87] The statues saw to that. [92] There were nine of them, marble keepers left over from whatever the orchard had been before the city grew teeth, and they walked the rows with the patience of things that had never needed to hurry. [128] Marisol had watched a scavenger climb the wall once. [137] A statue turned its blind face toward him before his boots touched grass, and he was carried out, unharmed and weeping, and never came back. [162] They did not see, she decided. [168] They listened. [170] Not for footsteps; for hearts. [175]

She tested it on a spring evening when the smog thinned enough to show the fruit. [191] She played a slow piece, sixty beats to the minute, and the nearest keeper stopped mid-stride and tilted its head toward the bridge. [214] She quickened the tempo to a runner's pulse, and it took a step toward the wall. [230] She slowed again, and it settled. [236] The violin was too bright, though. [242] Whatever the keepers heard in a living chest was softer than steel strings, and she needed to speak their language, not shout at them in her own. [269]

The answer was clinging to the underside of the bridge rail: a locust shell, split down the back, translucent and whole, left by something that had climbed out of itself and flown. [301] There had not been locusts in Tollhaven for thirty years. [311] She set it over the violin's bridge like a mute, and when she drew the bow the sound came out resonantly soft, a hum with a husk around it, less a note than a pressure in the ribs. [349] It sounded like a heartbeat. [354] It sounded like hers. [358]

She went down the maintenance ladder at midnight with the violin strapped to her back and a trowel in her belt. [379] At the wall she stopped, tucked the shell into place, and began to play the rhythm of a body at rest, the way the statues must have heard the orchard when it was full of gardeners and no one needed guarding. [420] Then she climbed over. [424] The nearest keeper turned. [428] Its face had no eyes, only a smooth worn place where eyes might have been, and moss had tarnished the marble to the green of coins left in fountains. [457] It listened. [459] She kept playing, her own pulse hammering against the calm of the music, praying the shell would smother the difference. [479] The keeper stood in the gap between knowing and understanding: it knew this rhythm, knew it in whatever passed for memory in stone, and had not yet worked out that it did not belong to a gardener. [516] That gap was all the time she had. [524]

She chose a sapling near the wall to uproot, no taller than her shoulder, its two small fruits dimmed to a dull amber. [547] She knelt, propped the violin against her collarbone, and kept the bow moving in slow strokes with her left hand while her right worked the trowel into the roots. [576] Open strings, no fingering, just the drone and the beat. [586] The roots came up slowly, and every one that tore made a sound like a held breath let go, and she felt the whole orchard shiver. [612] Two more keepers had drifted closer. [618] Still they only listened. [622] When the sapling finally came free, earth and all, she stood with it cradled against the violin, roots dripping on her boots, and she understood at last what the shell had been trying to tell her. [658] The keepers were not guarding the trees from the city. [668] They were guarding the last thing that had a pulse, and they had been waiting three decades for a pulse to come and claim it. [693] The nearest one lowered its head, slow as sediment, and stepped aside. [705]

She planted the sapling in a cracked flower box on the sky-bridge, where the morning shift would have to walk past something alive. [728] Then she took the locust shell off the bridge of her violin and set it in the soil at the sapling's foot, a small brittle proof that things could climb out of what held them. [763] At dawn the two fruits glowed, faintly, the color of old brass. [775] A riveter stopped to look. [780] Marisol lifted her bow, unmuted now, and played loud enough for the wind to lose. [795]
