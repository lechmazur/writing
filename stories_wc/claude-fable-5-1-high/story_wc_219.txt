Three weeks after first contact, Ilse Varga still had no laboratory, so she built a pilgrimage instead. [17] The university had let her go in the autumn for a paper arguing that homecoming was not a feeling but a reaction: geosmin from wet soil, terpenes from warm resin, a few molecules meeting old receptors and firing the word home. [58] Her department had called it reductive. [64] She had called it a hypothesis, which was the more rebellious word. [76]

Then the visitors arrived, luminous and drifting, and among them was one who doubted the entire premise of home. [95] The translators rendered its name as Vess. [102] Its people had traveled so long that no one aboard remembered a world; they had been born in transit and expected to die there. [126]

"Your species keeps saying homecoming," Vess told her at the first open session, its skin giving off a pale, steady light. [147] "I think it is a story you tell to make arrival bearable." [159]

Ilse, who had been fired for saying nearly the same thing, felt something close to kinship. [175] "Then let me test it on you," she said. [184]

She chose the old sculpture park on the north hill, closed for a decade, where bronze figures stood waist-deep in nettles along a path nobody maintained. [210] At the top stood the water tower, brick gone dark with a week of April rain. [226] It was an artificial pilgrimage, she admitted, designed on paper: wet earth for geosmin, cut grass for the green aldehydes, a climb long enough to raise the heart rate, a destination tall enough to be seen from below. [264] Every real pilgrimage was once designed by someone, she reasoned; the design simply got forgotten. [279]

She brought one object she could not justify scientifically: a thumb of fly amber from her grandmother's windowsill, the only thing left from a house that had since been demolished. [309]

They walked up in the late afternoon, rain finished, the light coming low and coppery through wet leaves. [327] Vess moved without hurry, and Ilse noticed that wherever it stepped, it left a faint dusty print, a fine shed of whatever made it glow, pale against the dark soil. [357]

"You are watching my feet instead of my face," Vess said. [368]

"I am watching for data." [373]

"There will be none." [377]

Past the bronze runner, past the woman holding a broken bowl, the path narrowed and the nettles closed in, and Ilse warmed the amber in her fist without deciding to. [407] Warm resin gives off a trace of pine. [415] Her grandmother's hallway had smelled of exactly that. [423] She recorded her own pulse rising and told herself it was the hill. [436]

At the tower they sat on the wet steps and Ilse held out the amber. [451] Vess turned it toward the light. [457] Inside, the fly hung mid-flight, wings tilted, forty million years from wherever it had been going. [473]

"It never arrived," Vess said. [478]

"No." [479]

"And you keep it because it did not arrive, or because you did not?" [493]

Ilse did not answer, because the question was better than her paper. [505]

Then she saw the lights below. [511] They were coming up the sculpture path, one by one and then in twos, the other visitors, pale and unhurried, stepping exactly where Vess had stepped. [537] She had told no one the route. [544] They were following the dusty footprints, the way a people who had never had ground might follow the only ground-marks their kind had ever left. [569]

They converged on the tower steps in silence, dozens of them, and the light they gave off gathered until the wet brick looked like bronze. [594] Nobody spoke. [596] Vess sat very still, and its skin, which had been pale and steady, brightened by a degree Ilse could measure with her eyes alone. [620]

"You designed a place," Vess said at last, quietly. [629]

"I did." [631]

"But they did not come to the place." [639]

"No," Ilse said. [642] "They came to you." [646]

She thought of the chemistry then, honestly, the way she had wanted to think about it all along: not geosmin, not pine, but whatever molecule a body releases when it is followed, when its track is found and kept. [685] Homecoming was not a place with a smell. [693] It was the resin closing around the fly: the moment the going stopped because something else had arrived to hold it. [714]

Vess offered the amber back. [719]

"Keep it," Ilse said. [723]

"I remain a skeptic," Vess said. [729]

"Good," said Ilse. [732] "So do I." [735]

Its hand closed around the amber, and the light of it passed faintly through the resin, so that for a moment the fly seemed lit from inside, still going, finally held. [766]
