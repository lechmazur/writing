Tobin Marrow had spent thirty years learning what locks wanted, and he had come to believe that most of them wanted to be understood rather than opened. [27] The timer in the marble cavern was not a lock, but it had the same stubborn silence. [44]

The hail had come in the third week of September and stripped the orchards in an hour. [61] What followed was not autumn so much as the lull inside a wounded season, a stretch of grey weeks when nothing ripened and nothing quite died. [87] Without fruit to bring in, the town remembered the other harvest, the one it gathered every year from the old quarry cavern, and remembered that the mechanism which made it possible had stopped in spring. [122] They asked Tobin because he was the only one in the valley who owned tweezers. [137]

The cavern had been cut two centuries earlier by quarrymen who left the walls polished where the marble was too flawed to sell. [160] High in the ceiling, a brass shutter covered a shaft to the sky, and beside it hung the timer: a box of gears the size of a bread loaf, its escapement snapped, its spring unwound, half its teeth missing. [199] When it worked, the shutter opened for a few minutes each afternoon, and a blade of sun fell onto the marble and broke into reflections, and anyone who spoke then heard their words return from the walls, not once but in layers, folded and refolded until a single name lasted the length of a breath held too long. [257] The town called it the harvest of echoes. [265] Every October the bereaved came and spoke the names of their dead into the light, and carried the returning sound home like something picked. [289]

Tobin had never attended. [293] He distrusted wonder, not because he thought it false but because he thought it perishable, and he had watched too many people spend theirs all at once. [320] What he wanted, he told himself on the ladder, was to understand the thing well enough to put it away somewhere dry, in the cellar of reason, where it would keep. [351]

The trouble was that the timer could not be repaired by looking at it. [365] Too many parts were gone, and no drawing survived. [374] To rebuild the gearing he had to reverse-engineer the interval it once kept, and the only record of that interval was written on the walls themselves, in the paths the reflections took. [406] So he propped the shutter open with a broom handle and came back every day at a different hour, with chalk. [427]

At first he stared straight at the bright patches and learned nothing; they slid across the marble like spilled water and would not hold still to be measured. [455] It was only when he gave up and sat against the far wall, eyes on his cold hands, that he noticed the pattern at the edge of his sight. [484] Peripheral vision does not care about brightness; it cares about movement, and sideways the reflections revealed themselves as a slow procession, each one arriving at a worn dark groove in the marble at a precise moment and then departing. [523] He learned to look at nothing and see everything. [532]

Over five weeks he chalked the angles, morning and noon and the thin light of four o'clock, and found the grooves were not accidents: the quarrymen had cut them where the reflections converged, so that the walls sang only when the light struck all of them together. [579] The timer had never measured hours. [585] It measured the sun's return to that convergence, a window that drifted eleven minutes later each week as the year leaned toward winter. [608] From that drift he worked backward to the gear ratios, cut new teeth from a brass door plate, and wound the spring. [630]

On the last day of October the town came up the quarry road in the cold. [646] Tobin stood at the back and did not look at the shutter. [658] He watched the wall beside it, and at the edge of his vision the brass moved, and the light came down and shattered into its old procession, and a woman near the front said her son's name. [695] It came back to her from the marble in layers, and Tobin knew exactly why: the grooves, the angles, the warmed stone shifting its pitch. [720]

He had expected the knowing to make it smaller. [729] Instead it felt like a key turning in a lock he had cut himself, the pins settling one by one, and the wonder did not leave but went down, quietly, into the cool dark room he had built for it, where he could visit it whenever the season wounded him again.
