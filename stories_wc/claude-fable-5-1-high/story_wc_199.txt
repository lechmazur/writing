The Ashen Arbor hung above the valley of Kest on a raft of grey cinder, and Ilse Marrow had sorted its seeds for thirty-one years. [25] She liked the work because it was orderly: a seed was either pale or dark, ridged or smooth, heavy enough to sink in the tin dish or light enough to float. [56] Every tray had a place, and every place had a label written in her small square hand. [73] The leaves were another matter. [78]

In Kest they told it this way: when the arbor sheds, the valley reads. [92] Grey leaves drifted down each autumn and settled across the rooftops, and long ago the old readers had walked among them, tracing letters no one else could see. [120] The last reader had died before Ilse was born, and the valley had shrugged and gone on with its harvests. [140] But Ilse lived up here, where the leaves began, and it bothered her that anything could fall from a place she kept so neatly without meaning something. [167]

The other saying of Kest was older and less comfortable. [177] Whatever passes through the ash emerges transformed, always. [185] She had proved it on a hundred small occasions: a chestnut dropped into the cinder-bed came up as a bulb of some plant no one could name; a copper pin came up as a green twist of wire that hummed. [225] So it stood to reason that the leaves, which grew out of that same ash, had been something else once too. [246]

The clue came on a leaf that landed in her sorting tray. [258] She prodded it with the blunt end of her tweezers, meaning only to flick it away, and it turned over and winked at her. [282] Caught in the grey vein was a speck of dust no bigger than a grain of pollen, milky and blue-lit, shifting as she tilted the tray. [308] Moonstone. [309] She knew it because every child in Kest knew the tale of the shrine cabochon, a moonstone the size of a thumb that had sat in the valley chapel until a bad winter, when it was ground to powder and sold as medicine. [352] Nobody had thought about it in sixty years. [360] And here was a crumb of it, carried up somehow on the wind and through the cinder, come out the other side clinging to a leaf. [386]

Ilse sat back and felt something green unfurl in her chest. [397] The leaves were not letters. [402] They were carriers, and what they carried was everything the valley had let go of. [417]

She began that evening, in her orderly way. [425] For each leaf she examined, she found what it held: a fleck of red clay from the kiln that had burned down, a hair of the wool the Tesk sisters used to spin, a grain of the salt they mined before the seam closed. [469] And for each forgotten thing she built a monument, no taller than her knuckle, out of the seeds she knew best. [490] A cairn of dark ridged seeds for the kiln. [499] A spiral of pale floaters for the wool. [507] For the moonstone, a ring of the heaviest seeds she had, with the leaf laid on top so the speck could catch the light. [531] She set them in rows along the arbor floor, labeled, and the moss that grew between the roots seemed pleased to have company. [554]

Kest folklore kept one more piece, and Ilse had always thought it the least practical. [569] Each dusk there came a hush, a held breath between the wind dying and the stars coming, when what had been and what would be stood in the same room. [599] The old readers had done their reading then. [607]

On the ninth evening, with forty small monuments in their rows, Ilse waited for it. [622] The wind dropped. [625] The leaves that had been falling stopped in the air, just for a moment, like seeds deciding which tray they belonged in. [647] And in that hush the monuments answered. [654] The moonstone speck lit the leaf beneath it, the leaf lit the cairn beside it, and down the row the tiny shapes glowed grey-green and threw their shadows onto the ash in a pattern she could finally read, because she had built its alphabet herself. [699]

It was not a prophecy of flood or plenty. [708] It said, more or less: we are still here, we only changed shape, keep sorting. [723]

Ilse laughed out loud, alone on her raft of cinder, and the moss under her boots went soft and bright as if it had laughed too. [749] Then the stars came, the wind returned, and the leaves went on falling toward Kest, each one carrying a small thing the valley had misplaced, each one changed by the crossing, each one now, at last, expected. [786]
