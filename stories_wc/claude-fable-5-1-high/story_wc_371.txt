Ilse kept a ledger of things that left slowly. [9] The bench outside the library, gone one bolt at a time. [20] The night bus, its route shortened every spring until it no longer reached anyone. [34] The bakery on Kessel Street, whose windows went from bread to butcher paper to a sign promising luxury apartments that never came. [56] Nobody else wrote these things down, so nobody else remembered them. [67] That was how it worked: a city forgot in increments too small to grieve. [81]

This year she had noticed the birds leave early. [90] The starlings that usually thickened the October sky over the old palace were gone by the first week of the month, and the sky had simply closed over the gap. [120] Her neighbors said the weather was fine. [127] Ilse wrote it down and felt the familiar, useless ache of being the only witness. [142]

In her kitchen cupboard, in a tin, was the last loaf from Kessel Street. [156] Hard now as a paving stone. [162] She had bought it on the morning she stamped the rezoning file at the housing office, a file she had read and understood and passed along because that was the job, because one clerk's hesitation had never saved anything. [201] The baker, Amos, had wrapped the loaf in brown paper and tucked a twist of raw grain in with it, saying the rye was the last of a seed his grandmother had smuggled from somewhere, and that someone ought to have it. [243] She had said, "See you Thursday." [249] There had been no Thursday. [254]

She could not give the loaf back. [261] But in the tin's corner the paper twist was still there, a spoonful of grain, and she had thought of what she could do with it. [287]

After the birds flew south she began to plant. [296] Nights, with a trowel in her coat, she pressed rye into the cracks of Kessel Street, into the dead planter outside the shuttered bakery, into the mortar of the palace wall where the fence had rusted through. [333] The palace had been closed for restoration for eleven years. [343] The restoration had become the closing; the closing had become the way things were. [357] Inside, the courtyard had gone to bramble and ivy, and Ilse, slipping through the gap, planted a line of rye across it toward the far arcade, a green sentence she did not expect anyone to read. [393]

She was wrong about that. [398]

She came back in the last dark of November to find the rye standing, thin and stubborn, and the ivy at the end of her line pulled back like a curtain. [429] Behind it, in the ruined arcade, was a room made of salvaged doors and a stovepipe, and a kettle breathing steam, and a man watching the rye. [456]

"Nobody grows this," Amos said. [461] "Nobody has it." [464]

He had built the tea house himself, he told her, when he had nowhere left that was his. [482] The walls inside were papered with rules in his handwriting: Let them sit before they speak. [498] Pour the second cup without asking. [504] Do not ask why they came. [510] He had not been born gentle, he said; he had been born angry and had reasoned his way into kindness one rule at a time, and the rules held even when he did not. [544] The ivy was his and the neighbors' doing: three of them, quietly, planting to hide a place where anyone who found their way could be given something warm. [572] She had uncloaked it by planting the one thing he could not ignore. [585]

Ilse set the tin on his table and told him about the stamp. [598] She did not shape it kindly. [604] Amos followed his rules. [608] He let her finish, poured the second cup, and broke the fossil loaf over the tea to soften it. [627]

"I don't know if that's mine to forgive," he said. [637] "A lot of hands closed that shop." [644] "Yours was one." [647] He chewed a piece of his grandmother's rye. [655] "But you kept it." [659] "And you planted it." [663] "That's something." [665] "I'll settle for something." [669]

It was not absolution, or it was the only kind on offer: thin, provisional, held up by a rule he had written to keep himself from cruelty. [696] She would have to keep earning it, cup by cup. [706]

Outside, the empty sky sat over the courtyard where the birds had been. [719] The rye moved. [722] Ilse drank the ordinary tea in the hidden room and felt, for the first time in years, that she was not the only one keeping the ledger, and that keeping it might itself be a kind of prayer. [760] Somewhere the sacred had been folded into bread and dirt and a rule about second cups, and she had nearly missed it, the way she had been taught to miss everything that went quietly. [794]
