The ghosts had been visible for three years when the first one came to Mara's oasis. [16] She had expected none. [20] The dead went where they were remembered, and nobody remembered a woman who had walked into the scorched flats a decade ago and never walked back. [46]

The oasis had been abandoned when she found it: a spring, forty date palms, and eleven hives still humming under a roof of warped tin. [71] Whoever had kept them was gone. [77] She learned the bees the way she learned everything, by watching what the world left behind. [93] Scrapings of wax on the shed table told her when frames had last been pulled. [108] Two worn hollows on the bench told her the keeper had not always been alone. [123] On the highest shelf, wrapped in a veil and untouched by the dust that claimed everything else, sat a stained glass box no larger than a loaf of bread. [152] Its lid was soldered shut. [157] Amber and cobalt panes held the shadow of folded paper inside, and in ten years she had never opened it, though her hands had wanted to. [183]

The ghost came at dusk, when the bees were settling. [193] Mara stood in the doorway with a smoker cooling in her hand and stared, astonished, though astonishment did not move her face. [215] He was an old man, thin as a fence post, and he did not look at her. [232] He walked to the third hive and tapped its lid twice with two knuckles, exactly the way she had always found it tapped smooth. [256]

The ghosts did not speak. [261] That was the first thing the world had learned about them, and the thing it least forgave. [278] People had crowded into the cities to shout questions at their dead, and the dead had only looked back. [297] Mara had left the cities long before that. [305] Silence had never been a wall to her; it was a language with its own grammar of pauses and placements. [325] So she set down the smoker and let him show her. [336]

He moved from hive to hive, laying his palm flat on each lid, and at the seventh he paused for a long time. [359] Then he went into the shed and raised his gaze to the highest shelf. [373] Mara followed. [375] She lifted the box down, and he did not reach for it. [387] He did not gesture at the soldered lid. [395] He looked instead at the bench with its two hollows, then at the seventh hive through the open door, then at her, and his hands stayed at his sides. [424]

She read it the way she read wax and weather. [434] Keepers told their bees everything; that was the old covenant, older than the burning. [448] Births, deaths, departures, all of it whispered into the hives so the colony would not take offense and leave. [467] But there had been someone on that bench, and something between them that he had promised never to say aloud. [487] Two vows, knotted against each other. [493] He had honored both the only way he could: he had written the words down, sealed them in colored glass where light could touch them but no ear ever would, and set them above the hives, a confession made in the bees' hearing without a sound. [539] Someone was buried beneath the seventh hive; Mara had noticed the stones her first week and let them be. [558]

Her hands had wanted to open the box for ten years. [569] Now she understood that opening it would not free anything. [579] It would only make the words light, and they were not meant to be light. [594] Some words are kept precisely so they can keep their weight. [605] She had her own, carried out here from a city she never named, and she had never once been tempted to set them down. [629]

She wrapped the box in its veil and returned it to the shelf. [642] Then she went out to the hives and did what he had never been able to do for himself. [661] She did not use her voice. [667] She tapped each lid twice with two knuckles, the keeper's knock, and at the third hive she held her palm against the wood until the hum inside changed pitch, the way it does when a colony has heard something. [706] The keeper is dead, her hand said. [713]

When she looked up, the old man was watching her from the doorway of the shed, and for the first time his gaze rested on her face. [740] He did not nod. [744] He did not need to; the light through the shed window had gone amber and cobalt behind him, and that was enough. [766] By full dark he was gone, and the hives went on humming, having been told exactly as much as they needed to know. [789]
