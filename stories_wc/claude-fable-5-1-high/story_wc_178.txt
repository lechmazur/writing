The palace at Sippar had been rebuilt three times, and each time the rebuilders had guessed. [16] The last guess belonged to the fallen ministry, which had wanted a Babylonian palace with glass corridors and a rooftop observatory, so that the state could claim both antiquity and the sky. [48] Now the ministry was gone, the new council had not yet chosen a flag, and Nadira Halloun kept the observatory running on what she could scavenge. [74]

She had always been frugal, even when funded. [82] She wrote star positions on the backs of old requisition forms, trimmed candle wicks to make them last, and refused to switch on the dome lights when the moon would do. [113] Frugality, she believed, was simply accuracy applied to living. [122]

In the interregnum the palace emptied of guards and filled with dust, and she found the tile on the third evening, prying up a loose square in the throne room floor to level a wobbling tripod. [158] It was glazed the blue of late dusk, and its underside carried a grid of sixteen numbers cut into the clay. [179] Every row summed to thirty-four. [184] So did every column, both diagonals, and the four corners. [194] A magic square, then, and not an ancient one; the numerals were modern, and the hand that had cut them had used a machinist's scriber. [219]

Nadira knew the hand. [223] Tomas Ibn Rashid had built half the instruments on the roof before the ministry dismissed him for refusing to report a comet as an omen of national renewal. [251] He had left without a farewell, which she had told herself was efficient. [264]

She carried the tile up to the dome and did not light a candle. [278] The sky over the glass corridors was going from copper to violet, and Venus stood low and steady in the west. [299] Tomas had never trusted words; he had trusted drawings. [308] So she treated the square as a schematic. [316] On the back of a requisition form she drew the grid, then mapped each cell to a quadrant of the sky as their old observing log divided it, sixteen fields, the same sixteen he had wired into the dome's tracking motor. [357] The numbers were not values; they were an order of visitation. [368] One, then two, then three, a path across the ceiling of the world. [381]

She traced it with a pencil stub and watched a figure uncloak itself from the grid: the line went to the field where the dismissed comet had appeared, then to the field where she had first shown him the double star she loved, then to the empty patch of sky where, nine years ago, they had waited all night for a predicted nova that never came. [447] Every cell was a night they had shared. [455] The sums were the cipher's lock; the sequence was its confession. [466] He had encrypted his devotion into the one language she would bother to read, and buried it under a throne no one would sit on again. [492]

She laughed, once, quietly, at the irony of it. [501] She had spent her life insisting that only the measurable counted, that sentiment was noise, and now the most rigorous measurement in the building was a love letter. [529] The data had proved her wrong by agreeing with her methods. [540]

Below, in the city, no one governed. [547] The old decrees were void and the new ones unwritten, and in that gap the palace belonged to whoever kept its clocks. [569] Nadira understood, then, what Tomas had really been doing. [578] He had known regimes would come and go through these halls, and he had wanted something to hold across the changes: a covenant not with any council but with the unknown itself, the nova that had not arrived, the sky that owed them nothing. [622] The tile was his signature on that contract. [630] It needed hers. [633]

She did not waste paper on a reply. [641] Instead she set the tile beside the eyepiece, opened the observing log to a fresh page, and wrote the date with no regime's name above it, only the year. [670] Then she turned the dome to the sixteenth field, the empty one, and began to watch it as if it were owed a witness. [694]

The dusk finished falling. [698] Somewhere below, a generator she had refused to run stayed silent, and the palace's reimagined ziggurat cast a shadow that no king had ever seen. [723] The moon rose, and it was enough to work by. [733] Whatever came next would find the log kept, the clocks wound, the grid drawn, and one frugal astronomer who had decided, empirically, that devotion was the cheapest instrument she owned and the only one that had never needed recalibrating. [772]
