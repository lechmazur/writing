The dead called at twilight, and Marit Voss was always in the atrium to answer. [15] The hospice had been a glasshouse once, and mist still rose from the old irrigation channels under the floor, climbing the iron ribs until the dome vanished into gray. [44] Her title was contact tracer for hearts, which made visitors smile until she explained it. [59] When a resident died, something of them lingered in whatever device had lived closest to their body, and for one hour after sunset it pulsed toward the people they had loved. [90] Marit's job was to follow the pulse, find those people, and tell them gently, before a form letter or a stranger's careless mouth could do it first. [117] Some voices only got one chance to be heard, and she had decided long ago that hers would be the one guarding them. [140]

Tonight the device was a hearing aid. [147] It was beige, the size of a walnut, and forty years old at least; Teodor Halvard had refused every replacement the nurses offered. [170] Marit held it in her palm and felt the faint tick against her skin, slow as a tired heart. [189] His file listed no next of kin. [196] She could have logged that and gone home. [204] But the pulse was there, and a pulse meant someone, and Marit had never in her life been able to leave a question sitting unopened on a table. [232]

She walked. [234] The aid ticked faster toward the east wing, then faltered when she turned, so she turned back. [251] Mist swallowed her knees. [255] The benches rose out of it like moored boats, and somewhere above, the last of the sun bled orange through the fogged panes and went out. [281] The pulse quickened until it was nearly a hum. [290]

There was a woman on the farthest bench. [298] She sat very straight, coat still buttoned, a small suitcase at her feet, and she was looking at a photograph the way people look at a door they have just heard lock. [330] Marit knew before she reached her. [336]

"You came for Teodor," she said. [342]

The woman flinched. [345] "I'm his daughter. [348] Liv. [349] The train was late. [353] I got here and the nurse said this afternoon, and then she got called away, and I have been sitting here because I do not know what a person does next." [384]

Marit sat beside her. [388] The photograph was old, its corners soft. [395] A man with a beige lump behind one ear held a girl of six on his shoulders in front of a boat. [417]

"It's the last one of us," Liv said. [425] "Thirty-one years. [427] My mother left and took me, and I told myself he never wrote because he never cared, and that was easier than checking." [450] Her voice was thin and level, the voice of someone standing on a ledge and describing the weather. [468] "I nearly turned around at the station. [475] I think I am going to turn around now, and not come back to anything." [490]

The atrium tilted a little, the way it did for Marit when a case stopped being a case. [508] The mist held everything in a reeling hush, and the hearing aid in her fist was nearly frantic. [526] She had come to close a file. [533] She opened her hand instead. [538]

"This was his," she said. [543] "He wore it every day and would not let anyone touch it. [555] Tonight it has been pulling toward you like a compass since the sun went down." [570]

Liv looked at the walnut of beige plastic and did not seem to breathe. [584] "It is the same one," she said. [591] "From the picture." [594]

"The nurses told me he asked for a new one exactly once, in 1993, and then changed his mind," Marit said. [615] "I did not understand why until now." [622] Marit set the aid in Liv's palm and closed her fingers over it. [635] "I can't tell you why he never wrote. [643] But I can assure you of this, because I have followed a hundred of these and they do not lie: the last thing his heart was doing was looking for you." [674]

The tick against Liv's skin slowed, satisfied, and went still. [684] Liv folded over the photograph and the dead thing that had found her, and the sound she made was small and terrible and alive. [708]

Marit stayed until the mist began to settle back into the floor. [720] Later she would write "one contact, notified" and nothing else, because the form had no line for a woman who unbuttoned her coat and asked where she might sleep tonight. [750] She had only wanted to know who the pulse belonged to. [761] That, she thought, walking home through the dark, was usually how it went: you followed a question because you could not help it, and somewhere along the way it turned out to be a person, waiting.
