The rain had quit an hour before, and the islet steamed under a breeze that came in off the strait in even, patient breaths. [24] Osric Hale worked his shears through a shore juniper at the same tempo, one snip, one pause, one snip, as if the tree and the wind had agreed on a rhythm and he had simply joined it. [61] His student, Pell, waited for him to finish a sentence he had started before the rain. [77] "A tree," Osric said at last, "remembers every place you cut it." [89] Pell had stopped asking him to hurry a month ago. [99] She looked instead at the brass compass on the shed's windowsill, its lid green with salt, its needle pointing steadily southwest, where there was nothing but water. [126] "It's never once pointed north," she said. [133] "No." [134] He set the shears down and wiped them, which took a while. [146] "It points at the Coriolis." [151]

Everyone on the islet knew the Coriolis, though only the old ones had seen her whole. [167] She was the last ferry to run the causeway channel before the storm that people now simply called the severance, the night the sea took the road to the mainland and did not give it back. [203] She went down with eleven aboard. [209] On fog nights she still crossed, lightless, and each dawn tide after her passing left something on the shingle: a spoon, a shoe, once a whole unbroken teacup. [237] The islanders called them the tides of remembrance, and they were rhythmically placid, never violent, arriving like a hand set gently on a shoulder. [261]

"My sister Wen was aboard," Osric said. [268] Pell had not known that. [273] "We argued that morning about the trees, about her leaving, about nothing," he went on, each clause arriving after the breeze had turned over once. [298] "I meant to apologize when she came back Sunday." [307]

The compass had come ashore the third dawn after. [316] He had kept it because he believed, in the unhurried way he believed things, that an apology never spoken must still exist somewhere, and must have a color, and that if he could find that color he could grow it in a pot and tend it. [362] He had spent nine years failing to. [369]

Pell went into the shed and came out with the lace. [380] It hung in his window, Wen's last piece, worked over an antique pattern their grandmother had brought from the mainland: waves, a border of eyelets, a strange irregular run of knots along the hem that Osric had always taken for a flaw. [422] "My grandmother made lace," Pell said. [428] "Lacemakers wrote in it when they couldn't write." [436] She turned the hem to the light and read the knots the way he read a branch, slowly, with her fingers. [457] "Low water after rain, carry the needle down, open it, let north go." [470]

Osric stood very still, and then he understood: the pattern was the old islet remedy for those who kept what the sea returned, and Wen had worked it into his window years before either of them had reason to. [509]

The tide was falling. [513] He took the compass down the wet path, Pell behind him, the breeze pushing at their backs with the same regularity as his shears. [537] At the tideline the puddles among the stones still held the rain, and the sky in them was a pale, washed blue. [559] He knelt, which took a while, and pried the lid open. [570] The needle swung once toward the drowned ferry and then, freed, simply let go of its bearing. [587] Its enamel, a deep hard cerulean he had never noticed under the salt, began to diffuse into the pool like ink, spreading in slow rings, thinning, coloring the water and the reflected sky until there was no telling where the blue had started. [630]

"That's it," Osric said. [634] "That's the color." [637]

It was not a color a man could pot. [646] It was the color of the thing you could not say, carried off by the water that had taken the person you could not say it to, and it did not vanish so much as become too wide to hold. [686] The tide came in over the pool and took it, placid as ever, and left on the shingle nothing at all. [707]

Walking back, Osric was quiet for so long that Pell thought the lesson was over. [722] "The juniper," he said finally. [727] "We'll wire it to lean toward the strait." [735] "Not to point anywhere." [739] "Only to remember which way the wind came from." [748]

Behind them the puddles, rinsed clean, went back to holding a plain sky. [761]
