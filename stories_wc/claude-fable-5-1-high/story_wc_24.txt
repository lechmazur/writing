<story>
The horizon measurement station sat at the mouth of the fjord where the water went black and stopped moving. [19] Solveig was its hydraulic clock shepherd, which meant she kept eleven water clocks running in step so the surveyors upstairs could mark the exact instant the sea met the sky. [49] Meltwater came down a copper pipe from the glacier, filled each brass drum, and drained through a pin hole thinner than a hair. [72] Her job was to listen for the drum that fell behind and coax it back. [87]

The station existed for one measurement. [93] Twice a day, the surveyors laid a knife-edge sight against the horizon and recorded how far the line had risen or fallen against the mountain marks, and the clocks told them when. [125] Solveig had never seen the horizon from upstairs. [133] She saw it only after blowing the whistle. [141]

The whistle was hers to blow. [147] When the last drum emptied at the close of the evening reading, she put the tin whistle to her mouth and sent one long note up the stairwell, and the surveyors set down their pencils. [182] Then the station belonged to the water and to her. [192] That was when she went out onto the rock shelf with the mantle clock key in her fist. [210]

The key fit nothing here. [215] It had wound her grandmother's clock in a farmhouse three valleys south, a clock that struck the hour with a small dry cough, until the house was sold and the clock went with it and only the key stayed in Solveig's coat pocket. [258] Her grandmother had wound it every Sunday and said the same thing each time: a clock does not measure time, child, it measures something else, and nobody has bothered to find out what. [291] Solveig had taken the job at the station to find out. [302] The surveyors found her eminently peculiar, and wrote as much in the log, because she asked them what the horizon was made of and they had no answer except a number. [333]

She sat on the shelf with her boots over the black water and squinted west. [348] Squinting made the horizon sharper. [353] It stopped being a smear of grey on grey and became a razor edge, a line with no width at all, the one place in the world where two things touched without overlapping. [386] She had begun to think the clocks were like that. [396] Each drop through the pin hole was an edge between the water that had left and the water still waiting, and the drum did not hold time so much as hold the gap. [429]

To test it she used the only method she trusted: counting backwards. [441] Forwards, a count only added; it told her nothing but that she was still there. [456] Backwards, from the whistle, each number was a drop she had already heard fall, and she could feel where it had gone. [478]

Sixty. [479] The whistle, the pencils going down, a chair scraping. [488] Forty. [489] The eleventh drum emptying with the sound of a held breath let out. [502] Twenty. [503] Her own hand on the copper pipe, feeling the glacier in it. [515]

She kept going past zero, because zero was only where she had chosen to start the whistle, not where anything had truly begun. [538] Minus a hundred, and the surveyors were still climbing the stairs that morning. [551] Minus a thousand, and she was on the mail boat coming north with the key in her pocket. [569] Further, and the farmhouse door was closing, and further, and her grandmother's hand was on the key, turning it, and the clock coughed. [592]

Solveig opened her eyes. [596] The horizon had not moved, but the light had left it, and the line was gone into the dark as if it had never had an edge at all. [625]

That was the answer, or as much of one as the fjord was going to give her. [642] A clock measured the distance between where you were and the last thing you could still hear falling. [660] The surveyors measured the horizon the same way, without knowing it, marking each evening how far the edge had slid from where it had been. [685] Neither of them measured time. [690] They measured what was no longer touching. [697]

She turned the key once in the empty air, the way her grandmother had, and felt the small resistance of nothing. [718] Then she went inside to refill the drums, because at first light the surveyors would need to know when, and the clocks would need to be ready to tell them. [748]
