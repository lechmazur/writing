Odum had lived inside the library's walls for eleven years, and he knew the building the way a hand knows its own pulse. [23] He knew which floorboards in the crawl space above Reading Room C would groan, which steam pipes ticked at four in the morning, and which pipe chases had been sealed carelessly enough to hold the city's forgotten things. [61] The outage came at five, without warning. [68] The hum of the HVAC died, the exit signs went dark, and through the clerestory windows Odum watched the city's lights stutter across the skyline like a slow, failing heartbeat. [98] Dawn was still an hour off, and what light there was came blue and thin, pooling on the long oak tables like water. [121] In that hush, the building belonged to him. [129]

He lowered himself from the access hatch behind the periodicals, as he did most nights, and went to work. [148] Odum was methodical about it. [153] Each chase got its turn: unscrew the panel, lay the screws in a row on a folded handkerchief, reach past the warm pipes with a bent coat hanger, and draw out whatever had fallen through the gaps over seventy years of renovations. [195] Library cards, a child's mitten, a brass key, a wedding photograph curled with damp. [209] He cleaned each one and cached them in biscuit tins above the ceiling, labeled by floor and year, waiting for a day he had never quite believed in. [237]

Tonight the chase behind the old audio archive gave up something different. [249] His fingers closed on a curve of brown ribbon, brittle at the edges, wound around a shard of plastic hub. [269] A fragment of a tape reel, quarter-inch, ferromagnetic oxide dulled to the color of dried tea. [285] He did not need the light to know it. [294] Thirty years ago his hands had done this for a living, threading reels through the heads of a Studer in a basement studio, and the body does not forget what the mind lets go. [328] The faint vinegar smell of aging acetate. [335] The particular slickness of the backing against a thumb. [344] He ran the ribbon between two fingers and, absurdly, almost heard it: a woman's voice, low, telling a story to a microphone in a room with a ticking clock. [373]

Then he remembered where he had heard about such a voice. [384] Two weeks earlier, lying flat in the crawl space above the archive office, he had listened to the new cataloguer, Ines, on the phone with someone, her voice cracking. [413] The oral history reels from 1974 had been water-damaged and deaccessioned decades ago, all but a few strays. [431] Her grandmother had recorded one. [436] Ines had never heard the woman speak, and now there was nothing left to hear. [451] Odum had lain there above her, one plank and a world away, and done nothing, because doing nothing was how he had survived. [474]

He looked at the fragment in his palm. [482] It might hold forty seconds. [487] It might hold nothing but hiss. [493] He could tuck it into a tin with the mitten and the key and go on being a rumor, a draft in the stacks, the reason the night guard never quite finished his rounds. [527] Or he could admit what the tins had always been for. [538]

Outside, the city's lights guttered again, whole blocks going dark and returning. [550] The sky over the rooftops had deepened from black to a bruised sapphire. [563] Ines came in at six; she always did, using her key at the side door while the building was still asleep. [584] Odum replaced the panel, screw by screw, and folded the handkerchief. [595] Then he did not climb back up. [602]

He sat at the end of the long table nearest the archive door, where the blue light was strongest, and set the fragment in front of him on a clean sheet of paper with the date and the chase number written in pencil, the way he labeled everything. [650] His hands shook. [653] He let them. [656]

The side door opened at four minutes past six. [665] Footsteps, a muttered word about the lights, the beam of a phone sweeping the floor. [680] Then it found him, and stopped. [686]

"I'm not going to hurt you," Odum said, and his voice was rough from years of whispering. [703] "I found something in the wall behind your office." [712] "I think it was your grandmother's." [718]

Ines did not move for a long moment. [726] Then she came closer, slowly, and looked at the brown ribbon on its labeled square of paper, and at the old man who had set it there like an offering. [756]

"There's more," he said. [760] "Upstairs." [761] "Eleven years of it." [765]

Behind them, the exit signs flickered, caught, and held, and the city outside came back on all at once, and Odum sat in the returning light and did not look away. [796]
