Ines Vardo had inspected the summit crater of Cerro Calado for eleven years, and she knew it would not survive a twelfth. [22] The tracking station was coming in the morning, bulldozers and floodlights and a dish the size of a cathedral, because a nation racing for the Moon needed to hear its rockets from the highest ground it owned. [59] The crater would be leveled for the foundation. [67] So she climbed on the last full-moon night with a camera, six glass plates, and a wooden case that rattled. [87]

She measured the fumaroles, sampled the gases, noted whether the rim had slumped since spring. [102] But Ines had never been curious the way tourists were curious, wanting a fact to carry home. [119] She was curious with a purpose, the way a doctor is curious about a pulse. [134] And what she wanted to know now was whether the crater's lake would hold the moon one more time before it was filled with concrete. [159]

The lake was a shallow disc of snowmelt no wider than a barn, and it existed for perhaps three weeks a year. [181] At moonrise it turned to hammered silver. [188] Nobody had ever photographed it, because nobody had ever thought it mattered. [200]

Teodor had thought it mattered. [205] He had been the nightwatch at the summit weather hut for forty years, timing his rounds with a brass hourglass because the cold killed every clock he owned. [233] When the hut closed he had walked down the mountain with the hourglass in his coat, and when it shattered on the trail he had gone to his knees and saved a single grain. [267] "The smallest piece of a watch kept," he told her, pressing it into a fold of paper. [284] He had died in March, before the surveyors came. [293]

The wooden case was his, too. [299] Inside, padded in wool, stood nine stoppered vials of clear liquid, each tagged with a numbered card in his square handwriting. [320] Teodor had never trusted clocks, but he trusted chemistry. [329] Iodate and sulfite and a pinch of starch, he had explained, mixed in careful proportions, would sit colorless for exactly as long as you told them to, and then turn ink-black in a single heartbeat. [364] He had built her a night of them, one vial for each hour, and she was not to read a card until its vial went dark. [390]

She reached the rim at dusk and set the vials in a row along a flat stone, moonward, where she could see them. [413]

The first vial blackened as the moon cleared the eastern ridge. [424] "Tripod on the north lip. [429] The lake looks widest from there." [435] She moved the tripod. [439]

The second vial's card read simply that she should not expose yet, that the wind always dropped after nine. [458] It dropped at nine. [462]

By the fourth vial the cold had gotten into her hands, and she understood what he had actually built. [481] Not instructions. [483] Company. [484] Each card was a small dry joke, or a memory of the hut, or an order to eat something, timed so that his voice arrived at exactly the hours when a person alone on a summit begins to bargain with the descent. [526] He had known she would want to go down. [535] He had known she would have to outlast the wanting. [545]

She exposed four plates across the deep of the night, the moon crawling west, the lake giving it back a little more bronze each hour. [570] Far below, at the trailhead, she could see the parked machines, a line of yellow shapes patient as cattle. [589]

The ninth vial turned when the sky behind her had gone the gray of old pewter and the moon hung low and swollen over the lake, about to touch the far rim. [621] The wind was entirely gone. [626] This was the hour Teodor had called the hush, when even the fumaroles seemed to hold their breath and the whole mountain listened for the sun. [652] The last card said only: "Now." [658]

She uncapped the lens and counted the seconds aloud, her breath the only motion on the summit, while the moon laid itself down in the water one last time and the water accepted it. [692]

Dawn came up thin and gold behind her and the lake turned ordinary, just a puddle of meltwater with a rusted rim, and somewhere at the trailhead an engine coughed. [722]

Ines took the fold of paper from her breast pocket and opened it. [735] The grain of sand was so small she had to tilt it into the light to be sure it was still there. [757] She knelt at the water's edge and let it fall in. [768] Concrete would pour over it by noon, and it would keep its watch anyway, one grain outlasting the dish and the rockets and the age itself. [794]
