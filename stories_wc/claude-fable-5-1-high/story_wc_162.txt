The corn maze went quiet the way a held breath goes quiet, and somewhere in it Elias was calling his daughter's name. [22] Wren heard him and did not answer. [29] She was not lost; she had simply walked out the far wall where the stalks thinned, because for three autumns her father had built his mazes with what he called necessary distances, long blind corridors where no one could see anyone, and she was tired of them. [76] Beyond the field stood the old airstrip, and on its edge the terrace her mother had glassed in with salvaged panes. [97] The panes had cracked into prisms, and at sunset the light inside drifted across the floorboards in slow bars of color, so that the whole room seemed to be moving without going anywhere. [130] Wren stopped at the door. [135] Some thresholds felt sacred and some did not, and she had never understood why. [149] The maze entrance did, every year, the moment the first stalks closed behind you. [163] This door did too, and she had come to find out what the two had in common. [180] She stepped through. Her mother's desk still sat under the west panes, built from the oak that had fallen across the runway the year Wren was born. [207] On it lay the pilot logbook, closed, its cover furred with dust. [219] Wren did not open it yet. [225] Instead she laid her palm on the desk and read the grain, the way her father had taught her before he stopped teaching her anything. [250] Wood remembers weather, he used to say. [257] Tight rings for hard years, wide for easy ones; a swirl where a branch fought the wind; a dark seam where lightning had come close and the tree had chosen to keep living anyway. [291] Under her fingers the oak told her what it had survived, and she felt her mind loosen into the reading, ready to take the codes the same way. [319]

Then she opened the logbook to the last page. [328] Her mother had written weather in code, the way pilots do: BKN, OVC, FG, small stacks of capitals that meant sky. [349] Wren did not know the code. [355] But she knew grain, and she let herself intuit the page like a plank: where the letters bunched tight, the weather had been closing; where the pen pressed hard enough to dent the paper, her mother had been afraid. [394] FG appeared three times before the final time stamp, each one darker than the last. [409] The final stamp was 07:14. [414]

Wren sat down slowly on the floor, in a bar of drifting violet. [427] Everyone knew the story of the butterfly. [434] Her mother had been ready to take off at 07:12 when six-year-old Wren shouted for her to look at a monarch on the fence, and her mother had looked, had lost two minutes, and two minutes later flew into fog that had not been there before. [480] That was the whole of it, in her father's mouth: a small wing, a huge weather, a child who had made her mother look away. [505] Everything after had been after the butterfly effect, and he had built the distances to hold it. [522]

But the first FG was stamped 06:50. [529] The fog had been in the book before the butterfly was on the fence. [543] Her mother had known, had written it down three times, and had walked out to the plane anyway, the way the oak had kept growing past the lightning. [695] He had not crossed this threshold in three years, and she could see the distance he kept from it was the same one he kept from her. [722] "Look at the time on the first fog," she said, and held the book open toward him. [739] He looked for a long time.
