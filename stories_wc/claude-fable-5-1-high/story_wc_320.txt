The moon came in close that night, low and swollen at perigee, and the whole tower city leaned toward it on its cables like a field of iron sunflowers. [29] Below, the gorge went down farther than sound could follow. [39] Ione Marrow climbed the ladder of Tower Nine with a thermos in one hand and a chunk of Hollywood sidewalk in the other, because that was the job. [67] She was a celestial signal interpreter, the last one on the payroll of a company that no longer existed, and the moon at perigee was the loudest thing in the sky. [98]

The city had been built to broadcast, a hundred masts strung across the gorge on steel so the signals could fall clean into the valley towns. [124] Then the towns stopped listening, and the engineers left, and the abandoned towers hummed to nobody. [140] Ione stayed, because endings were her specialty. [147] Every one of them, she was certain, had a beginning folded up inside it, the way a seed is folded inside a rotten apple, and she intended to find this one. [178]

Three nights ago the sky had dropped something. [186] Not a meteor. [189] A hush. [191] It had come down through the static on every band at once, a shard of pure quiet about the size of a held breath, and it had lodged somewhere in the girders of Tower Nine. [226] Since then the tower had refused to hum. [234] Birds would not land on it. [240] Ione's instruments read it as a dead spot, which she knew was wrong, because dead spots did not make the hair on your arms stand up. [266]

At the top platform she set down the thermos and unwrapped the sidewalk. [279] It was a wedge of pink terrazzo with half a brass star still set in it and three letters of a name nobody remembered. [303] Her predecessor had brought it back from the coast decades ago and used it as a grounding stone. [321] Something that a million strangers had stepped on while saying a name aloud, he told her, holds a charge like nothing else, and it will not let a signal drift. [351] She had thought he was being poetic. [358] He was being practical. [362]

She clipped the copper lead from the receiver to the brass and knelt over the dials. [378] For an hour she tuned the way she had been trained, sweeping the bands for a carrier, waiting for the hush to announce itself as a gap she could bracket. [408] Nothing. [409] The moon climbed, huge and close, pulling at the cables until the whole city creaked. [424]

Ione sat back on her heels and did what her predecessor had taught her to do when the sky refused to cooperate. [446] She started throwing things out. [451]

First assumption: silence is the absence of signal. [459] Gone. [460] If the hush were absence, the tower would still hum around it, and it did not. [476] So the hush was a thing, with weight and frequency. [486]

Second assumption: a thing that fell from the sky is ending, a fragment of something broken. [502] Gone too. [504] She looked at the terrazzo under her hand, itself a fragment, itself fallen a long way from where it had been laid, and still holding a charge. [531]

Third assumption: you tune toward sound. [537] She turned the dial the other way. [544] Down past the last station, past the hiss, into the band where the needle usually went slack, and there it was. [565]

The hush locked in. [569] It was not quiet at all. [575] It was serenely electric, a calm so dense it prickled, the feeling of a room the instant before someone speaks. [595] The brass star grew warm under her palm. [603] Every mast across the gorge stopped creaking at once, a hundred iron sunflowers holding still. [618]

Then, inside the hush, small as a heartbeat, she heard it: a test tone. [632] Three rising notes, the kind a station plays before it goes on the air for the very first time. [651] Not a station dying somewhere out past the moon. [660] A station clearing its throat. [665]

Ione laughed, once, and wrote the frequency on the back of her hand in grease pencil so she would not lose it. [687] The moon would pull away by morning and the hush would thin, but she had it now, bracketed and named. [707] Somebody, somewhere, was about to start talking, and Tower Nine, dead for thirty years, was going to be the first thing to hear them. [731]
