The Gilt Arcade caught the first light the way it had for two hundred years, its glass roof turning the color of old honey while the city outside stayed the blue of screens. [33] Wren came at dawn because the brass box was emptied at seven, and because the street drones did not reach the arcade flagstones until half past. [59] Between those two hours, surface mail still existed. [67]

The Heritage Postal Authority called it a living exhibit: one collection a week, paper only, every envelope signed by hand and scanned against the civic register. [93] A signature had to belong to someone. [100] That was where Wren came in. [106] She made people who had never existed and gave them just enough weight to pass. [121] Not a fake, which the scanners caught, but a plausible stranger: a Marguerite Osei who might have taught piano in a suburb that had since been renamed, a Tomas Vell with the faint statistical shadow of a man who paid his taxes late. [164] It took care. [167] Too inventive and the name rang false; too careful and it matched a living person, who then received questions. [186]

Her clients never met her. [191] They left their requests in children's sidewalk chalk, beneath the dry fountain at the arcade's center, drawn as hopscotch grids. [211] But the numbers in the squares were never one through ten. [222] Read as a column, they gave a destination district; read as a row, the sender's age and profession; a squiggle of pink in the corner meant the letter was a confession, yellow meant an apology, blue meant love. [260] The drones washed it all away by eight, and the city read only the absence of a game. [278]

This morning there was one grid. [284] Wren knelt with her ebony clipboard, the finish worn pale where three generations of thumbs had held it. [302] Her grandmother had carried it on a real route, when letters were ordinary, when a postman's satchel and not an algorithm decided what reached whom. [327] Wren copied the chalk figures onto her sheet and frowned. [337] District seven, which was hers. [342] Age thirty-four, which was hers. [347] Profession: the ninth square held a symbol she had never assigned, a small drawn envelope with an eye inside it. [367] And no color in the corner at all. [375]

The envelope itself waited under the fountain's lip, where clients always left them, already sealed and addressed in someone else's careful print. [397] To the woman with the black clipboard, the address read, Gilt Arcade, poste restante. [411] The signature line was blank. [416] Someone wanted her to invent a name for a letter meant for her, so that it could be mailed to her, so that she would receive it properly, through the box, the way letters were once received. [453] She had never had one. [458]

She held the envelope beside the chalk grid, juxtaposing the two as she always did, paper against stone, the permanent against the washable, looking for the small disagreements that told her who a client really was. [494] It was the margins she trusted. [500] The register read the center of things: names, dates, destinations. [510] But people leaked out at the edges, in the pressure of a pen, in a hopscotch square drawn slightly larger than the others, in the chalk dust that clung to a hem. [542]

The ninth square had been drawn twice, the first attempt rubbed out with a palm, and the envelope's flap bore a thumbprint in the same pale green as the grid. [572] The sender had hesitated, then hurried. [578] And the eye inside the drawn envelope was not an eye. [589] It was the maker's mark her grandmother had inked into the back of the clipboard, sixty years ago, when the Authority still let its carriers sign their own bags. [618] Only one other person alive knew that mark, and Wren had not spoken to her mother in eleven years, since her mother had chosen the register and Wren had chosen the margins. [650]

The light in the arcade went from honey to brass. [660] Somewhere a drone hummed awake. [665] Wren uncapped her pen over the signature line and considered the names she might give a woman who had spent her life inside the system and now wanted, once, to reach her daughter from outside it. [701] A plausible stranger, she thought, and for the first time the phrase hurt. [714]

Then she did something carefully inventive: she wrote her grandmother's name, Ada Marlow, a woman thirty years dead, whose record the Authority had long since archived beyond reach of any scan. [745] Not a stranger. [748] A shared one. [751] She slid the letter into the brass box at four minutes to seven, and stood beside the grid to wait for next week, when it would come back to her, delivered. [782]
