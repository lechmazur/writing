The Cormorant was older than anyone aboard her, an antique airship of varnished ribs and patched silk, and for eleven days she had hung stranded above a sea of fog with her engines coughing and her crew speaking five languages. [40] Wren, the map apprentice, spoke only one of them. [49] The navigator who had stitched the crew together with patience and a phrasebook had died on the third day, and since then the galley was full of smiles and soup and nobody Wren could ask a question. [86] She kept his phrasebook anyway. [91] It was a polyglot's phrasebook, six columns to a page, and one corner had been folded down so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. [118] The dog-ear marked a single line: Where does the light come from? [130]

Wren had not understood why he marked it until the fourth dusk. [142] That was when the starlings came. [148] They rose out of the fog in a murmuration so vast it dimmed the sunset, thousands of birds folding and unfolding like a single breathing sheet, and beneath them, deep in the grey, something glowed. [183] Not a fire. [186] A steady, patient luminance, stranded under the fog the way the Cormorant was stranded above it, and no chart in the map room showed anything there but blank vellum. [215]

Blank vellum was the reason Wren had signed on. [224] Her master had told her the age of discovery was over, that every coast was drawn, and she had chosen the oldest, slowest ship in the yard because it would go where nothing sensible went. [259] Now something unsensible was glowing beneath her, and she could not ask a soul about it. [275]

So she did what a diligently curious apprentice does: she noted. [286] Bearing, hour, the tilt of the deck, the way the glow seemed to slide a little each night as the fog carried the ship. [310] But a bearing from a drifting ship is a line with no anchor, and one line charts nothing. [328]

The answer was on the cargo deck, chalked in a corner. [339] Someone's child, on some voyage long before Wren, had drawn a hopscotch board there, nine squares climbing toward a half-moon, and nobody had ever scrubbed it off. [366] Dust had settled into it. [371] Wren passed it on the seventh evening and stopped, because the dust was not lying flat. [387] It had gathered into ridges, fine as frost, a different figure in each square, and the figures trembled. [405] She knelt and put her palm to the planks. [414] The hull was humming, too low to hear, and it hummed hardest when the starlings passed overhead and the glow below brightened, as though the light itself were sounding a note through the fog. [448]

Each night, as the ship drifted, the dust redrew itself. [458] Wren began to note the patterns, square by square, in the back of the phrasebook where the dead navigator had left blank pages. [481] The ridges leaned. [484] Whatever side of the hull faced the source, the dust in the nearest squares stood in tight arcs; the far squares blurred. [506] Three nights of drift gave her three leanings from three positions, and three lines cross where one cannot. [524] On the tenth night she inked the crossing onto blank vellum and sat back, her hands grey with chalk and old dust, and understood she had charted a place that did not officially exist. [558]

What it was, she still could not say. [566] A beacon left burning by a lost expedition, a town swallowed when the fog rose, a signal fire tended by someone as stranded as she was. [592] The navigator had folded that corner for a reason, and Wren finally thought she knew it: he had not been marking a question he wanted answered. [618] He had been marking the one he wanted asked. [627]

She took the phrasebook and the chart to the galley. [637] The engineer, the pilot, the cook and the rigger looked up from their bowls with the friendly helplessness of people who liked her and could not talk to her. [666] Wren opened the book at the dog-ear, laid the chart beside it, and ran her finger along the line, once in each of the six columns, careful with every syllable. [696] Where does the light come from? [702]

The pilot's eyes went to the inked crossing. [710] The engineer set down her spoon and said something quick and low, and the rigger laughed, not unkindly, the way people laugh when a long silence finally breaks. [738] Then the engineer tapped the chart, tapped her own chest, and pointed down through the deck toward the fog. [757] Wren did not need a phrasebook for that. [765] Outside, the starlings were gathering again over a light that now had a name on paper, and for the first time in eleven days the Cormorant's crew rose from the table together. [797]
