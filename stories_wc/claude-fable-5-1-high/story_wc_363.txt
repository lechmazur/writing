Ilse had not touched the loom since the winter Aurel died. [11] The house had gone quiet in the way a hem goes quiet when the thread is pulled, one loop at a time, until there is nothing left to hold the cloth together. [43] She called it the raveling silence, and she let it widen. [54] She had always been wistful by trade; a loom spinner learns to look backward, since every row is behind her the moment the beater packs it down. [81] Seven springs passed through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry, each one a thread pulled loose. [97]

At the church rummage sale she bought a shoebox for a coin because the woman selling it had looked at her with pity, and pity was easier to pay off than to endure. [130] Inside were jigsaw pieces, edges only, forty-some, the whole middle gone. [141] She almost threw them out. [146] Then she turned one over and saw Aurel's handwriting, small and slanted, a single letter in pencil. [163]

He had cut puzzles every winter of their marriage, a saw and a board and a photograph, and he had always written her something on the backs, a sentence she could only read once the frame was closed. [201] This one he had never given her. [208] He must have died with it half cut, and someone clearing the workshop had swept the finished edges into a box and the unfinished board into the stove. [236]

She laid the edges out on the kitchen table. [245] Picture side up, they framed an emptiness she recognized at once: bamboo, pale green and dense, and along the bottom edge the gray knuckles of old fruit trees. [273] The forgotten orchard. [276] Deep in the bamboo forest behind the village, a farmer had once planted plums, and the bamboo had taken it back before Ilse was born, and Aurel had found it anyway, the way he found everything. [312] He had asked her to marry him there, and pried a bead of hardened sap from a plum trunk and pressed it into her palm because he could not afford a ring. [344] She still wore the tree sap amber bead on a cord at her throat, stoically sparkling, the one thing in the house that had not gone dull. [371]

She turned the edges over and fitted them by their tabs and notches until the frame closed. [388] The letters ran around the border like a hem. [397] IF I GO FIRST GO TO THE ORCHARD AND TELL ME WHAT THE PLUMS DID. [412]

Ilse sat a long time with her hand over her mouth. [423] He had known, then, or guessed; his heart had been bad for years and he had never said so. [442] And he had asked one thing of her, and she had spent seven years not doing it, because no one had handed her the frame. [467]

A message wants an answer, and the distance to the dead is the only impossible one. [483] She could not speak across it. [489] But she had never been a speaker; she had been a spinner and a weaver, and cloth outlasts voices. [508]

That night she walked into the bamboo with a lantern and found the orchard by memory, the stalks closing behind her like a curtain. [532] The plums had done what forgotten things do: three trees dead, two leaning, one still bearing small sour fruit that no one picked. [555] She counted, and she remembered, and she went home. [564]

At dawn she recommenced. [568] The loom complained, then agreed. [573] She warped it with the last of the good wool, the skein she had been saving without admitting what for, and she wove the answer the only way she knew how to send it: a border of interlocking tabs and notches copied from the puzzle's edges, and inside the border, in dark thread against pale, six trees, three of them bare. [634] It took her until the plums were ripe. [642]

She carried the cloth back through the bamboo and hung it from the living tree, where the wind could read it, and where she could not. [668] Then she untied the cord at her throat. [676] A message across that distance needs a weight to carry it, and she had only one thing that had ever belonged to both sides. [700] She pressed the amber bead back into the wound in the trunk it had bled from, and the bark, in time, would close over it. [725]

Her throat felt bare for the first time in thirty years. [736] She did not weep. [740] She walked home through the hush, and the bamboo shut behind her, and in the kitchen the loom stood waiting with its shuttle still warm. [765] The silence had stopped raveling. [770] It had a hem now. [775]
