The rain had stopped an hour before, but the parapet of the old sea fort still whispered, water finding its way down through cracks in the stone as if the wall were telling itself what it remembered. [37] Okuro sat where he always sat, a torn net across his knees, and did not look up when the girl's sandals stopped behind him. [61] "The fishermen say you can mend anything with a knot in it," she said. [75] "The fishermen say many things when the catch is poor." [85] She knelt anyway, and set a bamboo tea whisk on the wet stone between them. [100] Fireflies drifted over the seaward drop, dozens of them, rising and sinking above the black water in a slow scintillatingly somber dance, like a procession that had forgotten who it was mourning. [132] Okuro finally looked. [135] The whisk's tines were intact, eighty of them split so fine they trembled in the air off the sea, but the black thread that bound them at the base had snapped and unravelled, and the whole delicate cage was slowly opening like a hand letting go. [181] "A whisk is not worth thread," he said. [189] "Buy another in the morning." [194] "This one was my teacher's." [199] Her voice was soft, folded small, but it did not retreat. [210] "She died in the spring." [215] "Tomorrow I serve tea to the magistrate in her place, and I intend to use this." [231] Okuro grunted. [233] A mended whisk before a magistrate was the kind of quiet insolence that could end a young woman's career in a single sip. [256] She knew it; he could see she knew it, kneeling there with her hands demurely in her lap and her chin set like a mooring post. [282] "Why?" he asked. [285] "Because nobody looks at the thread." [291] She turned the whisk so the frayed base caught the moon. [302] "They look at the foam, the bowl, the hands. [311] Somebody cut this bamboo eighty times without splitting it wrong once, and then hid all that work under a wrap of thread nobody would ever praise. [337] My teacher was like that. [342] I want one person in that room to have seen the effort, even if it is only me." [360] The wall whispered. [363] Somewhere below, a weep hole coughed and ran clear. [372] Okuro picked up the whisk and held it beside the mesh in his lap. [386] "Look," he said. [389] "The thread on this is tied the way I tie a net. [401] Over, under, lock, repeat. [405] The man who made your whisk never met a fisherman, and yet his hands found the same knot mine did, because there are only so many ways to hold things together, and they are all hidden in plain sight." [444] "Then you can fix it." [449] "I can show you how to fix it." [457] He cut a length of black cord and wet it in a puddle on the stone. [473] "Old menders do not work by lamp. [480] Lamps make shadows, and shadows lie. [486] We work by following moon paths." [492] He laid the wet cord along the parapet, and she saw it: where the cord was sound, moonlight ran down it in an unbroken line, a tiny silver road, and where the fibres were frayed the road broke into scattered lights. [533] "The eye cannot count eighty tines in the dark," he said. [544] "But the moon will show you where the old thread lay, because bamboo that has been bound for years holds the wet differently. [567] Follow the pale path. [571] Your fingers will know the rest." [577] She followed. [579] Her fingers did not know the rest. [586] On the third failed knot Okuro began to whistle, low and tuneless, four notes and a rest, four notes and a rest, and without deciding to she found her hands moving to it: over on the first note, under on the second, lock on the third, breathe on the fourth. [636] "My wife taught me that," he said between phrases. [645] "She said a knot you cannot whistle is a knot you do not understand." [659] The fireflies came closer, drawn perhaps by the sound, perhaps by nothing, lighting and dimming over the wet stone until the whole parapet seemed to breathe with them. [687] When the last lock was pulled tight, the tines drew back into their patient circle and stood. [704] She turned it in the moonlight. [710] The new thread was invisible unless you knew to look. [720] "The magistrate will not see it," Okuro said. [728] "No." [729] She bowed, small and correct, and rose with the whisk held before her like something that had cost a great deal. [750] "But when I whip the tea tomorrow, I will be whistling in my head." [764] Okuro watched her go down the wet steps, following the moon path along the parapet without once looking at her feet, and picked up his net, and began again. [793]
