Ines Varga painted in colors that made people squint. [9] Her murals shouted from parking garages and underpasses, all cadmium and cobalt, and the city loved her for it. [28] So nobody understood why she took the job at the Amber Pavilion, where the light was the color of steeped tea and nothing had shouted in a hundred years. [57] She did not explain. [61] She rarely did. [64]

The pavilion sat at the far end of the botanical garden, a glasshouse built from the bones of a demolished cathedral. [85] Its panes were amber, so that inside it was always autumn, even now, with the last snow of winter still lying in gray drifts against the foundation. [112] The head gardener wanted a mural on the blank north wall, something for the tourists. [127] Ines wanted the wall for a different reason, and she kept it folded in her coat pocket. [144]

Forty-one postcards, rubber-banded, soft at the corners. [151] Her grandmother Dora had sent them, one a year, during the decades she worked nights at this very pavilion, tending beds that no daytime visitor ever saw open. [179] Moonflower. [180] Night-blooming cereus. [182] Evening primrose, four-o'clocks, the queen of the night. [190] Each card showed a flower that refused the sun, and on the back of each, in the same slanted hand, the same sentence: Still don't know why. [217] Dora had died in October, still not knowing. [225] Ines, who could not paint a question she did not understand, had come to find out. [241]

She worked at night, because that was when the pavilion was honest. [253] Under the work lamps the cereus unfolded its white throats, and the air went thick and sweet, and Ines primed the wall and sketched and did not yet touch a color. [284] Outside, the snow shrank a little each morning. [292] She measured her progress against it. [298]

The east wall held the cathedral's one surviving window, tall and clear where the rest was amber, its glass etched with vines so faint they read as scratches. [326] Ines noticed on the third week that the etching was not one layer but several, laminated panes each carrying a fragment, and that they did not line up. [354] Not from any angle she could stand at. [362] Not by lamplight. [365] She began staying past the lamps, past the hour when the flowers, one by one, folded themselves shut. [383]

There is a hush that comes just before dawn, when the moths have gone and the birds have not yet started, and the pavilion held it like a cupped hand. [413] On the morning the last drift finally gave up and ran off toward the drains, the sun came in low across the bare ground, unbroken by snow glare for the first time, and struck the window at an angle Ines had never seen. [456] The layers aligned. [459] The scratches became vines, and the vines became a moonflower, fully open, and hovering at its mouth, drawn in a single line so delicate she gasped, a hawkmoth with its tongue unrolled. [491] Whoever had made the window had hidden the answer inside it and left it for the one hour of the one season when someone might be looking. [518]

Ines sat on the cold floor and reckoned with it. [528] The flowers did not bloom at night for any reason of their own. [541] They bloomed for what came to them in the dark. [551] The cereus opened because the moth was coming, and had been coming for a million years, and to open at noon would be to open for no one. [579] It was not a refusal of the sun. [587] It was a faithfulness to something the sun would never see. [598] She thought of her grandmother walking these beds alone for forty years, writing the same four words to a granddaughter who painted for crowds, and understood at last that the postcards had not been asking. [633] They had been inviting. [637] Come and see what opens when nobody is watching. [646]

The mural took her another six weeks. [653] It surprised the head gardener, who had expected cadmium. [662] Ines painted the north wall in every shade of shadow she owned: slate and plum and the violet-black underneath a leaf. [683] Across it, forty-one flowers opened, one for each postcard, and each was visited by something with wings. [700] The only vivid color in the whole wall lived in the moths, a single hot stroke of orange on each underwing, so that in the tea-colored daylight the mural looked almost empty, and only after the lamps went off, in that hush before morning, did the eye adjust enough to find them. [752] In the lower corner, very small, she painted a woman in a gardener's apron with her back turned. [770] She did not sign the wall. [776] Some things are not made for crowds, and are seen all the same. [789]
