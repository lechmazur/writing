The letters came up in a fishing net, sealed in cobalt glass, and after they were found nothing aboard the Meridian was quiet again. [24] Ines Varela read them by lamplight in the reading gondola, the library's hull creaking above the coast like a held breath. [45] The Meridian was a zeppelin because the ground below had outlawed memory; the republic kept its archives in the sky where no magistrate could burn them. [71] Ines had spent nineteen years cataloguing the names of the marchers killed at Salt Reach, and she did it the way some people pray, with her mouth shut and her hands steady. [103] The letters were written by Calla Mendes, the march's stenographer, and they said one thing plainly: I kept a diary of that night, I buried it where the dunes open onto the black water, and I ciphered it because I no longer knew who I could trust. [150] Every official history said the militia fired first. [158] Every letter hinted that the history was incomplete, and incompleteness was the one thing Ines could not shelve. [176]

She took the tether down at dawn. [183] Beneath the dunes the sand fell away into a lagoon of obsidian, a volcanic throat that had filled with seawater so still and dark it looked less like water than like a door. [216] The marchers had camped here. [221] The militia had come over the ridge here. [229] And here, on the glassy shore, an old woman sat arranging driftwood and shell and bottle-caps into long deliberate lines. [249]

Ines knew her from the archive photographs before she knew her from the letters. [263] Calla Mendes had never left Salt Reach. [270] She could not. [273] The fishermen called her the woman who lived yesterday, and Ines understood the phrase when she spoke Calla's name and the old eyes slid past her toward a ridge where, in 1961, torches were still coming down. [310] Calla was trapped in that night the way a fly is trapped in amber; she saw the past laid over the present so thickly that no living voice reached her. [340] But the debris was new. [345] Nothing in 1961 had been made of plastic, and so a bottle-cap on the black sand was the one thing Calla could see that belonged to now. [372]

Ines read the lines already laid out. [379] WHO SENT YOU. [382] She knelt and gathered kelp-stems and answered in the same crooked alphabet: THE LIBRARY. [396]

The parley took the whole day. [402] Calla dug the oilskin packet from beneath a flat stone but would not release it. [417] She spelled her terms in wet wood while the sun crossed the lagoon and lit the obsidian like a coal. [437] ALL OF IT OR NONE. [442] Ines asked what all meant. [447] Calla's hands shook as she laid it out, shell by shell: REVEREND OKONJO CARRIED A GUN. [463]

Ines sat back on the black sand and felt the archive she had built for nineteen years tilt under her. [483] Okonjo was the martyr on the Meridian's bow, the face on the folios, the reason the library flew at all. [503] If he had carried a gun, the militia's lawyers would say the massacre was a battle. [519] If she buried it, she would become the magistrates she had spent her life flying above. [535] She understood then why Calla had ciphered the diary and why she had never sent the key: the truth was a live wire, and she had held it alone for sixty years, unable to let go and unable to hand it to anyone who might drop it. [582]

Ines pulled the letters from her satchel and set them in the sand between them, a stack of proof that someone, finally, had come. [605] Then she wrote: ALL OF IT. [611]

Calla looked at the words for a long time, or looked at the torches coming down the ridge behind them; Ines could not tell which. [636] Then the old woman began laying out the key, and it was not a phrase but a list: thirty-one names, in the order they fell, which no history book had ever gotten right because only one person had been there to count. [678] Ines copied each one into her ledger with the same steady hands she used aboard the library, though her face was wet. [700] When the last name was down, Calla pushed the oilskin across the sand and turned back toward her ridge, where the night of 1961 went on and on. [728]

Ines climbed the tether at dusk with the diary against her ribs. [740] Below her the obsidian lagoon held the last light like a struck match, and above her the Meridian waited, its shelves full of a story that was about to get harder and truer. [773] She would open the diary tonight. [779] She would shelve all of it. [785]
