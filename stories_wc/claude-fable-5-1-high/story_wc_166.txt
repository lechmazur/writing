Marisol Vey had been the finest tide dancer on the Ashen Coast, and she chose exile the night the nostalgia ban became law. [23] The ban was simple: no remembering aloud, no heirlooms, no repeating what the dead had done. [39] A tide dance is nothing but repetition of the dead. [49] Every step Marisol took on the wet sand had first been taken by her mother, and her mother's mother, back to whoever first learned to stand where the sea was about to be and not be swept. [86] The Ministry offered her a choice: recant and be re-educated, or take a plot in the twilight garden on the headland, where the sea was only a sound. [114] She took the garden. [118]

Before they let her go, a clerk ground her mother's moonstone cabochon to powder on a steel tray, so that no one could inherit it. [143] Marisol signed the form with a wet thumb and came away with a single speck of dust, blue-white, no larger than a grain of salt. [168] She kept it under her thumbnail for a year. [177]

The garden was a black terrace of night-blooming things, cereus and moonflower and tobacco, planted by other exiles who had since died or drifted. [201] Nothing there could be danced. [206] But a tide dancer does not learn the sea by watching waves; she learns it by observing gaps. [224] The silence after the seventh wave is where you step. [234] The hollow the water leaves before it returns is where you turn. [246] So Marisol sat on the cold stone each dusk and observed the gaps between stars, the gaps between the patrol lights that swept the fence every eleven minutes, the gaps between what she was permitted to say and what her body still knew. [289]

In the gaps, a cosmic verse assembled itself. [297] It was the old tide calendar, the one the dancers had kept in their feet: thirteen constellations that told which nights the sea would come high and which it would come hungry. [329] She wanted to pen it on mortal skin. [337] Not on paper, which is an heirloom, and not on stone, which is a monument. [352] Skin dies with its owner; nothing written there could be confiscated as the past, because it would never become the past. [373] It would simply be a present tense that walked. [382] She meant to ink it into her own forearm with soot and cereus sap, and to set the moonstone speck as its brightest star. [406]

Then, one violet evening, a girl came through the gap in the fence. [419] She was perhaps fifteen, city-pale, with a Ministry school badge she had not bothered to remove. [435] "I don't remember the tide dancers," the girl said, which was the correct thing to say, and which no one says unless they do. [459] Marisol listened to the gaps in the sentence. [467] Inside them stood a small child on a seawall, watching a woman step into the silence after the seventh wave. [487]

"Sit," Marisol said. [490]

The girl's name was Tam. [495] She could not stay; the patrol would count her missing by morning. [507] So Marisol worked by starlight and by the eleven-minute dark, needle of thorn, ink of soot, thirteen constellations unspooling across the inside of a wrist that would go back into the city under a school sleeve. [543] Tam did not flinch. [547]

When only the last star remained, Marisol pried the speck from beneath her thumbnail. [561] A year of her mother, reduced to a grain of light. [572] She pressed it into the open point at the base of Tam's thumb and felt something she had not felt since the sand: the sensation of the ground agreeing to hold her. [604] She had believed exile was a kind of drift. [613] Instead, spontaneously, with no ceremony at all, she was anchored, not to the coast, not to the Ministry's plot, but to this: a verse leaving her, alive, on skin that would outlast her by fifty years and then vanish like a wave. [655]

"When the sea comes hungry," she said, tracing the ninth constellation, "you will know it before they do." [673] "Tell no one how." [677] "Teach it only in the gaps." [683]

Dawn greyed the moonflowers shut. [688] Tam pulled her sleeve down over the tide calendar and went to the fence, and Marisol watched her the way one watches a fledgling on the lip of a nest: braced for the drop, already certain of the wing. [727] The girl went through the gap and did not look back, which is how you fledge. [743]

Marisol stayed, rooted on the black terrace under the last stars, listening to the sea she could not see. [762] Somewhere below, the seventh wave withdrew, and in the silence after it, she knew exactly where to step. [780]
