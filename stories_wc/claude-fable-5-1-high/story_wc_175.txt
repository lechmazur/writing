Ines called herself an esoteric realist, which meant she believed in hidden orders only after she had touched their evidence. [20] A pattern was real when it left residue: pollen on a wing scale, a rut worn in stone, a stretch of silence where a rhythm used to be. [48] That was why she had climbed above the clouds. [57]

The grotto opened out of the fir slope like a held breath, its walls furred green, its floor soft with moss that drank the mist and gave back a faint, constant dripping. [89] Below, the cloud deck lay white and unbroken, hiding the valley towns and the watchers stationed in them. [107] Above, the sky was the pale blue of very early morning, though it was nearly noon. [123]

Ines had been here eleven days, which was long for her. [134] She lived transiently rooted, a season in each place, tent pegs set just deep enough to hold and shallow enough to leave no scar. [158] She had promised herself the same discipline with the mountain. [168] Everything she built would come down. [174]

What she built were signals. [179] At the lip of the grotto stood a frame of fir twigs lashed with twine, three knots at each joint, a square of orange cloth stretched across it. [207] When the clouds thinned at dusk, the watchers below could read it through their scopes: still here, still waiting. [226] A second frame, smaller, meant seen. [232] She had not built the second frame yet. [240]

The monarch they were tracking was the last one anybody had tagged, and probably the last one anybody would. [259] Its transmitter had chirped up the continent in a cadence the whole network knew by heart, three pulses and a rest, three pulses and a rest, a heartbeat drawn across two thousand miles. [292] Then, nine days ago, somewhere under the cloud deck, the cadence had broken. [305] Two pulses. [307] A long rest. [310] One pulse, then nothing for a day, then two again. [320] The others called it interference. [325] Ines called it what it was: an interrupted cadence, a creature stopping to rest more often than any of its ancestors ever had, because it was flying a route built for millions and flying it alone. [361]

On the eleventh morning she found the moth wing. [370] It lay on the moss near her boot, the size of her thumbnail, grey and umber, patterned with an eye that had never seen anything. [395] Some night creature had been eaten here, or had simply worn out. [407] She turned it over and thought of everything she knew about the moths of these mountains, which was almost nothing, because almost nothing had been written. [433] The monarchs had drawn the funding, the festivals, the painted murals in the valley towns. [448] The moths had pollinated the same firs and flowers in the dark for longer than anyone had counted, and no one had counted them. [472]

She followed the famous thing so that she could stand where the unfamous things had been, and note them, and leave a mark that said they had been noted. [501]

She cut four twigs and began the second frame. [510] Her hands knew the work; the twine went around, under, through, and she pulled each knot tight with the small patience of someone who expects to untie it later. [539] At the last joint she paused, then laid the moth wing against the twig and knotted it in, a fourth knot where protocol called for three. [565] The watchers would not see it. [571] It was not for them. [576]

She had not yet raised the frame when the light in the grotto changed. [590] Not brightened, exactly. [593] Warmed, the way a face warms. [599]

The monarch came in low over the moss, wings beating in a rhythm she recognized from nine days of broken pulses: two beats, a glide, one beat, a long glide, as if it had learned to rest inside its own flight. [640] It settled on the upright of the first frame, beside the orange cloth, and folded itself closed. [657]

Ines did not move. [661] The dripping of the walls seemed to hold. [669] The whole grotto took on a faint flush, orange thrown back off wet green, a stillness with color in its cheeks. [690]

She could have reached for the net. [697] She could have checked the tag, confirmed the number, closed the record. [709] Instead she looked at the two frames side by side, one standing, one lying in the moss with a grey wing knotted into its corner, and understood that this was the evidence she had climbed for. [745] Not the monarch alone, but the monarch and the moth, the counted and the uncounted, resting for a moment on the same temporary thing. [769]

When the butterfly lifted and went on, she raised the second frame, and left both standing one night longer than she had promised. [792]
