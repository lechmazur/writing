Storm season came to Halloway Nursery the way it always did, in three-day fits that left the greenhouse gutters roaring and the outdoor beds flattened. [25] Marit Halloway had owned the place for nineteen years and had rebuilt the east trellis after eleven of them. [44] She kept the records in a canvas notebook, each rebuild dated, each vine retied along the path she had chosen for it. [66] This was the study, or what she still called the study when nobody asked: how climbing plants behaved under guidance. [86] She had left the university to do it properly, without committees, and had done it so properly that the honeysuckle no longer surprised her at all. [112]

On the fourth morning the rain quit, and Dov came to find her in the potting shed. [129] Dov was seventeen, worked weekends, and had never in two years said a plain thing when a sideways one would do. [150]

"The moss on the east trellis has a lot more sky than it used to," he said. [167]

Marit put down her trowel. [172] "It's down." [174]

"It's resting," Dov said, which meant yes. [181]

They walked out together. [185] The trellis lay in the wet grass like a dropped ladder, its cedar slats furred green with a decade of moss, every strand of honeysuckle and clematis torn loose from the twine she had tied last spring. [222] Water still beaded on the moss and caught the thin light. [233] Marit knelt, already planning the sequence: posts first, then crossbars, then the vines lifted and bound back into their assigned lanes. [254]

Something pale lay pressed against the moss where the top rail had been. [267] She peeled it up. [271] A linden leaf, heart-shaped, lopsided at the base the way lindens always were, its edges finely toothed and its stem still green. [293]

There was no linden on the property. [300] She had made sure of that when she bought the land, because her mother's yard had been all linden, one enormous untended tree that dropped sticky honeydew on everything and was never once pruned. [334] Her mother had said a tree knew its own shape better than a woman with shears did. [351] Marit had spent a career disproving it, one trellis at a time. [363]

"Storm carried that a long way," Dov said. [371] "Nearest one I know is by the river." [379]

"Three miles." [381]

"Didn't need anybody to tie it to a post to get here, though." [394] He was looking at the clematis, not at her, which was how she knew he meant it. [411]

She wanted to argue. [415] The argument was already written in eleven years of notebook: honeysuckle went where honeysuckle was put, and the study depended on that. [437] But she turned the leaf over in her fingers and admitted, to the moss more than to Dov, that she had not actually learned anything new about climbing plants since the fourth rebuild. [470] She had learned a great deal about twine. [478]

They reconstructed the trellis that afternoon. [484] Marit reset the posts and Dov held the crossbars, and the moss, which she had always meant to scrub off, stayed where it was, because he pointed out that it had survived more storms than the cedar had. [522] Then came the part her hands had done a hundred times. [533] She lifted the first honeysuckle runner, found the lane she had planned for it, reached for the twine. [551]

And stopped. [553]

It was harder than she expected, simply setting the runner against the slats and letting go. [569] Her fingers wanted the knot. [574] She stood there with her arms at her sides feeling foolish and slightly bereaved, as if she had set down something she had been carrying so long she no longer knew its weight. [607]

"You could always tie them tomorrow," Dov said, "if they turn out to be idiots." [622]

"That is not how a study works." [629]

"No," he agreed, and went to put the twine back in the shed. [642]

She opened the notebook that evening to a fresh page and wrote the date, and then, after a long minute, wrote nothing else. [665] There was nothing yet to record. [671] She pressed the linden leaf between the pages instead. [680]

Over the following weeks, between storms, she went out every morning to the mossy trellis and did the one thing the study had never required of her, which was to watch. [711] The clematis climbed the moss itself, ignoring the slats. [720] One honeysuckle runner doubled back and wound around its own stem for no reason she could name, and she wrote that down, and underlined it. [745] The next storm took a crossbar and left the vines. [755] Marit replaced the bar and did not reach for twine, and noticed it hurt a little less than the first time, and wrote that down too, though it was not strictly about plants. [788]
