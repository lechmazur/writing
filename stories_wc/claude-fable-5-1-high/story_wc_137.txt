Ines did not believe in her aunt's theory, but she had promised to finish the transcription, so she climbed the tower anyway. [22] Marisol had built it without a roof, a stone cylinder open to the sky on a headland where the gray whales passed every autumn on their way south. [50] Ines was a marine acoustician; she recorded whale song with hydrophones and turned it into spectrograms, and she trusted what she could plot. [73] Her aunt had trusted something else. [79] Memories are entangled, Marisol used to say, the way physicists say particles are: when two people live through the same moment, each carries half of it, and neither half is whole alone. [111] Ines had loved her aunt too much to argue and doubted her too much to agree. [127] Now Marisol was three weeks dead, and the notebooks waited on the tower floor beside the tile. [144]

The tile was set into the center of the flagstones: a square of fired clay divided into nine cells, each holding a number from one to nine. [171] Every row, every column, and both diagonals summed to fifteen. [181] A magic square, Marisol had called it, and Ines had smiled at the word magic, because it was only arithmetic. [201]

The notebooks were the problem. [206] There were nine of them, each page a dated fragment, and read in order they made no sense, sentences beginning in the middle of one thought and ending in another. [236] Her cousins had wanted to burn them and sell the tower to a man who planned a roof, glass walls, and a wine bar. [260] Ines had said no, not because she believed the notebooks held anything, but because Marisol had asked her, in the hospice, with a voice like paper, to transcribe them. [289] Kindness to the dead cost only time; kindness to the cousins, who called her sentimental, cost a little courage, and she paid it. [312]

She sat on the cold floor with the laptop open and the wind pouring over the rim of the tower. [332] Far below, a whale breathed, a sound like a door closing in another house. [346] She began typing the first notebook, and on the third page she noticed that every fragment was headed not by a date alone but by a small numeral, one through nine, in Marisol's hand. [380] She looked at the tile. [385] Four, nine, two across the top; three, five, seven; eight, one, six. [397] She pulled the fragments marked four, nine, and two and laid them side by side, and the broken sentences closed like a wound stitched shut. [422] It was a pattern nobody used anymore, the kind of thing a stargazer who read old almanacs would know: the Lo Shu square, a reading order older than the alphabet. [452]

Ines worked through the rows, then the columns, then the diagonals, transcribing each completed passage. [467] The passages were about her. [472] Not the Ines who owned hydrophones, but a girl of six, the autumn her mother left, brought to this tower because there was nowhere else to put her. [500] Ines remembered none of it; that year was a locked room in her, and she had long ago stopped trying the handle. [522] Marisol had remembered all of it. [528] The girl who would not speak for eleven days. [537] The night Marisol carried her up the stairs wrapped in a blanket and laid her on this floor, on this tile, and told her to count the stars while the whales went by beneath them, singing a song too low for either of them to hear. [583] You cannot hear it, Marisol had written, but it is there, a whole symphony passing under the house, and you are part of it whether you listen or not. [612] The girl had spoken then, for the first time in eleven days, and asked how many stars made fifteen. [631]

Ines stopped typing. [634] She still did not believe in entangled memories, not as physics. [645] But she understood now what her aunt had meant: that Marisol had kept the half of that autumn Ines could not carry, kept it for thirty years, and encoded it in a pattern only this floor could unlock, so that it would be found here, in the open, under the sky, with the whales passing. [700] The tower had never been for looking at stars. [709] It was a room shaped for exactly this: a place with no ceiling, where a person could lie down and let something enormous move underneath them and finally think. [738]

Ines closed the laptop and lay back on the tile, the nine numbers cold against her spine. [755] Tomorrow she would call her cousins and tell them the tower was not for sale, that it would stay open, free to anyone who needed a floor to lie on and a sky to count. [790] Below, unheard, the whales went south. [796]
