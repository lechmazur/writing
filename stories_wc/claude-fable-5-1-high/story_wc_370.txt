The Marrow Street Reading Room closed to the public at nine, and at nine Ines Vail turned the sign, locked nothing, and stayed. [23] The room belonged to the city, and to anyone with a card; it also belonged to the Society, whose entire library was shelved in plain sight among the atlases and gardening manuals, recognizable only by a small pencil dot on page forty of each volume. [68] Nobody hides a book better than a library does. [77]

Ines had come to the job twenty years ago because it was the loneliest work she could find in a crowded city. [99] She felt people too clearly. [104] A stranger's grief on a bus would settle into her chest and stay for days; a child's boredom made her restless; a lie told two tables away tightened her own throat. [135] So she had withdrawn, not from caring but because of it, and the reading room after dark was the one place where caring did not cost her anything. [163]

At twenty to midnight the door opened anyway. [171] A girl of perhaps seventeen came in out of the rain with her fist closed around a wad of cream-colored paper. [192] Ines knew the paper before she knew the girl. [201] She knew, too, without being told, the exact temperature of the fear in the room: not fear of harm, but fear of being chosen. [225]

"I need to hide," the girl said. [232]

"From what?" [234]

"From midnight." [236] She uncrumpled the invitation enough for Ines to see the words she already knew: You have been observed to notice what others miss. [259] Come to the Marrow Street Reading Room at twelve. [268]

"I don't want to be a person that people write to," the girl said. [282] "I just want to be left alone." [289]

Ines opened the bottom drawer of the desk, where the overdue slips lived, and lifted out a second sheet of cream paper, crushed flat by two decades, its creases gone soft as cloth. [322] "I said the same thing," she said. [329] "I crumpled mine on the front step and went home, and I have thought of it every day since as the one mistake I could not take back." [357]

"Then why do you still have it?" [364]

That was the question. [368] Ines had come back a month after refusing, not to join but for a job, and the head librarian, an old woman with ink on her cuffs, had hired her without an interview. [401] Ines had spent twenty years inside the Society's walls while remaining outside its circle. [415] She had read every dotted book. [421] She had learned that the pencil marks were not a code but a conversation, generations of readers correcting one another gently in the margins, each note beginning with the same phrase: I was wrong about this. [457] The Society kept no other doctrine. [463] Its whole library was an archive of mistakes, patiently mined. [473] She had been trying, all this time, to find what was buried in her own. [488]

"Come here," she said, and led the girl into the stacks, to the dim aisle between geography and botany where the shelves stood close and the light did not reach. [518] "If you want to hide from them, this is where." [528]

"They'll look here first." [532]

"They won't look anywhere." [536] Ines pulled a volume of Alpine flora from the shelf, opened it to page forty, and touched the pencil dot. [556] "No one is coming at midnight. [562] Nobody comes. [564] You are simply meant to be in the room when it turns, and to notice what you notice." [582]

The girl looked at her, and Ines felt the fear in the aisle change shape, becoming something quieter and more curious. [603] "Then what did you get wrong?" [609]

The clock above the desk read four minutes to twelve, close enough that Ines could have reached up and stopped it. [630] She did not. [633] She understood, with the sudden wide calm of a thing seen whole, that she had never actually refused. [651] She had left the Society by staying in its house. [661] She had hidden from people by sitting at a public desk. [672] She had guarded a secret library by keeping it open to everyone. [684] Her mistake had not been crumpling the paper. [692] Her mistake was believing that refusal and acceptance were different rooms, when the Society had only ever been a way of reading, and she had been reading all along. [721]

"I thought you had to walk in through the front to belong," she said. [735] "I was wrong about this." [740]

She took a pencil from her pocket and wrote it in the margin of the Alpine flora, beneath a note in the old librarian's hand that she had read a hundred times without understanding: She will come back on her own. [781]

The clock reached midnight, and the two of them stayed. [791]
