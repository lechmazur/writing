Ilse Varga had wandered eleven years after legends other people had abandoned, and she had learned to tell the abandoned ones from the false ones by the way they sat in her stomach. [33] The Wessel barge was neither. [38] It was her grandfather's. [42] He had told her about it every summer of her childhood: a coal barge that went down in the Rhijn canal in 1911 with a lockmaster's strongbox aboard, marked forever by the brass pocket sundial he kept on his watch chain, which he swore he had pulled from the mud himself. [93] The sundial sat now in the chest pocket of her wetsuit, useless in the failing light, a small round argument she carried against the dark. [118]

The canal at twilight was thoughtfully clear, the way water gets when the barges have stopped for the day and the silt has had two hours to think about settling. [148] Ilse sat on the towpath with her fins beside her and did not go in yet. [164] She was not here for the strongbox. [171] She had stopped believing in strongboxes the winter her grandfather died and she found, among his papers, the receipt for the sundial from an antique dealer in Utrecht, dated 1974. [201] What she wanted was the architecture. [207] She wanted to understand how a lie was built so that a child could live inside it for thirty years without once bumping her head. [232]

The salvage license had taken two years. [239] The rest had taken longer, because instinct is not a thing you are given; it is a thing you train, dive by dive, until your hands know the difference between a hull rib and a drowned branch before your brain has finished asking. [282] Her grandfather had trained her instincts too, she thought. [291] He had trained them to believe. [297]

The lockkeeper's radio came on across the water, the evening bulletin, water levels and a weather warning for the coast. [317] It would play all night, the way it always did, and go silent at six when the morning program ended and the first barges began to move. [344] That silence was her deadline. [349] Whatever she was going to find, she would find it by then, or she would have found nothing, which was also an answer. [372]

She went in. [375] The cold was honest. [379] She followed the bank line east, hands out, letting the current tell her where the bottom dropped away. [397] On her fourth pass her left palm met something that was not branch and not stone, a curve too regular for the canal to have made, and her body knew before she did. [430] A hull. [432] Coal-blackened oak, split along the keel, half swallowed by a century of silt. [445]

She surfaced, breathing hard, and floated on her back while the lockkeeper's radio murmured about tides she would never see. [465] The barge was real. [469] Of course it was real. [474] That was the first beam of the architecture, and she saw it now with the flat, late clarity that only arrives after the thing is already built and lived in: a lie does not begin with a lie. [512] It begins with a true thing, a wreck anyone could check, and it rests a second true thing on top, a canal that is exactly where it was said to be, and only then does it lay the false thing across them both, a strongbox, a brass dial dredged from the mud, and by then the child is already standing inside and the roof is over her head. [580]

She dove four more times through the night. [588] There was no strongbox. [592] She had known there would not be, but knowing and having checked are different rooms, and she had needed to stand in the second one. [617] What she salvaged instead was a single iron cleat, worked loose from the gunwale, heavy as a fist. [635] Something true from the true part. [641]

She sat with it on the towpath as the sky went from black to pewter and the radio ran its morning program, farm prices, a hymn, a man reading the lock schedule in a voice like a hand on a shoulder. [682] Then, at six, silence. [686] The canal did not change. [691] The water stayed clear and calm, a heron stood in the reeds as it had stood all night, and the first barge came around the bend with no hurry at all. [722] Everything in Ilse had been rearranged, and nothing outside her showed it. [734]

She took out the pocket sundial and held it up to the first real light. [749] For the first time since she had owned it, it cast a shadow, and the shadow pointed, correctly, at six. [769] It had never been lying. [774] It had only been waiting for someone to stop using it in the dark. [788]
