Ilse Marren climbed the last switchback to the observatory with her dream ledgers strapped to her back like firewood. [19] For eleven years she had earned her living as a lucid dream chronicler, sitting beside sleepers in the valley clinics and writing down what they whispered, then teaching them to whisper back. [51] Her own dreams she had never needed to chronicle, until the temple. [63] It came every night now: a roofless hall of black stone, its walls cut with rows of bars and hollow circles, and above it a sky where certain stars swelled and faded like slow breathing. [98] She had copied the inscriptions awake, line by line, and could not read a word of them. [115]

The dome's door opened before she knocked. [122] "You're the one who wrote about the stars that breathe," said the man inside. [136] "Tomas Wehl." [138] "I map variable stars." [142] "Nobody else answered my letter." [147]

He led her up the iron stairs into the celestial mapping observatory, where the great refractor pointed through the open slit at a cold sliver of sky and every wall was papered with charts. [181] Ilse spread her copies across his desk. [188] "I want to decode them," she said. [195] "I've stopped believing they're only mine." [201]

Tomas studied the rows without speaking. [207] Then he opened his logbook and turned it toward her. [217] Between two pages lay a telegram, its paper gone soft as cloth, and pressed flat against the type was a daylily, orange faded to the color of old tea. [246] DELTA CEPHEI BRIGHT AGAIN TONIGHT STOP I KEPT YOUR WATCH STOP SLEEP NOW. [259]

"My wife sent that from the valley the night before she died," he said. [273] "I had fever, and the star was due to peak, and I couldn't bear a gap in the record." [292] "She went out into her garden and counted its brightness against the neighbors for me." [307] "The daylily was blooming beside her." [313] "Daylilies last one day." [317] "I have not missed a night since." [324]

Ilse understood then why he had answered. [331] The stars in her dream did not breathe at random; she had counted them without knowing it, and the bars beneath were the count. [355] "Show me," she said. [359]

He put her at the eyepiece. [365] Through the refractor the star was a steady yellow point, and beside it a fainter companion that he told her to hold in memory. [389] "Come back to it every hour," he said. [397] "Variables don't reveal themselves to anyone who won't wait." [406]

So they waited, the way she had waited beside a thousand sleepers. [418] Midnight settled over the dome like a hand over a lamp. [429] Between observations she asked him to describe his wife's garden, and she wrote what he said in the margin of her ledger, and when she read it back to him in her chronicler's voice, level and unhurried, something in his shoulders let go. [472] It was what she was for. [478] Sleepers woke calmer when their nights were given back to them as sentences. [491]

At three the star had dimmed; at four it was climbing again. [503] Tomas drew its curve on graph paper, the slow swell and quicker fall, and Ilse laid her first inscription beside it. [524] Five bars rising, two falling, a circle for the night the star stood at its dimmest. [540] Five days, two days, one. [545] Line after line, the same rhythm, running down the black walls of a temple older than any calendar she knew. [565]

"They were keeping a record," Tomas said quietly. [573] "Generations of them." [576]

"No one lives long enough to see a variable drift," she said. [588] "So they passed the watch on." [594] "A burden meant to be shared, or it crushes the one who carries it." [608]

He looked at the telegram and did not answer. [617]

The slit was graying. [621] She turned to the final inscription, the one she had never been able to finish copying because the dream always ended there, and understood at last what the empty space after it meant. [654] It was not a message to be decoded. [662] It was a line waiting for the next hand. [671]

"Tonight goes here," she said, and wrote it in: five bars, two, a circle. [685] "Then the next line is yours." [691]

Dawn came up behind the mountain, and Tomas Wehl, who had not slept a full night in nine years, closed the logbook on the daylily and let the shutter down. [721] "You'll keep it tonight?" he asked. [727]

"I'll keep it," Ilse said. [732] "Sleep now." [734]
