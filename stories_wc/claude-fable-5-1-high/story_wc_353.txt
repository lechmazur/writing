The hedgehog uncurled at dawn, and Wren counted that as permission. [11] It had lain against the base of the old alder all night, a tight knot of spines, while she sat three paces off and did not move. [38] Now it stretched, sniffed, and trundled into the leaf litter as though she were another root. [54] Nothing here feared her yet. [59]

The world had been silent for nine years. [67] Not quiet; silent. [70] Sound had stopped carrying, the way a river stops when its source is dammed, and no one had ever learned why. [91] Voices moved lips and nothing more. [97] The siskins still came to this alder grove each spring to feed on the cones, and still opened their beaks, and Wren had watched them do it with a grief she could not have explained to anyone. [134]

She had been a tree-song sculptor before the silence. [143] Her trade was shaping living wood so the wind could play it: coaxing a branch into a reed, hollowing a trunk into a drum that breathed. [169] She had always been symphonically minded, never hearing one voice without reaching for the others it implied, and the silence had not cured her of that. [195] It had only moved the orchestra inward, where it ached. [205]

The sculpture she had come for stood at the grove's heart, and no one alive remembered who made it. [224] An alder, long dead, had been carved in its living years into a column of open throats, each one angled toward a different quarter of the sky. [251] Lichen had taken the surface. [256] But the cracks were what she wanted. [263]

Wood remembers where it was pushed. [269] She laid her palms on the trunk and read the stress fractures the way her mother had read her face: this long split where the west wind leaned hardest, this spiderweb where two tones had fought and neither won, this clean fissure where a note had been driven so often the grain gave up. [323] Each crack was a score written by decades of weather. [333] The forgotten sculptor had tuned this tree to sing, and the tree had recorded, in its own slow breaking, exactly what it sang. [356]

Wren traced the fissures with chalk, measured their depth with a thin blade, and worked the numbers in her head until she had the intervals. [381] A fifth. [383] A slow, low fourth beneath it. [389] Something high and unsteady that must have been for the siskins. [400]

She could not make sound. [405] No one could. [408] But sound was only air being moved, and the silence had taken the air's part, not the earth's. [426] Wood still shook. [429] Ground still carried a footfall to a hedgehog's belly. [438] If she could not give the grove its music back through the ear, she would give it through the bone. [458]

From her pack she took the porcelain insulators, eleven of them, salvaged from the dead telegraph line along the old road. [479] Their glaze was the blue-white of skimmed milk. [487] Porcelain does not dampen; it hands a tremor on whole, and the old linemen had trusted it for a century to hold a wire without letting anything leak. [515] She set them into the sculpture's cracks as bridges, each one seated where the wood had already proven it could bear a note. [538] Then she ran wire from the dead alder to the living ones around it, taut as tendon, so the whole grove became one instrument strung against itself. [565]

The hedgehog had returned and was nosing at her boot. [575] Above, a siskin landed on the wire and did not startle. [586]

Wren waited until the light was level, then knelt, pressed one hand flat to a root so she would feel what they felt, and strummed. [611]

Nothing came to her ears. [616] But the root under her palm woke. [623] The fifth went down through the dead alder into the soil and came up through the living ones, and the porcelain held every part of it and let none of it leak. [655] The hedgehog stopped. [658] It lifted its head, and she watched the tremor reach it, watched its spines lie flat rather than rise. [677] On the wire the siskin shifted its feet, once, twice, in time. [689]

She strummed the fourth beneath it and felt the alders answer through her knees. [703] This was not the music she had lost. [711] It was smaller and slower, and it lived in the body instead of the air, moving from root to spine to bone the way understanding moves. [737] But a hedgehog was hearing it with its feet, and a bird that had forgotten its own song was keeping time, and somewhere under the lichen a stranger's dead tree was singing again the only way it had left. [776]

Wren kept her hand on the root and played until the hedgehog, unafraid, fell asleep against it. [793]
