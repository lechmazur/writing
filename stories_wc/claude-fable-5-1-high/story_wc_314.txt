Behind the waterfall, the light never agreed with itself. [9] It came through the falling water split into violets and ambers and greens that slid over the orchard like slow fish, so that the same apple could be gold one moment and bruised blue the next. [45] Ilse had measured it for eleven years and still could not say what color anything truly was. [62]

This bothered her more than she admitted. [69] She was an alchemist, and alchemists were supposed to prefer what could be weighed. [83] She kept her auburn hair bound back with wire so it would not drift into the crucibles, kept her notebooks ruled and dated, kept her voice flat when she spoke to the trees. [116] That she spoke to the trees at all was a thing no one knew. [130] No one knew, either, about the cracked spectacles in her apron pocket, which had belonged to old Teodor and which she had never once considered mending because mending them would mean they were only spectacles. [165]

Teodor had found the orchard, and Teodor had found the crystal. [176] It sat now on a slab of slate at the center of the oldest trees: a drop of dew the size of a plum, frozen, he had insisted, on the first morning the world ever had. [212] Ilse had tested it. [216] It did not melt in summer, did not crack under a hammer, did not register on any scale she owned, and yet when she held it her palms remembered being a child in wet grass. [251] She recorded this as "anomalous warmth" and underlined nothing. [260]

The Work had three stages, and she had completed two by arithmetic alone. [273] The third, the final transformation, required flame, applied to the crystal at a moment Teodor's notes described only as "when the orchard breathes in." [297] She had tried to convert this into hours. [305] She had charted the waterfall's pulse, the soil's temperature, the angle of the fractured light. [320] Four times she had lit the burner on schedule, and four times the crystal had simply sat there, colder than before, as if disappointed in her. [346]

Teodor had watched starlings. [350] Every dusk a flock came over the ridge and poured itself through the spray, and he would stand with his head tipped back and his ruined glasses fogging, saying nothing, and afterward he would know things he could not have known. [391] She had called it superstition to his face. [399] He had laughed and said that logic was a lantern and intuition was the dark it was carried through, and that neither was much use without the other. [427] She had written that down too, in the back of a notebook where no one would look. [444]

On the fifth evening she did not consult the charts. [454] She set the burner beside the slate, struck it to a low blue tongue, and turned her face to the ridge. [475] The starlings came as they always came, a smoke of small bodies, and passed through the waterfall's light so that the whole flock flushed rose and then jade and then the color of old coins. [510] Ilse made herself stop counting them. [516] Instead she watched the shape. [521]

It folded, thinned, and swelled like something taking a breath; and she understood, with the part of her that had never stopped being a child in wet grass, that the breath was not a metaphor. [556] The orchard was very old, and everything in it was arriving for the first time, and the flock was showing her where those two facts met. [582] The starlings drew inward to a single dense knot above the slate. [594] The trees leaned. [597] The waterfall's colors slowed, hung, held. [603] Ilse lifted the flame and touched it to the crystal, not with the steadiness of a technician but with the hand she had used, as a girl, to touch the frost on a window to see if it would notice her. [644]

It noticed. [646] The dew did not boil. [651] It let go, all at once, the way a held thing does when it is finally asked kindly, and ran off the slate in a single bright thread into the roots of the oldest tree. [686] The starlings burst apart and were gone over the ridge. [696] Above her, on a branch that had not blossomed in Teodor's lifetime or hers, a bud split open, white and wet, smelling of a morning nobody had ever used before. [726]

Ilse stood a long time with the dead burner in her hand. [738] Then she took out Teodor's spectacles, wiped the spray from the cracked lenses with her sleeve, and put them on, though they were not her prescription and made the orchard swim. [769] In her notebook, that night, she wrote the date, the temperature, the duration of the flame. [785] Under it, in the same careful hand, she wrote: it noticed me. [797]
