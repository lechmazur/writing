The physician's letter arrived on the spring supply boat, folded between a sack of lentils and a crate of lamp oil. [21] Ines read it twice on the jetty, then tucked it into the back of her botanical diary, where she kept the pressed leaves that had not yet been identified. [50] Six months, the letter said. [55] She did not intend to rest. [61]

For thirty-one years she had been caretaker of Saltcairn, the island temple whose orchard grew inside a ring of living stone pillars. [83] Pilgrims called the pillars miraculous; the Wardens on the mainland called them structurally uncertain and forbade anyone to touch them. [103] Ines knew what they actually were, because she had spent three decades measuring them. [117] Her diary held the proof in columns of patient ink: pillar girths in spring and autumn, the height of the oak canopy, the slow creep of the stone toward wherever a branch had leaned the year before. [154] The stone followed the trees. [159] The pillars were not built; they were grown, the way a hedge is grown, and the temple's founders had known exactly how to steer them. [184]

She had found the method by accident, twelve years earlier, when a storm split an old oak and exposed a green-crusted thread buried in its heartwood: copper wire, wound in a spiral, pointing at the nearest pillar like a finger. [224] Wherever the founders had wrapped copper around a branch, the stone had reached for it and hardened there, and the temple had taken its shape from the argument between metal, wood, and mineral. [257] That was the architectural secret the Wardens' surveys had never found. [268] It was also, she decided on the jetty, why she would not spend her last half-year sitting quietly. [286]

The temple had been shaped for people. [293] Its arches sat at the height of a tall man's reach; its niches held candles and nothing else. [311] Yet Ines had spent thirty-one years sharing the orchard with swifts that nested in the pillar cracks, with bats that wintered in the eastern colonnade, with the bees whose hive the pilgrims complained about every summer. [347] They were the temple's true congregation, and the building ignored them. [358] She would remodel it. [362]

She began the next morning, gently systematic, the way she had always done everything. [376] She numbered the pillars on a fresh page of the diary and beside each one wrote what it should become. [396] The northern pillars would lean outward and roughen, so the swifts could nest without being scraped away by the spring cleaning. [417] The eastern colonnade would grow a deep hood where the bats could hang above the height of any broom. [436] The southern arch, where the main path entered, would drop by two hand-spans, so that every pilgrim who came after her would have to bow to the hive on the way in. [468]

She stripped the copper from the temple's broken lightning rod and from a coil the last caretaker had left in the boathouse. [490] Then, one tree at a time, she climbed the oaks and wrapped the wire around the branches she wanted the stone to follow, winding it in the same spiral the founders had used, checking each turn against her drawings. [529] Her hands shook more each week; she wrote that down too, in the margin, with the date. [546]

The summer boat brought a Warden. [552] He stood under the southern arch and stared at the copper glinting in the canopy. [567] "You have been told not to interfere with the pillars," he said. [579]

"I have not touched a pillar," Ines said, and offered him tea. [591]

"You are altering the temple." [596]

"I am pruning oaks," she said. [602] "Caretakers prune." [604]

He threatened to have her removed. [610] She told him, pleasantly, that she would be removed by December regardless, and that he was welcome to file the paperwork if it comforted him. [635] He left on the same tide, and the swifts screamed over his boat like an escort. [651]

By autumn the stone had begun to answer. [659] Not much: a thumb's width of new lean on the northern pillars, a fresh rind of roughness where the swifts had already begun to test it. [685] The southern arch had dropped less than a finger, but the wire was set, and the stone would keep following the oak long after anyone remembered why. [712] That was the point. [716]

Ines recorded the measurements in her usual hand, a little larger now. [728] On the final page she wrote instructions for whoever came next: where the copper was, what each pillar was meant to become, and how many years the bowing arch would take. [759] She added one line beneath, underlined once: The building belongs to everyone who lives in it. [775] Then she closed the diary, set it on the altar, and went out to check the wire. [792]
