Ada Marsh taught probability to undergraduates who believed luck was a personality trait, and she corrected them gently, the way you correct a child's grip on a spoon. [28] Her father, Sol, had believed it too, and had spent forty years proving it with other people's rent. [46] After the heart attack, he lay in a ward that smelled of lemon disinfectant and asked her for only one thing. [67] "Ride the Green Line," he said. [73] "Read the seats." [76] "I left you a route." [81]

She wanted to refuse. [85] Ada was rebellious the way a theorem is rebellious: on paper, elegantly, with no consequences for anyone. [102] She had lectured for a decade on the gambler's fallacy without once placing a bet, dating a stranger, or driving above the limit. [125] Still, she had told her department that her summer project was to widen her students' perspectives responsibly, to teach risk from the inside rather than from the blackboard. [153] She could hardly do that from her office. [161]

So she rode. [164]

The scrawls were there, in silver marker on the blue plastic, small enough to pass for vandalism. [181] Sol had always written his bets in a private shorthand, and she knew it the way other daughters knew a father's handwriting on birthday cards. [206] "3:1 she gets off at Cass." [212] She did not get off at Cass, and felt, absurdly, that she had won something. [227] Two stations later: "Even money she's angry." [234] She was, and she laughed, and a man across the aisle moved his bag closer to his knees. [252]

Each seat gave a direction disguised as a wager. [261] "5:2 she transfers at Grand, 20:1 she reads the whole thing." [272] She traversed the city that way through the long light of a midsummer evening, following her father's odds like a woman reading her own horoscope aloud in a voice meant to mock it. [305] The last scrawl said only: "Library. Bring light." [313]

Rain came as she climbed out at Woodward, a warm sideways rain that made the pavement smell of iron and cut grass. [335] The public library had been closed for months, its budget lost to a bond measure Sol had probably bet on. [355] Clover had taken the courtyard, thick and wet and blooming white, and the side door stood open the width of a hand. [377]

She had brought light because he had asked, and because the only light she owned that did not need a wall was his. [400] The candle lantern had a single pane of mica, cloudy and gold, salvaged from some poker room that ran on generators. [421] He had carried it to every game for luck and had lost anyway, which he called proof that luck was real, since it so clearly favored other people. [449]

Inside, the rain ran down the tall windows and the stacks breathed damp paper. [463] She lit the candle, and the mica turned the flame into something softer than fire, a light you could look at directly without flinching. [487] The mathematics section was where she knew it would be. [497] On the study carrel, in silver, he had written his last line: "Even odds she comes, and I'm taking the under." [518]

Beneath the carrel was a library copy of her own textbook, overdue by two years, its margins crowded with his handwriting. [539] He had worked every problem. [544] He had gotten most of them right. [551] Beside the chapter on expected value he had written: "I understand this, I have always understood this, and understanding is not the trouble." [574]

Ada sat down on the wet-smelling carpet with the lantern between her knees. [587] This was what she had never been able to teach: that a person could hold the true odds in one hand and the bet in the other, and see both with perfect clarity, and choose the bet. [624] Her father was not confused. [629] He was a lucid contradiction, a man who could compute his ruin to the second decimal and walk toward it whistling. [650] And she was his mirror, equally lucid, equally contradictory: a woman who knew exactly how safe her life was and could not forgive herself for it. [676]

The rain eased. [679] Through the window the clover shone in the last light, all those three-leaved certainties and, somewhere among them, the odd fourth leaf nobody would find. [704] She took out her phone and called the ward. [713] "I read the whole thing," she said, when he picked up, his voice thin as the mica. [730] "You owe me twenty to one." [736]

He laughed until it hurt him, and she listened to it hurt, and did not tell him to stop. [755] In the fall she would bring the lantern to her first lecture and set it on the desk, lit, against every fire code the university owned. [781] A small bet, badly placed, in full knowledge of the odds. [792]
