Elias pressed the chisel into the granite with deliberate pressure, feeling the stone resist before yielding. [16] The ancient greenhouse loomed around him at dusk, its iron framework rusted and draped with ivy, the glass panes cracked in patterns that resembled frozen lightning against the darkening sky. [46] He was a thoughtful gravestone carver, fiercely meditative in his concentration, each strike of his hammer a prayer against the entropy of forgetting. [69] Outside, beyond the broken walls, the monarch butterflies had begun their annual migration, orange clouds rising from the meadows, drifting inside to roost among the orchids and ferns that had gone wild decades ago. [103] His motivation had crystallized three winters past, when the town had suffered a collective loss so vast that the cemetery could not hold the names; he had taken it upon himself to discover the shape of collective grief by carving monuments that did not honor individuals but rather the spaces between mourners, the silence after the eulogy, the weight of a shared meal where no one spoke. [170] Tonight, as the sky bruised purple and the greenhouse filled with the scent of damp earth and dying blooms, he worked on the final piece of his series. [198] From his leather apron, Elias withdrew a woven ribbon roll, its crimson and gold threads catching the last amber light. [218] He unrolled it across the central potting bench, letting the silk length snake between half-finished headstones like a river of blood and sunlight. [241] The ribbons marked his progress, each section representing a different stage of mourning he had observed: the initial shock, the anger that followed, the strange peace of acceptance. [269] He had placed them during the early days of the butterfly migration, and now, as the insects settled for the night in whispering thousands, he felt the atmosphere shift, becoming electric and utterly still. [303] "Retrace your steps," said a voice that seemed to come from the granite itself, soft yet resonant. [320] Elias lowered his hammer. [324] He knew this spectral mentorship well; since the death of his teacher Mara, she had appeared to him in this greenhouse, her form composed of dust motes and butterfly wings, teaching him that stone was merely paper for the stories of the dead. [367] She had been a poet who carved epitaphs, and she understood that grief required architecture, blueprints drawn in sorrow. [386] "The ribbons will guide you," Mara continued, her translucent hand gesturing toward the winding path of silk. [403] "You must retrace the path you laid when the migration began, remembering how the town first gathered to mourn." [422] Elias nodded, understanding. [457] It was their family custom, maintained across death itself: trading favorite poems at dinner. [496] Tonight, as the dusk deepened into violet shadow, Mara recited a poem about the weight of silence, and Elias responded with verses he had carved into the town's new memorial, lines about how grief binds strangers together. [533] "You are learning the geometry," Mara said, her voice like wind through broken glass. [547] "Collective grief is not a stone dropped into water, but the ripples themselves, touching every shore." [563] Across the butterfly migration, which spanned six weeks of autumn, Elias returned to the greenhouse each dusk with fierce meditative focus. [756] Together, they tied the woven ribbon roll around the stone's base, the bright silk contrasting with the dark granite, marking it as sacred ground.
