The last house on my back belonged to my wife, who had taken the wheels off it.  She sat on the step shelling peas while the rest of our town floated a cable's length away on barges.  I came up from the dock with the wheels, one spinning on either wrist.  People used to come down just to watch me work a cargo sling, never guessing how easy it is to look graceful when the ground under your feet is also yours to move.  Edith put a pea in my mouth and told me where I could stick the wheels. 

She had known what I was since we were girls collecting crabs along the sublittoral fringe, our chins barely clearing the water.  Once she drove a spade into the sand and the little girl beside her yelped.  Long before that, a pirate had wintered on me and cut my likeness into a strip of whalebone, the only portrait for which I can be said to have sat still.  On the reverse he had scratched the terms of his tenancy: one winter, no fires in the grass, leave when the island asks.  He left as promised; it was the people who came after him, with their splendid deeds and seals, who staked me through the fins. 

Then came that queer morning when violence became impossible everywhere, and the stakes slipped out of me like pins out of an old woman's hair.  All over the world knives still cut bread but turned in the hand that meant them for flesh.  I stayed another forty years. 

Edith had the pirate's scrimshaw in her lap now, its grooves black from the thumbs of all the councilors who had passed it round last winter.  They had come fully prepared to argue that an island had no business disappearing after so many people had put in plumbing.  Then the oldest of them had read aloud those few civil words from a cutthroat, and the youngest had begun to laugh, and somebody had asked me what it felt like to be staked.  I told them, and by morning we had a plan for the barges.  That bit of bone could open any heart in town except the one I wanted. 

"You told them how it hurt," Edith said, biting through a pod that would not split.  "You never told me why you stayed once it stopped hurting." 

I started on the wheels again, though the rising tide had already set the float knocking against the step.  Out beyond the shelf my sisters were assembling, invisible even from the highest roof, their great blunt heads inclined toward the dark.  Each spring I had felt them pass and let Edith think they were going somewhere I no longer cared to go. 

"I liked having hands," I said.  "I liked being the one you sent for when there was something too heavy for anybody on this miserable rock to lift." 

"And now?" 

By way of answer I took her hand and laid it on the step between us.  We felt it together, the note rising through leagues of black water, a sound so low it seemed at first a failure of our own two pulses.  By the call of a whale from the deep I knew my sisters still had a place for me between them, though I was grown over with potatoes and had forgotten how to sound. 

"I want my weight back," I said.  The ground shivered with the effort it took not to say I was sorry. 

Edith tipped the peas into her apron and put her shoulder to mine, and together we rolled the house onto its float.  She made me detach the painter myself.  "None of your noble business about how I let you go," she said. 

I kissed the salt crease under her nose and slipped off the float, leaving my apron and its two good hands folded on the step.  At first there was only the tickle of the potatoes washing loose, then the delicious ache of my fins taking hold of water.  When I rolled my eye toward daylight I saw the little dark bottoms of the barges, and one small shower of peas came pelting down.  That was my wife all over, to hear the first song I had sung in centuries and throw vegetables.
