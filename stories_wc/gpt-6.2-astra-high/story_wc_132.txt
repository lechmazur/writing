On the first dry morning of storm season, Nell Voss went out to the mossy trellis behind her nursery and found the town linden lying across it.  The rain had washed the earth off its roots, which stood up taller than her delivery truck and held, among other things, a blue glass marble.  She pocketed the marble before anybody could tell her it belonged to the town. 

For a hundred summers the tree had been trained over the trellis to make a long green room, open at both ends, where couples came to be photographed after their weddings.  Nell's own photograph was still in the cash drawer, although the young man in it had been gone for twenty years.  By noon the mayor had brought her a photograph of his parents and a purchase order with the amount left blank. 

"Can you reconstruct the room?" he asked. 

"You can still see where your mother put her heel through the hem," Nell said, which was her way of saying she understood what he was buying. 

She had bought the nursery to study the things plants did when nobody made them do anything, a purpose she had somehow managed to put off for decades.  Her bread and butter was getting six hundred privets to do exactly the same thing by Thursday.  Now she drew up a plan for steel uprights and a dozen matched young lindens, and called her daughter Kit to help clear the wreck. 

Kit brought a saw and a small boy who had never known his grandfather except as a man looking slightly startled at one end of a green room.  While the women worked, he collected things to float down the gutter that ran beside the trellis.  He came back carrying a linden leaf as big as his face, with one edge scalloped out by a hungry mouth. 

"I pulled and pulled," he said, showing Nell the torn place where the stalk ought to have been. 

All the leaves on the fallen crown had gone limp as wet handkerchiefs, but the ragged green heart in his fist was stiff enough to sail.  She asked him for a tour of his shipyard. 

Out beyond the last upright, where the trellis had sagged years ago and let its burden touch the ground, a spray of leaves stood level with his knees.  Nell got down beside it and used the blue marble to rub wet grit off a length of buried bark without cutting it.  Little reddish roots came away twined around her fingers; the branch had been feeding itself long before the trunk went over.  Together they found five such places, none of them anywhere near the straight lines on her plan. 

She could lift the rooted limbs and set them where the uprights were to go, if she was willing to lose a few finding out how far their roots ran.  Kit waited with the saw while her mother measured the distance twice, then stood for a long time with the metal tape stretched tight between her hands. 

"I used to wonder what these things would get up to behind my back," Nell said, and let the tape go rattling home. 

That afternoon they cut away only the dead weight, and the purchase order went back with a bill for labor and five little wire guards against rabbits.  The mayor came out to look at the crooked stumps and said he couldn't see where anybody was going to stand. 

"Your father never stood where the photographer put him either," she said, and he stayed to help her fork mulch. 

It took Nell three summers to quit carrying the tape in her apron, and five for the limbs to begin to look less like pieces of a wreck than trees with opinions of their own.  She kept a record of which way each crown leaned into the available light, on the backs of order forms she no longer had much use for.  By the time her grandson brought somebody there to be photographed, the green room had become a succession of smaller rooms, each opening a little farther from the road.  The first picture she took was the one he asked for; the second caught them both looking up, trying to see where one tree ended and the next began.
