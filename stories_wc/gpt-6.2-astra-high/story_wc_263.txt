In Vey, if you woke with an idea you could not afford to follow, you could take it down to the harbor and put it in the light.  A woman with six children had put a whole opera there, meaning to come back for it when the youngest could butter his own bread.  He was the harbor pilot now, and on foggy nights he steered by the light of his mother's drowned soprano. 

His daughter Nel kept the lamp under the aurora-lit archway that bridged the harbor mouth.  She was a moonstruck dreamer in a family that could find a channel by the taste of mud, but she had one useful faculty: she heard unspoken words.  People came to the arch quite unable to say what they had in mind, and Nel could coax the bright stuff out between their lips.  Remembered stardust, her grandmother called it, on the theory that matter never quite forgot having once been light. 

The bargain carved in the arch said ON LOAN, but nobody living knew how to give anything back.  Each time Nel dusted the lamp she could hear her grandmother trying a high note, getting it wrong in precisely the same beautiful way.  At home the old woman sat tearing strips off a loaf, which was about all the music her hands remembered how to make.  One morning she slid the bread knife between Nel's fingers and thought, quite distinctly, Before I die, I want to change the ending.  That was a worse thing to hear than a plea for help; until then Nel had thought it was a finished opera. 

She took down the tracking telescope that the first keepers had used before there was enough borrowed brilliance to steer by.  The barrel swung on a brass axle, and under the axle a roll of black paper unreeled across a drum.  By tracing the path of falling stars with the telescope, she could make its little silver stylus score their courses on the moving paper.  Night after night she worked across an unwritten calendar, advancing the drum one notch at dawn and wondering why the man who cut ON LOAN had signed it with a falling star. 

All the tracks led back to a single point, as the old star books said they should, but the point was not in the sky.  It was three feet above the lamp.  Nel had been charting sparks that got out.  The stars she had followed were falling inland, over the roofs of the town, and when she looked again at the scratches she recognized one particular roof.  She ran home and found her grandmother at the kitchen window with her mouth open to the dark and crumbs shining on her tongue.  A single note came out of her, pitched too high for comfort, and then the old woman laughed and took it down a third. 

In the morning Nel asked her father which nights the harbor light could rest.  None, he said, and what he did not say was that he had heard his mother singing from the stairs and had stood there barefoot, afraid to break the spell.  The tide tables lay between them on the desk.  He put his brown thumb on the hour of lowest water, when even a rowing boat would stick between the piers. 

"Give me that much," she said. 

That night he took his boat out early and lay across the channel with a storm lantern swinging from his mast.  Nel turned the great reflector on its back, so that instead of flinging its catch out to sea it opened like a palm beneath the sky.  Then she let the lamp rest. 

The arch went black against the green silk writhing overhead, and for one sick instant she thought she had spent everything and given nothing back.  Then the roofs began to tick as if the town were a hot pan under the first drops of rain.  Not all the sparks found anybody; some of the houses they fell on had been empty for a hundred years.  Out of one such house came a marvelous smell of quince, and then it was gone. 

When the tide turned there was still enough light left for her father to bring his boat in by, though it was a near thing.  After that he kept the hour of slack water for her, and the dark interval traveled round the clock with the moon.  Nel never did hear the opera as it had been written in the lamp.  The woman who had once meant to end it on a high sustained note now gave the last line to a boy whose voice was breaking.  Each night it broke somewhere new.
