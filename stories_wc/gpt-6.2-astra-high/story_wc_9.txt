On my daughter's ninth birthday I got out the good linen and found three hands I could not account for.  In our family we traced hands on birthday tablecloths instead of notching a doorpost that somebody might paint or sell.  I knew the splayed pink star of Nell at five; I even remembered how the soap had stung a paper cut when I washed her fingers afterward.  But here were six in green, seven in yellow, eight in the expensive black I ground from onyx for my cards. 

"You said not to touch that bottle," Nell said, "but that year there wasn't anybody else to draw around me." 

After the birth of my second child, when the midwife had carried him away without once asking his name, I had dealt myself a mercy.  An onyx-ink cartomancer can turn a card face down on an hour she cannot bear and go on stirring porridge or folding sheets until it passes.  I had meant to lose a winter.  I hadn't counted on how often a pot lid falling sounds like the little shovel striking a stone. 

Nell took my pencil to trace her ninth hand, and this time I noticed the white seam across the pad of her thumb. 

"I kept what did it," she said, with the weary readiness of somebody answering a question twice.  From her dolls' tea set she brought a saucer with a crack the shape of a swallow's tail.  "I made you tea," she said, "and when you didn't drink it I tried to break the whole service." 

She had been six then, or seven; for the first time in my professional life I was willingly perplexed, setting aside the deck that always gave me an answer and asking her to tell me more. 

"The tea ran uphill," she said.  A drop had climbed out along the crack and gone skittering up the window, black as a beetle, and every time she filled the saucer in my absence the same thing happened.  We had between us a question worth the asking: if I had stayed in the kitchen and so had she, where had the time gone? 

At dusk we took the saucer and a flask of cold tea out into the steep streets of our town.  Wherever the road forked Nell let a drop go, and we followed its little lacquered back by lamplight even after the road gave out among the pines. 

In a midnight glade above the clouds we came to birches so laden with shining drops their tips brushed the grass.  The beauty of those trees took me by the throat; I had to unfasten my collar before I could lean close enough to see what made them shine.  In each bead a kitchen window burned, and in each window a woman with my capable hands got on with the business of keeping a small person fed.  From inside one came the workmanlike sound of crockery being smashed, a pause between blows in case I might yet look up. 

"There's my birthday," Nell said, pointing past it to a bead in which a small black hand held perfectly still while its mate drew a shivering line. 

I thought the proper penance was to take every drop on my tongue and swallow three years of my daughter's life in one bitter draught.  Already I was opening my mouth to the branch when Nell cupped her hand underneath my chin. 

"That's just how you made it go fast the first time," she said. 

So my cloudbound repentance began with an amateur's lesson in how slowly a minute actually goes.  I sat beside her in the wet grass and watched a child struggle to draw around her own wrist, a feat the fingers are not made for.  When she managed it at last, she raised the cloth to show me, and the woman in the window took it from her and shook the crumbs out.  The drop fell without a sound. 

"Enough for tonight," Nell said, and we went down together carrying nothing heavier than a cracked saucer and our empty flask.  There would be another night for the breaking of the cups, if she wanted; meanwhile the ninth hand was still to trace. 

At our table I steadied the linen and let her arrange her fingers just as she liked, even the crooked one with the scar.  When she said it tickled I eased up on the pencil, and the line went a little faint but it never broke.
