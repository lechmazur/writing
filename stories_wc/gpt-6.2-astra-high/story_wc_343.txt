The first thing Nell learned as a map apprentice was never to wave back at a light.  Over the drowned salt works, whole afternoons of stranded luminance hung in the air, and a man could spend his life waving to a woman who had once hung a shirt on a line.  Still, Nell waved when nobody was looking. 

She had gone aboard the antique airship Minnow to chart the next great discovery, preferably something that might wave back.  The survey office wanted a cheaper thing: one last circuit to certify that the salt towns were empty, after which the Minnow's brass could be sold by weight.  At supper the crew passed Nell a little dish of mustard for her cold beef and told her which bits of the ship they would buy.  Nobody wanted the schoolroom, not even for the stove. 

Once the Minnow had carried the children of the salt gangs, who moved too often from pan to pan to send them to school ashore.  In the schoolroom a swiveling mirror and a lens projected the country slipping by onto the floor, so a child doing sums could see his father's roof pass under his slate.  Nell ate there alone on duty, with a speaking tube at her ear through which the cook sometimes sent the sound of himself chewing.  It was his opinion that nobody should have to eat in silence. 

She was diligently curious even about dust, and when the first starling murmurations of autumn darkened the lens, she took her pencil out instead of her broom.  On the dusty hopscotch board beneath the lens, minute scratches showed up wherever the wheeling birds took the glare away.  By noting their secret patterns, the little runs of three and four incisions beside particular squares, she began to see what the children had done.  The mirror handle had a catch, worn smooth, at just the angle that laid the long embankment down the spine of the game.  One house to a square, and in every square a pattern of long and short scratches, like the nicknames sailors whistled to one another in thick fog. 

She asked up the tube if anybody remembered how the children had played.  After a while the cook came down with his mother's phrasebook, a polyglot's jumble of dockside Greek, salt French and pictures for things no two gangs called the same thing.  The phrasebook's one dog-ear marked a drawing of a child tipping a hand mirror toward an upper window.  Underneath, in the cook's own childish fist, he had penciled the rule that needed no translation until you grew up and forgot it.  Call me by my name and I will call you by yours. 

His nickname, he said, had been Dumpling, two long flashes and a short.  He had not heard it since his mother took him off the pans.  He set one floury finger on square seven and left it there. 

Nell swung the survey lamp out over the rail and flashed the name scratched into square seven at the seventh roof, where a pale figure eternally shook a rug.  Nothing happened to the rug.  Then from a patch of empty water well to the left of the house came two long flashes and a short.  The cook sat down on the dusty board so hard his teeth clicked. 

Where she had seen water, Nell could now make out a low raft tarred the color of wet mud, with a stovepipe at one end.  She had been looking so hard for roofs that she had never thought to look for wheels of onions drying flat on wire, or wakes spreading slowly out from anchor ropes.  By dusk they had hailed nine rafts, and in the dark the rafts hailed one another, passing news of Dumpling clear to the edge of sight.  Nobody down there wanted taking off; what they wanted, spelled out with some impatience for these thickheaded people in the sky, was mail. 

Nell had meant to put her own name on something large and permanent.  Instead she borrowed a clean sheet and began to note the names by which one moving household might hail another, and the winter moorings where the Minnow might deliver a sack of letters.  That winter the mail sack lay in the schoolroom, and every time the cook sent chewing noises down the tube Nell sent back the sound of a letter getting its stamp.
