On Eel Island you had to be twelve to carry a light for the dead, old enough, the reasoning went, to set your own sleeve on fire and put it out again.  At eleven, Nell had already spent two lantern walks in the kitchen with the other little ones while somebody else carried her mother's light.  This year she put a green lump of sea glass on her grandfather's workbench and said she meant to share light without using fire. 

He put down his file.  "Who told you where she kept that?" 

"Nobody," said Nell, a little hurt, because she had found it herself.  It was the bull's-eye from a bottle bottom, not much to look at until you held it up and found a whole green afternoon inside. 

Her grandfather turned it in his fingers, and presently he fetched a shallow drawer that smelled of the soap her mother used to cut with a wire.  Under the soap lay a dozen glass leaves, each bound about its waist with a fine copper belt.  "I thought she meant to make earrings," he said. 

Two and two they might have been earrings, but Nell, an earthbound star watcher who knew the sky chiefly from its quivering likeness in the tidal flats, spread them out as the stars of the Swan.  At the sight of that long neck her grandfather took a breath that whistled like a kettle.  "She used to lie down on the quay to see it come over the roof," he said.  "Lord, the backs of her dresses." 

It was the first time in two years he had remembered his daughter out loud as anything but a woman in a boat that did not come back. 

Nell spent the week at his wheel, learning the gem polishing he did for the summer people, who liked to wear bits of island on their wrists.  She kept one face of the bottle bottom rough, so that whatever light went in would come out soft enough to look at.  For the Swan's bones they stripped willow switches and hung them one below another on loops of her mother's copper wire.  To balance the wings they had to weigh each leaf with its belt on, and once her grandfather made her weigh a knot.  Halfway through the work he remembered that her mother always laid her pliers in her lap, and the next day he remembered how often they fell.  By Friday he could do the noise she made when they landed on her toes. 

On the morning of the walk they took the bird down to the serpentine grotto in mist so thick that the children coming along behind sounded like children in another country.  The walk would start there at dusk, when the big ones lit their wicks and the little ones were marched away.  Nell hung her bird where the first bend put the pale wet limestone in shadow, but even when the mist thinned nothing shone except the copper belts.  Somebody asked if that was all. 

Her grandfather felt along the rock until his wrist went gold in the light from a cleft above them.  "Used to come here with her," he said, and stood with his wrist in the light while Nell moved the hook. 

Now the limestone was reflectively vibrant, flecked with green from the bottle and the faint blue that in its first life had been somebody's medicine.  A breath from the sea unfolded the wings, and on the wall the bright feathers drifted apart, came together, drifted apart again.  Only the green heart hung quiet, a pearl of stillness around which the swan continually came to pieces and made itself anew.  One of the kitchen children blew on it and ducked as his own small gust came back to him in the form of a wing sweeping over his head.  Until the sun moved on they took turns stirring the air to let the light come to rest upon somebody else. 

At dusk her grandfather offered Nell the lantern he had carried for her mother, and for a moment she felt the old fierce want of it.  Then a little boy tugged her cuff and asked where the bird would go when the dark came. 

"Nowhere," Nell said, taking him by the hand and leading him out to the flats to wait for the other one.
