The first thing Ada asked for after the reincarnation memory was a bolt for her door.  The custodian explained that it was not a door but the glass front of a case, and that its hinges were on his side.  He spoke gently, as if it were the hinges that had refused her. 

The tidal library stood knee deep in the inland sea of a high plateau, a mile above the clouds.  Twice a day water climbed through holes in its floor and lapped the undersides of the shelves.  The water deposited a skin of salt that was atomically reflective; it could give back, atom for atom, the last living thing to touch the object it coated. 

Ada had come back from a brass sundial, her own little joke on the scholars who had expected an astronomer.  She had polished the dial at a lighthouse in Orkney for sixpence a week.  It was her distinction to have been a contemporary of the great auk; nobody had yet asked what sixpence bought.  She knew the auk well enough to disappoint the people who wanted to hear its song.  It had made a noise like a man having trouble with his boot. 

At low tide she went back into the brass, usually in the middle of answering somebody.  She could stand the solitude in there; what wore on her was that there was nothing she could do with it, not even finish a thought.  At the lighthouse she had sometimes gone days without company, but those had been days in which she could take a boat apart and put it back together.  Here she had the apart without the putting together. 

The custodian dreaded what he called her brittle awakenings, when the rising water struck cold brass and she came up crazed like an old plate.  One morning a child saw her pressing the two halves of her wrist together and began to cry.  That was the morning he finally opened the case, meaning to put a screen around her, and Ada walked past him to look at the plumbing. 

The cases stood on open trays; all the water that came in at flood went out again at ebb. 

"Give me a length of hose," she said, "and that bucket you keep for when you're seasick." 

He got them for her, although he drew the line at taking the dial out of its mount.  She had him listen for the turn of the tide, which in that country you could tell even in thick weather by the hum of an ancient stone circle.  Its feet were drowned on the flood; as the hollow stones emptied, one after another began to sing.  At the first bass note she filled the bucket and hung it above the dial, then sucked on the hose until the bitter water came.  By pinching the hose she let just enough through to keep the brass wet while the sea slipped away under the floor. 

The custodian stayed beside her all through the scale, his hands cupped loosely beneath her wrists.  When the last stone sounded he was holding nothing, and she was standing there rolling down her sleeves. 

"I suppose you'll want to go ashore now," he said. 

She looked across the water at the empty boathouse inside the ring of stones.  Light fell there long after the library was in the shadow of the ridge.  It was a good place for a person with a sundial to keep appointments of her own. 

"I didn't say I wanted to stop seeing people," she said. 

At the trustees' meeting the custodian had a drawing to show how a boathouse and a reading room might coexist under one roof.  Ada had drawn it herself, down to the little arc that showed which way the door between the rooms would swing.  The trustees wanted to know what guarantee they had that she would keep the posted hours. 

"The same as you have for me," the custodian said, and that was the end of the meeting. 

On clear nights as on foggy ones the first note woke her in time to see to her bucket.  On the south wall she set the dial straight, and beneath it she chalked the hours when she would talk about birds.  She made the bolt herself from two bent nails and the handle of the bucket, which had never needed a handle as much as she had needed a bolt.  The first afternoon somebody knocked outside the chalked hours, she kept on gutting her fish.  She had almost forgotten the pleasure of getting the backbone out whole.
