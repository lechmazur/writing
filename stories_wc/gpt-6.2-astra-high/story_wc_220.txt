After the last resource war I translated distance into closeness at the rate of two buttons a mile.  That was what I charged to carry a letter across the scrapyard settlement, which spanned a cracked wasteland the way stitches span a cut.  My partner, a bloodhound retired from finding bodies, could cross the cable bridges without once looking down, provided I carried his lunch.  His name was Acre because that was how much ground he used to clear before breakfast. 

One evening he sat down outside a newly opened crate of government salvage and declined a piece of liver.  That sit had once meant somebody's sorrow had acquired a location.  Inside were a phonograph cylinder in a burial sock and an autumn leaf preserved in wax, red as the web of my thumb against the sun.  The sock smelled of the cedar soap my mother had issued to her burial crews, and for a minute I sat down too.  I had sent out a hundred letters looking for her and never charged myself a button. 

Up in my shack the cylinder went round under the needle and my mother's voice counted out the dead from the evacuation of the eastern wells.  She read evenly until the end, when she said the words she always used to riddle me to sleep.  What does a tree put out when it needs a little light?  A leaf, I used to say, and she would kiss my eyelids shut. 

I laid the leaf across the lamp chimney and took my mother's old watchmaker's glass to the turning wax.  In ordinary light the grooves looked scabbed with rusty freckles, a common affliction of anything kept in a sock.  In that red light the rust went pale and the blue pinpricks among the freckles drew together into letters.  I stopped the mandrel and began turning it by hand, reading along the floor of the groove while Acre snored against my knee. 

Beside each name she had pricked a destination, then a request in the homely speech of the person who had made it.  Tell Pa the dress fits fine.  Take my good plane to the fellow with the harelip.  These people had ridden through the levy gates alive, under the canvas covers that nobody ever lifted on a burial cart.  She must have meant to deliver their messages when she came home, only she never came. 

Her own destination, under her own name, was "where the kettle takes a breath before it sings."  She had always been intuitively descriptive like that; ask her the way to a house and you learned what it was like to live there.  I knew the sound from my rounds: a whole district of boiler shells that sighed as the cold came up through the cracks, then whistled until dawn.  I had carried letters there myself, all addressed to names that weren't on any roll.  I had even let the old levy clerk address some of my letters for me, because his beautiful hand was known from one well to the next. 

I copied out what the families were meant to know, then melted the cylinder in my soup pot so nobody could trace those cart rides backward.  I have never done anything as hard as stirring out that voice. 

Just before dawn Acre and I stood among the boilers as the whistling fell away, leaving the wild hush in which mice dare to wash their ears.  I was about to call her name when I felt the old dog lean forward on his lead.  His nose took us past six doors to a seventh where a sliver of cedar soap lay on the step to dry.  He didn't sit.  He scratched, the ordinary impolite scratch of a dog that expects to be let in.  There was a rustle behind the door, but nobody lifted the latch. 

"What does a tree put out," I asked, "when it needs a little light?"  Through the gap underneath I slid the waxed leaf, which had come all that way without losing so much as the tip of its stem.  Two buttons rolled out in exchange. 

"For the last mile," she said, and then the latch went up and there wasn't even that between us.
