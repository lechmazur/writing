An hour after the whale beaches, I see what the others on the quay cannot see from where they stand: the calf tucked like a spoon against her seaward side.  From the top of my ladder I can see it trying to nurse, and the milk going loose in the water like smoke.  Being the dock-side lantern lighter has given me a few such privileges, most of which I would as soon do without.  The mother is dead, and by the turn of the tide the calf will be lying on its side in six inches of silver. 

Once I knew how to call a calf away from its mother; once that was how I earned my bread.  I go down the ladder and tell the harbor girls to get my skiff afloat.  Then I take my old tin of pressed wildflowers to the salt crystal growing chamber. 

It is twenty years since I lay on the warm tiles here and gave up everything I could remember about the trade.  They put a flower under your tongue while the salt draws, something to taste besides your own tears, and afterward they let you keep it.  I had to come back eleven times, which is why my tin looks like a summer meadow someone has shut in a door.  For years I have wanted to find where deleted memories go, not because I wanted mine again but because I feared somebody else might have them. 

It was the night cleaner who first showed me how to look, through patterns in coffee grounds.  We used to take our break together here between her last round and my first light.  She would slop the dregs across the tiles and watch the black grains run uphill to gather over certain seams.  Little pockets of gravity, she called them, as if the earth had stitched places into its apron to keep what we gave away.  The pull fluctuates mysteriously; some mornings the grains only tremble, and once we had to fetch a crowbar to lift the cleaner's pail. 

Today the grounds from her abandoned cup race across the white floor and stand up in little cones, each with its needle of reflected light.  I open the tin and let my flowers fall.  The others scatter, but the blue flax I remember choosing on my last visit flies straight to a cone by the drain.  That was the visit when I gave up my mother's voice, the last thing about the work I could not bear to lose.  With the iron heel of my lamp hook I pry up the tile and exhume a cake of salt as thin as the slice of lemon my mother used to lay on her tongue before she sang.  I put it in my mouth. 

There is no great flood of years, only my mother's two fingers on my jaw, easing it open another inch, and the note that comes crawling up out of me.  I had forgotten that what we sang into was wood; the water took the sound from the belly of the boat.  I had forgotten, too, how the little ones would rub their heads along the planking, sure of a welcome. 

The girls have my skiff waiting, and I ask the smallest to take the oars because my hands will not be much use for a while.  With my cheek against the thwart I sing us down the channel past the ladders where the unlit lanterns hang.  There is no answer I can hear, but presently the girl on the oars begins to cry without making a sound.  Something is rubbing along the planks. 

At the harbor mouth three backs are lifting out of the dusk, each carrying on its ridge a little of the light left in the world.  I lift my cheek and the girl ships her oars, and in the gleaming silence we feel the small blunt head ask once more for what we haven't got.  Then the boat rocks hard, and four backs go out beyond the bar. 

The girl asks if I will go back tomorrow to have this taken away.  I shake my head and give her the flattened flax to keep between the pages of her tide book.  On the long pull home I tell her what we used to have waiting at the end of that song.
