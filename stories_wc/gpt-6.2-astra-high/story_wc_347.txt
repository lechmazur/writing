At dusk a small nut rattled down the speaking pipe and landed in Ada's gravy.  She knew the custom, though nobody had used it since her mother died: a coin if you could pay for a guide, a nut if you could not.  She put her mouth to the greasy rim and said, "How many?"  Three knocks came back, then the skitter of iron claws as one of the mechanical birds that patrolled the cliffside labyrinth landed on the other end of the pipe.  Ada heard it imitate her question in her own voice, and the three knocks came again.  She shut the valve before the thing could learn anything else. 

The orchard had been cursed for so long that people mistook its arrangements for nature: pears grew teeth, gulls nested inland, strangers left impressions on the keeper's skin without touching her.  Already three pairs of soles were making little bloodless dents between her wrist and elbow.  Her mother had worn sleeves to the knuckles, even on the day the doctor asked where it hurt.  Ada had taken the job for the house and the house for its roof; she had never meant to put on those sleeves. 

She fetched her mother's coat from the nail where it had hung for eleven years and put her arm into a sleeve for the first time.  Something crackled, and out of a split seam showed a little triangle of blue linen ruled in white.  She knew that blue; copies of engineering drawings had come to her clock shop on just such sun-printed cloth.  Out came her seam knife, and with it the two dozen scraps her mother had used to stiffen the cuffs.  At first she tried to assemble them on the table, matching the white lines end to end like streets.  They were streets, but whoever had drawn this forgotten blueprint had done it to the scale of a woman's arm, and a table had no elbow. 

She rolled the scraps around her forearm, blue side out this time, and fixed them there with the pins from her mother's old tomato pincushion.  Directly under a fork marked LIME KILN she felt a sharp repeated jab; the smallest of the strangers was stamping.  Seven hundred and twelve yards, give or take the length of the kitchen, she told the empty room in her mother's offhandedly exact manner.  She pocketed the nut and set off at a run; she had to cross the sloping kiln tiles before the night frost formed, or all four of them would go skating into the sea. 

She took the back way through the espaliered pears, whose black horizontal arms bore hard little faces with their cheeks lashed tight against the wire.  Halfway up the kiln steps she heard herself calling kindly from out over the drop.  One set of heels moved on her arm.  At the top she found a man and two girls roped together with the sleeves of a coat, moving obediently toward that kind voice in the dark.  She dared not call, since every brass throat along the cliff would take up whatever she said.  Instead she bowled the small nut along the tiles; it struck the leading girl's shoe and spun like a bad penny.  The girl caught it and ran her thumbnail along a notch in the shell that Ada had not noticed.  Then she turned the whole awkward animal of father and sister around by leaning backward in her harness until they had to come with her or fall.  Ada held out the arm in its blue splint, and the girl took it, and in that fashion, with the birds begging behind them in voices of increasing sweetness, they got home. 

Over supper the father tried to explain about payment, and Ada showed him the notch in the shell.  It was her first fee and she proposed to keep it.  While they slept she let out the seams of her mother's coat and set little windows of watch glass into the cuffs so that the next time she needed a road she would not have to tear the thing to pieces.  In the morning light it was an ugly coat, all elbows and eyes, and for the first time in eleven years it hung beside the door instead of behind it.
