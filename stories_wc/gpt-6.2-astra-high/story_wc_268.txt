On the morning of the coronation, Minn took her shoulder out from under the palace and let the north wing settle half an inch.  That was the trouble with being a diminutive titan: everybody remembered what you could hold up and nobody remembered you might want to go somewhere.  She had seen the new king put his thumb to the coronation roll, and in the wet print she had seen a little white crescent.  Thirty years ago she had shut a carriage door on that thumb, and had kissed it better, and had handed the child back to his mother, who washed the palace sheets.  Before noon she meant to unmask a pretender to the throne. 

She went by way of the laundry, where the old mangle still had one short leg propped up with a volume of hymns.  The laundress had been dead ten winters; when Minn opened the hymnal, a pressed violet fell out at the song for a missing child.  Under the last stave someone had written, in the laundress's square hand, What the river takes, the ferryman enters.  No mistress ever questioned a washerwoman carrying a hymnal, although a letter could get her pockets turned out and her month's wages docked. 

Minn knew the ferryman in the song had once been a god, and she knew where his temple had stood before there was a palace to need holding up.  It was where books discarded by the living went to be read by gods.  She climbed into the crack her shoulder had made.  Under the foundations she waited until the Lion dipped his paw and the Ewer tipped to fill the space he left.  Only across the pause of shifting constellations could a living creature walk the length of the ancient temple library lost in time.  From the door it looked no deeper than a cupboard; once inside she saw the lamps of other readers burning centuries apart. 

The ferryman's book lay open at a list of fares, with a second violet at the price of passage for two boys.  Below it the same hand had written, Even the free passenger runs up a debt.  Minn remembered how the river had come into the nursery that spring, and how the queen had come out carrying one dripping bundle.  Even the servants had bowed their heads to let her pass without uncovering what she held.  Only the sovereign rode the ferry free, so Minn passed by the royal genealogies and pulled down the slim account of the queen's own debts. 

An iron gall ink pellet rolled from the binding into her palm; on the page where it had lain was a rune of drift, two shores and the stroke between them.  Minn knew the sign that let a dying river sunder into channels, each with an equal claim to have come from the mountains.  She spat in her palm and worked the pellet with her thumb until she could draw the missing branch.  Slowly the account divided, the gold on one side and on the other what gold would not repay.  To the woman who gave me her living boy, read the first entry, the right to call him hers before the world.  Underneath in a child's straggling hand was a message neither woman could have meant for her to see: I visit on Thursdays when we change the sheets. 

She had come prepared to drag a liar off his chair; she went back carrying the weight of all the Thursdays on which he had been both boys at once.  By the time she reached the dais, the tilt of the floor had set the crown sliding gently down its cushion toward the uncarpeted steps.  The young man caught it and sat down on the top step to read the book she held out.  She had never seen anything so regally unpretentious as the way he made room beside him for a person no taller than his shoe.  She showed him the debt, and he showed her the crescent, as if it were still his worst hurt and she might still do something about it. 

He named his mother to the people waiting below, then named the other one, and set the crown between his feet.  Nobody knelt, which was as well, for the wing was settling into its old riverbed with the deliberation of a swan folding its legs.  Minn let it settle; there were hands enough to help the children and the old ones down.  All the way to level ground he carried his own book.
