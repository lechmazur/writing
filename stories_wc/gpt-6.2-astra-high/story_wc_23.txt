After the hydraulics failed at the sea barrage, the drowned island of Lis began keeping time with the moon again.  Twice a month the ebb uncovered enough of the ridge road for somebody on foot to reach the old burial ground and get back dry.  My mother wanted to make the trip carrying my father, who had spent his last months on the mainland in a biscuit tin above the stove.  That was the start of our reverse migration, although all we meant to do at first was bury one man. 

People called me the patchwork navigator because I made my charts on sailcloth, stitching on patches that could be picked off when a channel shifted.  Paper went to pulp in an open boat, and besides, my first trade had been taking hems up for women who could no longer afford new clothes.  I set up my frame in the starlit signal station at the mainland end of the road and waited for the moon to show me where to put my needle.  All it showed me was a glittering mile of mud with eight or nine plausible paths across it.  The depth there fluctuates with moon phases, but the difference between mud you can stand on and mud that takes you to the waist is invisible even at noon. 

Help came through cryptic phone calls at midnight, the meal break at the mainland cannery, where half the islanders still worked.  "Take the lame cat by the nose," said a voice I knew from the line where they packed sardines.  "Turn where the bride sat down," said another, and I had to wake my mother to learn that the bride was a granite block on which a girl had once ruined a good white skirt.  Nobody could tell me about the cat until my mother saw what I was using to hold the cloth taut on the frame.  It was a bronze censer foot, green with patina, shaped like the paw of a lion and heavy enough to pull a seam straight.  My father had kept it in his pocket all the years I knew him, rubbing one toe bright. 

"He was going to mend it," she said, and drew me a little picture of the lion that had stood smoking outside the chapel door.  The old road forked around that chapel, one arm dropping down to the ferry slip and the other climbing six feet to the graves. 

On the night of the crossing my mother brought the biscuit tin, and behind her came eleven people with parcels about the same size.  I had imagined tears or singing, but mostly there was the noise of people clearing their throats and asking if anybody had a pin for a loose cuff.  At the granite block I ran a finger across the chart, reading my stitches by touch so nobody would have to let go of anybody else to hold a light.  The next mark meant lion, but nothing stood there except a long green lump in the mud, and on either side of it ran a strip of gravel.  Then my mother went down on her knees with the little foot and felt along the lump until bronze clicked on bronze.  The long toes pointed along the left fork, as the nose must have done before the salt and grit had eaten its features away.  Nobody hurried her when she put her forehead against the cold metal and said into its ear that he was sorry it had taken him so long.  She left the foot there and we climbed. 

On the ridge we used the loose stones to build a low wall, each household tucking its dead into the gaps and closing them up again.  I picked a stone with a hollow just the size of a thumb, so the next time we came my mother could find him even in the dark.  Later there would be more crossings, more green stitches, and children born on the mainland who would learn the road without ever having to lose it.  That night, going back, my mother kept reaching up to the crook of her arm where the tin had rested and finding nothing there.  After the third time I put my elbow through hers, and that was how we came off the island, carrying what we could.
