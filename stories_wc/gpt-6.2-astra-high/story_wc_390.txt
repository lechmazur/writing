On the day the sun came back, the first thing Mara heard at the drift-ice marina was her father trying to clear his throat.  He had been dead eleven weeks, and she had forgotten that small, disgusting noise although she could still have drawn his face with her eyes shut.  She stood among the boats until somebody tugged the hem of her azure coat and said they needed the lighthouse engineer down at the crypt.  Only then did she remember putting on the coat that morning in the hope that somebody would need precisely that. 

At the foot of the ramp an old man stood with the crypt keeper's key already turned in the lock.  "You can hear it on the boats," Mara said.  He took her by the wrist and laid her fingers on the iron stem of the key, which shivered like a needle sewing leather. 

Inside, a bar of gold lay across the hallowed salt-glass basin in which the people of the marina had kept their winter dead for seven generations.  Not their bodies, which went under the ice, but the last breath each had spent in the company of another human being.  Mara had held the basin for her father and felt it grow heavy with what he could no longer carry.  Now a wet little cough kept circling its rim, gaining strength on each circuit, and a hairline crack followed it like a skate mark.  Usually there were a dozen people here at the return of the sun, ready to answer their dead, but this winter the marina had lost just one man. 

"I can stop it," she said, slipping off her coat to wrap the glass.  The keeper let her try, and for a moment the voice fell silent under the blue wool and the weight of her two hands.  Then she felt the cough in the roots of her teeth.  All she had done was send it down the pedestal into the rock on which the lighthouse stood.  Through the open door she could see the gilded tips of the masts sinking into shadow as the sun went down almost where it had come up.  If the glass cooled with that much strain still in it, there would be nothing left to hold anybody else's dead. 

The old name for what had to happen here was sonic absolution, though as a child she had mistaken the second word for a promise of forgiveness.  It meant loosening, the keeper had told her, as of a belt around a belly after supper.  One undid the grip of the glass through vibrational matching, by making a sound so like the captive one that it could pass without a jolt into living flesh.  She had come prepared to sing the tune her father had hummed with her most of his last night, past the point where either of them could bear it.  What she had not prepared for was the indignity of his needing to spit. 

Her father had been fastidious, a man who went outside to blow his nose even when outside meant putting on boots.  She had gone on humming for both of them when he could no longer carry the tune, and now she wondered if he had wanted her to shut up. 

She pulled the coat away.  All her working life she had been able to adapt a signal to whatever poor instrument was at hand, and this time the poor instrument was her own good throat.  She let a little spit pool behind her tongue and listened not for the pitch but for the place where the breath snagged and folded back.  On the third try the key stopped shaking in the lock.  Something salty and surprisingly warm came up into her mouth, and she turned her head and spat into the palm of her hand.  The crack was still there, but it had stopped a finger short of the foot of the basin.  She could mend that. 

Outside, the last gold lifted from the boats, leaving a fleet of blue hulls gently shouldering one another in the dusk.  She waited for something noble to occur to her to say about her father, but what came instead was the memory of his boots lined up beside the door, always with the laces loosened ready for his feet.  She sat down on the step with her sticky hand open and let the keeper sit beside her without finding him anything to do.
