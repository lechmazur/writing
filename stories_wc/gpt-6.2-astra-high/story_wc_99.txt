After the severance, the tides of remembrance began running through the gap where the road to the mainland used to be.  You could dip a piece of linen in that water and bring it up stained with whatever the dead had said about you.  For thirty years I had owned a yellow handkerchief from the day my father called me his little canary in front of the entire school.  Now that I was older than he had been at his death, I wanted to find the color of unspoken apologies.  In particular I wanted his. 

On a breezy morning after rain I carried the last clean square of his sailcloth across our islet to my old bonsai tutor.  Miss Linn was wiring a juniper in the lee of her kitchen wall, and I watched her diffuse the pull of the wire along the branch with six rhythmically placid turns of her wrist.  She spoke as slowly as she worked, leaving room after every few words for a pupil to discover what came next.  When I showed her the cloth she said, "A man can die sorry," and I had time to hope before she added, "without being sorry for the thing you think." 

My father had piloted the dredge that cut the road to let the flood through before it took the town.  I was the girl on the bank with the signal flags, and when the dredge went under I was still holding up the green one that meant keep going.  Ever since then I had been waiting to hear him say that a child of twelve should never have been given that flag. 

Miss Linn had boarded the dredge one moonlit night on its annual return to finish digging a channel that was plenty deep enough already.  She brought the ghost ship's compass out to the garden and showed me the shred of antique lace that served it for a card.  Instead of the points of the wind there were eight tiny flags worked in thread, with an eyelet at the hoist of each.  Before there were signal schools, mothers taught the children on the banks through instructions woven into an antique lace pattern like this one.  I knew seven flags, but had never learned what the eighth one meant because the sample at school had been cut down to fit its frame. 

The needle pointed to that eighth little flag, which hung in deep folds as if the thread itself were tired.  Miss Linn let me puzzle until I remembered a juniper bough I had once tried to train by hauling on the tip alone.  I eased the damp folds apart one by one, spreading the strain so that none of the old threads snapped.  What I had taken for folds in a single flag were in fact two flags lowered side by side.  Beneath them a pair of hands let go a wheel and another pair took hold, four clean little hands scarcely bigger than grains of rice.  The pilot was taking over from the bank. 

I had seen my father give that signal with two red dish towels just before the wheelhouse filled; all these years I had remembered him waving goodbye.  He had tried to take the weight off my arm, and I had stood there on the bank holding it rigid for the rest of my life. 

At Miss Linn's gate the rain barrel was overflowing, and a shallow stream ran from its lip down through the thrift to meet the incoming tide.  I knelt at the place where the two waters met and put the sailcloth in, not to catch a word from him but because I finally had one for him.  I was sorry about those towels, sorry he had spent the last of his time on earth trying to tell me something and had seen me standing there unmoved.  The blue that came up was the blue of his work shirt where the seams had faded, so light you could see every thread of the sailcloth separately.  I could not say which of us was sorry, only that the color held when I wrung it out. 

That autumn I stitched two little drooping flags onto the blue square and took it to the signal school to hang beside the narrow frame.  These days the first thing a child on our islet learns is how to let somebody else bring a ship home.
