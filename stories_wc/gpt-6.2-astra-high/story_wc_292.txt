After the jet stream shifted, the rain at Salt Mouth came from inland, carrying the smell of wet mattresses out to sea.  At the experimental weather station the walls swelled until the instrument room could only be opened by putting a shoulder to the door.  Its map librarian, Nell Vale, kept the charts in the bread oven and ate her bread cold.  She believed that anything worth knowing could be made public if somebody found it the right shelf.  This was not a belief encouraged by her director. 

On the morning the harbor floor came loose, she was lettering a card for a copper nail from her grandmother's coffin.  She had picked it out of the ashes because the museum wanted an object to go with the name, and nothing else of Ruth Vale's had survived except her disgrace.  Thirty years before, Ruth had called a fleet of banished pilots home.  The harbor board maintained that a woman employed to mend signal flags could not possibly have understood what she was doing.  It honored the pilots for saving sixty people and charged Ruth for the flags. 

Now a brown tongue of water was licking the station steps, and out in the channel a ferry lay over at an angle that made Nell straighten her head to look at it.  Where the chart gave six fathoms there was apparently a hill.  The director sent for a sounding boat; the boat came back with its propeller wrapped in willow roots.  Half the upstream bank was traveling to the sea under a perfectly smooth skin. 

Nell took the binoculars to the roof.  Beyond the harbor stakes three narrow boats rode the brown swell, each with the blue square of a pilot banished for taking part in last winter's strike.  Exile here was reversible by an old provision of the guild charter: a port that asked for a pilot's skill acknowledged the pilot's right to live there.  The provision said nothing about a pilot having to say yes. 

"I can ask them," Nell told the director, sounding so certain she had to hide her fingers, which were fussing at the nail in her pocket.  He looked at the water working its way up the stairs like gravy soaking into bread. 

"You can ask if they want a tow," he said. 

She went to the oven for the three shipwrecked journals on which she had built her case for Ruth.  Salvaged from three different wrecks, they contained the same set of bad verses; the pilots had once used the numbers of the lines as a code to save on flags.  Set a red pennant above the numbers and the statement in a stanza became a question.  Ruth's bill from the board itemized every flag she had used, even the red one.  Nell had meant to exhibit the bill beside the nail.  Only now did it occur to her what a miserable sort of justice it was to hang up the question behind glass and never ask it again. 

She bent the soft copper nail around the torn eye of the red pennant and ran it up over two and nine.  Who knows the road that walks beneath the river? 

On the farthest boat an oilskin figure lifted both arms, then pointed not at the ferry but at the station.  In all three journals the answering stanza was the same: Begin your road where I begin my thread.  They needed a fixed end for a ferry rope. 

Nell got the director to help her fasten a reel to the stair rail; together they stood thigh deep on the steps while a pilot drew the light line out across the moving shoal.  Twice the line went slack and twice the director took it in, his face puckered with the effort of feeling for another person's hand.  When the pilot made the ferry, they used the line to draw a heavy rope across.  By evening the pilots had hauled every passenger ashore by punt, and three blue squares lay bleeding dye into the station sink. 

The director signed the pilots in under their own names.  Under the box marked Authority he hesitated, then passed the book to Nell.  She put her grandmother's name down first, but the director shook his head. 

"She answered for hers," he said. 

It cost Nell rather more than she expected to cross that out and write her own.  When she dried the page in the bread oven both names came through, one beneath a single honest stroke of ink.
