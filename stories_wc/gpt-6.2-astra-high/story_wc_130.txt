The first time my mother failed to know my father, she still knew that the glass eyes in his hand had been made for a hare.  He had taken the pair from her taxidermist's cabinet to mend the puppet they worked together on market nights, and she objected to their use in a fox.  "A fox has business after dark," she told the man she thought was stealing them.  He closed his hand as if he had been caught with somebody's change. 

Our market was held in a cavern orchard, where the stalactites dripped so faithfully that each pear tree had been planted under its own particular needle of stone.  Farmers came down from the scorched terraces to trade, and stayed after the moon came over the sinkhole to watch my father pull a live laugh out of a dead fox.  He was a secretive conjurer even at home; I had to learn to dovetail the sides of his trick boxes before he would let me put a hand inside one.  But I knew it was my mother behind the curtain who gave the beast its terrible manners and its tender regard for small children. 

When she began to forget the names of things, he took to counting them instead.  In his pocket book he kept columns for names remembered, meals enjoyed, fingers offered freely on a walk.  He wanted to understand the mathematics of emotion well enough to know whether it was kinder to take her back onstage or to pack the fox away.  I wanted an answer too, because I had begun to miss her most when I was sitting right beside her, and the shame of that was considerable. 

For a month the fox stayed blind.  Then one night, after the orchard grew silent beneath the moon and the last barrow rattled up the ramp, my mother asked to be taken to the little door.  My father fetched his book, thinking she meant the privy, another journey she had lately needed help to make.  But she went down on one knee at the mosaic stepping stone in front of the curtain and felt along the chipped patterns with her thumb. 

Years before, when they could not afford a lamp backstage, she had chipped one groove for the henhouse door and two for the trap through which the fox raided it.  Even in her inventions she was gentle: a child invited behind the curtain could find the levers without having to confess he could not read.  She found the double groove and followed it under the hem to the latch, and the fox came up on her arm without its eyes. 

My father opened his book to the column for names and asked if she could tell him what she had found.  I could see her drawing away from the question as you draw your tongue away from a bad tooth.  Instead of answering, she made the fox nose along his sleeve until he fished the eyes from his waistcoat pocket.  One went in each of the fox's outstretched paws. 

"Would you mind looking for my spectacles?" the fox asked me, in a voice I had never heard before. 

I laughed so hard I had to sit down on the stone, and my father put his book away, which spoiled everything, for she had been about to make the fox read his figures.  She had to ask him to take it out again.  Together they worked up a bit in which the fox inspected each entry with one glass eye, then solemnly checked the addition with the other. 

Afterward she thanked my father for a pleasant evening and asked him what he charged for the hire of the animal.  He told her it was free to anyone who could make it do sums. 

That was the act we offered on market nights thereafter, but only when she found the little door herself; the rest of the time we sold pears.  Some nights she sat with me on the barrow and held my hand going up the ramp, to save me from being frightened of the dark.  She didn't need to know who I was to know how steep it was, or which side had the drop.
