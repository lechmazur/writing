When the carnival left town, it left one attraction standing: a blue chalk line six inches up the doors of the abandoned spaceport passenger terminal.  That was where the water had stood after the wheel crew emptied its ballast tanks, and where it stood after every decent rain.  Alma, the civil engineering intern charged with photographing the damage, was curious about the color of the line, since nobody at the works office carried blue chalk.  The same blue dust streaked the apron of a man dismantling a gingerbread stall just outside the doors. 

"My wife marks it every year," he said.  "She wants to know when it's safe to bring the ovens back." 

Before the last launch, the concourse had been a market where a person could buy supper on the way home from either of the two shipyards.  The two yards still sounded their whistles ten minutes apart; between them now there was one shop selling tins at whatever price it pleased.  Alma had grown up eating those tins.  At college she had tried to puzzle out new equations for hope, which in her notebooks generally came down to how many hours somebody had to work to eat.  Now she put her camera away and asked the baker where the water used to go. 

"Into our houses," he said. 

Across the concourse lay a maze of intentions, all of them recorded on separate sheets of tracing cloth in the little dispatch office.  One superintendent had walled off the channel to the workers' cottages; another had raised the floor of the customs shed to keep the mail dry.  Somebody with no authority at all had cut a notch in the kerb to save a fig tree, still bearing against the southern wall.  Laid one over another against the window, the sheets made a lake with a market at the bottom. 

Only the apron beyond the departure gates had never asked a thing of anybody since the rockets stopped coming.  Alma could see the tops of reeds out there, but whether their roots were above or below her boots she could not tell.  Her boss had the survey kit upriver until winter.  The baker, however, had a short telescope off an explorer's sextant, the last piece of his father's navigation gear and the only one a pawnbroker hadn't wanted.  He used it to read the names on the ships his daughter helped build.  He looked dubious when Alma lashed it to a spirit level on the flat top of his oven crate. 

"It's functionally creative," she said, the phrase her supervisor used for things which had better work because he hadn't authorized them. 

He took the measuring rod out among the reeds, holding it plumb while she sighted on its painted bands, and when they traded places their readings agreed within a finger.  The apron lay two feet below the concourse, pitched toward the old settling ponds; only the concrete lips of the departure gates held the water back.  She would not have to resurrect the market at the expense of the cottages. 

Her supervisor came down on a Sunday, on his own time, and knocked his knuckles against what she had taken for solid concrete.  The lips were hollow covers for the cables that had once fed power to the boarding bridges.  With a borrowed hoist they lifted every third cover, leaving broad crossings between the gaps, and waited for weather. 

The first rain carried a season's worth of paper stars out through the gates and laid them in long necklaces among the reeds.  At the notch by the fig tree a little brown water went on running precisely where somebody had wanted it to run.  The baker's wife waited through two more storms before she rubbed out the blue line. 

On the morning the ovens came back, Alma bought a loaf still too hot to hold without shifting it from hand to hand.  From the apron came the high thin calls of birds settling in the ponds, and from either end of town the whistles blew ten minutes apart.  She stayed to count the loaves that changed hands in those ten minutes, because here at last was a quantity worth knowing.
