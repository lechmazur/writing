The map tore when the clerk tried to roll it, precisely where the black road met the blank wash of the downs.  Nell, who framed maps for the county, put a sheet of board under each half before he could bring the edges together.  Across that little gulf the council proposed to draw a red line denoting the end of public passage.  Beyond it lay an ancient henge with a chapel tucked into its bank, a rain-scoured fresco vault just tall enough for a man to stand in. 

The clerk said nobody had contested the temporary closure, which was proof enough for him that it could be made permanent.  Nell had learned to be methodically curious about blanks; she had once found a whole ferry service under the lip of a gilt frame.  She asked what counted as proof in the other direction.  Anybody could come to Thursday's meeting, he said, and tell them what they went up there for. 

Her mother had called it the whispered chapel because anything said against its curved wall carried clear to somebody at the other end, however softly spoken.  You could stand with your face to what was left of a painted orchard and never have to see the face of the person listening.  After Nell's brother drowned, their father had gone stone deaf at the mention of his name, though in all other respects his hearing remained exemplary.  On Sundays he went to the downs to look for mushrooms and generally came back without any.  She had believed him, being seven, and at forty-seven she didn't particularly want to stop believing him. 

Still, she went down to the county archive on her lunch break and asked for the film of the last sheep fair held inside the henge.  She had framed a still from it once; she remembered the chalky little doorway at the edge of the shot.  For comparison she got the video the surveyors had taken last winter, just before the council put up the fence.  She ran the two pieces of silent footage side by side, forty years apart, lining up the doorposts with strips of paper stuck to the screens. 

At the fair a woman sat in the doorway shelling peas into a basin while two men bargained over a ewe.  In winter a delivery boy parked his bicycle there and bent to the interminable business of rolling one trouser leg down.  Each looked up just once, not at whoever was coming but at whoever was holding the camera.  Nell ran them back and covered the people with her hands as she sometimes covered a busy cartouche to see where a river went.  Between the feet of the stool she saw two pairs of shoes go in, and a little later the same shoes coming out.  Behind the bicycle wheels there was the same small procession, this time a pair of work boots and two pink slippers dark with wet.  Neither watcher moved aside until the walkers were well out of shot. 

Somebody had gone to that trouble for her father, perhaps, when it was beyond his strength to pronounce his boy's name where a kind face might crumple at the sound.  She lifted the paper strips and let the screens go dark without winding back to look for his shoes. 

On Thursday she took the torn map in its new frame and the two clips, with everything above the watchers' knees cut away.  She showed the council what there was to see of the feet, and when the clerk asked whether that was all, she said it was all she had brought.  To her surprise the clerk played the clips again, then got up out of his chair and stood between the little procession of shoes and the rows of council seats.  He put his hands on his hips; on the screen behind him the pink slippers passed into perfect privacy.  The council voted to reopen the path without anybody having to stand up and tell what had taken them there. 

Nell was given the map to hang in the hall, the two halves mounted with a hair of daylight between them and no new ink on either.  That Sunday she walked as far as the henge with a basket over her arm, thinking she might like to try the wall herself.  At the door a man was helping a woman out of her coat, and beyond them a family with cameras was coming over the bank.  Nell sat down in the entry and began to sort the mushrooms she had actually found, earth and all, into several quite unnecessary piles.
