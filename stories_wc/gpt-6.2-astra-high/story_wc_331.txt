At three in the morning the scientific poetry library had seven patrons, each inhabiting the little island of light that a green shade let fall.  From her desk Nell could see six pairs of hands turning pages; the seventh patron had his hands tucked under his arms.  He was waiting for the others to leave before he asked for what he wanted, and since the others were mostly insomniacs, Nell took her tea over to him.  She had been the odd-hour librarian long enough to know how far apart two chairs had to be for a person to speak.  She left an empty chair between them. 

"I lent you people my boy's voice," he said. 

From his coat he drew a tattered silk scarf that kept its ruffles even under the weight of his broad palm.  Nell knew the scarf from a photograph of the first librarian, a sanely eccentric woman who had kept no portrait of herself except the one required for her bus pass.  In that tiny square you could just see the silk foaming up around her chin. 

"My sister," he said when Nell brought out the pass.  "She had the children do a poem about rain, and when my boy died she offered me his tape, but I thought I had better not." 

Now he had got old enough to want to know what had happened to the rain after it fell out of his son's mouth.  There were nine hundred cassettes in the rain cabinet, none with a name, since children who knew they would be identified tended to sound like the same obliging child.  Each label bore a number and a line of verse; inside was a child's reading of that line followed by a line of the child's own.  Nell had spent a good deal of her working life trying to find the place where stories live in such a cabinet, and had privately concluded it was the hinges.  Nobody ever opened it. 

She touched a loose corner of silk and saw a blue numeral on the underside, in the indelible ink once used to mark children's clothes for camp.  Seven silk triangles came together at every interior point where a flat patchwork of equilateral triangles would have taken six.  She had led enough school parties through hyperbolic geometry to know why the scarf ruffled: too much circumference for its radius, too much silk to lie obediently flat.  Room for more and more answers, too.  The first librarian had stitched a map she could wear into the classroom, letting children choose whose rain to start from by taking a pinch of silk. 

"His patch came from his mother's wedding dress," the man said, turning a piece the color of weak tea toward the light.  "She let him cut the bit with the cigarette burn because it wouldn't make much of a christening gown anyway." 

They found the burn and its number, then three patches that shared its edges, one for the line his boy had taken and two for the children who had taken his.  While the six patrons browsed, Nell fitted fresh pads to a pair of headphones and threaded the slack back onto the little hubs with a pencil.  She knew how to reawaken these analog memories without tearing them; what she did not know was where to put herself while he listened.  He solved that by holding one padded cup against his ear and the other out to her across the empty chair. 

A boy said that rain put rivets in the river, and somewhere very near the microphone he knocked his knuckles twice against the table.  The man listened dry eyed, attending to those knocks with the air of somebody checking a visitor's credentials before slipping the chain. 

On the next tape a girl took up the rivets and said the river held the town together, although on maps it looked like something coming apart.  It was here, in the voice of a girl neither of them knew, that the man had to take his glasses off.  Nell moved her chair one tile closer, enough to let him use his sleeve without either of them losing the sound.  When the girl knocked twice, he knocked back.
