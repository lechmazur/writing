Every night between acts, when the acrobats had gone to powder their hands and the seal was still eating her wages, Nell Pike played twelve games of chess at once.  The tidal arcade had six arches open to the sea on either side of its little theater, and she put a board under each.  Under the stars the boards looked like twelve square holes cut through the pier to some much tidier universe below.  All around them the black water did long division on the pilings and came up with a different remainder every time. 

Her customers were mostly arcade workers who could tell you, without consulting a clock, exactly when the seal would bark for her third herring.  Nell had invented her turn for them; she couldn't stop that bark from coming, but she could give it something new to sing against.  Six customers drew white shells numbered one through six, and six drew black shells with matching numbers.  Nell carried each white player's move to the corresponding black board and brought the answer back, singing the squares in her carrying fishmonger's contralto.  Nobody knew whom the tide had sent them for a partner.  By ordering randomness this way she had once arranged for the mayor to lose in nine moves to the boy who boiled his shirts.  People remembered the boy's name now, which they hadn't before. 

She had the lightly attentive air of somebody out for a stroll, although every crossing was calculated to get her over the low stretch of decking between waves.  On this particular night she was coming back from the black boards when something hard struck her shoe.  It was a white bishop with a crooked pewter head, the very piece her sister Lou had mended with solder from a can of peaches.  They had been girls then, playing for the privilege of the pillow on the dry side of their bed.  Lou ran the theater now and Nell ran the interval, and for eleven years neither had had to concede so much as a pillowcase.  At breakfast they discussed the evening's tides; at supper they counted change. 

She picked up the bishop and looked toward the lighted booth where Lou sat every night with her finger on the curtain bell.  It was empty.  At the nearest board the laundry boy sat with his arms folded, waiting for Nell to notice that he had already received his answer.  From across the arcade came a soprano singing another move, and then a bass replying, half a beat behind the slop of the water.  All twelve were playing without her, passing the song from arch to arch wherever the wind tore a hole in it.  Somebody had had to teach them that. 

Behind the last piling Lou was sitting on an upturned crate with their old board on her knees and an empty square beside Nell's king.  "I get seven minutes before the seal," she said.  "I've been trying to make this happen since Easter, but we needed twelve who could hold a tune." 

Nell's first impulse was to tell her that the laundry boy was flat, and her second was to demand to know who was minding the bell.  Instead she fitted the bishop into its place and advanced the pawn in front of it two squares.  Five moves in, she found herself waiting for somebody at the other end of the pier to supply a reply that belonged to her alone.  Lou waited too, resting one thumb on the white scar the hot solder had made on the other.  Then Nell saw where she could give up the bishop for a murderous little attack, unless Lou saw one square farther, as she sometimes did.  Nell slid the crooked piece the length of its clean white diagonal.  Lou took it. 

The seal barked, the laundry boy rang the curtain up, and both sisters stayed where they were to see what would come of it.  At supper, over the uncounted change, they were still arguing about whether it had been a sound sacrifice.
