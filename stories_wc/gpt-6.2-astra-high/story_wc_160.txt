As the building breathed in, the flame at the end of Ida's match leaned toward the cheap seats.  On the exhale it stood straight long enough for her to light the lamp.  In the crumbling amphitheater inside the dormant volcano, this was how you told what the sea was doing in the dark.  Each swell crowded the air out of the tunnels under the stage; each retreat drew it whistling back through the stone.  Tonight there was hardly time to strike a match between breaths. 

The island prison lay on the seaward side of Ida's floodgate, and the town, including her sleeping boy, lay on the other.  At nine the prison would be given up to the storm.  The governor had sent across his furniture and thirty guards, precisely the load his evacuation order specified.  His laundress, who was Ida's sister Ruth, had sent a bill for washing the machinist's spare overalls.  Ida owned one pair and was standing in it.  Two coppers due for the removal of gravy, the bill said, with Ruth's customary faith in her sister's ability to afford meat.  Even the guard who slit the envelope had smiled. 

When they were girls their mother had taken in washing from the prison and sometimes delivered a message with a clean shirt.  She would never carry names, only the hour a wife might expect to see a face at a window.  Ida held the envelope flap over her lamp and studied the leftover adhesive, turning it until the dried ridges threw shadows.  Ruth had pressed pieces of thread into the soft gum and picked them out again with a pin.  Six long grooves and two short ones: the sixth retreat of the sea after the lamp signal, two boatloads.  Under the flap was the only place a censor never looked once he had cut an envelope open. 

Ida felt her hand tremble as she fitted the lamp into the sight hole without opening its shutter.  She could lift the gate, but she could not get two boats through before the sea followed them into town.  On the wall hung the antique surveyor's chain with which her grandfather had laid out the high water boundary.  Every winter she used it to settle arguments over whose cellar was legally a cellar and whose was a well.  Now she lowered it from the street sill until the brass handle rang on the floor of the orchestra pit.  A whole sea would not fit in the theater, but one swell would, if she could send it here instead. 

That meant knocking out the brickwork in the scenery chute, which meant waking the town.  It also meant telling people what she meant to do with the building they had taxed themselves to save.  She rang the fire bell and showed the first arrivals the chain stretched from the chute to the door.  She showed them where the water would come and where, on her wages and her name, it would stop.  She did not tell them who was in the boats.  Nobody swung a hammer until the baker asked whether she herself would stand below the mark.  Ida chalked it across the breast of her overalls. 

It took eleven hammers less time to open the chute than it took the baker to get his boots off.  He came down barefoot to stand beside her as she opened the shutter.  Five times the little flame bowed and recovered; on the sixth she threw the lever, and two laundry barges scraped over the sill.  The baker put his weight on the lever beside hers and together they got the gate down behind them.  The captured swell came up through the chute in slabs of green light that broke and danced on the vaulted ceiling.  It stopped a button short of the chalk. 

The rowers sat blinking in their prison stripes, no longer sure, with all these people looking on, whether to jump out or stay put.  The baker held out his floury hands, and the first wet pair of hands closed over them.  Ruth stepped ashore carrying a hamper from which a small boy regarded the ruined theater with grave approval. 

Next morning the baker presented Ida with a bill for eleven hammers, two hinges and two boots gone to sea.  She turned it over, half expecting to find a thread mark, but he had written what he wanted in plain ink.  Kindly advise whether the young gentleman takes a whole roll yet or only the soft inside.
