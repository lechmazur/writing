At the tidal arbor above the clouds, it was customary for the station pianist to play the last ferry out whether anybody had got aboard or not.  Usually Ada played something wistful enough to make the people left behind feel that missing a boat was a distinction.  Today the boats themselves were left behind. 

All morning the ferry captains had carried their flickering compasses from branch to branch, hoping to find a patch of deck where north would hold still.  The iron in the rising cloudbank made the needles flash like minnows, and no captain would take a hundred souls blind into those shoals.  Below the platform the tide was already stripping leaves from the silver boughs; by noon it would strip the skin from anyone still outside. 

To Ada the worst of it was that she had once been taken into a storm room here and could remember nothing useful about it.  She remembered her mother's wet wool skirt and the humiliation of being small enough to wipe her nose on it.  The company had since walled up the entrances rather than keep the rooms provisioned, and its little film about the arbor showed only passengers drinking tea.  Ada had accompanied that film six times a day for thirty years, precisely as long as her mother had been dead. 

She had taken the black-and-white film reel off the projector that morning to save it, of all the stupid things to save.  When the boy helping her pack dropped it, the hub split and a second little roll spilled out onto the flags.  He held it up and said there was a lady on it with her mouth full of nails.  Ada snatched it hard enough to cut his finger, and had the grace to bind it before she ran. 

Up in the projection booth she threaded it by touch, and the lady with the nails became her mother again, young and cross at having her work interrupted.  Through hidden sections spliced between lengths of blank leader, the camera followed her repairing a door that folded out of sight into the grain of the living trunk.  Trust the carpenters to keep a room for themselves and the projectionist, who drank with them, to put the directions somewhere safe. 

The boy raced for the south stair as soon as he saw the sweep of the handrail on the screen.  Ada nearly followed; then she saw the broken anniversary clock that had stood on her mother's workbench and now stood on her piano, its hands perpetually ten minutes short of payday, as her mother used to say.  On the screen it was ten past.  She had threaded the film emulsion out. 

"North stair," she called, and heard her voice die in the soft grey stuff that had already swallowed the boy.  The station had no alarm for refuge, only bells for boarding, but there was one tune that had brought people running to the north stair for thirty years.  It was the waltz that meant the cheap seats on the homeward boat were being released. 

She got two captains to roll the piano to the stairhead while she walked crabwise at the keyboard, never once missing the beat.  Out of the wool came porters with their barrows and women with babies carried high against their throats; the boy came hugging his bandaged finger to his chest.  The hidden door gave under his good hand just where the woman on the screen had set her shoulder.  Beyond it the dry stairs spiraled down to a room smelling of resin, with benches cut for people who had spent all day on their feet. 

Last through, Ada caught herself reaching back for the clock; the cloud was in the piano now, drawing a thin metallic singing from the strings.  She let the door shut. 

How long, somebody wanted to know, and the oldest captain gave the old tide pilot's answer: until the horizon invites night.  Through the heartwood she could feel the grit going over them, polishing away everything the company had thought worth keeping.  Her hands lay useless on her knees until the boy tapped his heel to ask for the waltz again. 

All her working life she had wanted to surprise fate with an unplanned encore, and now she had the audience for it and not one key.  She took the boy's good hand and showed him how to slap the bass against the bench while she whistled the part the cheap seats never stayed to hear.
