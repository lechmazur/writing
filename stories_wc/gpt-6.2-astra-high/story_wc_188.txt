At dusk the fortress bloomed pink from a thousand peach stones spat over its walls by generations of soldiers.  Under the trees, on the last evening before the valley was flooded for the reservoir, Nella unpacked a sky full of punctuation.  She was a wandering fireworks designer, and the ferrymaster had bought the cheapest thing in her catalogue: a full stop.  When the last passenger was aboard she would put one white dot above the keep, and the other boats would know to cast off.  She liked a job that could be explained to a horse. 

The trouble was the last passenger.  He was sitting on the landing with a fire spinner's kerosene rag knot in his lap, combing its fringe with his fingers.  His mother had gone upriver with the spring fair before the engineers advanced the date; she would come back by the ridge road to collect him.  All the houses along that road would still be there, he explained, which would make it worse when she looked down and saw water where their house had been.  She had left him the knot to practice his wrist work, strictly without the interesting part. 

Couldn't they leave a note?  Nella looked up at the ridge gate, which the water would spare, and asked him what they ought to write.  He said his mother could read a crowd of five hundred but not a word on paper.  Besides, he added politely, had Nella ever seen paper after rain? 

By then the blossom had gone gray, and a lid of cloud had settled on the hills.  In the hush of that night without stars Nella walked uphill, chanting as she always did when she paced out a show.  One for the near eye, two for the far.  A designer who thought only of the duke on his balcony could give a whole marketplace of people a splendid view of a chimney.  She let the place come at her without looking for anything particular: cold on one cheek where a doorway opened, the smell of the boy's knot behind her, a click beneath her shoe.  From all that her practiced senses could filter the one thing that did not fit.  The gatehouse floor should not have clicked. 

She stooped and lifted a loose piece of the blue and yellow mosaic that once showed the duke receiving the keys to the fortress.  Almost his entire face came away in her hand. 

"At last," she said, "a contribution from the nobility." 

They would leave a note in mosaic, high on the wall where anybody coming over the ridge could see it.  On the dry wall under the gate arch she drew the river's fork with the greasy black fringe of the knot.  At its western tip the boy drew three fat pots, the sign above the tileworks where the boats were taking them.  Left to her, Nella would have put in little yellow people waving from the pots, but the boy shook his head.  "From the far eye," he reminded her. 

For two years now she had been putting a good face on things that closed: pits, mills, whole streets of condemned houses.  It made a change to be designing something for someone still to come.  She fetched the ferrymaster to look; he fetched a mason and a bucket of the mortar the engineers had used to seal the lower gate.  By lantern light they pressed blue river and yellow pots into a bed that would be hard by morning.  Yet the boy stayed at the wall, clutching the knot. 

"She'll know where people went," he said, "but she won't know if I went with them." 

Now Nella understood what had kept him sitting on the landing: he had been imagining his mother searching the water, unable to believe he was anywhere but underneath.  She held out her hand for the rag, but he had already pushed the knot deep into the mortar beside the pots.  He pulled it free and there stood every twist and crossing of the thing his mother had made for his hand. 

On the way down he chanted with her, getting the words wrong on purpose until the ferrymaster threatened to charge them both as musicians.  When the boat took their weight Nella sent up the white dot, no bigger than a peach blossom from where they stood.  It hung for a breath in defiance of everything that must come down.  Then it came down, as advertised, and the boy tucked the knot into his belt and asked if he might steer.
