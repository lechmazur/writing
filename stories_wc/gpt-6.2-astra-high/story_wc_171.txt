At midsummer, on the ancient terrace where the two canals parted, Ina set her father's ebony clipboard across her knees and waited for the sun to muster the villages.  They came out of habit, even those who owned no cup to dip in the channels and no land to wet.  In the slow dance of celestial bodies there was one figure everybody here could follow: when the shadow of the bronze goat's horn touched the little copper nail in the paving, the water year began. 

For thirty years Ina had negotiated truces in what both villages called the forever war; she had once got them to stop shooting for the length of a difficult birth.  The child was here now, a wiry young woman named Bet who could splice a pump belt so well you had to feel for the join.  Bet had brought the leaking leather cup that had belonged to her grandmother, last keeper of the southern canal before the division.  Nobody troubled to take it from her, because nobody imagined she would be asked to drink. 

Ina had been nineteen when the fever emptied the southern village into tents on the high ground, leaving its orchards untended.  Her father, the water clerk, had gone up with blankets and not come down.  On his board she had signed the order declaring the land abandoned, allowing the north to turn both villages' water into one channel.  What Ina remembered was not the signing but the relief of being able to promise her hungry little brothers there would be apricots again.  What Bet remembered, though it had happened before her birth, was theft. 

Year after year Ina had brought up proposals for sharing, to which the northern elders always replied that charity was theirs to grant or withhold.  This year, instead of another handsome statement of principles, she had brought a page out of the book of terrace law, blistered from the steam of her father's kettle.  It laid down the old rite for correcting a false entry: the clerk who made it must drink from the cup of the person wronged and surrender the board.  After that the new clerk would call the turns, and no clerk's kin could take water until the last house served had had enough. 

Ina's brothers were standing in the shade of trees that would cease to be theirs the moment she took a drink.  One had brought his picking ladder to mend during the speeches; he had never yet heard anything on this terrace that required the use of both hands.  When Ina called for the cup he set the ladder down. 

Bet examined her as she might have examined a belt brought in for the third time in a week.  "Does it mean I have to forgive you?" she asked. 

"It means you can tell me when to open the sluice and I have to go and do it." 

Bet dipped the cup in the northern channel and held her thumb against the split while Ina drank.  It was the gentleness Bet used on failing machinery, exactly enough pressure and none to spare; Ina knew better than to mistake it for affection.  Ina passed her the ebony board as the horn's shadow touched the nail, and the elders took off their hats before they thought what they were doing. 

Then Bet called the southern turn, and Ina went down the worn steps to the sluice, where her younger brother was already waiting.  She thought he had come to help her with the crank, but he had only come to return the key to the orchard shed.  She put it in her pocket and worked alone until the first brown rush cleared and she could see the old tiles under the water, blue with yellow fishes.  Whole stretches of them were missing, and in one place a fish's tail swam half a yard ahead of its head. 

From above came Bet's voice asking how long the water took to reach the last house, something no one living could now tell her.  Ina hung the shed key on the crank where Bet would find it and set off along the bank to see.
