After the egg hatched, Nell carried the wet rook into the icebound atrium under starlight and set it where its mother had broken shell.  She laid an oak acorn cap beside it, the only thing in the seed house for which she had never made a label.  As an heirloom seed saver she could put most of a garden to sleep in paper envelopes, but an acorn dried for storage was an acorn killed.  The cap had come from the deep shade of the sweet oak, which the town had burned by inches through the first terrible winter.  Nell herself had fed the saw. 

On warm nights before that winter, people used to lie beneath the oak to discover the texture of borrowed time.  When a rook hatched overhead, a pocket of the night on which its mother had hatched would open around the nest.  Someone always climbed up and put a hand in, and afterward the arguments would go on until dawn: silk rubbed the wrong way, the hide of a peach, a trout holding still in a stream.  Nell had been the child who stayed below to hold the ladder.  She had listened so hard she could still tell you who had felt the trout. 

Now a curl of air lifted the down on the chick's neck and went its way around the atrium, furiously tranquil, not disturbing so much as a flake of frost.  In the old textbooks it was called vortex inheritance, and there was a diagram of how the current did its work by decreasing entropy inside the circle while shedding heat beyond its rim.  It was one thing to see the neat red arrows and another to see the ash between the flagstones rise in filaments and knit itself into bark.  A root shouldered Nell aside with the old familiar indifference, and there above her were the low forks polished by the seats of children's pants. 

In the dark cup of her hand she felt a small insistence.  The cap wanted to go home.  Only instead of rising to a branch it dived into the leaf mold, where a mouse had tucked the nut from which it came.  She hooked a finger under the white elbow of the sprout and lifted; the nut went through her palm like a bubble through a net.  She sat back against the bole, surprised mostly by how much she wanted to cry over something that had never been promised her. 

Through her shirt the bark felt like washboard ridges, so exactly like them that she turned to see what idiot had been doing laundry up against a tree.  It was a saw cut, older than she was, puckered over at the edges; what everybody called the trunk was a shoot from a stump wide enough to dance on.  Somebody else had needed a winter's worth of wood, and somebody who knew more about trees than Nell did had left the stump uncovered. 

The rook sneezed, the circle came undone, and she was sitting on the iron lid the town had laid across the hole to make a level floor for cots.  At its edge a trickle of meltwater was disappearing with a soft, industrious gulp.  She put the bird inside her shirt and went to reopen the seed house, ringing the brass bell that usually meant there was something to give away.  This time she needed something: six backs, two bars, and anybody who remembered what they had done with the ladder. 

No one who came that night would ever quite agree on the color of the shoots they found flattened under the lid.  Some said candle wax and some said the yellow in an egg, and one old woman swore they were as pink as the palms of her hands.  Before they went home they took turns on the ladder knocking ice off the glass, until there was a clear square of sky for daylight to come through.  Nell held the feet steady with her boots, both hands busy feeding the rook little twists of meat that people passed her from their supper pails.  For the rest of her life she could call up the rasp of those old saw marks, the feeling of being neither forgiven nor condemned, just handed the next thing to do.
