After the story famine, when a whole supper table might fall silent over the question of what happened next, the city paid an orchestra to remember on its behalf.  Nell Vey copied the parts, ruling each stave with a five-nibbed pen and making the rests big enough for the old men at the back to see.  On Fridays the orchestra broadcast one of the nine surviving ballads, and people put saucers of house dust on their wireless sets.  Dust kept whatever it heard, so that a finger drawn through a saucer on Sunday could still raise a little music.  But it had learned to repeat the orchestra's mistakes as faithfully as its triumphs, including a squeak from a cello chair that now occurred in every kitchen in the city. 

Nell wanted to teach dust to tell stories, a different accomplishment altogether.  Her father had left her a saucerful from his workshop, and she could get nothing out of it but the steady rasp of a plane.  She knew he had built somebody a cradle because she had found a single curved runner, too slight for a rocking chair, among his effects.  She would have given all nine ballads to know whose baby it was. 

One evening she spilled some of his dust across a fresh part and swept it up before the ink was dry, taking three notes with it.  When the cellist reached the gap in rehearsal, his bow hung motionless above the string, but from the saucer in Nell's lap came three strokes of the plane.  Under her cupped hands the dust shone green; the secretly luminous stuff had been wasting its light all these years in broad day. 

That night she began copying a tenth ballad, setting down only the bits of her father's work song that she could almost remember.  She made its elusive lyrics recede at each return, the way a name will slip away just as the tongue prepares to say it.  She measured each rest in the dark, waiting for the green to touch her chin before singing again. 

She took her score and the workshop saucer down to the ember alcove, a niche in the gasworks wall where stokers once warmed their dinner tins.  In the mist off the river nobody could see the retired cellist waiting there with his instrument and the battered field set he used to call his brother on the fishing boats.  He had spent forty years counting rests for Nell and could be trusted not to fill one out of kindness.  She hung her coat across the niche to cloak the shortwave radio dial glow; an unauthorized broadcast meant confiscation, and the old man needed his set.  They waited for the Friday ballad to finish before beginning their pirate broadcast, so nobody would have to choose between a good song and an experiment. 

The old man played, and Nell sang of a tree with a fork in it and a bird with a nest in the fork.  At the first rest her father's plane went skimming over a board that must have been longer than the workshop, so long did the shaving curl.  At the second there came the shrill complaint of a saw trying to follow a curve.  Then she lost her nerve and sang the word baby into a space she had measured for two beats of silence.  From a hundred open windows along the water came the thin obedient echo of a baby crying.  She shut her mouth, sick at having put a child into all those empty arms. 

The old man took the tune round again without so much as lifting an eyebrow.  Thistles brushed the coat with a small dry sound, and this time she let the space stay open.  Out of her saucer came a knock, the squeal of the workshop door, and a voice saying, quite distinctly, that a boat that size would never float.  Her father laughed the laugh he kept for people who did not yet know what he could do.  Whether he had built it or the dust had dreamed it up for him, she wanted to hear about its maiden voyage. 

All along the river other saucers were speaking, not loudly enough for Nell to make out what they said, and she was glad of that.  Soon someone would ask her to copy down the tenth ballad exactly, baby and boat and all.  She would copy the rests.  She sat with the old man until the mist lifted, drawing a finger through the dust to feel the little hull rock.
