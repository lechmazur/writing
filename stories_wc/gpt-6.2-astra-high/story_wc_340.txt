The obituary wire allowed thirty words free, and at the bottom of the lift Captain Bell's widow had managed one.  It was his name, which the girl who worked the key would have sent for nothing anyway.  Nora, the servant deputed to carry his effects up to the wireless room, shut the gates with her hip.  The cage began its ten-floor ascent through the icebound spire, whose shadow at that hour lay several miles to the west on the untroubled backs of clouds. 

"What did he put on his eggs?" Nora asked.  When people could not tell her what a man had been like, she found they could generally tell her what he ate. 

"Mustard, till we lost the boy, and then I couldn't have told you if he'd eaten the shell," said the widow. 

Their son had gone down with a mail ship, and for eleven years practically everything said about his father had been a footnote to that fact.  Nora wanted something for the wire that had not come out of a wreck.  She tipped the captain's effects onto the bench between them: his airship goggles, his certificate, and the little red book in which he had done his sums. 

"I used to wonder what he was adding up," she said, and the widow laughed in spite of the excellent reasons she had not to. 

"Nothing ever came out even in this house." 

At the third landing the sun came over the sill and they both reached for the goggles, an old domestic reflex that nearly knocked the book off the bench.  Up here a person could get a headache just looking at a spoon.  The widow unclipped the smoke lenses and held them to her spectacles; Nora squinted through the red filters that the captain had used for fog.  On the open page the sums vanished, and a line of tiny gray writing rose up like stitches under a pulled thread.  She had dusted that book every Friday for eleven years without once suspecting that a man could keep a diary in plain sight. 

She fitted the filters over the widow's spectacles and turned the book away from the glare that silvered the pencil marks.  It was through scattered light, through the patient agency of all those little splinters of ice on the shaft windows, that they could read what he had written.  The red glass disposed of the red ink; as for the writing, it was the old weather shorthand the widow herself had taught him when they flew together.  She began to unfold the pages, which were really one long strip of chart paper tucked between two boards. 

"Oh, the sneak," she said with something like professional respect. 

He had been taking the weather indoors.  Alongside the date of their son's first missed birthday stood the sign for thunder diminishing, followed by a sketch of a saucepan.  The widow put a finger on it; as the sixth landing went by she tipped her head and Nora tipped hers to keep the light. 

"There was a leak over our bed and he put a pan on the quilt between us, and when we rolled together it got my nightdress all wet."  For the first time Nora heard what must have been the widow's voice on the radio, low and admirably clear in rough conditions.  "I said we might as well have gone to sea for our honeymoon after all." 

Farther along there were other signs for thunder diminishing, but never once the tidy circle that meant a cloudless sky.  Nora was glad of that; she would have disliked having to pretend that either of them had got their boy back for being good. 

At the ninth floor the widow handed Nora the book to keep it from springing open again and smoothed the wireless form against the shaking wall.  Underneath his name she wrote in letters as square as a pilot's on a manifest. 

"He flew the northern mail for thirty years and could still be surprised by water falling out of a saucepan in bed." 

As the cage settled level with the wireless room, Nora gave the book back, and the widow gave her the slip to carry the last few feet. 

"Mustard," she added, quite distinctly, "even after."
