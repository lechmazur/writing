At my mother's memorial I got as far as the dimensions of the quarry office trailer before my throat closed.  I had meant to tell the people on the folding chairs what it felt like to come off tour and see her yellow window from the switchback.  Instead I gave them eight feet by thirty and the year the county hooked up the drain.  Somebody started to clap to let me get down.  The opposite wall of the valley sent the applause back thinner, as if the dead had smaller hands.  I let myself into the trailer and shut the door on the second round. 

Over the little sink Ma had kept the notice she painted when the quarry first rented out its floor for concerts: LOST PEOPLE, COME IN.  I used to bring her whatever turned up when we struck the stage, wallets wet straight through and once a set of teeth nested in a paper boat of chips.  By the time I quit hauling amplifiers and started appraising antiques, I could pick up almost anything without thinking about the person it had come away from.  It was a skill until Ma died, and then it was the only thing I knew how to do. 

A woman in a good blue suit came in without knocking and put a scrap of speaker grille cloth on my palm.  She asked if I remembered cutting it.  I said the silver thread was actually aluminum, and that half the bands on our circuit had carried cabinets faced with the stuff.  Even to myself I sounded confidently uncertain, the voice that could wrest another week's research out of a client who wanted a signature by lunch.  She waited until I ran out of metal.  Then she turned the scrap over and showed me the crooked R in red ink. 

"Rosie," I said, and there she was, six years old and trying not to be sick on my boots.  Her dad had gone down under a rush of people at the end of a show.  Nobody would tell her anything, so Ma had sat her beside me with a busted speaker across our knees and asked her to pick out a patch.  Rosie had marked the piece she wanted with her initial, the way the men on the crew marked their tools.  I remembered the ambulance pulling out without its light and the absolute childish conviction that I could fix even that if only I got the patch on straight.  We never got our patch sewn on because the cops came in for her and took her away to the hospital with the silver square still in her fist. 

"I always thought he died," I said. 

"Good Lord, no," said Rosie, "he's outside trying to fold a chair with his foot." 

Through the flyspecked glass I saw a man in shirtsleeves engaged in precisely that ridiculous business.  She had come to ask if he could sit in here until the cars thinned out, because he still couldn't stand to be in a crowd when it broke up.  He had been prepared to stay home, she said, but he wanted to clap for Ma.  He'd asked her to bring the scrap in case I didn't know their faces. 

I cleared the appraiser's folding rule and my inventory sheets off the bench where small people used to swing their legs.  When he came through the door I nearly told him the weight the bench would bear.  Instead I told him about the yellow window, how the bus driver used to wake me for it even on nights when we weren't stopping but going on through.  He said that from the quarry floor you couldn't see the window until the stage lights went out, which was when you needed it anyway.  By the time the last car climbed the switchback we had got around to laughing about the teeth. 

Rosie offered me the silver square to keep, and for once I didn't feel obliged to slip a thing into a numbered envelope.  I had her hold it over the split in the screen door while I fetched Ma's needle and the spool of fishing line from above the sink.  Between us we got it on pretty straight.  On Saturday, when the next band came, the patch held against the moths that beat all night to get to my yellow window.
