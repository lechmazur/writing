After the lady of the fountain told Ada who had proposed drowning the city, Ada took her hand off the lady's knee.  Inside her copper helmet there was suddenly no voice but the little whine of air coming down from the pump.  Her grandfather had proposed it; the men who built the dam had only come afterward, with their levels and their big boots.  Above water there was a square named for him in which people gathered every year to sing about the city he had tried to save. 

Down here the forgotten garden of that city was busy forgetting him too.  Blind eels went in and out of the mouths of his stone lions like tongues trying out a speech.  On the only surviving street plan he had penciled a cross and the words, Ask this lady what ought to be said of me.  Ada had come for a sentence to cut into the base of his new monument.  She was the letter cutter in the family; it was she who would have to make a lie look good for three hundred years. 

She put her hand back. 

"You needn't smash one grandfather to make room for the other," the statue said inside her head.  "Try asking what he wanted to save." 

A current lifted the lady's skirt of weed and showed Ada a foxglove-stained glass ampoule wedged between two fingers of her lowered hand.  Her grandfather had carried just such little purple bottles on his rounds among the washhouses, where he treated failing hearts and delivered more babies than ever got put in the parish book.  Inside this one, tight against the glass, was a curl of something white. 

"He left his terms with me," said the lady. 

Ada pulled, and felt a pain so distinctly not her own that her own fingers opened.  Over sixty winters the stone knuckles had grown together around their charge.  The lady directed her attention to a hairline fracture running from the wrist into the palm; through that weakness Ada could thread the wire saw she used for cutting marble. 

"It will hurt less if you keep hold of me," Ada told her, without knowing whether that was true. 

"One of us will know when to stop," the lady answered. 

What came along Ada's arm with the first stroke was not pain but the smell of hot soap.  She saw the washhouses with their doors propped open and sheets of lye running down the gutter to the river that supplied the city's wells.  She saw her grandfather sit down on a doorstep beside a dead child and stay there while the afternoon shadows climbed his coat. 

"A clean lake," he said at last to the woman beside him, "and houses on the ridge, every one with a tap." 

The woman had needed both his hands to get to her feet. 

Ada felt the wrist give and stopped sawing before the hand came off.  When she eased the ampoule free she could see that what she had taken for paper was a tiny roll of linen worked in red thread. 

"His terms, but not his writing," said the lady, and for the first time Ada heard something like envy in that voice which had never had to draw breath.  "We spent a whole summer getting those thirty words small enough, and I could do nothing but hold the cloth taut." 

On the pump boat Ada broke the neck and unrolled the linen on her knee.  The washhouse women had specified houses not for every owner but for every household, including single persons and lodgers, with a separate key and water running at no charge.  The ridge had been built exactly so.  Ada herself had grown up taking both a key and a tap so much for granted that she had supposed the great fight of her grandfather's life to have ended in defeat. 

That autumn she cut no sentence of his into the monument.  She copied the linen instead, even to the backward S that somebody had picked out and done over; the ghost of it showed in her stone as it did in the cloth.  At the dedication a woman who had sung in the square all her life asked Ada whether they were meant to stop singing now. 

"Oh, sing," Ada said, and filled a cup at the foot of the monument and gave it to her for her throat.
