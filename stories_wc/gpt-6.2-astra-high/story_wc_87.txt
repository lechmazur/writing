On the morning they named the coastal kayak launch after him, Len found a little brass sun screwed to the sky above his head.  On the dedication panel he was forever holding his astronomical compass up to that sun, while six children in yellow kayaks strung out behind him like ducklings.  What bothered him first, absurdly, was that whoever had drawn the instrument had put the eyepiece at the wrong end.  He had come prepared to let the rest go by. 

Since the stroke people tended to gravitate toward his good side, the way guests at a party will cluster around the only person they know.  His daughter Nell stood on his bad side and did not offer to hold the compass case.  At thirteen she had whistled through the whole business that had made him famous, a merrily melancholic tune that the children still remembered better than they remembered the cold.  Five of those children, now variously bald and gray, had come to the launch; she was the sixth. 

The mayor wanted him to demonstrate how he had found the way home, and Len had practiced the one swift motion of opening the case against his hip.  After that he had expected Nell to help.  She always supplied the missing words at the doctor's or the bank, and he supposed, without quite admitting it, that she would supply the missing sun.  Only when the mayor beckoned did Len see what Nell had brought in the canvas bag between her feet.  She produced a tin whistle and planted two sticks with little crosspieces upright in the wet sand.  Then she stepped back among the other five and waited to see which version he would tell. 

No one had actually lied at first.  Coming in from the islands that afternoon, they had seen the grain elevator disappear from the horizon as completely as if somebody had taken an eraser to it.  Without a patch of sun or a single star, his lovely instrument was so much brass to ballast a boat.  He had been a broken navigator well before the stroke, at least for those twenty minutes when he'd kept saying the fog would lift and the little yellow boats began to scatter. 

Nell, who delivered papers along the waterfront, recognized the staggered arms of the utility poles looming out of the gray shallows.  Where the old ferry road went under at high tide, there was a pair with their crosspieces set at right angles, one for the road and one for the spur to the landing.  She had drawn the children after her with the whistle, navigating by utility poles the last half mile to shore.  To the reporter who asked if her daddy had brought everybody home, she had said yes, and tucked her dripping hand in his.  The backlash of regrets had come in mean little stings, like the smile Nell gave him every year when he handed her another clipping to put away. 

Now the mayor was bending down so Len could speak into a furry little bulb, and Nell, God bless her or damn her, was not coming up to rescue him again.  He got the compass out, and for a moment his hand went up toward the sun as obediently as the hand on the panel.  Then he put it down on the sand. 

"Useless," he said, and the mayor, with a sad, efficient smile, started to take the microphone away.  Len took hold of it with the hand everybody crowded around and pointed at the two sticks with the hand nobody trusted.  His finger wavered between them as it should, because that was the way through.  "She knew," he said. 

Nell did not rush up and hug him, although he stood there a little while in case she might.  She took the microphone and moved the sticks farther apart to show how the road had curved, and one of the bald children got up to hold a stick against the incoming water.  By the time she had them all in place, the mayor herself was standing ankle deep in the old road, shading her eyes to pick out the turn. 

Len sat down on the launch beside the case, not certain what they would do about the panel or if Nell would come sit with him afterward.  Presently she started up the tune, and when he found he could still whistle he took the low part that nobody ever wanted but without which the high part sounded thin.
