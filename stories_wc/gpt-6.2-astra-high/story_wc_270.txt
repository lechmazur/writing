During the reconstruction, when the lower baths were still full of pumice, my grandmother came home to find me practicing my limp.  I thought they wouldn't appoint a lame girl Keeper of the Boil.  She watched me drag the wrong leg twice, then sat down on the step and showed me how my mother used to do it.  That was how I learned my mother hadn't wanted the honor either. 

In the war Gran had been an insurgent, cutting the pipes that took our hot spring water to the governor's winter palace.  She had fought, she always said, so we could keep our own customs.  Now the council had come for the smallest of my mother's daughters, the one still young enough to take the graft.  The embryo in amber lay on a twist of cotton in the chairman's palm.  Set in the hollow behind my ear, that little curled willow would teach my fingers every valve between the mountain and the laundry sinks.  It would also wake me whenever the pressure rose, and if I tried to leave the valley it would turn my feet around.  My mother had made it halfway across the bridge to hear me sing at school before we saw her walking backward in her best shoes. 

Gran asked the chairman for the length of time it would take to heat a bath.  He gave her that much because half the town still called her Captain.  She took me to the boiling terraces, where women were fitting fresh boards over the channels that had scalded three masons since my mother's funeral.  There had been a teaching pool here once, where a cutting from the Keeper's graft grew in warm mud.  Apprentices could put their hands in the water and feel the tug of whatever valve needed turning.  Gran had sealed the pool when the governor started sending soldiers to dip their hands and learn which valves to seize. 

"I came back to unseal it," she said, "but the boy who set the lock never got to tell me how."  His last dispatch kite had gone down in the steam the night they took him.  She had spent her first morning home looking for it among the rocks.  Now she took off her mittens and laid her palms against a stand of coarse grass.  A willow splinter driven under her thumbnail in the wreck of the palace pipes had left her privy to the slow opinions of green things.  Mostly they concerned shade.  This time she asked about silk, which doesn't rot in our valley's mineral water, and presently we were up to our elbows in a drain.  Something in there had been keeping the grass roots from a lovely patch of silt for seventeen years. 

The kite came out with its ribs stove in and its long tail wrapped around my wrist.  Down the tail ran what looked like a string of little gallows, some with two crossbars, some with none.  Gran turned it sideways and I saw the laundry sinks as my mother used to draw them for me, a stroke for cold and a hook for hot.  The boy had been a laundry boy before he was a soldier.  I read the marks aloud while Gran worked the two brass taps beside the sealed pool: cold twice, both together, hot, both together again.  The stone lid lifted on a cushion of hissing water. 

By then the chairman was there with his cotton twist and half the women had left their hammers on the boards.  Gran held out her hand for the amber. 

"Nobody ever planted the whole thing," the chairman said. 

"No," she said, "and we might lose it, so you'd better all pay attention." 

She gave it to me to plant or keep, and I pushed it into the mud until I felt the shell give like a wet kernel of salt.  At first there was only the heat, almost more than my hand could bear.  Then I felt a banging in my little finger, and beside me a woman who had put her hand in too said, "That's the laundry elbow again."  She went off with her wrench, not running, just a woman who knew where she was needed and expected to be back for dinner. 

That winter we kept the old name, Keeper of the Boil, and painted it on a peg instead of entering it beside a birth.  Whoever hung a coat there took the watch.  The night of the school concert Gran took her coat down and put on my mother's shoes, which pinched her all the way across the bridge.
