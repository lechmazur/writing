After discovering aliens, Pelagos had to expand its school by exactly one desk, which was harder than it sounds.  The surviving passenger was the size of a piano, and the only place in our benthic city where she and six children could comfortably turn around was the drained ballast hall.  We moved my navigation class there and painted out the line on the wall that said FILL TO HERE. 

She called herself Minnow, having acquired from the dock crew an accurate grasp of English and a taste for inaccurate names.  As navigation instructor I had to put something under Temperament, so I wrote "balancedly curious," the highest mark in our quaint municipal vocabulary.  It meant you wanted to know what was on the other side of a door but would wait to find out which way the pressure ran. 

Minnow's stated ambition was to learn to navigate by starlight.  It was on the syllabus, but I hadn't taught it since the last patch of my native island slipped beneath the tides.  With no place left on the surface that I wished to go, I had made the rather large assumption that nobody else wished to go there either. 

I took my mother's boat anchor chain link out of the drawer where it lived with some obsolete coins.  She had used the last link of a wrecked trawler's chain for a star sight, and the cord she had spliced to it still had her tooth marks.  Holding the cord by one particular knot between her teeth, she could stretch it taut and see the sea touch the bottom inside edge and the pole star touch the top.  That was the latitude of our kitchen table, our mulberry tree, the step where we sat to take the scales off fish. 

At the board I drew an eye and two diverging lines and showed the children how the edge geometry worked.  Minnow asked if the knot would work for somebody whose mouth was considerably farther from her eyes.  It would not; before we went up in the lift we made each pupil a cardboard oval and fitted each with a cord of the proper length. 

It was beautifully calm on the roof of the lift, and for a while they all sat looking at the stars without making any practical use of them whatever.  Then Minnow brought out of her belly pouch a long strip folded back and forth as fine as gills.  I knew the stuff; it was cut from the tough white membrane that had kept her alive inside a hull otherwise full of cold salt water.  On the first fold she had drawn from memory the stars as they looked from the launching field on her world.  On the next was the sky at the first place the ship had stopped, where they had taken aboard a man who could mend practically anything except his own spectacles.  A whole company of people had once stood up from tables or workbenches to walk out under those skies and go with her. 

She laid those unfolded horizons along the wet deck, weighting the oldest down with my iron link and leaving a blank fold open at the end.  I finally understood that the question was not how to get her back where she came from, which was the question occupying the cleverest people in Pelagos.  She wanted to be able to come back here.  Even if we changed the pitch of our beacon, even if we fell as silent as the people she had sailed with, she would still know the place. 

We took the height of the pole star and knotted her cord, and I explained that the knot by itself would bring her only to a whole ring of possible homes.  She would also need a clock, and another lesson.  Meanwhile there was the matter of what should go under the stars, where in every previous panel she had drawn the face of whoever had come aboard.  The children wanted to be drawn at the rail with their sights, but Minnow drew us as she had first seen us, shoving desks through a pressure door. 

On the way down she gave me back the link, its old cord dark where the dry fibers had drunk a little of the sea.  It struck me that I could put the tree and the step on paper, and that I knew exactly where above them to put the star.
