At dawn in Pearl Bay I went down to the prism atrium in mist so thick I found the door by the chip my own file had left in its brass lip.  I had glazed that room three times, once for ordinary breakage and twice because the bank on which it stood was beginning its long slide into the harbor.  My last job was to take a likeness of the town for the waiting room up on the ridge, where by noon we would all be waiting for something.  The council had asked for the fleet under sail, and given me a sheet of their best silvered glass and a stone from the ballast of the first boat built here.  Even on a morning like that they wanted things done handsomely. 

We called these sittings noctilucent reckonings, for the dead gave off a light of their own, too faint to be seen once the morning star went out.  The atrium stood over a pond fed by rain, and its prisms let you look into the water without putting between yourself and the dead the discourtesy of your own face.  I set the silvered sheet in its runners above the pond and felt someone take hold of the other end.  It was Nell, my brother's girl, wearing his good jacket with the sleeves still turned up where he had turned them to wash dishes.  She wanted to see him at the wheel, she said, as he must have looked the night he went down.  All she could remember was the back of him at the sink. 

To commune, you threw the stone and read what came up through the patterns in the spreading rings; to keep what you saw, you lowered the silver till it just kissed the water.  After that there was no rubbing it out, as anybody who had ever tried to polish a dead face off a secondhand mirror could tell you.  Before everything shifts, I thought, let me at least get this one thing level, and put a folded tram ticket under the shorter foot of the frame. 

The stone made a noise like a cork going back into a bottle.  Out strode the fleet, sail behind sail, so white and sweetly curved that for a moment I was content to let anybody believe that boats went out and came back by themselves.  Nell had her hand on the lever when I noticed the little kinks between the long clean lines of the yards.  A glazier learns to read between lines, because what appears to be a single crack is often two, with a splinter riding loose and ready to cut the hand.  I asked her to wait, and tipped the nearest prism until we could look along those kinks instead of across them. 

Now we could see the backs of people bending, some to their oars, some to a table where bread was being cut and wrapped, one to a sink below a square of early sky.  Each small motion sent the boats a little farther out, though of course you cannot put wind in a sail by buttoning a coat around a child.  Nell put her fingers to the turned cuff and the man at the sink, without turning round, shook the water off one hand and reached back for a towel.  She made the motion of passing it, easy as breathing, and he took it from its peg where her hand would have been.  Then she leaned her whole weight on the lever. 

When we hung the glass in the waiting room the council chairman complained that he had to stand all the way back by the lavatories to see so much as a sail.  From his chair he had an excellent view of a woman picking weevils out of flour, whom he presently recognized as his aunt.  He brought his chair a little closer, scraping the new floor, and for the first time that day somebody laughed. 

Nell and I sat under the window drinking the weak tea they had managed to provide for so many, out of cups that had come up from the bay wrapped in people's spare clothes.  When we had finished she washed and I dried, a fair division of the work, and from where we stood neither of us could see a boat.
