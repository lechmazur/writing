Every year the harbor children put the same promise into the captain's mouth and sent it up in a paper lantern.  "I will come back for you," they read from the brass plate on the pier, and their breath made the words shine through the paper.  Elsie, the late-shift pier lamplighter, had been the child for whom somebody actually came back.  Whenever they asked her about the captain she said what she had said at seven: "It was too dark to see a face."  At seventy she knew precisely how much shelter a person who hides behind truth can find in one little sentence.  The captain had been her father, and for years the story on the brass plate was what got her mother out of bed. 

That autumn her granddaughter Kit was chosen to carry the lantern, and asked a question nobody else had thought to ask.  "Well, what could you hear?" 

Elsie took the hotel skeleton key tag off the chain around her neck and struck it twice against the iron railing.  Even after sixty-three years it gave one clear note with a smaller, wavering note tucked inside it.  "That was how we followed her along the passage when the lights went out," she said, and felt the girl's hand tighten on the word her.  "She left me the keys to ring for the next lot while she went below again." 

Below their feet the old hotel ship lay on her side in mud, her upper portholes plugged with concrete except for one the pier crew used to inspect the pilings.  Beyond that hole was the forgotten library where, before the sinking, passengers had gone to see what became of their luminous vows.  During the sky lantern release the library made a copy of every promise uttered aboard, whether you sent up paper or simply swore to somebody beside you.  Each year the copies flared again, taking their colors from whatever sounds had attended the making of the promise.  The ship had gone down on just such a night. 

Elsie had spent her working life passing that hole with a flame in her hand, afraid to take it through.  Now she wanted to discover the color of sound, of one sound in particular, before Kit had to stand up and read.  "Bring the tag," she said. 

As the first sparks drifted out over the black water, they eased themselves through the porthole onto the ends of the bolted shelves, now arranged like a narrow, rather slippery staircase.  Below the last shelf the tide was turning, lifting loose pages like the skirts of jellyfish.  Kit picked up a blank one.  "I'll tell them what we find," she said, and struck the tag against a stanchion as she spoke.  A stripe the yellow of a cut pear appeared on the sheet, edged with that faint bloom of pink you sometimes find around the seeds.  Underneath it stood Kit's promise in her own untidy hand, with her signature that always ran uphill. 

They searched by wandering purposefully, as Elsie used to do when the library stood upright, letting one patch of color lead them on to its near neighbor.  Tucked into the ceiling where a bubble of air had kept the plaster dry was a whole sheaf of pear yellow, every leaf pink at the heart.  Ten were children's promises to hold hands or keep moving, signed in the enormous script of those just learning their letters.  On the eleventh a woman named Ada Pike had written herself down forever: "I will come back for you."  Elsie handled that leaf less tenderly than the others; there was a stubborn corner and in her hurry she tore it getting it free. 

Above them the children were waiting for Kit, and the captain's lantern was still tied to the rail.  Kit started to peel the old name off, but Elsie handed her the paste brush and held the torn sheet steady.  "Leave the tear showing," she said; then, because at seven she had been excused from saying what she knew and at seventy she was not, she took her granddaughter's place. 

"When I said I couldn't see a face, you took that to signify it might have been his," she told the children.  "I let you think so, but it was Ada Pike who put the keys into my hand." 

Kit rang the tag twice as the lantern rose, and in the hush that follows a falling star they watched the little rent in the paper let out its needle of light.
