Sixteen years after the last human was born, he knocked on Ada's workshop door and handed her a photograph of another sixteen-year-old boy.  She knew the notch in the boy's eyebrow; she had put it there cutting his hair with ship's shears.  "Where did you get my brother?" she asked. 

"Behind the steel they took off the nursery wall." 

The Mercy was a recycled spaceship, a prison transport stripped down to make a hospital, and now they were taking the hospital apart to make houses on the yellow plain outside.  The boy, Kit, had been given the nursery steel for the roof of his own house, there being no conceivable use for a nursery anymore.  He had brought her the other photographs too, a whole congregation stuck face to face as if whispering.  She had searched for those prints until she knew the inside of every cabinet aboard better than she knew the face in her mirror. 

During the transport mutiny she had folded the infirmary into a pocket of space to keep the wounded out of reach of either side.  To push open a locked dimension you needed a hand on either side of the fold, and both hands had to be yours.  That was why the transport nurses taught shadow work: whatever part of yourself you refused to take with you when you left would still be there, still capable of answering your touch.  Seventy years she had been laying her palm against cold steel.  Seventy years her brother had lain on a cot within it, halfway through a breath. 

She took down Kwan Yin's Tear, the little drop of optical glass the nurses had salvaged from their smashed microscope and strung on a chain for her.  Through it she followed the faint shimmer of long-dried tears on faded photographs, the only tracks that led across a fold.  Each print carried a piece of the same dim ward, seen not by the camera but by the woman who had wept over it.  She showed Kit how to stitch the prints together edge to edge with infirmary silk until the tracks ran true from bed to bed.  Under the glass a blanket stirred.  Beside her brother lay the commandant who had broken his back. 

She had tended that man herself, had stood between him and the mutineers with nothing but a basin of his bloody water in her hands.  Even when a brass belt buckle opened her scalp she had not raised the basin to strike back.  She had believed the woman who stood there with the basin was the whole of her. 

Kit bent closer.  "Why are your feet off the floor?" 

Sure enough, there in the crooked panorama her shoes hung an inch above the deck, their laces trailing like dirty roots.  In Ada's family happiness diminished the body's gravitational weight; her brother used to need a lap belt when she read him the funny pages in bed.  She used to tease him that he could never keep a good secret. 

"The commandant was dying," she said.  "I had my thumb on his pulse, feeling it go, and I thought, there, at least he can't hurt anybody else." 

Nothing in the picture moved.  She could feel her soles coming free of the deck again, the small delicious tug of release. 

"No," she said, "it wasn't anybody else I was thinking of."  "I wanted him to hurt the way my brother hurt, and for a little while he did." 

Across the stitched ward a woman set down a basin and lifted a wet hand.  Kit carried the panorama against his chest all the way to the nursery, sheltering it from a wind that smelled of cut steel.  Ada laid it where the wall had been and put her palm against the print of that other palm.  She pushed, and the yellow plain gave back the smell of soap. 

They came out on their cots, one after another, rolling on casters so long unturned that each sang a different note.  Her brother finished drawing breath; the commandant on the next cot finished letting his go.  She covered the dead man before she let herself look at the living one. 

"Your feet," her brother said, and hooked a finger through her shoelace to keep her close.
