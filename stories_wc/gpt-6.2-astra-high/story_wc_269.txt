At the sea level monitoring station, Mara had a brass pin for every path that showed its back at low water.  Once a month she leaned out the kitchen window with her binoculars and moved the pins on a page torn out of the school atlas.  The page had been torn out by her son, who wanted to take their island with him to the mainland and then, at the dock, decided against it.  She had never asked him why.  She rather liked being allowed to keep the island without having to promise him anything about it. 

The station rode a little way out on its chains and came back, giving her first the eastern and then the western edge of the sill.  Three paths had gone missing since spring, though the water had risen only the thickness of the glass on her watch.  All three led to empty houses, but so did several paths that still lay white and straight as seams in a shirt.  After the fossil fuel ban, when the mail launch stopped running, those old walking paths had kept the outer keys in bread and company.  She wanted to discover why some disappeared before she promised the council it was safe to signpost the others. 

Mara had been the bleaching witness for the island's compensation claim, the one who took the same underwater photograph every Friday until even the skeptics could assemble the sequence and see the brown reef turn to bone.  She knew what could be learned by holding still, and also what a long while a person might waste that way.  On the lowest ebb in August she put the atlas page inside her shirt and stepped off the station ladder into water warm as spit. 

Where the first path should have been she found two parallel grooves in the mud, and in the grooves small square sockets.  Her grandmother had called the paths periodically eternal, a joke Mara had taken to mean that twice a day eternity got its feet wet.  Now she put her fingers in a socket and felt the clean corners left by a stone levered out whole. 

From the mist toward the village came three knocks and a space and two knocks more.  When she was small and too light to lift a paving block, her job had been to tap that rhythm on its surface with a cobble.  While she tapped, the women rocked the block; when it sat firm and gave back five identical notes, they let her call for the next.  She had heard the sound all summer from the station and put it down to somebody mending a doorstep. 

At the village slip her binocular view ended in a row of overturned boats; on foot she could go around them.  Behind the boats a low wall curved around the yard where the island children waited for the school skiff.  Her sister was kneeling beside a boy with a cobble, steadying his wrist; in the new top course Mara recognized a block with an M cut crookedly into it.  She had spent a whole ebb on that initial and been scolded for blunting a chisel.  Her sister saw her thumb travel down the cuts. 

"We did the empty ones first," she said.  "When the wash came over here last winter the little ones had to wait for the boat up to their knees." 

Mara thought of all those Fridays underwater, how faithfully her pictures showed what was leaving and how little they said about where anything went next.  She took the page from inside her shirt and laid it on the wall.  Instead of crossing out the path to her old house she bent the paper until the path lay against the school slip, and showed the boy how far his stone had traveled. 

The remaining paths would need signs that could be moved, and the council would have to ask the women where to put them.  She would write that up when the station swung in close enough for her to catch the ladder without getting her notebook wet.  For now her sister had a block that would not sit true, and the boy was holding out the cobble to her, blunt end first.  She took it and tapped, and by the fifth knock the stone had quit rocking under her sister's hands.
