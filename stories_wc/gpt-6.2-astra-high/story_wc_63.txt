At the landward limit of its swing, the clock tower pendulum bob caught a little upside down woman in its deepest dent.  Nell knew how short a stay the woman had there: a body could have no speed at all without being granted any time to rest.  Yet into that pause came the whole long glare of the tidal obsidian ridge, where even with your eyes shut you saw red.  She could smell the kelp cooking on the black glass and her father's wool cuffs drying on the stove.  She could smell, with a distinctness that made her mouth fill, the orange he had once brought home in his cap.  The orange had been hers, but not the money. 

In those days her father held the contract to rake storm weed off the causeway before the tide came up and made an island of the town again.  From the tower Nell watched his crew emerge from the cut in the ridge, dark bent figures that straightened one by one as they set down their rakes.  She was the local clock winder, precise enough to be trusted with the payroll book at sixteen, and precise enough to know what her father meant by a clean page.  It meant entering the time the last rake came clear of the cut, not the time the last cart was emptied at the public scales.  The men and women who pulled those carts were paid by the minute; the town paid her father by the ton.  All that summer there had been oranges, peeled in one long ribbon and divided to the very segment between father and daughter. 

She had supposed the crews knew nothing until the day old Kit laid a broken rake handle across her open book.  Along the pale wood, in letters so tiny she had to turn the handle in the light to read them, he had carved the names of everybody who had hauled a cart.  Under each name ran two rows of figures, what the tower clock had said when the last basket tipped and what the pay slip said.  Beside each tally he had left a space just wide enough for a thumbnail.  She had wanted him to shout, or break something that had not already been broken.  Instead he asked her to be careful of the splinters when she took it to the council.  Even now what scorched her was that when she had gone, she had worn gloves. 

The council took her father's bond, which covered a little more than half of what he owed.  For the rest he sold their stove and went to work pulling a cart, and she put up the fee she earned each month for winding.  When they had enough to settle with one worker she took that worker the money and a knife to cut a receipt into the wood.  Some invited her to sit; some counted twice and shut the door with her still standing on the step.  Kit made her tea so weak she could see the flowers at the bottom of the cup, and said that if she came again on Sunday there would be milk.  She went on Sunday, though he had already cut his mark.  It had taken two winters to fill the spaces, and considerably longer for her father to bring home another orange.  Once more they took half apiece, and the peel on the new stove gave off a sharp oil that stung her eyes. 

Now in the brass a second woman stood on her head beside Nell, with the red ridge running like a seam of fire through both their throats.  Kit's granddaughter had come up to learn the winding, and it was her first evening with the book.  Below them every rake was down, and the old handle lay across the open page to keep the sea wind from turning it.  The last carter had stopped halfway to the scales while somebody tied a strip of rag around his bleeding palm.  The girl's pencil lay on the sill, and both her hands were in her pockets.  There was no need for Nell to tell her to wait.  The bob swung back toward town.
