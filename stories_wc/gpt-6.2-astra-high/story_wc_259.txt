In the archive of the lost city of Senn, the children on the last ferry have been waving for forty years.  I am the one with a mitten in her mouth, and as the saffron-veil historian I am paid to keep us waving.  It is an easy post until a child comes in with wool between her teeth and asks what I was trying not to say. 

My granddaughter has my trick of biting down on an ugly wish because once it gets out it might come true.  This morning she told her father, who takes the dredger downriver tomorrow, that if he went she hoped he never came back.  I used to think this meant that wickedness ran in our family.  Now, looking at the wet thumb of her mitten, I find I would rather lose my post than let her think so too. 

At the heart of the archive stands a bronze prism pagoda, no higher than a man, built from the bells that once warned Senn of floods.  At twilight the sun shines level through its three glass doors and wakes the silt we scraped from the ferry's hull.  The atoms of nostalgia in that silt are real matter, not a priest's pretty figure; they collect on my veil and make it heavy enough to hurt my ears.  Each holds a speck of what happened and a skin of what somebody has since wished had happened instead.  Usually I let the skins join up into the bright film you can buy a ticket to see.  All of us wave, although in the crush there could hardly have been room for so many lifted arms. 

To purify the silt I must draw my veil across the doors in a steadily unpredictable motion, neither stopping long enough for the skins to stick nor letting them anticipate my hand.  By avoiding patterns a historian can recover even a whisper, which is why our charter permits this work on bells and timbers but never on a human voice.  The dead cannot put a hand over the mouth of the living. 

I take the girl inside the pagoda anyway and ask her to move my elbow whenever she pleases.  The light comes off the silt in curls like apple peel, and underneath is a dirty boat with a child in it screaming at her mother to drown.  It is an immense relief to hear the actual words, so much smaller than the space I have had to make for them. 

My mother on the landing takes out her teeth.  She does it furtively, turning her back to the other parents who have just put their children aboard the only thing in Senn that still floats.  Then she faces me again and makes the dreadful toothless fish mouth that has got us both into trouble at funerals.  I had forgotten how the fish had to look surprised at its own existence or it was no good.  Beside me the girl lets out a snort, and from the silt comes its exact echo, four decades late. 

For years I have carried my childhood up to that jetty and my grown life away from it, with nothing to carry across the break but a curse.  Now I get to take that ridiculous fish with me too. 

My hand slips and I hear, behind my mother's back, a man begin to tell somebody where he has hidden something.  I drop the veil before he can finish.  The curator will know what I have done by the white streak of clean silt, and there is time while the light lasts to smear it over.  Instead I press the seal of my office into it, so nobody can say the child was at fault. 

Outside on the steps she asks if my mother would have made the fish even if I had kept my mitten in. 

"She might," I say, "but what I know is she heard me and made it anyway." 

We sit until the bronze goes dark, practicing the surprised look, because she wants to get it right on the dock tomorrow.
