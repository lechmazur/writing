At noon the rescue boat came home three times, once with Nell's brother aboard, once with his cap, and once with neither.  All afternoon the crews tried to land on the same few feet of stone, their oars going through one another with the soft resistance of needles through wool.  Nell sat crestfallen on the harbor steps with her paint case between her knees.  She had made her living getting a likeness in a single sitting, and now she had got her brother so exactly that his little girl kept wading out to him.  Each time the child came back with an armful of cold light. 

"Who could blame a woman for wanting to know?"  The harbormaster meant it kindly, but Nell had always questioned rhetorical questions, the way other portrait artists questioned a mouth that smiled too readily. 

"Is that the same as saying it wasn't my doing?"  She gave him the little girl's wet hand and went back up to the lighthouse.  The beam went over her like a sleeve of warm silk, and three shadows went before her through the door. 

The forgotten library inside the lighthouse had once lent out futures the way other libraries lent out books, for a strictly limited term.  A pilot would prop a tarot card in its box lid under the lens to see where his ship might come to grief; one sweep later the librarian shut the box, and the sea was merely the sea again.  Nell's mother, the last librarian, had taught her to mend the faces as the salt ate them away.  That morning, frantic for news, Nell had painted her brother onto the Fool and lifted the lid too fast.  The silk hinge had given, spilling the Hanged Man and the Moon into the light beside him. 

Now three versions of the reading room were trying to occupy one foundation, and the walls were closing in.  Already the table had buckled, pinching the broken tarot card box between its halves.  She took hold of the Fool by one bright corner and heard the child down on the steps cry out as though her father had been hurt.  She let go.  To take away any one of these futures in sight of the others was to make a death of it.  She needed to get below the reach of the beam, into the necessary darkness where a painted man could be no more than pigment again. 

The table stood over the cellar hatch; below it the three rooms still shared one patch of absolute black.  As a girl she had watched her mother lower heavy books through that hatch in a canvas sling.  The box was canvas too, one square cut at the corners and stitched up with silk.  She could redesign it by untangling threads, not adding them.  On hands and knees between the advancing shelves she teased apart the corner seams, easing the freed panels under the cards without cutting off their light.  Each loose length of silk she hitched to a corner, carrying the ends around the back of her neck as her mother had done. 

When she straightened, all three futures lifted together, bright enough to show her the child's fingerprints on her brother's painted cheek.  The shelves touched behind her just as she got her foot on the cellar ladder.  She went down looking up, the sling held level with her face so that she could watch the gold leave his skin.  At the last rung she wanted terribly to strike a match.  What harm could one more look do?  This time she knew precisely what to answer, and she stood there in the dark until she felt the canvas go slack. 

From the landing came the knock of an oar against stone, and then one small furious voice asking for news.  Nell climbed out carrying three rectangles of ordinary pasteboard.  At the landing there was one boat with a smashed bow and her brother's cap in the bottom.  They had heard him calling from the rocks but could not get their broken boat out again.  She had rowed that channel with her brother long before anybody paid her to paint.  She kissed the salt parting in the little girl's hair, put her paint case down, and took the bow oar in the next boat out.
