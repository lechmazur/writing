At midnight the last ferry leaves, and for twenty minutes Bellport belongs to the people who cannot sleep.  My mother used to spend that interval at the kitchen window, documenting wind patterns between buildings in the margins of the tide tables.  She could tell by the flutter of a curtain above the pawnshop when the laundry on our roof was about to go horizontal.  I thought this a rather small accomplishment for a woman who had pulled men out of the sea. 

Then one night she came down on a cable into the towering labyrinth of shipping containers behind the ferry slip, and came home six months later with a hand that would not close.  I was an accident investigator for the railway, which disqualified me from investigating the fall on two counts but did not prevent me from reading the report to her.  She made me read the words "singular convergence" twice.  A gap had opened between stacks at just the place where a descending gust met the sideways wash of the hovering aircraft.  The load on the cable, which was what my mother had become in the report, had swung like a bell clapper. 

"Singular means they can go on doing it," she said. 

The port invited her to the first drill after the suspension, intending to present her with a framed commendation before the crew went out to lift a dummy from the same alley.  She asked instead to be allowed to conduct the drill, and asked me to bring her old flight engineer's patch, the one with the red mitten hauling on a rope.  Her crew had designed it after she lost a glove on a rescue and finished the job wearing a fisherman's mitten.  I had expected to pin it on her good sleeve.  Instead she laid it on the dispatcher's map to stand for the aircraft, since her fingers could slide a thing they could not pick up. 

"Show me where you'll be when you call it off," she told the young engineer who had taken her place. 

He slid the mitten right up to the black square that represented the dummy.  They had given him high marks, he said, for rapidly adapting to changes in the behavior of a load. 

"I'll bet they have," she said, without moving the patch. 

We went up on the dispatch roof to survey the yard, silver in the mist except for the orange lights that swam at the corners of things.  Across the water the ferry bell struck twice and the engines in the slip fell quiet.  Only after the town goes silent can you hear the canvas awning of the pawnshop lifting and settling on its frame, a sound like somebody shaking out a newspaper.  The engineer turned toward it before either of us did.  Above the awning a pale curtain sucked itself flat against a screen, and a little later a twist of packing film rose from the alley where the dummy lay.  Nothing yet stirred the yellow streamer they had tied to its boot.  He took out his watch and counted until the streamer stood straight out, then looked across at the pawnshop as if he had just discovered a second hand on a familiar clock. 

Downstairs the dispatcher had already put a finger on the switch that would send the crew running to the pad.  The young man put his palm over the red mitten and drew it back to the slip, where there was room to bring a stretcher out on wheels. 

"I'll need two people on the ground," he said, and for the first time that night my mother looked tired.  She sat while they wheeled the dummy out past the last stack, its silly yellow ribbon hanging limp again by the time they reached open ground.  I thought she might want the patch back, but she left it on the chart for the next shift to move. 

All the way home I kept noticing things a little before I needed to, the lift of her elbow before a step, the turn of her good hand toward a rail.  Once I put out my arm in time for her to take it without asking, and once, in time, I let her walk alone.
