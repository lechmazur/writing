At dawn in Fig Court, the first thing the city men did was hang a yellow number on the locked mailbox.  Around it the breakfast trade went on, apricot jam shining in its saucers and a finch in a green cage doing the coffee grinder's squeak exactly.  The arch was coming down before it fell on somebody, and everything fixed to it would go in the salvage truck. 

Mara came out of her thrift store carrying the velvet tray on which she sorted good silver from the beautiful lies.  Her mother came as far as the sill.  From her fig stall opposite, her mother's sister watched the men lay out their tools.  The two women had conducted business across those twelve yards for twenty years without once having to hear each other's voice. 

When the mill closed, the courtyard workers had slipped their bus money in pay envelopes through the mail slot above that box; somebody had to go to the capital to press their claim for back wages.  Mara's mother had unlocked the box in front of everybody and put her hand into nothing.  There had been just the one key.  It was on the same string around her neck this morning, the cotton gone gray against her brown skin. 

Mara wanted to know what five minutes of hearing each other out might do for them, after twenty years of the other thing.  In the shop she could tell a mended cup by the note it gave her fingernail; you learned nothing by telling porcelain how it ought to ring.  She set the tray under the slot and asked her aunt to show her how it had been that day. 

"I had no money," her aunt said.  From beneath the figs she brought out a flat stone, the weight she used on the paper bags when the wind got up.  She wrapped the stone in paper and slid it through the slot.  "I put my wedding locket in like that, for your mother to pawn." 

They heard the packet go down with a dry swish and then, well to the left of the iron door, a knock.  Mara picked up her tray and followed her aunt to the other side of the arch. 

"There used to be a shelf here for the night porter," said the man who ground the coffee, coming along with his little cup.  "My father slept on it once and went home with a receipt for three trunks stuck to his cheek." 

The jam seller remembered her father sealing the shelf up when they made the porter's lodge into a room for her and her baby.  "Only did the front," she said, tracing a long rectangle in the plaster with a sticky thumb.  "We needed every inch." 

Mara moved her tray to the bottom of the rectangle and nodded to the city man with the thin bar.  It took very little to bring the plasterboard away; out came the stone and a flat brown fan of envelopes that the tray was hardly big enough to catch.  In the old days the porter had only to tip a little wooden tongue to send the mail down to the locked box or sideways onto his shelf.  The tongue was still tipped toward the shelf. 

Her mother picked up the smallest packet, eased the worn locket out of its tissue, and pressed the catch that a stranger would have had to hunt for.  Inside were not bride and groom but two girls in identical blouses, their hair wet from the same comb. 

"He never believed I gave it away," her aunt said.  "All those years he thought I had debts." 

Mara felt the old argument rising like grit in her mouth: whose fault was that, and who had been free all along to walk across the court?  Her mother sat down on the curb and shifted over to make room.  "What else did he say to you?" she asked. 

Around them the men went on unbolting iron, and the dust moved in long gold sheets through the level light.  Mara carried the tray indoors by herself; her aunt was still talking when the finch began to do the socket wrench.
