The last petal had dropped from the old rosebush, and on the platform at Bellwater the youngest of the orphans was trying to get a wheelchair through the ivy.  At seventy, Kit still hated being the youngest; it meant he had to do all the pushing.  His passenger, the historian Ada Vale, had brought the register back to the disused station on her lap. 

Every summer they gathered here after the last petal fell because the woman who put them on the train had said the roses would be over by the time their mothers came.  Only nine had come this year, counting Kit and Ada, and nobody looked down the track anymore.  A catering student from the town had put out cups and a jug of water as if for the end of a race. 

Kit parked Ada beside the old parcels scale, where there was enough level iron to support the register without straining its rotten spine.  She opened it to the illuminated letter O, a wreath of little red and yellow houses which began the word Orphans.  The rest of the page was blank except for the border inspector's stamp and a figure in violet pencil: 46. 

As a girl Ada had thought the town inside the O was where a person went to live when she died.  She had since spent forty years drawing parallels between things usually filed in different buildings, such as a landlord's list of arrears and a schoolteacher's record of absences.  It had occurred to her rather late to do as much for herself. 

From under the register she took the fire brigade's survey of the lost quarter across the border, drawn before the reservoir went in.  Kit bent over it and found the house with two outside stairs, one for each sister after a quarrel he could still recapture in all its magnificent obscenity.  He found it again in the O, six houses along from the bakery with the stork's nest.  The painted street followed the surveyed one until the painter ran out of room and curled hers back to meet the bakery. 

Kit touched the stamp.  "Well, we knew we weren't orphans," he said. 

"We knew it here," said Ada, laying a finger on his temple, "but at three in the morning I have generally had my doubts." 

She showed him what she had seen under magnification: the dots on the door lintels were the same violet numbers the inspector had penciled on their cardboard tickets.  Her own ticket had been buried with her foster mother, who wanted something in the next world to remember all her children by.  But Kit carried his in the window of his wallet, behind the picture of his wife. 

"Eleven," he said, and there it was on the left of the double stairs, so small he had to borrow her glass. 

The border had been closed to families but open to unclaimed children under the treaty, and they had all been raised on the story of the kindly inspector and his noble lies.  In his memoir he said their mothers had understood that adoption was the price of passage, a price exacted not by him but by history.  That last word particularly annoyed Ada, who knew what a pit of lies it could be and how little anybody thanked you for hauling out even a cupful of truth.  Still, she could not tell Kit who had drawn the houses or what either sister understood when she let go of his hand. 

"Then all you've proved is somebody thought we might need directions," he said. 

"Yes," she said, and watched him work out the direction in which those directions ran.  His finger went up the left stair, came down, went up again. 

"I'd been thinking all these years she was meant to come to me," he said. 

Across the platform the student was gathering up the cups; the others had begun to go home in twos, as old people do after the formal part of anything is over.  Kit called them back, asked for their numbers, and with each answer his voice lost a little of the special patience he used for speaking to the old.  When he came to Ada she said she didn't know, and he said to try twenty-two, on account of her having sat between the twins. 

She found a door under a little red balcony and felt nothing at all until she saw the white stroke the painter had put on the railing.  It was a towel, if she remembered right, to keep a child from burning her hands on the iron in summer.  She wrote that down in pencil.
