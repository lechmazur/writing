The consent form had a line for what I hoped to recover, and on it I drew a little boat.  I was the first healthy person they had let into the sound wave garden, if you could call a person healthy who had begun to remember her own birth from the wrong end. 

The clinic had used my sleeping brain to keep my mother's memories alive while they cut a tumor out of hers.  Afterward she knew what it was to be picked up, slick and purple, by the heels; I knew what it was to look past those purple heels and see a clock.  Being an experimental treatment guinea pig had seemed a small price to pay for getting her back.  Now each of us would sometimes sit waiting for the other to feel hungry. 

The garden was where doctors went to prune entangled memories, and the doctor said there was very little time left before our two appetites, among other things, became one.  She put a cap on each of us and gave us our mallets and told me to play whatever grew.  At first there were only reeds, bars of green light that bent toward whichever of us was breathing out.  Strike one and it sang back a scrap of the life we had lived together.  Whoever recognized it first was supposed to claim it, and the other was supposed to let it go.  My mother claimed the clock and I claimed the heels. 

Between us appeared an auric lagoon under starlight, with one little boat knocking against a post.  Neither of us reached for it. 

In our basement flat the pipes had kept up such a din that the only way to put me to sleep was to give the noise somewhere to go.  My mother made a boat out of the knocks and a sky out of the pinholes in the blind, and I made the bilge water gold because we were rich down there.  I used to perch on her hip as she carried me from pipe to pipe, drumming on everything, asking the water to take us a little farther tonight.  My mother had always been quietly rebellious about what belonged to whom; in a building where nobody owned so much as a doorknob, she had made me the gift of an inland sea. 

The doctor said we could keep the boat or the water but neither one of us could keep the whole.  I had drawn the boat because I wanted us both to wake up still able to build it.  Across the gold slid ribbons of a color I had only seen on the undersides of my mother's eyelids, that sad northern green of the country she left before I was born.  She was letting go of the boat.  Already I could see the bottom through its boards. 

I didn't want to inherit a place where she could never come again.  I turned my mallet over and tapped its handle on the post, not the old boarding signal but the three uneven knocks the night nurse gave before she brought our pills.  My mother laughed and answered with the rattle of the little paper cups.  That sound belonged to her bed, not mine; from my end of the ward the pills arrived on rubber wheels that squeaked.  I played her the squeak. 

On the black shore opposite the golden one a landing stage grew out from between the reeds.  The doctor leaned over her board, her hands quite still; for the first time two lines were moving on it instead of one.  We were not remembering now. 

My mother lifted her mallet and held it high, the way she used to hold her spoon before the last great thump on the heating pipe.  At the hush of the universe inhaling, as she called that instant before a beat, I felt a pang low down and knew it was mine.  On the other shore my mother lowered her arm and waited, not feeling what I felt, not knowing what I would do.  I asked if she had anything to eat.  She sent the boat across with a single bright orange rolling in its belly. 

When they took our caps off I could no longer remember the color of the clock, but I could tell my mother what it cost these days to buy an orange.  She asked me to buy two.  At breakfast we sat peeling them for each other, each making a boat of the skin.
