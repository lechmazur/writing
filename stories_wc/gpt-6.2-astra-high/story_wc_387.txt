During firefly season, on the first clear evening after a rain, we took our dead down to the harbor in the shape of wooden bowls.  My mother was pearwood, worn thin on one side where she used to drink.  I had sent her to sea six times before I thought to ask my father if he would like a turn.  Or rather, that was the polite way I put it when I meant to ask why he never came. 

He was our net-mender philosopher, a man people consulted about marriage because he would go on working until they had answered their own questions.  That evening he was sitting below the seawall with a length of mesh that shone wherever his hands had been.  "Somebody has to be here," he said, which was true, and was not what I had asked.  "Just this once," I said, "I would like to know what it costs you to stay down here instead."  He moved his oilskin off the thwart to make room for me. 

Above us ran the whispering parapet, so called because after rain its little stone throats went on emptying into the harbor long after the gutters inland fell silent.  Along its ledge the families were lining up with their bowls, some newly carved and so light they had to be held against the breeze.  My aunt was waiting at the steps with the kettle and my mother's bamboo tea whisk tucked through her sash.  We whipped a little tea in each bowl before we launched it; nobody we loved had ever been sent out hungry.  "May I trouble you for my usual place a little later?" I called up, as if I had reserved a table at an inn.  My aunt, who could spot insolence at fifty paces, took the whisk from her sash and bowed. 

Everybody knew my father caught the bowls before the tide took them, but I thought that meant stretching a net across the harbor mouth and waiting.  He showed me how to steer by following moon paths, the smooth streaks of silver among the little hammered scales of light.  Each smooth streak ran out of a separate drain, and any bowl that entered one would ride it straight past our net into the piles beneath the quay.  We had to get there first.  I began to see the hidden patterns he had spent his evenings learning, even how the fireflies winked out in patches when a breath of wind came off the reeds to push a bowl astray.  "Scintillatingly somber," the schoolmaster had called the ceremony in his annual address, and from up there perhaps it was.  From down here it was mostly my wet father trying not to mash his fingers between somebody's grandmother and a post. 

We shipped the last bowl with a chip of blue paint on its lip, my aunt's signal that the others had all gone out.  All but one.  When I laid my mother's bowl on his knees he put his thumb in the worn place and held it there.  "I always get to lift her out," he said, with the astonishment of a man who has only just heard his own excuse.  "Who says you shouldn't get to let her go?"  He glanced at the running water, and this time I could answer his question without making him wait. 

I put two fingers in my mouth to whistle my aunt down to the landing.  She stepped aboard without a word and put the bamboo whisk in his hand, damp and warm from all the tea it had made.  While he climbed the steps she took the oars and I paid out the net, letting it sag a little as I had seen him do to spare the paint.  Over the whisper of the drains I heard the quick familiar scrape of bamboo against pearwood, a sound I had not heard from his hands since she died.  The bowl came spinning toward me with a little collar of green foam, and there on the steps stood my father with his two wet hands still cupped.  I caught her before she could strike the post and held her up until his hands came apart.
