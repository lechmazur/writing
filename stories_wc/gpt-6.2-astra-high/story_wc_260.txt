By the time Mara carried the subscription board out to the workshop, the hall was empty except for a boy picking up the little pencils.  An hour ago he had stood on a chair and put his finger on the chameleon's tail tip in each of the twelve rescue photographs.  Whatever color the animal was, however burnt or battered the forest behind it, the last inch of tail bent sideways like a spoon somebody had tried to pry a lid with.  It was a good eye, and Mara had told him so in front of everybody, including her father.  She was the wildlife handler; it had fallen to her to say that yes, this was one animal, and no, it had never been in a fire.  Now the boy wanted to know if they still needed the pencils.  She held out her apron for them. 

Lake mist stood knee deep in the open workshop, where her father made his scenery, and through the bare rafters of the unfinished roof she could see stars.  On the workbench a shaded lamp lit the subscription board with its dozen thicknesses of paper, one campaign pasted over another as neatly as bark.  Each time the sheet filled up with names and sums, her father had photographed it for the hall of honor and taken it back to paste on a fresh one.  The photographs in the hall were too small to read the names.  To reconstruct the accounts she would have to get back to the beginning by shedding layers, without losing what was written on either side of a join. 

He brought her the kettle and the broad thin knife he used for lifting painted waves off painted seas.  "Flour paste," he said, and she heard in his voice his old pride in making things that came apart to be made into other things.  The first sheet came loose at one corner and rolled slowly back, quietly vivid on its underside where the red ink of the sheet beneath had taken in reverse.  They laid it on the drying rack where he usually set the little trees. 

What hurt was finding her own name halfway down the next sheet, beside a week's pay.  She had given it to start the others giving, knowing perfectly well who the chameleon was, telling herself the money went for splints and mealworms all the same.  Under her name came the baker's, and under his the woman who came to sweep up the flour and took her wages partly in bread.  Her father touched that line with the back of his nail. 

"I could sell the lamp," he said. 

She had expected an argument about all the living things that owed their lives to him, and had been looking forward to winning it.  Instead she found herself explaining that nobody would give much for a lamp with a cracked chimney. 

From the warm cage indoors came a dry scratching, the sound of a foot searching the wire for a branch that ought to have been there.  She went in and held a stick level until the small deliberate feet had taken hold; then the crooked end of the tail went round it, making an extra turn for purchase.  It was a perfectly serviceable tail. 

At the bench she took one of the boy's pencils and wrote on a scrap the words she meant to say at every door, before either of them could point to a single mended wing.  "I knew whose picture it was when I put my money down." 

Her father read it and sat down beside the kettle to wait for the next sheet to soften.  By dawn they had the names laid out to dry in twelve long rows, and had sold nothing and been forgiven nothing.  She put two pencils in her pocket in case a point broke, and he got his coat for the walk to the baker's.
