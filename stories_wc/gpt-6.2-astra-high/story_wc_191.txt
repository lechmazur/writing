The unclaimed throne stood on the dock with a ticket tied to one arm and a gull sitting on the other.  Of all the furnishings taken off the generation ship, it was the only thing nobody on the new planet wanted, not even for parts.  Its blue plush smelled of pennies, and its brass feet were four little wheels that faced the wrong way for rolling across a floor. 

Alma, the ship's last lore keeper, had put it down as a throne herself, on the authority of a song her mother used to sing.  Up the stairway of ascension, on a throne of royal blue.  She had never asked for the next line while her mother still had breath to sing it.  That week her mother had asked to ride the stairs once more, the first thing in months she had admitted wanting. 

Now the tugs were giving them until sunrise to get clear, and her mother sat beside the gangplank in a hospital cart, watching the gull. 

"It went sideways," the old woman said. 

"What did?" 

"Me." 

With two hours left to find the stairway of ascension, Alma had been about to make a sally through the engine galleries on the strength of a rumor about spiral steps.  Instead she tipped the gull off the chair and turned it over.  There in the grease on one wheel shone a ruby flake, a bit of the crushed gemstone the founders had mixed into the nursery paint so that spaceborn children would know what strawberries looked like in sunlight.  Her mother had been a nurse there for forty years. 

"Will you show me?" 

"Get me a blanket." 

Alma was wisely adventurous in the manner of people who have taken children camping: she also packed water, biscuits, a torch, and the spare cylinder for her mother's oxygen.  They went aboard with the throne riding upside down on the cart, its wheels spinning at every bump like the feet of a delighted beetle. 

In the nursery the bunks were gone, leaving pale rectangles on walls that had been cream for the last hundred years.  At the height of a child's thumbnail Alma found a little nick of red.  She lifted an edge with the auction ticket, and through the peel of paint layers the hidden murals of the children's ward came back into the light.  First came a painted pear, then a branch bent under its load, then a nurse in a white cap holding out her apron to catch the fruit.  Alma remembered that apron against her cheek, the starch going soft where she had cried into it.  Without quite knowing what she was doing she pressed the painted pear, and the wall folded inward on greased hinges. 

Water still came down behind it in the old glass irrigation tubes, a kaleidoscopic waterfall turning slowly in the beam of her torch.  Beyond the water lay a hidden orchard, planted in terraces so steep that every tree had its own patch of artificial sky.  Up the middle ran a flight of steps with a toothed brass rail on either side.  Alma set the little wheels in the grooves, facing across the slope, and felt them catch. 

"Which way did you ride?" 

"Always uphill with a baby, downhill empty." 

At the top she could just make out the round quarantine window through which parents had been permitted to see their sick children.  She had catalogued it from the other side as a porthole overlooking a disused tank. 

Getting her mother onto the throne took both of them and a certain amount of language not generally addressed to royalty.  Her mother patted the plush beside her, but there was no longer room for Alma on her lap.  She walked beside the chair instead, winding the crank, while the little wheels made the noise she had heard for years in dreams and taken for the sound of someone sharpening scissors. 

Halfway up, her mother gave her the rest of the song. 

"Here's the sky your daddy sees, and here is daddy seeing you." 

Through the round window the dock lamps hung like tired yellow moons; in the glass Alma saw her own old face beside the one that had once been the whole of safety.  She wanted to say so, but her mother was asleep.  Before she took her down, Alma filled both pockets with pears. 

When the tugs came they found the throne on the dock again, with two women under one blanket and the ticket turned over.  On the back, in the lore keeper's neat black hand, was a single word: Claimed.
