After the last lighthouse keeper retired, the town laid out an arboretum on his seaside cliff and let couples marry there for the price of a tree.  A brass tag on each new trunk recorded the two names, even when, as sometimes happened, the marriage did not outlast the stake.  Mariam took the wedding pictures and, in winter, went around loosening the ties so the trees could go on getting fat.  She was quietly talented at getting people to look like themselves, chiefly because she knew what to do with their hands.  She gave nervous grooms a handful of dirt to tamp down around the roots and let the brides decide whether to risk their sleeves. 

Only when her daughter Leila booked the little kitchen in the keeper's cottage for her reception did Mariam discover what a good hiding place a camera was.  Leila put a crumpled photograph on the table and smoothed it with the heel of her hand.  It showed a courtyard an ocean away, a bride with one eye shut against the sun and a small girl crouching to buckle the bride's shoe.  Six people in that picture, including the bride, had died in the drone strike before the musicians could unpack their instruments.  Mariam, who had taken it, and Leila, who had never managed the buckle, had lived. 

"If anyone asks me where you were that day, I have to put my finger on this side of the paper," Leila said, turning it over.  "I want one with you on the front." 

Mariam almost said there was nothing she wanted more, which would have left her daughter to puzzle out why she kept polishing the same clean lens.  Instead she described what happened when somebody arranged her in a group and told her to freeze.  Memory came back through somatic markers, the clinic woman had explained; for Mariam that meant a copper taste and knees already bent to take the weight of a falling child.  From behind the camera she could put a border around what she saw and keep the rest out. 

Leila didn't tell her it would be different this time.  She knew the value of a hope small enough to test.  "How about if we don't hold still?" 

They spent the afternoon in the kitchen taking pictures of their hands with the camera propped on the bread bin and the timer set for ten seconds.  In one they were peeling an orange, in another passing the bright coil of skin between them.  At supper they got their faces in, though all that could be seen of Leila was the tip of her nose going into a bowl of lentils as she laughed. 

On the wedding morning a hard wind came off the water, turning the leaves of the young whitebeams silver side up.  Leila had chosen one for the way it showed the weather acting on it, and now it lay on a square of burlap beside its hole, flashing like a whole netful of fish.  Once the couple had said their vows and bedded the roots, the guests took turns patting down the dirt.  Mariam set up the tripod and showed the groom where to press.  An uncle who had been asked to move out of the frame called cheerfully for everybody to freeze. 

The copper flooded her mouth.  Leila, already on her knees beside the hole, just reached up a dirty hand and offered her the end of the tie.  Mariam knelt and worked the soft black rubber around the stake and then around the slim trunk, giving each its separate loop.  Inside the work there was a pearl of stillness, no bigger than the space her thumb was keeping between the tree and its support.  She could taste metal and feel the give of the rubber at the same time.  She held the tie while her daughter fastened it. 

In the picture the groom took, both women are looking down, and the uncle's hand reaches in from the edge to hold the leaves out of their eyes.  On the back of her copy Leila wrote the date, the name of the tree, and her mother's instructions to come back in November and see how much room it had left.
