The morning my mother hired me, she paid the stranger's rate, right down to the penny for not looking through the cloth.  She put the money on the lighthouse step and stood on it with one boot until I agreed to take it.  Behind her on the beach, the families were sitting around the dent their campfire had made in the sand.  By custom nobody came up to me until the last spark died; as long as there was something left to tend, you tended it.  Now dawn was turning the tower pink, and a breeze off the water kept lifting the edges of the banknotes under her heel. 

I was the island's conscience for hire.  Once a year, when we read out the names of the drowned, anybody with unfinished business could come inside the lamp room and have both sides of a conversation.  I supplied the side the sea had taken.  My trade was sincerely insincere; nobody mistook me for the dead, but I had once spent six weeks learning to whistle through my teeth for a boy who wanted to hear his father call the dog. 

On the day my brother Kit went down, my mother had brought eleven other people home.  She had turned the lifeboat away from him because another wave broadside would have rolled her over.  For ten years I had been telling people she had no choice, and for ten years she had let me. 

Upstairs I drew the cloak of doubt between us, a length of sailcloth that admitted light but gave no clear outline of whoever stood behind it.  From her side hung the wind chimes, each on a separate cord that could be lowered into the draft from the gallery door.  By deciphering her selections I could follow her wishes without making her break off talking to Kit to give me instructions.  Oak meant listen; brass meant answer; for the hardest request of all there was a hollow mirror shard, a sliver of double glass salvaged from the old lamp.  It made a thin, divided note, and it meant try that again.  Almost nobody used it. 

She let down the brass.  "I saw you," she said. 

I gave Kit his little intake of breath, like somebody about to put a match to something.  "I know, Mam," I said in his voice.  "You did what you had to do." 

The glass sounded.  I tried it angrier, thinking anger might be what she had bought herself the privacy to hear.  "You could have come back for me." 

Again the glass, and this time she kept it ringing until I ran out of things my brother might have said.  Then came the small knock of oak. 

"What I saw," she told him, "was your hand making that shooing motion you used to make at supper when you'd got a mouthful of bone.  I couldn't tell if you were waving us away or just trying to get your arm above the water, and for one second I was glad I couldn't tell." 

All those years I'd thought I was protecting her by saying the decision had made itself.  I hadn't left her so much as a second of her own in the story of her son's death.  The brass rang, asking me to answer, and I wanted so badly to give her back a boy who had waved her away that I had to sit on my hands. 

"I never knew what I meant when I did that at supper either," I said at last.  "Sometimes I wanted you to take the bone and sometimes I wanted everybody to quit looking at me." 

On the other side of the cloth my mother made a sound so like Kit choking down a laugh that for an instant it was she who gave him back to me.  She reached up to unhook the glass and laid it in my palm under the hem, where we could touch without either of us having to show her face.  "Hang that one outside when you read his name," she said. 

At the rail I read the names as always, with the families below turning their faces up into the light.  After Kit's I used to add a little speech about the eleven who came home, as if they were so many coins laid in a scale.  This year I let his name stand, and in the pause before the next one the breeze found the glass.
