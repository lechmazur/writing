When the foster bureau called us the children of the wreck, it seemed a kindness not to ask whose children we had been before.  I kept that kindness longer than most, even after I became the wreck-diver who could whisper a question into a drowned ear and sometimes get an answer.  It was a trade that taught you to weave truth carefully; a dead man who said blue might mean his wife's dress or the color his hands had turned. 

Then a stranger came to the candle-lit shrine I kept carved into the glacier ice above the narrows, and asked why a woman kept spoiling his dreams.  Whatever he dreamed, somewhere at the edge of it she would be shaking a little pear-shaped bottle as if a dose of medicine had settled in the bottom.  He drew it for me in the frost beside a candle, and I knew the melted snow globe that had stood on my foster mother's mantel.  Inside its crooked dome a silver ship went through a fall of silver filings, stern first if you tipped it right.  It was the only thing, my mother said, that had come ashore with me.  I had never told her that sometimes in dreams I saw a woman's fingers close around it. 

The man was another ferry child, brought up inland, and through him I found others willing to sleep a night in the shrine.  The ice had its little windows of connection, seams where old air shone like borrowed starlight and a whisper carried much farther than a shout.  With our blankets spread along one seam, we could call softly to one another without coming quite awake.  I asked them to ignore the fine central business of their dreams and watch the woman who played so many small parts in them.  One saw the black hem of a curtain brushing her shoulder; one saw her lift the globe and look, not at the ship inside, but just to its left.  A third heard a click, and after the click his own name, which he woke up weeping because he could not remember. 

I set the globe on a shelf and crouched behind a hanging blanket, and my guests all turned their faces to the same spot beside it.  Any fool could have told me what you shake at a child to make him look into a camera.  It took a diver to find the darkroom. 

I went down with a line around my waist and the old deck plan sewn inside a square of oilskin.  Where the plan said linen cupboard I found a door with a strip of felt still clinging to its jamb.  I put my cheek to it and whispered that I had brought the little ship.  Not the faces, said a voice through the felt, so faintly it might have been my own breath working back into my ear.  The backs. 

In a gasketed tin beneath the sink lay a packet of portraits, each of a child on somebody's knee, each with two names and a forwarding address penciled on the back.  The wrapper bore a note in the photographer's hand: First set posted to bureau, duplicates to travel with children and remain their property.  The bureau had published those same faces in its appeal for beds, trimmed to ovals so neat you would never guess a child had sat on anybody's knee.  We had been put aboard to get us clear of the fighting; nobody had ever actually said our people went down with the ship. 

With the addresses I could finally make sense of a drawer at the bureau marked Undeliverable, full of letters from people who knew their children's names but not their numbers.  I had come hoping to salvage something pure from the wreckage, and instead here was my foster mother's tidy script across the drawer.  To expose what she had done I would have to leave her writing on it, recognizable to half the town.  I carried the drawer to the shrine without so much as dusting it. 

People came that winter with candles and spectacles, and read until the little windows above them ran with light.  My own letter gave me a street to walk down in the spring, if I could find the nerve.  My stranger found two letters, both about a dog he had been afraid would forget him.  He sat beside me reading the part about the dog aloud, over and over, while I held the globe for him to shake whenever the silver settled.
