The highway robber's son kept his father's collection of foreign paper money under glass in the ticket wagon of an abandoned circus train.  Visitors generally admired the engraved tigers and the ladies with wheat in their hair, but Ivo had mounted the bills face down.  On the backs were little maps that his father had made his victims draw to show where they had come from.  What troubled Ivo was that on nearly every map the traveler had gone halfway through the town and then circled back to the road where the robbery took place. 

I was painting the train for the squatters who lived in it when Ivo hired me to walk those maps with him.  We went at dusk, the hour when passengers used to land from the island ferry and set out uphill for the station with their cases.  We found the alleys still there, and at the end of each alley a court, and across each court the station clock looking near enough to wind.  I thought the travelers had turned back because they couldn't see a way through the washing.  Ivo, who had inherited his father's trick of standing so still that other people's uneasiness became apparent, pointed out a woman taking her daughter's drawers off the line as we approached.  At her gate he took off his cap and waited until she shook her head. 

Our startup was called By Leave, and for a while it was just the two of us in the ticket wagon with a kettle and a pot of blue paint.  I painted little wooden swallows for householders to turn outward on their gateposts whenever they cared to let passengers through.  An inward bird meant go around; even somebody just off the boat could understand our picture map without a word.  The ferry company paid us out of its ticket sales, since passengers who could count on making the evening train would risk the later crossing.  Each open gate earned its keeper a share, and by our third summer there were blue birds from the landing clear up to the clock. 

With the first big dividend Ivo had the scratched glass over the banknotes replaced, and in its polished calm the old pencil trails seemed to run under ice.  He could have told you by then why some paths circle back, but he hadn't yet followed one all the way. 

The trouble began when the ferry printed a guaranteed connection on its tickets and started charging us for missed trains.  Our commercial director proposed buying permanent passage through six courts, and brought the contracts down to the train one evening for the keepers to sign.  The money was good; my sister, who kept the middle court, already had a new roof picked out.  Ivo made no speech to dissuade her or the others, only asked that before signing they walk back with him from the landing. 

My sister was the first to spot the change in the territorial markers I had painted: every swallow faced out, though it was bath night and half the town had its tubs in the yard.  At her own gate she reached up to turn the bird and found the screw our director wanted fitted after purchase.  Her little boy was standing naked in the yard, holding his towel out to screen his grandmother while she got into the tub.  We went back down the alley, all six keepers walking in step without quite meaning to, and took the long road home. 

Nobody signed, and when the ferry took back its money we sold everything right down to the kettle.  I still paint the train for the price of a supper, and sometimes at dusk I hear the click of a last swallow turning inward answer the click of another up the hill.  Ivo gave the bills to the ferry museum on condition that both sides could be seen.  He wanted people to see what his father had taken, certainly, but also what those tired strangers, with a boat at their backs and a train to catch, had declined to take.
