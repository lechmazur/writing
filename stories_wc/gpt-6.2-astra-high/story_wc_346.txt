When the tide began climbing the sky for the thousandth time, Nell was still trying to fit the jaw back onto the broken puppet head.  She had whittled hundreds of jaws, but none quite fitted the little face in her palm.  Outside, the shadow of the ordinary lighthouse stretched across the sand until it touched a certain black rock, and the door of the hidden lighthouse opened inside the shadow.  It always opened at dusk, on the day there would be no more need of lamps. 

Nell's warrant as doomsday clock adjuster allowed her a thousand attempts to find a future in which the sea stayed down.  She could wind the world back as far as the morning her warrant had been issued, when her son Kit was six and she had promised to be home by supper.  She could alter any one thing on that morning, except the fact of her departure.  She had moved elections, earthquakes, the errand of a boy who took a match into a barn.  Sometimes the sea rose before Kit had a beard, sometimes long after his bones had gone to chalk; some arithmetic deep in the water always came out the same. 

She had kept the puppet because on that first morning he had entrusted it to her with all the gravity of a surgeon handing over a heart.  He hadn't said to mend it; he had said to make it talk again. 

Behind the clock hung a pane of hush, the stuff that kept each spent world from hearing the bustle of its successor.  Each winding had left a ring in it like the lip of a wet glass.  Looking through nested patterns of lamplight and evening sky, Nell could pick out the kitchen window of every house in which Kit had ever lived.  She had never touched the pane for fear of letting all those desperate seas in on her at once.  Tonight she meant to whisper a lullaby across a thousand lifetimes, if the glass would vouchsafe her so much as the breadth of a pin to whisper through. 

She set the puppet's long nose against the innermost ring and put her own lips to the hole where the jaw should go.  The face was beautifully flawed, one eyebrow carved higher than the other, so that everything it said seemed open to correction.  She had given it a pompous voice and Kit had given it the name of Government.  Government used to sing him to sleep by royal decree, ordering first his toes and then his ankles to bed. 

She got as far as the knees before she heard, quite distinctly, an old man say that she had forgotten the heels.  Then came another voice, still piping, to remind her of the dirt between the toes, which under maritime law was entitled to a separate bed. 

She took her mouth away.  All over the pane men and boys were leaning toward their windows, waiting for the majestic fool to admit his mistake.  Tears slid into the split wood, darkening it as if the puppet were coming back into leaf.  She had thought of these lives as so many bad sums crossed off a slate, and here they were with opinions about heels. 

The clock began to strike, and she saw what the jaw would have spoiled: through that gap, while she was singing, she could also listen.  She turned the head sideways so that no ring was covered by the broad wooden cheek.  Across the hush of time's final ripple went the decree about heels, in a voice made grave by the effort not to laugh, and back came amendments.  In one house a woman wanted sleep for her husband's stump, the leg having been lost at sea; in another the children put in for their father's tired hands.  When it came to the eyes she could hardly sing, but by then it was possible to leave whole verses to the houses. 

She sat down on the step in the dusk with the wooden head on her knee and listened to the bed being made for her too, bone by bone.  The last thing to lie down was the hand with which she had wound back the world, and she was awake long enough to feel somebody tuck it in.
