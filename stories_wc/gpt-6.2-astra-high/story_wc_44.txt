Every clear noon Nell set her father's cup on the sill and watched its shadow slip away a little before the sun got overhead.  It went handle first, like a cup being offered to somebody, and it always went downstairs.  She wanted to find where shadows went at noon because she had seen, just once, the shadow of a thumb hook through that handle. 

Downstairs was the nocturnal observatory, slung beneath the floating isle of Lark in a basket of riveted iron, with bunks for the astronomers along its curved wall.  Six years ago, when the spoons bent reality at the mayor's breakfast, the town had come loose from the earth as neatly as a poached egg.  The astronomers, who had just gone to bed, had been carried away asleep, and whatever part of them had woken up had woken up elsewhere.  The physicians called these sealed awakenings, and every Tuesday Nell helped trim the nails of the man in the third bunk. 

At fifteen she was the youngest rune scholar the observatory had ever taken on, principally because nobody else wanted payment in lessons from a library rather than wages.  She worked in the quietly deliberate fashion of someone accustomed to cutting another person's nails.  Instead of following the shadow, which she had tried until her knees were purple, she began to put things in its way.  A sheet of paper made no difference, nor did a mirror, but when she laid an old street plan across the stair a brown ring appeared on it.  It sat exactly where her father's desk had stood when the observatory was still a cellar cut into the hill. 

Not the town, then, but its address had stayed behind.  At noon the whole island must be casting its shadow not on whatever sheep pasture lay below, but on the one piece of earth that still fitted it. 

From the instrument cupboard she took the clockwork automaton key that used to bring visiting astronomers up the hill from the milestone, bowing at every turning.  Like all municipal keys it could learn an address by closing loops: walk it from the milestone to a door and back, and thereafter it could admit you.  Even if the door was painted on a wall, the book said, although whoever had written that had added a small, skeptical question mark. 

She inked the walking rune at each corner of the route on the plan and wound the key.  It marched as far as the brown ring and stopped dead, with five legs planted and the sixth lifted to take a step across her father's desk.  Of course the poor thing knew the way there.  So did a teacup.  What it had to relearn was the way home. 

The town had left its milestone sticking out of the naked hilltop, and for six years had been numbering its streets from a place it could not touch.  Nell went up to the square with a chisel and cut a zero into the doorstep of the public kitchen.  Anybody looking for the middle of town these days would go where the soup was.  Then she drew a second plan, with the kitchen as its heart, on the back of the first; the two observatories lay precisely one behind the other. 

At noon she sent the key down the old streets and, when it lifted its sixth leg, turned the paper over beneath its feet.  It came back to the kitchen trailing a thread of tea so fine she could smell it before she could see it.  Behind her a man said, in the cross voice of somebody woken early, that he took his without milk.  But it was not her father speaking; it was the old director in the first bunk, and beyond him all the blankets lay perfectly still.  She had forgotten that the key always escorted visitors singly, out of respect for their dignity. 

Her hands were shaking now, so she folded them together on the table until she could trust them with the key.  Six times more she wound it, though on the second journey the man in the third bunk sat up and she wanted desperately to stop.  He watched the little thing go round, understanding nothing, and when the last blanket stirred he put out his hand, palm up, for hers.  She had forgotten, in all those Tuesdays, how it felt when the fingers closed.
