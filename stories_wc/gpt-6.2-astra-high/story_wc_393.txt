On Tuesdays I opened my antique music box repair shop to customers who wanted second opinions, which was how the penitentiary chaplain came to be standing at my counter with the weather in his arms.  Rain had got into the elbows of his coat, but the little theatre he unwrapped was dry down to the gilt paper grass along its stage.  I knew it from the photograph in the report I had signed three weeks before.  My report had cost a man named Ellis his bench in the prison workshop. 

Father Bell set a kitchen timer beside the theatre and said Ellis would be allowed one call at four.  What I'd said on the strength of a photograph and a recording was that the long gap in the music sounded like a botched repair.  What Bell had put on the form, in the space that would take one line of type, was "Repair botched."  Neither of us had expected the other to look quite so ordinary.  I had pictured a bully; he kept glancing past me at the framed diploma that did not exist. 

From the street came the swish of bicycle tires parting water, then the luminous quiet peculiar to my shop when a dozen polished lids hold a little of the afternoon in reserve.  I wound the brass key that projected from the wing of the stage.  Through a knothole I could see its clockwork heart, twin lobes of bright brass lifting two hammers clear of a drum no bigger than a penny.  Tin swallows skimmed the grass to a running tune, and then, as on the recording, the melody dropped out.  In the calm before realization I found myself attending to a sound so slight it might have been Bell rubbing one wet cuff against the other. 

The phone rang.  Instead of beginning with my qualifications, as I usually did on Tuesdays, I asked Ellis what I ought to be looking at. 

"Not at," he said, "but through." 

At his request I wound it again.  Bell and I got down on our knees, two old people making expensive noises with our joints, and sighted along the boards as if we occupied the cheapest seats in heaven.  Where I had seen a patch of tarnish there was a sky, two sheets of smoked mica turning very slowly across each other. 

"You can tell the French ones by the way light breaks through storm clouds," Ellis said.  "In the Swiss ones the lightning comes down on a wire." 

One white rent opened in the mica sky and closed; four whole seconds later the hammers struck together, and thunder rolled out over the astonished grass.  The four seconds, so excruciating on the recording, were aptly deliberate: we had just heard how far away the storm was. 

"What did you actually fix?" I asked. 

"The thunder used to come first." 

Bell put his mouth to the phone as if it were a place on a child's forehead that needed kissing.  "I'm sorry, son," he said, and Ellis said, without any particular heat, that he already had a letter to that effect. 

To turn an apology into action proved more awkward than either of us had foreseen, because there was no space for a revision on the form.  I offered to certify the repair on shop stationery; Bell offered to deliver the certificate himself. 

"And next time?" Ellis asked. 

We had been thinking of the bench as something he must deserve and of ourselves as somehow born entitled to decide who sat there.  The timer showed a minute left when Bell finally asked how Ellis would set up the test. 

"Same bench," said Ellis, "but let the fellow be there when you listen." 

On the following Tuesday Bell brought me to the prison instead of bringing the work to me.  Ellis had his bench back, and beside it were two chairs, one for whoever had done the work and one for me.  The first man up hung back in the doorway until Bell, who knew more than I did about men and doorways, went out to walk in with him.  Ellis wound the man's little walnut theatre, and I kept my pencil in my pocket and let him come into the room to his own music.
