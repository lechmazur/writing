Inside the whisper of closing pages there is time for one experiment, provided nobody has to go looking for a pencil.  At the abandoned observatory on Gull Cliff, with the covers of the star atlas already falling together, Dot discovered she had forgotten hers.  Her brother produced the little yellow stump he kept behind his ear, and for the first time in twelve years they smiled at each other.  Outside, a raindrop stood on the sill like a miserable little jelly. 

Dot was a professional myth buster; her newspaper paid by the inch for miracles that turned out to have perfectly good plumbing.  She had come home to dispose of the local answer to that universal riddle, where do the dead go?  According to the fishermen, a compass appeared for the duration of the sigh the atlas gave as it shut, and pointed the way.  It mattered to Dot to know whether their mother had gone anywhere in particular, because on the available evidence she had died trying to get away from Dot. 

Ben set their mother's half-finished cello across the two empty telescope brackets.  She had been the last observer here, with whole months of cloud in which to learn a second trade.  The instrument still lacked its belly; you could lay your cheek against the curved back and hum into the hollow and feel your own voice coming up through your teeth.  In the middle Ben stood a saucer of water and floated the painted seashell their mother had used to teach them north from south, a scarlet N at its narrow tip. 

They had agreed to retell the conversation without words, because words were Dot's business, and Ben could no longer trust her with his.  She reimagined past conversations for a living too, trimming away the stutters and lending her informants the brisk certainty that kept a reader on the hook.  In print their mother's last words, spoken as the ambulance men eased her through the narrow door, had been a quite unambiguous "Let me go." 

Dot bent to the wood and hummed those three descending notes, so often rehearsed in her bath that they might have been the first bar of a hymn.  The shell did a brisk half turn toward the gap in the wall where the great brass telescope had been carted out for scrap.  Heaven, presumably, had bought the telescope secondhand and taken it home.  She wrote "outward displacement of water" on her cuff, but Ben had not even drawn breath yet. 

Now he put his cheek to the opposite rib and gave a small, absurdly jaunty uphill hum, followed by a long low note that made the saucer walk.  It was the sound the stretcher made when its folding feet went up, first a squeak of protest and then a most contented clang.  She had left that out of the paper, naturally, as she had left out the giggle her mother gave at the indignity of being posted feet first through her own front door.  Ben hummed the giggle, too, and the shell came round to him.  Dot repeated it before she knew she was going to, and the red N swung back to her. 

She moved the saucer onto the stone sill to break the connection with the wood, and they did it again, with exactly the same result.  Then Ben kept his mouth shut and merely lifted his eyebrows in their mother's wicked way, and the shell went obediently about.  This was where the dead went, then, not up through the hole in the dome but back and forth between the people who could still get a laugh out of that trip through the door. 

Dot crossed out the note on her cuff and underneath it wrote what she should have put in the paper twelve years ago: "I did not hear all of it."  The atlas covers were a hair apart now, and the ephemeral compass had begun to list as water crept over the painted lip.  There was just time for Ben to fish it out and for Dot to ask whether he would help her write the correction.  He slipped the pencil back behind his ear and hummed a pompous two-note imitation of their mother's "We'll see," which had nearly always meant yes.  At the very last edge of the whisper the raindrop on the sill got its miserable descent under way, with their two small, distorted smiles inside it.
