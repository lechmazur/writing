On the morning they came to melt the iron nurse, Sella asked the furnace men for the length of one more song.  Nobody in the ancient plaza had been singing, but one of the men took the hint and began.  His breath went up white against the shattered sky, where a few blue panes still clung to the ribs of the winter canopy.  After the great fire there was very little blue left overhead, and altogether too much at their feet.  Glass came up through the snow like spring onions. 

At the foot of the fallen minaret the nurse was sitting with her steel arms curled around a parcel of air.  From time to time she eased one elbow lower, as if to spare a small head the discomfort of a rivet.  Sella had built those elbows in the days when people still laughed at her idea of a public nurse.  She was the sort of radical who took the locks off the doors of the poorhouse, then spent the night sanding the holes so nobody would catch a finger.  She wanted a tenderness that could go where flesh could not, into plague houses and under burning roofs.  She had not foreseen what it might cost such a creature to remember. 

The nurse was trapped in backward sight, a condition familiar to anyone who had ever tried to move an iron worker off the site of its last unfinished job.  Each felt through its soles the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone, the city's way of giving orders even in smoke and pitch darkness.  Here the pattern said FERRY, and the nurse would see the night of the fire until she delivered her last passenger to the surgeons across the square.  But she had stayed to hold a little girl whose back was broken, and the girl had died before the roof fell in.  On the salvage warrant somebody had written aberrant mercy in a neat, almost affectionate hand. 

They had lost six more people under that roof.  The man who was singing had lost his wife.  At the end of his verse he stopped to say, quite reasonably, that a pair of arms could not be in two places at once. 

"I know," said Sella, who had spent three nights trying to devise a joint that would let the nurse divide herself. 

"My wife went out of this world by herself," he said, and rubbed his thumb along the smooth side of the nurse's elbow.  "I'm not saying she should have put the girl down on the stones to do the same." 

He was beginning another verse when Sella saw what she had got wrong about the unfinished job.  The nurse had not failed to finish it; the city had failed to give it a name. 

All around the ruined minaret lay pieces of the inscription the iron muezzin had read with his fingers before he sang.  She found the tessera she wanted by running her thumb through the glass until it bled: a shallow crescent chip for WITH.  Even people who had forgotten the rest of the prayer for the dying remembered that word. 

With the little square of blue she set about renaming the place under the nurse's feet.  She pried out the long slash that meant ACROSS and fitted the crescent into its socket, leaving the other two signs untouched.  Person, journey, with: instead of a ferry landing it was now a bedside.  Under the worn leather of one iron sole she felt the toes stir to read. 

"Are we there?" the nurse said in the voice Sella had made from a file drawn slowly over silver. 

"You were there all along," said the furnace man. 

The empty arms opened, and he slipped his big mitten between the elbow and the rivet as if there were still a head to ease away. 

Nobody tore up the warrant that morning, nor did anybody suggest that six lives were a fair price for a child not to die alone.  But when they raised the new infirmary they left the beds low enough for an iron woman sitting on the floor to reach.  If you woke there in winter thirsting, as the furnace man did in his last illness, you had only to turn your cheek against the cool wrist.  No matter how little noise you made, a cup came up to meet you.
