On the evening the town meant to bottle my sister's courage, her son came to my stall to buy her some.  You could buy almost anything from me except a feeling you had actually felt, which was generally cheaper to get at home.  He laid down the silver whistle the council had given him in place of a mother. 

Then he unfolded a square of silk and showed me her blotter petal, a slip of white rag paper cut to fit the stoppered throat of a perfume flask.  She had been chief chemist at the scent works, and that spring, when the still blew, she had gone back into the building three times for her apprentices.  The third time the building kept her and two girls of sixteen.  The council wanted the petal for the memorial because she had tucked it behind her son's ear just before she went back. 

"They'll know she was happy," he said. 

In my trade we call it reading, although the instrument is only a brass comb and the faculty is the same one that tells a cat a hand is coming in the dark.  I drew the comb through my hair and held the charged teeth over the paper, first one side, then the other, then over the silk to rule out anything the boy had left.  The static lifted the fine hairs of my wrist in a pattern I could have drawn in my sleep: joy so sharp it hurt the jaw. 

"She saw you were outside," I said.  He nodded, watching me to see how much worse it was going to get.  "One of the girls was outside then, too," he said.  "She ran in after my mother." 

We carried my case down to the amber grotto, where at dusk the day's last heat brought a sweat of clear resin out of the walls.  Each bereaved family had a niche; when the resin hardened it would subsume whatever you laid there, and anybody who put a cheek against the amber would feel what it held.  Already there were greasy little ovals on the older niches at the height of children's faces.  In ours the council had set a card that said SHE DID NOT THINK OF HERSELF. 

Half truths are my stock in trade, and I recognized a particularly expensive one.  I could give them courage, certainly; a little terror with all the exits taken out will pass for it almost anywhere.  But someday this boy would come here to remember what it was to have been, for one instant, entirely sufficient cause for joy.  What would it cost him to find my competent work instead? 

While I was considering this, a woman in a good black coat reached past me and straightened the council's card.  "It was crooked," she said, with the slight embarrassment of somebody who has found a job for her hands at a funeral.  It was the mother of the girl who had gone back in. 

Before I could mix anything, he took my comb, charged it on his sleeve, and gave her the petal to read.  "That's me being alive," he told her. 

She stood quite still until the charge died.  "I had that," she said, "when mine came out the first time."  She did not say anything about the second time, and the boy, who had learned something about the uses of silence, did not ask. 

I asked him what he wanted a stranger to know before putting a face to that wall.  He thought a little and turned the card over and wrote SHE HAD JUST SEEN ME.  Then he added his name, so there could be no mistake about whose mother she was or who had lived. 

I held the card against the resin while he bedded the petal just beneath it.  As the last orange slit of sun closed, the amber went hard and took the print of my thumb, which is as near as I come to signing my work. 

His silver whistle was still on my counter when we got home.  "You didn't make anything," he said, not accusingly.  I gave it back to him; I am particular about my accounts.
