At midnight, with the franchise buyer asleep on her banquette, June Pike received the latest installment of the Mystery Seed Mail.  It came by bicycle in the pocket of storm-born silence between the last clap of thunder and the first tentative call of a frog.  The messenger was a girl in a dripping band uniform, carrying a pickle jar against her belly like a hand warmer. 

June let her in by the brook door, the one that stayed unlocked for people who had nowhere to go between shifts.  It was an odd arrangement for an escape room proprietor, making such a point of the door that always opened.  But the old dye works beside the quiet brook had been a nocturnal haven long before anybody paid June to get locked inside it.  Her father used to leave the boiler room lit for the women waiting for the five o'clock bus. 

They had invented the mail to keep awake.  Each woman folded a puzzle into a seed packet and slipped it into the coat of the next.  You could solve what you got or change it, but by morning it had to have passed through every pocket on the rail.  June had been small enough to walk beneath the coats, feeling for the fat one. 

Now her packets went out with stamps, and the buyer wanted the name and all eleven years of back issues for his chain.  His check would pay for a roof that did not require buckets wherever the flashing had once hugged a chimney. 

"My mother's got an answer to the blue one," the girl said. 

The blue packet, which June had written the winter her father died, asked for a lock that could only be opened from inside.  She had printed a diagram of a hatching egg on the answer slip.  The girl unscrewed the lid and tipped out a twist of damp moss; inside lay a single corpse flower seed, pale as a milk tooth. 

"Mum got two from the crossing at the conservatory, one for them and one for us," she said.  "We thought you might like to watch yours open." 

The buyer had woken up and was looking over her shoulder, his cheek still pleated from the banquette.  "Do you get many wrong answers?" he asked. 

June had always been notably receptive to wrong answers; half the locks upstairs worked better because somebody had misunderstood them.  She went to the file drawer to show him what she meant, and he followed with his pen uncapped.  They came at the drawer through orthogonal approaches, his finger running down the dates while hers traveled across the colored tabs that marked a puzzle's changes of hand.  At blue he stopped, and she kept going.  There was an envelope from a prisoner who had drawn a fist, then one from a midwife who objected that a baby generally needed help.  The girl took out her phone and photographed both for her mother. 

"These are what I'd need," the buyer said, touching the tabs, and June understood that his offer wasn't for eleven years of her life alone.  He wanted an answer book thick enough that nobody in any of his rooms would ever surprise the person running it.  He was prepared to pay each contributor, he said, if she could get releases, and she believed him.  What troubled her was the thought of the girl opening a packet someday and finding her mother's seed already there, under Other Acceptable Solutions. 

"You can have the diagrams I drew," she said, "and I'll build you a room that uses the best ones.  Let the mail flow from pocket to pocket." 

He sat back down to do his sums, moving a bucket with his foot so his shoes would stay dry.  No new roof, then, but possibly enough to mend the valley where the two pitches met.  June found a pot, and the girl showed her how to bed the seed the way the conservatory did, with room underneath for what neither of them could see. 

Across the hush of a possibility just born came the small ugly scrape of the buyer pushing his chair back.  "Would a grudge count?" he wanted to know. 

June gave him a blue envelope and turned her back so he could have the pleasure of putting it in her pocket.
