The year they pulled down the foundling hospital, thirty gray heads gathered on the blooming jetty at twilight to call for a woman whose name none of us knew.  We knew the names of the doctors, because the doctors had named whole wards after themselves, but the woman we wanted had worked in the laundry.  We had thirty pictures, and she wasn't in any of them.  She had taken them. 

As the town's walnut-ink archivist, I had spent my working life trying to preserve the weight of small moments against the superior tonnage of official ones.  I had saved the slip for the hire of a camera, signed with a thumbprint, and the deductions from a laundress's wages for spoiling thirty pillowcases.  Gently thorough, my husband called me, until the night he caught me trying to prise the back off my locket with my teeth.  He opened it with the point of a needle and never asked why I couldn't bear to wait until morning.  The locket photo showed a baby propped against a white pillowcase with the word Ada embroidered crookedly above her head.  On the back, in pencil, was the price of thirty prints. 

At the demolition sale I had found twenty-nine little parcels in a laundry basket wedged behind the boiler, each addressed to a child in red thread.  It took me a winter to deliver them all.  Several of the recipients had never seen themselves younger than seven. 

Now the jetty was white from end to end with the little salt roses that opened once a year to let the dead come visiting.  Our covenant came from the days when the whole town ate from one oven: you called a name across the water, and whoever had worn it could choose to answer.  Flowers bent under invisible feet; a moment later a fishwife was kissing her son with such smacking relish that even the gulls reeled off shrieking.  We thirty stood aside to let them pass.  One man had brought the town's old burial book and proposed calling every woman's name in it in turn.  I told him to look at the water, where already you could see the faces of women listening hard in the hope of being wanted.  He put the book away. 

The people who shared that oven had never drawn wages by number or been sent away in infancy to live under a stranger's name.  The covenant could be regenerated when a change of circumstance defeated its purpose, but this required a mark of assent from both banks on one sheet.  My trade was to know a hand through proximate causes: a nick in a pen made a double downstroke, a bad knuckle made the pen skip.  What if a woman could hear the work of her hands called back to her as clearly as a name? 

I had brought my travelling pot to steep a handful of walnut husks against the need for an entry, and now I dipped a finger in it.  On a leaf of the register I wrote, "Let a gift freely given serve also as a name," and set my brown fingerprint below.  The man with the book took his picture from its parcel and held it printed side out toward the river.  One by one the others did the same, until there were thirty babies looking out from the arms of the old people they had become. 

I laid the leaf among the flowers and waited for it to be taken.  It lay so still that a moth settled on my wet print and dragged one leg through it.  Then the corner curled, as linen does under a hot iron, and on the paper beside my print appeared the thumbprint from the camera slip, whorl for whorl.  We had our regenerated covenant; we did not yet have our visitor. 

Down the jetty came the smell of scorched cotton, and somebody with a mouth full of pins said quite distinctly, "Nobody move."  We stood there laughing with all our teeth showing or no teeth at all, and she walked among us taking the pins from her mouth and turning our collars down.  When she came to me I asked if she would like us to fetch the camera and take her picture at last.  She shook her head and took my face in her two damp hands and turned it a little toward the dying light. 

"There," she said, with the satisfaction of somebody who has had to leave a job half done and comes back to find nothing spoiled.  She stayed until the roses closed, and not one of us thought to ask her name.
