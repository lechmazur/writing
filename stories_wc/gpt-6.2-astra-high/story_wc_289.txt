After the cloud bursts, the lotus temple rising from the massive lily pond looked near enough to touch from our attic, although a whole drowned market lay between.  My daughter and I passed the first morning there fishing things out of the water with the long hook we used to lower my sign.  The sign said Letters Taken Down, which was the discreet way of saying that I was a love letter ghostwriter and therefore a keeper of secrets. 

On our third cast we hooked a tin box, my own, with a wet tongue of blue paper sticking out.  Ruth knew that blue; her father had written to her on it every Friday of the three years she spent at the glass school upriver.  Until she saw the spoiled copies she had never suspected that he came to my desk like anybody else.  She read as far as the one about the thrush building in her empty window box, then laid the hook down very gently.  Of all the things my husband might have left me to explain, it seemed particularly like him to have chosen a bird.  He had been dead two winters. 

That afternoon she took the first punt to the temple to rejoin the glassworkers, without asking me what had been his and what had been mine.  Perhaps she was afraid I could tell her exactly. 

The temple steps had become our public square, and the glassworkers were making a map there of the streets we could no longer walk.  Each household could bring one small thing to be enclosed between two layers of salvaged window glass and set where its owner lived.  It was inevitably whimsical, this census of people by the things they fished up: a porcelain tooth for the dentist, a brass mermaid for the widow who sold eels.  Where our house should have been, Ruth put two panes with nothing between. 

I went out to see it when the water in the market fell far enough that the stalls made green aisles under the surface.  Under the temple's white stone petals, light came up through the map and lay in wavering colors on the undersides of people's chins.  Nobody spoke loudly there, any more than one raises one's voice over the face of a sleeping child.  Ruth was sitting beside our square with her father's letter on her knee. 

"He said he was trying to find the thread that connects all things," she said.  "I used to think if I got good enough at glass I could show it to him." 

"That part was mine," I said.  "He didn't go in for all things." 

She folded the letter once more along its soft white crease, and I nearly let her put it away.  It was hard to admit that in my hunger to make a sentence sing I had missed the point of what he brought me.  I unwrapped the little coil I had found in the bottom of the tin, hooked together still, though the mud that held it had washed out.  It was the thrush's nest twig spiral, and wound into it was a length of red wool with a knot at either end.  Ruth used to tie her braids with odd bits of wool like that and hang them on the window latch at night. 

"He made me look till I saw both knots," I said.  "He wanted you to know where your ribbon went." 

Ruth took out the brass clips that held our panes together and eased them a little apart, but the twigs would not fit without breaking.  I thought she'd pull the wool free, as I had pulled a fine sentiment out of what her father had actually held in his hands.  Instead she had me hold the panes while she cut four little pillars from a broken green bottle and stood them at the corners.  Together we bound the whole thing up with lead, a house a thumb's breadth taller than its neighbors, with room for what was there. 

Afterward, when people bent to see what made the tiny red shadow in our street, my daughter would turn the glass to catch the light.  "My father found that," she would say, and then, sometimes, "My mother kept it."
