On the ashen skyborne arbor of Wick, even a dropped pin had somewhere else to be by morning.  The roots sailed east while the crown crept west, and the moss between them went pleasantly about the business of swallowing pins.  Only Bess, the seed sorter, maintained anything like an address.  Her drawers were numbered, her socks were numbered, and her left pocket was reserved for things on their way back to drawers. 

She spent her evenings building tiny monuments to forgotten things, a pursuit considerably less silly than it sounded.  Once a year the old river valley rose like breath around the five arbors that had carried its people away from the fire.  During that hush, where past met prophecy, a monument could be fed to a hollow in the mother ash, and a living seed would come out.  In valley stories the offering always emerged transformed, because even a tree could not give you yesterday.  The trick was to decode the message in the falling leaves before you planted what you got.  Leaves fell in the tracks of feet that had not yet walked. 

Bess had chosen the bread oven for this year's offering.  It was not the bread she missed but the fine interval in which nobody could take a loaf home, and her mother sat down like anybody else and let somebody tell her a story.  Since the fire her mother had cooked for Wick alone, standing up.  Four other cooks on four other arbors stood over four other miserable little stoves. 

Bess built the oven out of chimney grit, down to the crooked shelf for cooling buns.  For the live coal in its mouth she saved one speck of dust from the grinding of a moonstone cabochon, the only grit she knew that held a traveling light. 

When the hush came, she set the model on the moss to read the leaves against it, as her grandmother had taught her.  All around her the other arbors hung within hailing distance, their branches soft with people leaning out.  Down came five yellow leaves, settling at five different distances from the oven.  Bess waited for them to come the rest of the way.  Nobody moved, not even her mother, who had taken off her apron for the occasion but was twisting it into a rope. 

Then Bess noticed that the coal had gone dark.  She bent to prod it with a pin; the whole oven slid a little, the speck winked blue, and the nearest leaf curled snugly round the door.  She slid the oven to the next leaf.  It too curled up, brown ribs showing like the fingers of a hand enjoying a cup of something hot. 

From the next arbor somebody called that the hush was thinning, but Bess was laughing too hard to answer.  In twenty years of sorting seeds she had never once been asked to send one anywhere but to one of five gardens.  She had been trying to give the oven an address.  She ran it past the last three leaves like a woman wheeling a barrow, gathering a crackling little queue, and tipped the lot into the hollow. 

The seed that came out had a keel.  Instead of planting it in the good fixed earth between the roots, Bess tucked it into a bolster of moss and pushed the bolster clear of Wick.  For one terrible moment it dropped so straight she could have drawn a line along its fall.  Then two green paddles opened, bit the air, and lifted a plump mossy bakehouse level with the lowest bough.  At its door, neatly pegged by their laces, hung five pairs of slippers for five cooks. 

Her mother came aboard in her stocking feet with a bowl of dough so warm from her belly that the linen cover rose and fell.  By the time they touched the second arbor she had flour on her nose, and by the third she had learned a scandalous thing about the man who ground moonstones.  On the roof Bess lay in the delicious tickle of new moss and listened for the small thump that would mean her mother had at last sat down.  It came, and then four more, and then the scandalous thing began its second trip around the valley.
