After the letters were found, the county sent its judge up to the floating library in a wicker basket, with sand still pouring from his cuffs.  The library zeppelin hung over the excavation at Mercy Beach, and what the dredge had opened in the dunes looked from that height like a pupil dilated by pain.  It was the obsidian lagoon, in whose black glass the last day of the voters' camp went on happening. 

The judge laid forty letters on Cora Bell's desk, each in a different hand and each declaring that the writer had left the camp of her own free will.  Cora had spent her life archiving the small solemnities of citizenship: the pressed skirts women wore to register, the receipts they kept for poll taxes paid to men who would not let them vote.  She turned the letters over with the care she usually reserved for cloth.  On every back she found a date and a line of tiny drawings, all in the same brown ink: corks, gull bones, splinters of green bottle.  Put in order, they made a diary of the occupation, encrypted in the beach alphabet her mother had devised when the deputies cut the camp telephone. 

Every morning the women had arranged beach debris as messages for the bookship, which came with milk in its ballast tanks and went away with affidavits.  Only the pilot and her mother had known the alphabet entire, and the pilot was in the ground.  Her mother was below it. 

Retrocognitively trapped, the doctors called the people who had looked into the lagoon as the armed men were driving them across it.  They moved now only in the glass, forever trying to finish what the living had the luxury of calling history.  For thirty years the dunes had kept even that much of her mother from Cora. 

At noon, said the judge, the county would auction the beach unless he could show cause to stay the sale.  Cora took him down in the basket with her eyes fixed on its wicker floor.  At the lip of the lagoon she could hear a woman's dry spit striking sand, a sound she had forgotten could mean disgust or simply thirst.  She remembered three signs: milk wanted, child coming, and parley, the invitation to meet without either side putting up its hands.  She made that last one of two cracked oars on the sand, not crossed but laid blade to blade. 

Looking through the weave of the basket's shadow, she saw her mother bend where the oars were reflected.  That was where the pilot must have stood on the last morning, waiting for something to take aboard.  Her mother set a gull bone between two loops of kelp, exactly the sign that closed each entry in the diary.  Then she opened her empty hands.  Cora knew that gesture from her own kitchen; her mother was waiting for an offer that she could bear to take. 

She laid out the letter with the earliest date, and in the glass her mother put a child's shoe inside one of the loops.  Cora added another letter; another shoe appeared.  She could see the bargain now, not as a charge to be proved but as her mother must have seen it, one child at a time.  Forty letters saying the land had been freely given, in exchange for forty children allowed to leave the camp. 

The judge had seen it too, but what he wanted was the name on every shoe, a living witness to set against each signature.  Cora could have asked for those names; in the glass her mother had already begun turning back the tongues.  Thirty years the children had lived here under other names, in a county that still had the same sheriff, though he walked now with two sticks.  Instead she took the two oars apart, closing the parley, and gave him the name by which she herself had been registered in the camp.  One witness was cause for a hearing, she said; the others could come to the library if they chose. 

While he wrote the stay across his auction notice, Cora knelt to gather the letters before the sand took them back.  In the black heat under her hands her mother was fastening forty shoes, pulling each knot tight enough to last a child all the way into another life.
