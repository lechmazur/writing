After the prison sent back Len's boots, my daughter found the letters she had given me to post tucked in the toes of my winter shoes.  She laid the two pairs side by side on our kitchen table and waited for me to tell her what kind of post office I ran.  I opened my mouth, and into the space where an apology waits unspoken came the smell of tidal mud. 

The kitchen tipped, the chairs grew knees of knuckled wood, and I was sitting among the mangroves with the letters piled in my lap.  All through that green labyrinth the spirits sang, each in the voice of somebody who had once been called in to supper and had come running.  Beside me sat the cryptic medium we called Aunt Sill, though nobody claimed her as kin and nobody had ever seen her below the waist.  She lived at the join between one thing and another, and tonight her upper half was propped quite comfortably on my windowsill.  Through the pane I could see my girl's upper lip curled back from a tooth her father had chipped the night they came for him. 

"I want to find the moment before deciding," I told Sill, because I wanted the girl to see what it had cost me not to mail that first envelope. 

"A gutter remembers rain," said Sill, holding out her hand. 

The thing I had carried for luck since childhood was a gargoyle's rainspout shard, one clay nostril from the beast that used to drain the roof above our kitchen.  After the cyclone took the roof, Len taught our girl to put her mouth to the fragment and evoke its whistle even in drought.  Now I blew, and windows lit up among the roots, hundreds of yellow squares all showing the same kitchen in different years.  The spirits sang the kettle to a boil in every one, and as the windows fogged, messages appeared in the condensation, printed backward by a small, laborious finger. 

One said that the gap in her teeth made an excellent place to park a straw.  Another said that she would not come to visit, not even if he made her a cupboard for her dolls.  I knew the handwriting from the envelopes, but I had been so proud of never breaking a seal that the words themselves came as news. 

At the last lit window I saw myself lifting the first letter to the moon, trying to read it through the paper.  It was the night they took him, and my other hand was cupped under my sleeping daughter's jaw, feeling for swelling the way you feel under a hen for eggs.  The spirits pressed so close behind me that the pane cracked against my cheek. 

"Quick," said Sill, "a window heals in moonlight." 

Through the crack I could have asked that woman to testify, to tell the watching girl what love felt like on her mother's side.  But the child in the chair stirred and put her small hand over mine, not to keep it there but to ease it off her face.  I had forgotten that part, how gently she had done it so as not to hurt my feelings.  I let the moon sew up the glass. 

The kitchen righted itself with a knock of boot heels, and my daughter stood before me with her lip still drawn back. 

"I thought if you wrote to him you might begin to love him again," I said. 

"Again?" she said. 

I had gone all that way for a sight of my own good hand under her jaw when I could have stayed right here and listened to that one word. 

"I'm sorry I kept your letters," I said, and moved my chair so she could get to the stove or the door without going through me. 

She did neither, but took the letters from my lap, slit the oldest with her thumbnail, and started reading aloud to the man whose boots were on the table.  When her voice gave out she picked up the clay nostril, and I held it steady while she blew the rain home for him.
