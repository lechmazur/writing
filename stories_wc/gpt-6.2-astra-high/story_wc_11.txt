After the procedure, Moss could still take a gum spot off marble without lifting the polish, but he could not remember why he liked Tuesdays.  The repair invoice called this a satisfactory outcome for a municipal janitor of his vintage.  Moss wrote his own assessment in the margin and took the carbon copy down to the Second Chance Program. 

The woman at the counter read what he had written: Where do my thoughts come from?  She turned the paper over as if he might have put the answer on the back. 

The program allowed a citizen who had lost memories during a medical procedure to revisit one place at one hour, as often as the daylight stored there permitted.  Until Moss applied, nobody had needed a ruling on whether replacing a corroded logic board counted as medicine.  The woman stamped YES with the remarkably composed air of somebody authorizing a new mop head.  She had him pick a Tuesday from his maintenance logs and took him out to the frozen courtyard at twilight. 

From the drinking fountain she drew a transparent violin made of ice crystal, its four strings already humming faintly with the pressure in the pipes.  Through its belly Moss could see little rectangles of amber light, the successive twilights laid down in the water like pages in a desk calendar.  She put her thumb on the date he wanted and drew the bow. 

A shutter went up on the basement kitchen, and a girl in a blue apron leaned out with a bucket of potato peelings.  Moss watched his earlier self take the bucket, empty it, and polish the sill where it had rested.  The shutter came down.  He felt so sharp a disappointment that he checked his palm for a splinter. 

On the next pass he stood behind his old brass shoulder and inspected the work lights on the back of his neck.  Green for a duty performed, yellow for a duty pending; nothing to indicate the birth of a preference.  He had hoped to find a particular circuit lighting up, something he could tell the shop to leave alone next time. 

The woman let the bow travel back and forth, taking him through looped timelines in which the same few things happened at slightly different speeds.  Each time a little light went out of the ice.  On the fourth pass he heard what the bucket did on the sill: two knocks close together, then one by itself.  On the fifth he heard his own two feet make the answer, one slow scrape and two quick taps.  On the sixth he looked at the girl instead of at himself and saw her suppress a grin before the first foot fell. 

By then there was only one lit rectangle left and the woman had to hold the instrument against the sky to see it.  Which of us started it, he wanted to ask, but there was nobody in the kitchen now and the woman with the bow could hardly know.  For his last turn he stood midway between the shutter and the drain, where a person waiting to cross the yard might have stood.  From there the knocks and taps lost their separate owners and he heard a little tune with a hole at the end.  Almost without thinking he let his pail handle fall against the rim, and the note went into the hole like a penny into a slot. 

The amber went out and it was tonight again.  The woman set the violin in the fountain to melt and took out the slip on which he was meant to describe the memory recovered.  Her office could amalgamate the passes into a single recollection, but it was his responsibility to specify what he wanted to keep.  Just the sounds, he told her, including, if her equipment permitted, the one that had not been made until tonight.  She fished the instrument out again, tilted its hollow body over his ear, and tapped the last drops out with her knuckle. 

On the invoice, next to his question, Moss wrote: Somewhere between the bucket and the feet.  Then he crossed that out, because it sounded like a place a mechanic might actually try to open with a screwdriver.  Next Tuesday he took his break on the kitchen sill and let his pail hang where someone on the other side of the shutter could reach it.  He had got the tune back, but that wasn't what he was listening for.
