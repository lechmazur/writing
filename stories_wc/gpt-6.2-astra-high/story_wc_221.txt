After the shutdown I got my daughter through the hatch of the clock well, but the iris shut against my knuckles.  Through the thick glass overhead I saw her land on her bottom on the time dilation observation deck.  She was still wearing the paper crown they had given her for our wedding anniversary, since neither bride would put it on.  Then the light began to beat like a fan blade and all I could see of Nell was a smear. 

By the time I thought to knock, somebody had already built a rail to keep people back from the glass.  Three knocks came back to me off the belly of the well; no sound came down.  An engineer laid a slate face down above my head and left it there long enough for me to read.  Without power the field would take seventy years to unwind outside, perhaps seven hours for me.  Breaching the temporally stretched well would bring the two clocks together inside my skull.  No one proposed to try. 

Next came a sheet of paper on which the city blessed me for pushing out the child, as if any parent needed a citation for that.  Somebody had altered the official seal to show a woman holding up a baby, although Nell was six and I had shoved her by the seat of her pants.  I was something new in the city's long ecclesiastical history, a blessed pariah whose very threshold nobody dared cross.  People brought their babies to the rail in such numbers that the faces made a pale continuous ribbon.  I wanted one face out of that ribbon with a hunger that frightened me. 

Then a familiar star appeared overhead, the gilded compass rose applique from the cover of our anniversary book.  My wife, Jo, had unpicked it and tacked it to the back of a mitten so I could glimpse her hand even in the milling crowd.  She laid the book open. 

Every year on the date of our wedding we sketched the night sky, first separately while we worked different boats, then side by side, complaining about each other's elbows.  We always drew the horizon and found true north by plotting the stars twice, an hour apart, to catch the pole about which they turned.  From the pole's height above the water each of us could tell how far north the other's boat had gone. 

This year's drawing was Nell's, unmistakably, because every star had eyelashes.  All she had drawn below them was the rail.  The next page showed the rail again, in rather better perspective.  I understood then what it cost two people to keep a mitten visible to me. 

I took the grease pencil from the instrument board and wrote on the glass, backward for their benefit: HOW FAR SOUTH BEFORE THE POLE TOUCHES WATER?  Jo would know that I knew; Nell would want to find out.  For what felt like ten minutes nobody came, and I had leisure to regret that question with every bone in my body. 

Then the mitten came down, salt stiff and split at the thumb.  Page after page slid by under it, the pole sinking until a black wave took a bite out of the star nearest it.  At the bottom Nell had written that on the equator a person could see both halves of heaven if she was willing to turn around.  I wrote back: WHAT DOES THE OTHER HALF LOOK LIKE? 

We got a life out of that pencil, sharpening it with the edge of a key.  One day the mitten came down on Nell's hand instead of Jo's, and stayed quite still while I put my palm against it.  By then she could afford to give me that much time. 

When the iris finally opened, there was an old woman sitting on the deck with her legs dangling into the well.  She had a paper crown on her white hair, and a book on either knee.  I climbed up beside her and waited for the echo of my boots to die.  She opened the second book to a clean page and asked me, not yet looking at me, where I wanted to go.  South, I said, and felt her elbow come up against mine as she reached for the pencil.
