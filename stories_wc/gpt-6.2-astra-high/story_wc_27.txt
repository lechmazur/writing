In the margin of daylight, when the prison yard was still dark but its wire shone pink, Ada climbed the crane in the abandoned spaceport repair bay.  She carried her father's vintage opera glasses in her teeth and used her bare toes to find the rungs the salvagers had cut short.  From up there a stargazer could still catch the morning star, though it had been weeks since she had climbed for that. 

Eight days ago the governor had announced that the detained dockhands had accepted bonuses to break the strike and had already departed for the orbital yards.  Their families had taken down the picket awnings, embarrassed to have stood in the rain for people who had evidently taken a better offer.  In that lull in the rebellion Ada had done nothing but sort through the mess her father had left under his cot.  She wanted it to mean something that he had taken neither his good boots nor the little brass glasses through which he had taught her the sky. 

Every dawn she watched the yard fill with men in identical caps, small as the print on his supposed contract.  This morning, instead of trying to see their faces, she looked where they looked.  Not one was looking at the gate.  They watched a man kneeling to lay his cheek against a standing stone, as if the poor fellow had fallen in love with it. 

Ada nearly dropped the glasses.  Directly beneath her crane stood the other half of that ancient stone circle, which had been old when the first surveyors divided it with a fence.  She had spent whole nights on its warm slabs, mapping constellations with her father and feeling through her soles the hum which came before the sun.  She had never thought to wonder what the hum sounded like from inside the stone. 

Down the crane she went, across the grit and onto the slab opposite his.  At first there was only the usual enormous chord, as peaceful as thunder heard by someone who knows the washing is in.  Then, with her ear pressed flat, she could hear what the man was doing to it.  He was breaking the note into lengths, the way riggers signaled through a girder when the rivet guns made speech impossible.  She knew only three signals, and he kept making one of them: Take the weight. 

She struck the stone with the brass hinge of the glasses: Weight taken.  The silence that followed lasted just long enough for her to imagine explaining to her mother that she had been talking to a rock.  Then every stone in the bay began to sing under the blows of unseen hands. 

By breakfast she was standing on an upturned pot in the dock canteen, steady as a surveyor from the ankles up, clutching its greasy rim with all ten toes.  "I can't tell you who's there," she said, "but I can tell you where to put your ear." 

A speech would not reignite the strike, but there were riggers among them, and a rigger does not lightly ignore a call to take the weight.  Next morning her mother took the crane and the glasses while Ada and six women knelt on the cold side of the circle.  The moment the first rim of sun touched the stones, the yard began sending names.  One woman wrote on the back of a flour sack; another, who had worked the girders for forty years, struck back each name to be sure. 

Ada's father was the nineteenth, and by then her mother had come down from the crane because she could no longer hold the glasses still.  His message after his name was not for them.  It was for the mother of a boy who had no name on any contract, a kitchen runner taken up by mistake.  He wanted somebody to get word to her that the boy was eating. 

That was where the picket started up again, at a kitchen door, with a mother who had never before been asked to join it.  By noon the flour sack was hanging from the port gates, and the dockhands had laid down their hooks until the listed men walked out. 

Under the crane that evening Ada wiped stone dust from the dented hinge with the hem of her shirt.  One lens had cracked; through it the first star looked like two stars leaning together, each taking a little of the other's weight.
