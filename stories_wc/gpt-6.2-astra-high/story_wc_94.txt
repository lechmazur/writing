Every year the last bus climbed through the clouds to the observatory, carrying eleven white poppies for eleven children who had failed to come home on that same bus.  As the air began to rarefy, people stopped singing and saved their breath for the last steep walk from the turnaround.  Nobody offered to carry Mara's poppy; everybody knew that even a censored poet was allowed the weight of one little brother. 

Above the clouds the world was all floor and no walls, and lightning walked about under that floor like somebody looking for a dropped coin.  In the stormlit observatory the old conductor was waiting beside the darkroom timer, his thumb on the switch that would start the customary minute of silence.  Instead of pressing it he gave Mara the look he used to give children traveling on somebody else's ticket.  From the pocket where he had once kept his punch he took a sealed envelope wedged in an old bus timetable, and put the whole thing in her hand. 

The timetable fell open at the children's camp, the stop the ministry had struck from every map after the dam gave way.  She had written a book to put that stop back, only to see the whole edition pulped and come home with ink still on her fingers.  Inside the envelope was a strip of negatives and a penciled message: We heard about the flowers. 

"Who is we?" she asked. 

He unhooked the timer and led her into the darkroom where astronomers had once developed photographs of the moon.  Through the partition she could hear a vigil in pause, the coughs and shifting feet of people suddenly deprived of the one thing they knew how to do together.  Under the red lamp he fitted the strip into the enlarger with a furiously tranquil precision she remembered from the day he had driven them down through rising water.  Then too his hands had been steady enough to make her angry, as if he were choosing to have an ordinary working day.  Now he set the timer for eight seconds and a rectangle of light fell on the waiting paper. 

In the developer there rose a man's open hand, palm up, with a white seam wandering from the base of the thumb to the little finger.  She had spent half her life trying to understand the cartography of scars, how a mark could lead you back to the precise inch of earth where somebody got hurt.  Here was their mother's kitchen, the blue milk jug on the tiles, the small boy kneeling to pick up the pieces before anybody could scold his sister.  Mara put one finger in the developer as if the hand might close on hers. 

Everybody knew that the last eleven children had gone in a bread van; everybody knew the van had been found upside down against the border fence. 

"So they got through," the conductor said, and his voice cracked on the word through like a boy's.  He had spent fourteen years sending letters by drivers licensed to cross the pass, and this was the first thing that had come back.  "No faces," he said, "because the border police open things, and there are people on the other side who must have taken those children in." 

He let her wind the strip through the enlarger: a crooked elbow, two toes webbed together, an ear with a nick like the bite in a baking apple.  Mara carried the wet print into the dome at that hour before dawn when masks slip for sheer want of strength to hold them up.  The mothers who always thanked her for her beautiful verses looked at the hand without even trying to be kind. 

"Not mine," the first one said, and Mara loved her for the naked hunger in it. 

"There are ten more," she said, and set her poppy down to show the conductor that she wanted him to teach her how to work the light. 

By sunrise the timer had measured not one minute for the dead but eleven small helpings of light for the living.  On the back of the timetable Mara began a poem about the children that nobody in the ministry would recognize as such.  It was me that broke the jug, she wrote, then passed the pencil on.
