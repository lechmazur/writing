The afternoon the river touched the black peg, Ada drew a brass button from the silt box and laid it on the lid of the jar.  Inside was a canyon echo, caught during the flood that had stripped the valley a hundred years before.  It spoke in a man's considerable voice: "There is only the high stair."  Ada, whose office as hourglass turner required her to allow petitioners precisely three minutes with the oracle, turned the glass for the next man.  He drew another brass button and received the same reply. 

By supper the able had started up the stair, and those who could not climb were waiting in the amber warehouse beneath the cliffs.  Among them was Ada's father, who had spent his good knees carrying honey-colored lumps of fossil gum down those two hundred steps.  He had brought his whetstone, as though wherever they went now there would be knives to sharpen.  Nobody had brought him a chair. 

Ada sat on the floor beside him and tried to unscramble what troubled her about the voice.  It was not the cruelty of the sentence but the little hitch the man took before saying stair, identical each time, like a place where a needle caught.  She fetched the silt box, which held the lost buttons dug from the old flood deposit when the warehouse foundations were shored.  Children learned that any one of them, given to the echo, would buy the valley a glimpse of its next great flood. 

She began by cataloging lost buttons on the whitewashed floor, with a piece of charcoal for her pen and her father for occasional advice.  Seventeen brass, all bearing the same dented crown; six of jet, worn flat on one edge; one of river shell, its holes still plugged with green silk.  Her father lay down and arranged the brass into two rows on his chest, the odd one at his throat.  "That was the tollkeeper's coat," he said.  "My grandfather had to polish the blessed thing." 

Seventeen chances for one man to speak, thought Ada, and she had been giving him three minutes every time.  She set a jet button on the lid.  "Haul away when I raise my hand," said a woman, so close that Ada felt for a hand on her shoulder.  The shell gave a child's thin complaint: "But the basket scratches." 

For a moment there was the silence that comes up with a spadeful of earth when the blade has struck something made by hands.  Then her father looked up at the high square hatch through which no amber had passed in living memory.  Across its lintel ran a black groove which the children called the giant's smile.  Ada had always supposed the amber came down the stair because that was the way her father had brought it. 

Quietly inspired by the child's objection, she took a tall wicker hamper from the packing room and sat down inside it.  From there she could see through the hatch to a pair of stone piers at the lip of the cliff.  "Where would you stand to see her hand?" she asked.  Her father got in beside her, an undignified business which made them both laugh, although the water was coming in under the door.  He tied his white handkerchief about her wrist, and she raised her hand as high as ever she could reach.  Somebody at the piers waved back. 

Within an hour the climbers had hauled a packing rope up the stair and slung it over a cart axle laid between the piers.  From his little window at the foot of the stair the tollkeeper could never have seen the hatch; he had spoken for the length and breadth of his view.  Ada put his buttons away with the others, not on top this time but wrapped together in a scrap of the packing cloth. 

She turned the hourglass on its side and stayed below to steady the hamper while the others boarded, one by one, each with a folded coat to sit on.  When her father's turn came he left the whetstone in her palm.  By then it was too dark for anybody to see a hand, but a struck jar gave a note that carried all the way to the piers.  There was nothing prophetic about the two clear strokes she sent through the hatch; they meant that he was ready, and that they could begin to pull.
