Every time a pledge came out of the strongbox, Nell painted it before handing it across the counter.  After the water wars, when the island court ordered the return of everything taken in exchange for drinking water, it also ordered a record.  Thus the poor got their spoons back and the court got a handsome book of spoons. 

Nell worked best between midnight and dawn, having long ago lost the knack of going under with the sun and coming up clean on the other side.  On the page she could at least thresh one thing from another: this was a dent a child had bitten in a spoon, and this was merely the shadow it cast. 

One evening a man came over the causeway on the last of the ebb to claim an amber brooch.  Inside it a winged ant had died with one wing neatly folded, as though there had been time to do half the necessary things.  The man knew about the ant; Nell set the brooch on his receipt and passed him the pen.  He crossed out the words "Water supplied by Island Council" and wrote "Our own water" above them. 

"I can give you back the pin," Nell said, "but you'll need a more expensive grade of painter to revise history." 

"I can wait for her," he said, and sat down. 

His name was Kit and he had helped his mother lay the gutters on the barracks where the mainland families were quartered.  Whenever it rained they heard water running under their floor toward the council tank, the very tank outside which his mother had stood undoing the clasp at her throat.  Nell remembered that throat, the red spot the pin had left, because she had been the girl behind the grille.  She had always supposed the barracks gutters emptied into the sea; indeed she had painted the blue arrow on the tank house mural that said they did. 

They went outside into a fine rain, each privately expecting the other to come round by breakfast.  Since the barracks had been pulled down, there was nothing to go by but the way the grass lay.  Yet by following rain trails in the flattened blades Nell found four hollows at exactly the intervals Kit remembered between the bunks where the downpipes used to keep the children awake.  At the lowest hollow she put her ear to the turf and heard the gulp of an enormous sleeper turning over.  Kit fetched the spade while she stayed listening, palm down, as if the thing might bolt. 

By lantern light they lifted a slate and saw two glazed channels meeting like the legs of a wishbone.  One came down from the council roof and one from the barracks; past their junction a single trough ran straight through the footings of the tank.  All these years salt had been growing its white fur in the empty channel, and now the rain off the grass was combing a dark parting in it.  Nell could scarcely lift the lantern, yet she wanted more than anything to keep looking. 

"How much of it was ours?" Kit asked. 

She nearly said half, for the two channels were the same width and it would have made a splendid picture. 

"I don't know," she said, and for the first time since they had met he seemed disposed to believe her. 

They took the slate to the counter and propped it against the strongbox, still wet on its secret side.  On the facing page to the little painted ant Nell drew what they had unearthed, giving both channels the same plain wash of brown.  Below it she wrote that the water received from the barracks had never been measured, although every cup issued there had been.  She signed where the claimant was supposed to sign; it seemed reasonable that somebody on her side of the grille should acknowledge receipt. 

Kit pinned the brooch inside his cuff, the ant folded against the beat of his wrist.  When the tide went out he left, and Nell got her paints and a ladder and went to the tank house wall.  Under the new brown lines she left the old blue arrow legible; people ought to see how straight a line she could draw to a place the water had never gone.
