When they caught the last fish, they found a hundred little fish in its mouth, all facing out.  Mara's father called this proof that the sky still trusted them, and ordered a silver grille fitted over the mouth of the holding tank.  He had lost three sons fishing the upper sea; he knew what could happen to anything you let out of your sight. 

Mara, his remaining child, was a lukewarm mage, which meant she could bring any quantity of water to the temperature of her palm and not one degree higher.  She had always been earnestly naive about the uses of this gift, offering to warm the feet of statues that looked cold.  Her father had put her in charge of the tank. 

By the seventh morning the lower sea had risen to the windowsills, and the upper sea had grown so shallow that stars showed through it even in daylight.  Everyone knew how water went home: a skyfish swimming up a shower drew a long shining wake of water after it, as a needle draws thread.  The council had charged Mara's father with keeping the last brood safe until a proper storm came.  Nobody liked to mention where storms were supposed to come from now.  Whenever Mara tried, her father tapped the grille and counted the small pale noses. 

On the eighth morning she carried the tank out onto the sapphire viaduct at dawn, as much to give the old fish a different view as anything else.  The blue stone sweated even in dry weather, and where a drop fell through the grille a little nose poked out, then withdrew.  Under a faster leak the whole head came out.  She walked the tank from arch to arch, counting drips against her pulse, until she understood that it wasn't the size of the drops that mattered.  At three drops a beat the little ones could climb, flicking from one falling bead to the next without losing the height they had gained.  She slapped a hand over the grille to stop the first one through, and heard her father in the sound. 

At the landward end of the viaduct stood the broken ship's bell from her brothers' boat, hung there by her father on the day he stopped waiting for them.  Children sometimes poured water into its upturned bowl to watch it leak from the hairline crack in a silver dotted line.  Mara ran for the mallet in the tollhouse. 

From the top of the tollhouse steps she could touch the flat bright underside of the upper sea; boats had once been launched there, bottoms up.  It took her until noon to rebuild one slipway backward, with the bell wedged in the old launch cradle so its rim breached the upper sea and its cracked bottom dripped toward the tank below.  The first drops came a thumb's breadth apart; the third froze in the crack, for it is cold where the stars shine through.  She put her palm in the bowl and the narrow stream resumed, too fast now, almost solid.  Someone would have to tip the cradle by degrees while she kept the water warm. 

Her father was already halfway up the steps, breathing hard, with the silver grille dangling from his hand.  He had twisted it getting it off, exposing the underside, where three names were worked in silver across the bars.  Under each name was the date and hour of the boy's birth.  She had thought devotion would have made a stronger thing; the grille had snapped across the middle where he had gripped it. 

"How fast?" he asked. 

She counted aloud and he tilted the bell until her voice and the drops agreed.  Nothing happened, and she was beginning to feel sorry for them both when a little fish went by her ear so close she felt the wind of its tail.  A thread fine as spider silk came up behind it, then another, then so many that the tollhouse steps ran with water going home.  Her father held the cradle steady long after the old fish passed, with its great mouth hanging slack and its work done. 

By evening the lower sea had fallen one brick below the windowsills.  At supper her father asked if the old fish would come back, and for once in her life Mara did not say yes just because somebody needed her to.  He put another potato on her plate, and they ate while the rain began.
