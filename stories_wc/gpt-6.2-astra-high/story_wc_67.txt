At the Vanishing Point Gallery, the best seat in the house is a rocking chair, because nobody ought to mistake the view from it for the way things are.  My sister Nell does the lettering and I am the charcoal portrait ash scatterer, a grand title for somebody who goes home with gray cuffs.  I draw on sheets of gummed glass, then powder the pale parts with ash through a tea strainer; seen in alignment, six flat drawings acquire the depth of a cheek. 

Lately I have been trying to make our father, who died in the earthquake when we were girls.  Nell has brought the mask she made from shattered porcelain shards that the men picked out of our coats when they dug us out of the china shop.  The white crescents come from cups, the pink of the ears from a platter on which a painted Englishman had once hunted a fox.  It hangs by fishing line behind the glass, supplying the face with its little chips of reflected daylight.  I have given him his good black eyebrows and the smiling mouth that goes with the account Nell has written for the wall. 

Nell weaves truth carefully, quoting only things that one or the other of us actually said to the man from the paper that afternoon.  Papa smiled at us the whole time, I told him, and for forty years people have been telling it back to me. 

Now Nell sits down to see what her words look like with a face attached.  She gives herself one good push with the heel of her shoe.  On the forward swing our father looks composed, even handsome; on the backward swing his mouth drops down his neck and his eyes go to pieces. 

Under the shop counter I could see him through a hole about the size of my two hands cupped together.  There was a confusion of white beyond him that I took for daylight on the street, and I understood his steady gaze to mean that somebody was coming.  What I wanted when I began the portrait was to crystallize that instant of enormous ease, when I could uncurl my fingers and let go of my sister's dress.  But the light had come from a heap of crockery under a crack in the roof, and there was another whole floor of the building on his legs. 

"Where were you sitting?" Nell asks, without taking her eyes off the glass. 

"Right beside you," I say. 

"Then how did you see his mouth?" 

I come round behind the rocker and lower my head until my chin grazes her hair, which smells of the oranges she has been peeling all morning instead of eating.  Each time the chair tips back I lose the mouth first, and what I get instead, in the glaze of a teacup, is the small wet flash of an eye.  Of course the hole was only big enough for him to show us one thing at a time.  It had been his eye that stayed there, so close that once I saw a lash come loose and lie along the lower lid like a drowned ant. 

"He wanted to see us too," I say. 

Nell reaches up without looking and squeezes the hand I have laid on the back of the chair.  The man from the paper had asked if Papa had smiled, and I had wanted then what I want now, for somebody to know how it felt to be looked after. 

I turn the mask sideways on its string and slide five sheets out of their grooves, until only the eye shows beside a jagged edge of china.  Instead of the mirage of safety I have spent a winter getting right, there is a man looking through a little window at the only part of the world he can do anything for. 

As the old rocking chair finally slows to a stop, Nell takes the card from her pocket and turns it over to the side where nothing has been written.  "This is what we could see," she writes, holding the card on her knee, and I blow the loose ash off the glass so he can see it too.
