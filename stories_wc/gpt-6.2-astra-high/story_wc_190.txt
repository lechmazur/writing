On the morning they hired her to carry a minute of silence, Nell put felt slippers on the four iron feet of her passenger bench.  The bench had squeaked through weddings and one difficult birth, but a commission from the rail authority could buy her a whole winter of coal. 

Waiting on the drifting prism terrace was a man with a storm glass vial in the breast pocket where a bridegroom might have worn a flower.  His other pocket sagged with a meter for monitoring noise levels.  He introduced himself as Abel, the mason who had built the waiting room at the new terminus for the drowned valley.  Under its great stone vault, he said, even a woman unwrapping a sandwich sounded as if she were tearing linen for bandages.  What he needed from Nell was the quiet of the old station, to lay like a wash over the stone.  She was the last gondola operator who could find that station without a steeple or a chimney to steer by. 

The terrace had once been its skylight, and when the valley filled, the putty had held and the whole glittering acre had floated free.  These days you could look down through its lozenges and see trout hanging over farms, their shadows gone ahead of them across the furrows.  Nell took Abel aboard and set her oar against the notch worn by her father's oar before it was worn by hers.  His reasoning had been fluidly logical: if you wanted to know where something lay under moving water, first ask what the water had done with everything else.  She followed the trout upstream to the submerged railway embankment, the one place left where the current ran fast enough to flush their eggs with air.  There she turned along the track until Abel's meter showed a notch in the water noise, the breadth of a waiting room. 

Now they had only to wait for a train on the ridge to blow its whistle.  It takes a long falling note to catalyze the silver in storm glass; whatever comes immediately after the note is what the silver keeps.  In the quiet that follows a train whistle's echo, Abel drew the stopper, and a thread the color of milk climbed the vial.  He held it to her ear. 

She heard the particular quiet of a house where somebody has taken out the clock, then the nail, then the wall.  Across the aisle in that handsome new waiting room there would be a boy with his feet on a suitcase, in transit like everybody Nell had once known.  She thought of that vacancy descending on him from a height of forty feet.  Without asking leave she dipped the vial and let the lake have its silver back. 

Abel looked at her for a long time before he said that the money for the glass was his own.  His wife cleaned the terminus at night; lately she had been coming home with lint stuffed in her ears.  He had wanted to teach silence how to comfort her, not how to make a better tomb. 

By then the terrace had come drifting down on them, bringing the slant gold of late afternoon into the boat in bars.  On its underside were the initials of the porters who had washed it every spring, including those of Nell's father, who had done anything for wages between fares.  She could remember lying on that bench while he poled home, counting each squeak as proof that he was still there, that she could go to sleep.  She knelt and took the slippers off. 

At the sound Abel glanced down at his meter, then laid it facedown on the bench and sat beside it.  One last westbound freight was crawling up the ridge; he tipped the few remaining drops of silver into the neck of the glass.  When the echo went, Nell let the boat rock as it would, and the bench gave its small confidential answer to the weight of a living man. 

That winter Nell climbed to the ridge to hear what they had made.  High overhead in the new terminus the hush went vaulting from rib to rib, bearing a little wooden sound that might have been a chair drawn up beside a bed.  A woman putting away her mop unwrapped a sandwich and tore it in two for Nell.
