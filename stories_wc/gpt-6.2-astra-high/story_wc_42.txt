On the night they shot my sister off the moon, I took her funeral receipt up to the lunar observatory above the clouds.  The receipt said that one ounce of Ada Vale, together with one ounce from each of six hundred other people, would be accelerated beyond lunar escape velocity.  On the back, in the small print, the company promised only to try.  Ada had spent thirty years cutting tunnels for that company and did not want to spend eternity on its property. 

Mara read both sides, then took off the spectacles that magnified her eyes to the size of peeled grapes.  People called her the sage of the mountain, but in the directory she had put herself down as a camera repairer, available after lunch.  She had taught both of us how to look through a telescope without shutting the other eye. 

We heard the cups rattle in their saucers before we felt the rock move under our shoes.  Through the slit in the dome the moon looked changelessly constant, an impression rather spoiled by the way the slit itself wagged back and forth.  When foundations shake, the first thing an astronomer does is stop the exposure.  Mara went to the cupboard instead and brought out the camera Ada and I had built as girls to photograph the bats in the orchard.  We had meant to harness the ephemeral, which in those days meant anything quick enough to get away from two girls with a net.  It was possible to trip its shutter by the sound of a leaf falling on the copper listening plate. 

Mara asked, "Could we make it do the opposite?" 

I had become an electrician, a fact generally remembered in my family only when a toaster died.  I knew how to restructure the circuit so that a tremor would interrupt the motor pulling film through the camera, but she put her thumb on the motor to stop me. 

"Let it run," she said.  "It's all right to have blanks if you know how long they last." 

I wired the plate to hold the shutter closed whenever the pier trembled, and we fitted the camera to the telescope.  At the edge of the film, in a little round window of its own, we recorded the image of a clock hand that would not stop spinning even if the mountain came down.  One revolution per second, courtesy of a spring I had wound for Ada every evening of bat season. 

At the appointed minute nothing happened that I could see with my naked eyes.  The cups went on with their miserable little music.  I had imagined something that would at least make me put a hand to my mouth, but I stood there eating the biscuit that came with the tea.  That seemed such a betrayal that I was still holding the last bite when Mara came back with the wet film. 

Between stretches of black we found three clear frames of the electric blue lamp riding the canister away from the moon's dark limb.  Taking our distances from a crater rim that showed in all three, and our times from that busy hand, we worked out how fast the canister had gone.  Even the part of its speed that lay flat against the sky was enough. 

In the space where the company had left room for an official seal, Mara penciled in our figures and signed her name.  She passed me the pencil for the second signature, and it was then, with my occupation to put after my name and a crumb stuck to the receipt, that I began to cry. 

All winter I answered letters from people who had bought that same small ration of freedom, enclosing a print of the three frames each time.  Some asked which of the pinpricks was their person.  I told them all three, which was true, and that in the gaps their people had been traveling too.
