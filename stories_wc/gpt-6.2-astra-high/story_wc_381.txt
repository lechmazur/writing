Each evening for nineteen years the dead ferryman predicted the flood that had killed him, and each evening the people on the hill went out to count their children.  It did no good to point to the willow growing in the old riverbed, thick enough now to take a swing.  You got up from supper when you heard the ferry code for water coming, because once, when you had done that, you had lived. 

Ada heard it best at her end of the valley, in the nocturnal haven the mill hands had built beside a quiet brook.  People came there after the late shift to tell the dead what had become of a dog or why a ring had been sold.  A solitary summoner could make a living there, amid whispered secrets, without ever having to raise the one man everybody wanted her to raise.  But tonight a boy had brought a dry crust wrapped in a sock and asked if she thought that would be enough to eat on the roof.  He wasn't born when the water came.  Ada gave him her supper and went down to the brook to hush a prophecy that had already earned its keep. 

She did her work by mapping silence, since the dead, however far their voices carried, could hear only in the little patches where nothing else made a sound.  She walked with a lighted rush, stooping to plant it wherever she ceased to hear its small spit of burning fat, then kindled another from it.  Seen from the hill her charts looked terrestrially cosmic, whole constellations burning steadily among the cowpats.  Tonight the lights stood in two parallel lines, a tranquil blaze that made a familiar shape before she cared to recognize it.  It was the aisle between the benches of her father's boat, with a gap on the left for the bench he had taken out to fit a pram.  He was not calling from the river bottom, as she had thought all these years, but from his place at the tiller, and he was calling into the boat. 

She took from her purse the crane's origami wing, still pink along one edge from the fare printed on the ticket.  Her father used to fold birds on empty crossings, pulling their tails to make them flap for the small girl who rode with him on Sundays.  On the last crossing he had handed her up to a stranger on the bank so fast that the bird had torn between their hands.  She had spent nineteen years wanting to call him back, and nineteen years knowing that to hear him ask for help would finish her. 

Between the rushes she laid the wing where she used to sit.  The pink paper lifted and dipped, lifted and dipped, tugged by a hand working a tail in another country.  For the first time she let the whole message flow through her, past the three long beats that meant the river would rise above the roofs.  There followed a smoothed hush, the kind her father used to make by laying his palm across the ferry bell to stop its ringing clean.  That was part of the code too: a silence exactly long enough for the landing bell to answer with the number put ashore.  The landing bell had gone downstream with its post that Sunday, and nobody had rung the last passenger home. 

Ada had no bell either, but she had the brass thimble she used for pushing rushes into hard ground.  She set it on the crook of her finger and struck it once with her door key.  Such a poor thin note to send a man in place of all the news she had for him.  Yet the wing rose high enough to brush her cheek before it settled in the grass. 

She sat between the benches until the flames went out, listening to the brook rub one loose pebble against another with no more urgency than a woman washing a cup.  At the shelter the boy was asleep, and on her empty supper plate lay the crust he had been saving for the roof.  Ada sat down on the step and ate it in the ordinary dark.
