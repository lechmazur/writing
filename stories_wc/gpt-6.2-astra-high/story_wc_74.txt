On the first night of the saffron harvest, Iona's mother asked her to paint out the writing on the greenhouse door.  From the street, sixty feet below, the roof looked like a dirty lantern somebody had forgotten to blow out.  Up here the smog left just enough sky for the growing moon to chalk its thumbprint on the glass.  The two women sat pinching three red threads from each purple cup, making a silence that would eventually sell by the gram. 

For forty years the door had told newcomers, in seven waterfront languages, that there was a bed behind it.  Now the mattress was going downstairs with Iona's mother, whose knees could no longer manage the climb.  Of the seven languages not one was spoken in the city anymore, but her mother took a promise seriously.  Iona, an insomniac who painted shop signs while their owners slept, offered to make her a copy to take downstairs. 

"What for," said her mother, "when nobody who passed through that door would remember what it said?" 

It was the first time Iona understood that her mother minded being forgotten.  She asked for the six nights left before the movers came.  She meant to prove something about strangers that her mother had once taught her simply by putting clean sheets on a bed. 

Among the things left in lieu of rent was a calligrapher's moon-tipped brush, its white bristles cut in a shallow crescent to fit the waterfront letters.  Iona had used it once to touch up a stroke by moonlight and fallen into a daydream so particular she still remembered the grit of somebody else's chipped tooth.  Now she propped the door wide under the waxing moon and ran the dry tip along the lowest line.  She stayed wide awake.  Nevertheless she was twelve, ashamed to be put to bed in front of strangers, and a woman was hanging her apron from two nails to make a little wall.  When Iona lifted the brush she could smell the onion on that apron, but she hadn't a single word to show for it.  Perhaps it was only the wood remembering, which was not what she had set out to prove. 

Next night she took two pots of paint down to her workshop, where half the waterfront passed each morning on its way to the trams.  She painted the apron on the window, with its two limp strings reaching out of the picture, and left a pot of blue beside it.  Each night she followed another stroke into somebody's half hour of safety; each morning there was another unfinished picture on the glass.  Her trade had made her methodically hopeful: if nobody stopped, she washed the window and tried the picture one pane lower.  She was trying to rouse a memory, not supply one, so she never painted what the woman in the pictures did next. 

On the fifth morning the tram conductor dipped a finger in the blue and put a nail at the end of each apron string.  Then he went inside to tell the painter she'd got the color wrong.  His sister had lain behind that apron, and he remembered yellow because for years afterward she could only sleep with something yellow beside her face. 

When Iona brought him upstairs that evening, her mother was trying to get the mattress rolled up small enough to tie.  He stood watching until she said rather sharply that it wasn't going to roll itself.  He took the loose end and began to sing under his breath, three descending notes and then a fourth that lifted the weight on the turn.  Her mother sat back on her heels.  She hadn't heard the little hauling song since the waterfront clearances, and neither of them knew the words that came after the first four.  Between them they got the mattress tied. 

His sister still had the apron, he told them as they rested, and if they liked he could bring it round. 

"Bring your sister too," said her mother. 

On moving day Iona painted the greenhouse door green and took the last pinch of saffron downstairs to put in the rice.  After supper the sister took the yellow apron from her bag and held it across the bare window where the streetlamp shone straight onto the old woman's pillow.  Nobody had to tell her where to put the nails.
