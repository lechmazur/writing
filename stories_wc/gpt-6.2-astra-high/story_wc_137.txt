The first time I remembered giving birth, I was a childless man of sixty with a mouth full of brass pins.  I was hanging the black bunting in the stargazer's open tower, and somebody had forgotten to tell me the whales were early.  By the parapet my sister Ada put a hand to her belly, where the scar from delivering Nell still pulled to one side.  She caught the pins in her skirt when I spat them out. 

All my working life I had fitted that tower for funerals during the migration, taking down its telescope and carrying up benches for the bereaved.  I never disputed what people felt when the great backs broke the water and the stone underfoot began to thrum.  I just thought that after a lifetime of hearing the same family stories you could be excused for getting the owners mixed up.  Nobody, though, had ever told me about the itch inside a belly already sewn shut. 

By noon we would all be up there to mourn Nell.  She had lived sixteen years, the last three of them very publicly, since it took the whole town to carry her chair wherever she wanted to go.  Ada had asked me for one thing that I had not known how to build: a place to sit among us and keep something of her girl to herself.  Now I had some idea what the thing I was building had to keep out. 

Over the stair was the old stargazer's magic square tile, a little copy of the nine numbered flagstones on the observation deck.  Everybody here knew that the numbers along any row added up to fifteen; it was one of the games she played from her chair.  She used to make me wheel her through forgotten patterns of the game, three stops to a turn, while she kept score on her slate.  Once she made fifteen the wrong way, with two and six and seven, and sat up so straight I thought I had hurt her. 

I got Ada to take my arm and walk that crooked line before the others came.  At two I felt her scar again; at six she tasted brass; at seven we stood together looking at a gull and that was all.  We went back along a straight fifteen and by the last stone our memories were so entangled that we both reached to adjust a nonexistent footrest.  Nell had been trying to teach me, and I had been too busy watching her wheels to notice where we went. 

Her slate was still in the chair pocket, both sides covered with sums and the little rests her music teacher used to mark between measures.  I sat on the stair to transcribe her routes onto strips of leftover bunting, with a white chalk rest wherever she had found quiet.  By noon I had pegged the strips along the floor and set the benches endwise so that even somebody pushing a chair could choose a way. 

The first person up was Nell's father, who had rowed all night from the herring grounds for the chance to hold his daughter again in somebody else's arms.  He saw where the black strip led and stepped across it to take Ada's hand.  When she stepped back he put his face in his wet cap, and I could see what my bit of building had cost him.  I wanted to pull the pegs out. 

Instead I offered him my arm and took him the straight way, past all the people who knew how bitterly we had fought about the cost of that chair.  Let him have the afternoon I planed its handles down to fit my palms, and the weight that came against them when Nell first dared to lean back.  In return I got a whole winter of hauling nets to pay my bill.  Neither of us apologized; for once the accounts could stand just as they were. 

Ada sat one stone over, close enough to lay her cheek against his sleeve without giving up what was hers.  When she was ready she told us Nell had called the chair the Herring, although not to her father's face.  He took a long, suspicious sniff at one of my hands, and that was how the laughing started.  Far below us a mile of water wrinkled as the whales turned south together, keeping time to something not one of us could hear.
