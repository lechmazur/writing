Every New Year's Eve, Ada Bell copied the city onto fresh linen, which was how a lie could survive thirty years without acquiring a crease.  Where the tracks bent around the reservoir, she shaded the water with indigo rubbed almost dry, the right texture for drowned cobbles.  She could place each cobble to within six inches, but she never drew the street. 

This year a girl sat beside her with a rent book that smelled of river mud.  "My gran paid for a room on Spindle Row until the day the dam gates shut," the girl said.  "The claims office says a book proves she paid somebody, but it takes a city map to prove there was a room." 

Ada opened the drawer where she kept a peculiar instrument for checking disputed stops.  No metal showed between her fingers, but there was the familiar weight of brass and a phantom ticket punch took a neat bite from the linen.  The last reservoir train still made its run on this one night, punching the map wherever it used to stop.  The bite fell squarely in the indigo.  Every year Ada trimmed that hole into the shape of a little boat. 

The girl put her finger through it.  "I dare you to show me where this goes." 

She meant the water's edge, but Ada knew that beneath the old terminus lay the fortress of unspoken truths, where the city kept what its servants left off the record.  If one particular sheet of linen was still there, the girl could carry home more than a promise.  "Bring the book," Ada said, taking the punch and leaving the fresh map unfinished for the first time in her working life. 

Midnight caught them under the starlit vault of the roofless terminus, in mist so thick that the girl held on to Ada's belt.  It was the hour that divides bad habits and new starts, although from inside a habit Ada could never tell which way the hour would go.  The first stroke of the clock set a loose tile rocking underfoot.  In the pavement cracks were forming arrows, not all alike: some forked, some doubled back, some ended at the imprints of buffers long since sold for scrap. 

Ada knelt and took a rubbing on the flyleaf of the rent book, using the soft indigo on her thumb.  What looked from above like a tangle of directions resolved on paper into the grain of a feather, each little barb feeding into one shaft.  They followed that shaft down the luggage ramp until it split a wall neatly in two.  Beyond it were battlements of pigeonholes, stacked to a height nobody standing in the mist could measure. 

Ada smelled hot starch and was nineteen again, stretching wet survey linen while the city engineer dictated the alterations for his dam.  He had not told her to erase the houses.  He had told her that the next clean copy would be displayed in the capital with the drafter's name beneath it.  She had understood him perfectly. 

The punch tugged at her wrist like a small dog recognizing its street, and closed its jaws on the corner of a roll just within reach.  Together they unrolled Spindle Row, all forty doors of it, down to the privy shared by numbers six and eight.  The girl found her grandmother's room by its odd triangular yard, just as the old woman had always drawn it in spilled salt. 

"Who did this one?" she asked. 

The signature space was blank; an unsigned survey was worth no more than a rent book.  Ada had spent thirty years wishing for the nerve to say what she now discovered she could say without nerve, her knees banging together on the flags.  "Both maps are mine." 

With her indigo thumb she made the print by which the city certified a mapper's work, whorls and ridges fine as streets.  The girl waited for it to dry before she rolled the linen; she had learned that much, watching Ada. 

At the head of the ramp the arrows had gone, and a milk cart was making two ordinary black lines in the frost.  "Will they pay her now?" 

"I don't know," said Ada.  "I'll tell them what I did." 

"Do you have to tell them why?" 

That was the part Ada had been hoping to leave blank.  "Yes," she said, and let the girl take her to the woman who had been drawing a map of her own in salt for thirty years.
