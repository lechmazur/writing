By the time I admitted what I had done, twenty-three women in our district were wearing pieces of me instead of their husbands.  The river had come through my workshop on a Sunday night and carried off every little envelope of hair entrusted to me.  I had cut my braid off at the nape and spent a month dyeing it to match my order book.  Not one woman had questioned my workmanship, which was worse than if they had all come hammering at once. 

After the confessions and the sale of my tools, I took a job polishing lenses at the timeless observatory.  Here any hour that had ever fallen on our town could be brought into focus, provided somebody in that hour had wanted to be seen.  Across the rest hung what astronomers called the curtains of intention; not even sunlight came through a dead man's wish to be left alone.  I liked working in an institution where refusing to show something was not considered a defect. 

Then paper swallows began to appear in the tin where I kept my polishing rags.  Each had two numbers under its wings and a date tucked into its pointed breast.  It was how apprentices flirted across the galleries, leaving origami figures with coordinates inside, inviting somebody to share a patch of sky or a particularly good thunderstorm.  Mine all pointed down, at the same narrow yard on different afternoons. 

In the first I saw a young man sitting for a tattoo, his sleeve rolled up and a laundry peg between his teeth.  The artist was an older woman whose face stayed dark behind the curtains, although her thick, capable fingers showed quite plainly.  She pressed a tattoo stencil on vellum against his wrist and peeled it back to leave the faint purple outline of a drooping poppy.  On the next afternoon she was pricking in the scarlet, and on the third the man held his arm out toward whoever might one day be watching.  When he bent his wrist the poppy lifted its heavy head; when he crooked his thumb the petals parted. 

That little trick took me straight back to my bench, to the pleasure there had been in making something before it became more important to pass it off.  I wrote on the last swallow, in the smallest hand I could manage, that I would like to meet the birdcatcher. 

The woman who came was my first customer after the flood, the one who had asked if her husband's red hair would fade in sunlight.  She had brought the vellum, still purple along the creases, and the locket I had made her.  When she spread it flat I knew the square knuckles that had held the needle. 

"We had eleven good years of that flower opening for me across crowded rooms," she said.  "Do you suppose you could teach a withering thing to bloom again, if I let you have the pattern?" 

She wasn't offering forgiveness; she wanted an estimate, including materials, and she wanted to see how the thing was made.  I turned the locket over in my palm.  Its back was thin gold, its hinge was sound, and inside was enough of my own red-dyed hair to furnish a dozen poppies.  I offered to remove the hair, but she fetched her scissors and snipped it into flock for the petals. 

"I know whose it is now," she said. 

We worked in the lens room, using the great tracking lamp to illuminate the almost invisible cuts I made in the gold with a borrowed graver.  The first poppy we made opened beautifully but would not bow; I had forgotten that half the pleasure of his signal had been seeing it subside unnoticed.  She unpicked the hinge herself and threaded a single long hair through it to draw the head down again.  The finished article was genuinely artificial: no relic in it, no claim upon the dead, just a little machinery requiring the attention of a living thumb. 

We did not take orders until the other women had been told precisely what we could and could not do.  For our sign she drew a swallow, wings unfolded, with the coordinates of the workshop in its breast.  Sometimes in the market I see a red flower lift above the collar of her coat, and have the immense satisfaction of knowing it is not for me.
