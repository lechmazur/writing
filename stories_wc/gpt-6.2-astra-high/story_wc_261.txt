After the anesthesia wore off, I put my new thumb on my mother's wrist and distinctly heard the geranium ask for water.  The surgeon had warned me that miracles sometimes came displaced; he had once given a blind man the ability to see quite clearly out of his brother's eyes.  Nobody had mentioned the possibility of becoming accidentally telepathic with plants.  Mother watered the geranium while I sat on the edge of the cot trying not to cry. 

Below us the harbor was beginning to stir, but up in the quiet tower at dawn you could have heard a cinder settle.  A meteor went soundlessly down the last strip of night between the cranes.  The thumb, grown on a willow stock, was supposed to help me learn to read the stories in scars.  I had a particular scar in mind, a white hook at the base of Mother's throat that deepened when she swallowed.  She had got it the night she helped forty children tunnel out of the besieged saltworks, and in thirty years she had never told me which of them I was. 

I kept the harbor register; I had reunited sisters, found husbands under other names, and made one old man so unhappy by finding his father that he crossed the street whenever he saw me.  People liked to say I gave things an ending.  Mother said I picked the stitches out.  She had agreed to lend me her scar because, she said, it hurt less than listening to me knock every Sunday. 

Now she laid the frosted hourglass from the saltworks kitchen on its side between us.  She used to turn it for the women who came to claim a child; waiting till the last grain fell gave a frightened child time to recognize a face.  Through the frosted glass no impatient grownup could tell how much longer that might be.  She turned it now.  One glass, she meant, and then I had to let her go. 

I laid my thumb against her throat again and heard, somewhere under my elbow, a small dry voice say, "You're warm."  The desk had put out a pale shoot through its ink hole.  It was willow too, the old kitchen table that Mother had hauled up here when the register was founded.  On its scarred top the rescued children had eaten their first breakfast in daylight.  By examining coffee rings under the slanting light I could make out where the cups had stood; the deepest ring was scarcely wider than a shilling.  I set my thumb inside it and the table remembered heat, not the taste of coffee but the exquisite relief of having something hot set down at last.  Then came the pressure of two elbows on either side of the little cup, and a chin, and the wet salt of somebody weeping.  Not a child's chin, said the willow when I asked; children had to stand to reach its top. 

My mother was watching the sand.  I moved from ring to ring, expecting forty breakfasts, and found the same elbows at every one.  All those years I had pictured the coffee going to the children, who in fact had been given milk.  Here was the record of a single woman sitting down again and again, waiting to see if I would know her. 

"Did she come every Sunday, too?" 

Mother touched the glass with a finger laid across its narrow waist, as though it were a pulse she could press still.  "She came every morning," she said, "until the ice went out and the packet boats could sail.  On the last morning you fetched her the little cup yourself, but you still wouldn't let her take you." 

"Why didn't you tell me?" 

"Because you would have gone looking for a woman who had already done the hardest thing you can ask of anybody.  She let a child who didn't remember her choose somebody else." 

Mother took my thumb and fitted it into the hook at her throat.  It was my old thumbnail that had cut her, she said; in the dark of the tunnel I had held on until we both came out into the light.  When the sand ran out I knew no more of my name than before, but I knew what I had been doing to her door on Sundays. 

I poured water into the little hollow in the desk and asked Mother if next week I might come for breakfast.  She thought about it long enough for the willow to drink. 

"Bring milk," she said.
