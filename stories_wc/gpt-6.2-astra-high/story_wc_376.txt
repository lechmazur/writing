Six days after the oasis dried, Ada turned on the kitchen tap and heard her dead mother ask for the blue cup.  She had it down from the shelf before she remembered.  Across the table her brother Jem said he hadn't heard anything, and the leaves at Ada's wrists went limp.  She wilted near dishonesty, even the harmless kind.  "All right," he said, "but I wanted to finish my egg before we did anything about it."  She sat down and let him. 

Their mother had tended the speaking post, back when water ran beneath the sprawling greenhouse city and carried talk from one glass ward to another.  Under a sky so toxic that even rain left scars on the panes, nobody took a roof off to lay a new line; the post simply borrowed the irrigation pipes.  Ada was its gilded-leaf calligrapher, lettering addresses on bay leaves in gold so the sorters could read them flashing past under water.  Sometimes a leaf slipped its load of voices, and both halves of a conversation went missing.  As children they'd asked where lost conversations went, and their mother had drawn them a treasure map instead of telling them. 

Jem finished his egg and got the crowbar.  He knew exactly which floorboards to lift, having fed the map through a crack strip by strip the afternoon Ada left to apprentice at the post without him.  "I thought it was rats," she said.  "I know," he said, and went on prying.  Down in the dust lay the little nest his spite had made, scarcely bigger than a fist. 

All morning they pieced the shredded map together on the kitchen table, matching the green veins of waterways as patiently as if mending a leaf.  By noon they had a red cross and a route to it that began, impossibly, at a fountain on the wrong side of town.  Ada recognized the little backward curl of the fountain, a flaw she had polished a thousand times on the zinc-etched postal plate fragment their mother used for a paperweight.  Too poor even in those days to waste ink on a game, their mother had taken a rubbing.  Ada held the map to the glass and reread the route from the other side.  It ended directly under the public baths. 

The drained plunge pool had steps for people who couldn't swim, and another flight below those, furred with white mineral wool, for people who mended things.  At the bottom stood a settling tank with a single handwheel and a crust of salt across its open top.  From a fissure came the sound of a boy insisting that he hadn't taken anything, followed by the soft clink of a coin hitting a counter.  Ada's wrists drooped, then straightened.  All the words that had slipped their addresses had settled here like silt; now the tank was drying and they were rising through the empty mains. 

Ada knelt and asked for her mother by name, using the formal cadence for an inquiry about missing post.  A hundred mothers answered, some so faint she had to lay her cheek against the salt.  "I wanted to hear her say she was proud of me," she told Jem, surprised and a little disgusted that this should still be true. 

"I wanted to hear her ask for the cup again," he said.  "That was me she used to ask." 

The handwheel bore the number of their own branch; one good turn would bring every voice in the tank up through their tap, and they could listen for days.  But the boy and his coin would come too, and all those other mothers, talking to children who might even now be standing at their sinks.  Jem took his hand off the wheel first. 

On the walk home he asked what it had been like to work beside her all those years.  Ada almost said wonderful, but the word caught like a bay stem between her teeth.  "She used to stand too close behind my elbow," she said, and Jem hunched one shoulder in exactly the way Ada remembered doing.  He told her what their mother did at home while Ada worked late, how she warmed the blue cup with both hands before she put her false teeth in it.  All these years Ada had been drinking from it.  She had to stop on the bridge by the fountain, she was laughing so hard, and Jem stood beside her until she could walk again.
