After the radio static cleared, I heard my sister say that somebody had fallen into the blossom net again.  Across the gorge her lamp described a little dip, as if the news had weight enough to bend her wrist.  From the watch platform I could see the whole nocturnal arbor above the clouds, its white flowers hanging face down from wires strung between the two walls of the gorge.  The pears opened for one night apiece, and since the moths had gone nobody slept much in blossom week.  Below each row hung a pollinator in an old parachute harness, a dab of lamplight moving from cup to cup.  Below them lay the net, white on white, so that a falling lamp seemed to stop in the middle of the sky. 

They called me the midnight pattern seeker because I could watch those hundred lights and tell where a wire was beginning to sag before anybody felt the slope.  I could also tell who stopped to rest, which was why nobody liked to drink with me in blossom week.  That was the watcher's burden, I thought then: to know things about your neighbors and keep your mouth shut about most of them. 

My sister brought the fallen girl up with one wrist splinted against the little wooden paddle we used for mixing pollen.  It was the third fall since Monday, and the third time the harness had come back with the parachute buckle open and nothing broken.  The other two had been boys on their first paid shifts, and I had put in the ledger that they had been insufficiently instructed.  The girl was the one who instructed them.  She put her good hand over her eyes when she saw the ledger, and the fine yellow dust on her fingers left a stripe across each lid. 

"I was changing tins," she said. 

All along the gorge the lamps were motionless now, though the flowers would close at first light whether we touched them or not.  If I sent those lights moving again on the strength of my signature, everybody out there had to believe it meant something.  I took her harness and her empty pollen tin to the practice wire where we taught the children, two feet off the planks.  My sister turned my lamp off for me. 

In the dark I let my hands remember the work I had done for twenty seasons before anybody thought to put me on a platform.  A sweep of the brush, a half turn on the wire, another sweep; when the brush came up clean I tucked it under my chin.  My left hand went to let the empty tin drop into its collecting sack.  I landed sitting down. 

In spring I had fitted the new tins with catches from the same heap of parachute salvage that had furnished our harnesses.  I had been rather proud of finding a catch a cold hand could work without putting down a brush.  With a full tin the catches hung a palm apart; empty, the tin rode up on its elastic until either tongue lay ready to the thumb.  My hand had been faithfully doing what I had taught it, and what I had given it to do was wrong. 

I switched on the radio before I got up, because I knew what I would be tempted to leave out once I was on my feet.  I said whose idea the tins were, and asked the two boys to come up and help me revise the entries beside their names.  While my sister ferried the girl to the infirmary, we sat on the planks tying the old cloth pollen pockets onto belts.  The boys wrote their own accounts in the ledger; one took half a page and the other wrote three words, "Same as you." 

Just before dawn the boys went back out, their lamps dipping together where the wire took their weight.  This time, watching them, I felt the little dip in my own wrist.  We did not get to the last row before the petals folded inward, yellow trapped inside some and nothing inside others.  In harvest week there would be a bare seam right across the arbor, and I would take my turn beside the scales and tell people why.
