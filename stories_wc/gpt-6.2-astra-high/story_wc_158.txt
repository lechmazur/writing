On the last day of the blossom armistice, Nell's mother forgot the word for daughter but remembered that Nell always got cold feet.  She sat through the farewell speeches with both of them tucked up under her skirts, kneading the left one for luck.  Beyond the quay the last pear petals drifted on water clear enough to cast their little shadows on the reef.  Nell was forty and her feet hung well out at the back. 

Their two towns occupied opposite lips of a gargantuan shell whose hinge sheltered the only orchard for a hundred miles of salt.  Across centuries of longing for the same small patch of sweet ground, the towns had perfected an agreement that let the bees work in peace.  While the pears stood in blossom nobody could lay claim to the hinge, so everybody could go there.  Once the petals fell, whoever crossed the white line forfeited the right to go home.  Nell had crossed it as a bride; she had children on the east lip now, and her mother had nobody left on the west. 

Before the guards came to separate them, her mother produced a galaxy postcard Nell had sent from the planetarium her husband kept before his eyes went bad.  On its black face a swirl of stars had been pricked out with a needle so a child could hold it to the light and have her very own sky.  On the back, under Nell's signature, her mother had penciled a small, decorous question mark.  Her mother pressed it on her with the hopeful air of somebody returning lost property. 

That night Nell went down to the shuttered planetarium, a drum of pink nacre that had made even her husband's lectures on orbital decay sound faintly merry.  She meant to ensconce herself in the projection booth until the dangerous wish to walk straight over the line had passed.  An insomniac poet gets through considerable paper, and for years she had gone there to photosynthesize pain into beauty, as she had put it in her first, regrettably published book.  Tonight beauty could go soak its head.  She wanted her mother to know who had her feet. 

She propped the card against the little lamp in the projector, and a familiar wheel of light climbed the dome, with one outrageous green star out on its rim.  She stood on the lecturer's desk and touched it; a cold breath lifted her hair.  The green was not in the card but in the dome, where a breathing hole no wider than a pencil wore a bright green ruff of moss.  She remembered her husband complaining that on still nights he could hear the western fish auction during the birth of the universe.  Now that she had her ear to the hole she heard, quite distinctly, a woman trying to get a baby to burp. 

By breakfast she had found six such holes and worked out that they opened, through the old shell's fluted chambers, under six different western eaves.  By supper the harbor watch had received five complaints about a shy woman who kept politely inquiring through people's floors where an elderly lady called Alice might be sleeping.  Nell was shyly unstoppable; offered the usual advice about what to do with her head, she merely lowered her voice and asked again.  The sixth hole gave onto a washhouse where somebody knew Alice and could get her there tomorrow if it mattered enough to warrant the loan of a chair.  It mattered. 

Nothing very sensible could be said through the washhouse floor; the tide was coming in and each syllable blew back like the note of a badly tuned reed.  But what had passed between them before Nell knew a single word had passed via half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind, her mother walking the winter quay with Nell buttoned inside her coat.  Nell tried the one about the crab that pawned its trousers for a ride on a jellyfish.  She got as far as the price of the ticket before a thin indignant voice corrected the fare. 

Nell punched a new star for the washhouse and left the card in the projector so that anybody who wanted to could find the place where her mother sat.  By winter a little queue formed there each evening, and the washhouse charged a penny for the chair, refundable if you did not hear from anybody you loved.  Sometimes all Nell heard was the soft scrape of a slipper shifting on a rung.  She would slip off her own shoes then, and let her bare heels knock against the desk for luck.
