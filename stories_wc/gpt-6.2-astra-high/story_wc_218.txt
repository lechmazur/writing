The island caretaker came home from the mainland with six months to live and a receipt for the return half of a ferry ticket.  She put the receipt in the stove and went to see about the blood on the temple floor.  Somebody had swept it into a fan with a palm broom, which was what you got for sending a boy to clean up after a flying fox.  The boy himself sat on the altar with the animal bundled in his shirt.  One long black thumb stuck out between the buttons. 

"You can charge me for the figs she ate," he said. 

"I'll send her a bill," said Ada. 

Together they took her to the woman who mended wings. 

The temple orchard stood inside a ring of living stone pillars, pink where the spring water ran down their flanks and the lime was still soft under its skin of moss.  Between the pillars grew figs for the island and holm oaks for shade, their branches trained into a green vault so dense a funeral could proceed beneath it in a downpour.  Ada had finished closing that vault the summer her mother was buried in a wet dress.  She had received a medal for it.  Now the foxes came in low between the pillars for the ripe figs and, startled by the first mourners at dawn, broke their wings trying to leave by the sky. 

The council wanted netting around the whole orchard before another funeral.  Ada asked for a week and took her grandmother's botanical diary down from the shelf where generations of caretakers had gone to consult it about scale insects.  Her grandmother had been gently systematic, recording not only what she picked but what she left and who came for it.  Beside each little plan of a fig bed was a patch of blue the shape of a coffin lid.  Ada had always taken these for graves. 

On the last page she found a sketch her grandmother had made lying down, judging from the two boot tips sticking up from the bottom margin.  Those blue shapes were pieces of sky, and in each hung a small departing beast with the sunrise showing red through its wings.  A dotted line marked the pallbearers' winding passage between them.  The temple's best kept architectural secret was a courtesy: it had two congregations, and neither had ever needed to duck for the other.  She had been making arrangements for her own funeral on the ferry, but it hadn't occurred to her to wonder who might have to get out of its way. 

"I'm going to remodel," Ada told the council, setting her medal on the table to keep the diary open.  "If you want a roof without holes, there's a perfectly good one on the bank." 

She took the boy up a ladder and showed him the old method, copper wire wrapped around oak branches in an easy spiral so the younger wood could be persuaded out of the way without snapping.  Where her own closures had thickened beyond bending she used a saw, and by Saturday there were three ragged windows full of weather.  She put his name in the diary beside the date the wire must come off lest it bite into the bark. 

"You'll be here then," he said. 

"I might," she said, and let him write her name underneath his. 

At the next funeral rain fell straight through the windows in three bright shafts, and the bearers stopped with their load before the first as if somebody had drawn a sword.  Ada offered the dead man's husband her arm and started around, but he stood listening to a small leather commotion in the figs.  Above them the mended fox hauled herself along a bough by her thumbs, a pale seam puckering one black sail. 

"Can she go in the rain?" he asked. 

"That's her business," Ada said. 

They watched the animal drop, catch herself on the spread of her wings, and climb the falling water into daylight.  The husband took Ada's arm then, and the coffin went the long way round. 

Afterward the boy started to chalk arrows on the floor for the next funeral, but the widower took the chalk from him.  His hand shook a little as he knelt, still in his good trousers, to mark the first turn.  He knew the way now, he said; let him do this much of it.
