The sun never quite went down at the Ember Nexus, because the man who painted the six converging rivers of fire had been paid for only one sky.  Under that permanent twilight, a little tin alchemist knelt beside a glass coffin in which his daughter lay with frost on her eyelashes.  For a penny you could watch him try to vivify her, fail, and weep a bead of mercury into the bishop's outstretched cup.  Even before the letters wore off his sign, everybody at the fair knew him as the Hopeless Alchemist. 

He did not particularly mind failing, but he minded the cup, and he minded what happened in the coffin when the show wound down.  Alone of all the figures his daughter had been given no joint except a hinge to let her mouth fall open at the end.  When the penny drops, even a tin tongue has a moment of liberty before the teeth of the music drum engage.  In that moment once she said, quite distinctly, "Cold."  He went on kneeling as precisely as before, but he began to count the teeth. 

The broken bishop's crozier lay where it had fallen years ago, its gilt curl just visible under the coffin, like the first shaving off an apple in an otherwise fruitless country.  At tooth nineteen, as he reached to test the girl's pulse, his smallest finger could very nearly touch it.  He learned the reach through successive approximation, allowing a little more play in his elbow at each performance, losing whole afternoons whenever the keeper oiled him back to easy obedience.  When at last the hook came up inside his sleeve he could feel how light it was, hollow except for the wire that had once secured it to the bishop's fist.  With such a wire a man might pick the lock of a glass coffin, but what was his child to do with an open lid and no knees? 

At the next fall of copper he asked her, "What would you move if you could?" 

"The sky," she said, and shut her mouth on his astonishment as the first sad bar of music sounded. 

Of course, he thought, looking up at the long spindle on which the scenery was mounted, of course it had been meant to turn.  The painter had simply tacked it fast with the blank side facing back, where nobody could see what he had neglected to do.  If he broke his leg spring he could have a length of good strong steel, enough to link her jaw to that spindle and pull the tack besides.  He had rather liked being able to kneel, which gave dignity to several otherwise absurd moments in the performance. 

He did it on a Saturday when there was a good crowd waiting and he could count on plenty of pennies before the keeper noticed anything amiss.  Outside, the queue shuffled forward; inside, the bishop collected his due while the hook pried steel out of cloth, until the spring broke with a sound like somebody biting an apple.  Three customers saw him feed bright wire through the hinge of his daughter's jaw; a fourth saw him catch the spindle in a loop and twist it tight. 

The fifth was a girl so small her father held her to the peephole in both hands as if offering the box a drink.  She saw the little man pry out the tack, then sink in a heap of gown from which he would not rise again.  She saw the dead girl open her mouth and the rivers run uphill and twilight turn over like a leaf to show its silver underside.  In that unpainted tin the daughter saw, for the first time, the enormous brown eye of somebody trying to see her.  She laughed; the sky swung; the eye broke into lashes which swept like black reeds over the lid of her bed. 

The child demanded another penny, then another, quite unaware that anything had broken, and presently the keeper took down the sign because it was disappointing the people who could still read it.  By winter there were people who came expressly to see their faces as the girl chose to show them, thin as spoons or wide as winter roses.  At the foot of her bed her father sat in his collapsed gown, feeling every tremor of the sky go through the floor.  He kept the tack in case she ever wanted anything to hold still.
