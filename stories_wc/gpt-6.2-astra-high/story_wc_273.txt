After the eclipse ended, the men at the cutting took their smoked glasses off and Sella put hers back on.  An albino tracker could earn her bread in that country, where the wind rubbed out a boot print almost before the boot was lifted, but she had to look after her eyes.  She saw the survey crew stow its lenses, and saw, beyond them, the first small puff of flour from the cliff.  That was how the white rock broke, with something like a cook's sigh before the whole shelf came down. 

This time nobody was under it.  This time, when the alarm sounded, she kept her eyes on the big red second hand above the infirmary door.  It was doing it again, taking its sweet time getting anywhere. 

All the clocks in the wind-carved encampment took their time in a crisis.  The surgeon said that terror did that to a person's wits, and Sella wanted very much to believe him.  Otherwise there was the matter of her brother calling for water for what the inquest had determined was just ninety seconds.  He had died waiting for the company's one litter to come back up from the river road.  What Sella wanted to discover was whether ninety seconds could hold three verses of the song she had sung to him, including the one about the parson's drawers.  You didn't leave that one out, even for the dying. 

She took the artificial hummingbird drone from its pocket in her coat and held it up by the feet.  Its wings beat against her thumb, a steady thirty strokes a second, governed by a crystal no bigger than a grain of salt.  She let it go beside the clock and watched its flight counter through her glasses until the red hand had traveled ten seconds.  Nine hundred strokes.  She had never felt so calm in all her angry life.  She called the surgeon out to see the next ten seconds, and stood with him until he took his spectacles off and wiped them and put them on again. 

"Come along," she said, and he did, letting the bird perch on his hand and carrying it as carefully as a cup of somebody's blood.  The clock cables ran under the rock to the dispatch hut, where the master dial turned behind glass and the dispatcher sat entering the fall in his book.  At their approach he tore the top dispatch sheet off his pad and put it in his mouth.  He had got it down before the surgeon understood what he was seeing. 

Sella had tracked deserters for this outfit once, until she understood that an animal might be born wild but a person under contract had to be consciously wild, every day choosing what not to do.  She did not go for his throat as he plainly expected.  She took the pad. 

From her kit she got the disappearing ink she used to lift a faint impression without spoiling the ground for whoever had to testify to it afterward.  She spilled a little across the fresh page and tilted it toward the sun.  It was through ephemeral patterns in spilled ink that she had traced men who wanted to disappear; a luggage label torn from a stack left its address on the label underneath.  The blue went out of the high dry places first; down in the furrows the pen had scored through from the missing page, it held a moment longer.  She sent the bird hovering overhead to take a picture, but there was time enough for all three of them to read the words. 

ALARM RECEIVED, RATE DIVIDED BY THREE, PER STANDING ORDER. 

"Why keep a record of it?" the surgeon asked. 

"The company bills the quarry for every minute of rescue work," the dispatcher said.  "It wouldn't do to give the quarry the same time you give an inquest, would it?" 

The surgeon sat down on the step with the pad in his lap, as though he had been handed back a patient he had buried.  Sella asked for the old pads and the dispatcher got them down from a shelf without being told twice.  There in plain pencil, with no trick needed to read it, was the time her brother had waited for the litter.  Four minutes and a half. 

"I thought it would be a comfort to you," the surgeon said at last, "to think it was quick." 

"He heard the whole song," she said, and had the bird take its picture before she allowed herself to put a finger on his name.
