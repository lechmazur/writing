In the first spring after contact, I kept a room in the rainwashed tower for a woman whose light I could see coming half a mile away.  Pell was a chemist, and the light meant she was still awake, which by then was no longer good news.  On the crossing her people had slept in shifts under gas; on land they expected to sleep by walking home.  It had not occurred to anyone, on either side of the sky, that a landing place might not qualify.  The gas made you forget a little, Pell said, and lately she could not call up the sound of her father's voice. 

To understand the chemistry of homecoming, the university had built an artificial pilgrimage along the old sculpture path that wound up to my door.  The route copied a walk that every child in Pell's country made at the end of winter, from the communal nursery back to the family house.  Speakers gave the calls of water birds, and at each turn a little pump breathed out the smell of thawing mud.  Pell had supplied the formula for the mud and published, in the same journal, an anonymous objection to her own formula.  I knew because both papers had come to me to be translated, with the same greasy thumbprint on the margins. 

On the ninth evening she reached the tower as bright as when she started, and I shut off the birds.  Into the bronze quiet came the sound of her nails tapping the specimen case beside my chair.  She liked the fly amber, a flat honey lozenge with a single little fly sunk in it, its two front feet touching as if in satisfaction.  It was the only thing in my collection that she could draw from memory, and that night she asked to borrow it.  I thought she wanted it for company in the gas tent.  Instead she went down the hill and hid it. 

At dawn she asked me to walk the path in reverse and fetch it, while she stayed behind to make my tea.  She had dipped her bare soles in the pale stone dust of my workshop so that I could follow her through the nettles.  There were gaps where the rain had been at the prints, but under the leaning bronze shoulders of the old kings they were dry and distinct.  Halfway down I found the place where the tracks of her outward and homeward journeys began to converge, toe against toe on a narrow plinth.  In the cupped hand of a king so overgrown with ivy that I had forgotten his face, I found my piece of amber.  I put it in my mouth, as my hands were needed for the wet climb, and tasted the salt she had left on it. 

When I pushed open the tower door she was sitting with her back to me and the tea had boiled dry.  The skin behind her ears was still gold, but the rest of her had gone the color of an unlit wick.  I laid the fly in her palm, and her fingers closed on it without waking her.  All that morning I sat where the light from the high windows fell across her throat, watching for the small regular swallow that meant she needed no gas. 

When she woke she said that in her country nobody asked what happened to the people who stayed behind and put the kettle on.  They slept too, of course, but there was no song about them.  Her father used to leave something precious out on the path for her to bring him, and she had always found him dozing by the stove. 

In the blood she had drawn while the water heated, we found the first trace of the dark hormone anyone had seen since the landing.  The university wanted the amber for analysis until Pell pointed out that she had fallen asleep before I got back.  She would not let them call it a cure, either; one tired woman was a small foundation on which to build a hospital.  By the end of spring there were six kettles in the tower, and six hooks by the door for things that had been lent and were wanted back.  The fly still lives in my specimen case, except on nights when it has to go out and get lost.
