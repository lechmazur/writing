The wind bells on the abandoned circus train still admitted one mourner for the length of a chime, or two if they went hand in hand.  My father gave me his left hand, the one with the thumb worn flat from pushing a gouge, and I shook the bells with my free one.  At once the rust on the coupling lit up like sugar on a cake, and the cars stretched away in both directions as far as we could see.  We had until the sound died to put my mother's ashes somewhere she had been happy. 

I had chosen the ring car, where for thirty years she had worn a canvas pair of wings and been paid to sing six feet above a revolving herd of horses.  The herd was my father's work.  In every town the playbills had called him the Silent Carousel Carpenter, as though a man who did not speak were doing a rather good trick by earning his bread.  My mother used to take a pencil to those bills and write his name, Eli, in letters big enough to cover the first two words. 

I carried a fragment of her halo, a crescent of silvered glass that had fitted into the crown of her wig and let her see the man at the winch behind her.  I meant to lay it down with the ashes so that if there was a show hereafter she need never go up frightened.  Already I could smell the hot grease of the winch.  Yet each time I made to push through the red door my father put his hard thumb on the latch and held it shut.  He pointed instead to the little mirror in my palm, where a green door shivered in the narrowing strip of light. 

Like a fool I turned the way the reflection went and took us left instead of right, straight out of the train.  If I had got it right we would have found the dressing room where he had courted her, and I might still believe that was the best they had.  Our wrong turn put us ankle deep in mint. 

We were under a bridge of coupled cars, in a hollow scooped out of the salt flats by a spring so small that its spray made a misting hush against the iron overhead.  All around this hidden oasis the desert lay white as the part of a page nobody has written on.  My father let go of my hand. 

On a sleeper above the pool sat a young woman rubbing two red grooves over her ears where a halo had been pinned too tight.  I knew that without the powder her nose would be brown with freckles, but I had not known she could put her whole fist in her mouth.  She did it now to amuse a young man who was trying, with a pocketknife and a piece of soap, to get the curl of her upper lip.  Each time he got it wrong he smoothed the soap with his thumb and began again, quietly persistent even when she spoiled his view by laughing.  My father beside me put his thumb to his lip. 

I had wanted him to tell me where to put her, and when he had written nothing on the slate at home I had taken that for leave to choose the place myself.  Only now did it occur to me that he had been waiting for her to show us.  The young woman slid off the sleeper, slipped her bare feet into the spring, and closed her eyes.  Though I had come such a long way to hear that voice again, I found I could sit with her and not want her to do a thing. 

I tipped the ashes in.  My father took the halo fragment and tilted a fleck of light onto the water, following the little raft of gray as it broke up and went where water goes in thirsty ground.  The last thread of the chime was drawing us back when he slipped the bit of glass into my pocket instead of dropping it into the spring.  I put my fist to my mouth to see how much of that particular talent I had inherited, and inside the last silver tremble my father laughed without a sound.
