After they found my plane, the dead began asking for my autograph again.  I took refuge in the library of a forgotten sunken ship, where the only other regular was Death, and she had known me long enough to call me Millie.  I called her Dot, a liberty she allowed me because I had once taught her to read a slide rule.  Dot was quietly analytic, inclined to let a thing happen twice before deciding it had happened once. 

Usually we played cribbage, but that evening she had set a snow globe on the green baize between us.  Inside it stood a little girl in a Kansas yard, watching two crows travel on the wind without a stroke of their wings.  It was how I had first understood that air was something you could rest your weight on.  I hadn't remembered the girl in years, although of course I remembered the crows. 

Dot tipped the globe, and instead of snow there rose from the grass a snarl of fine black threads.  I heard my last transmission come through the glass, with the flat vowels my husband used to tease me about.  Only it went on rather longer than I remembered, and in places I appeared to be speaking Japanese. 

"They've got the plane now," Dot said, "so all the weight of everything they still don't know comes down on you."  She turned the base toward me so that I could read the library stamp: EARHART, AMELIA, CHILDHOOD THROUGH TERMINATION.  I had supposed the shelves held the books the passengers had brought aboard, not the passengers themselves.  Dot said the distinction tended to disappear on a sufficiently long crossing. 

A narrow slot in the glass admitted the points of the library dividers; the handles had worn hollows in the baize.  By untangling threads with the dividers, she showed me, one could separate what had been sent from what had merely been wanted.  Here was a boy in Wyoming who had heard me calling through the springs of his bed; here was a widow who wanted me to say that drowning didn't hurt.  Each filament came away with the tiniest musical note, and the grass stood up where it had lain flat. 

At last there was only one thread, wound so tightly around the little girl's ankles that I couldn't get a point under it.  I put my ear against the glass and heard my navigator breathing just behind my shoulder.  Then I heard myself tell him we would be all right, in the confident, carrying voice I used on lecture platforms. 

"Did that go out over the air?" I asked. 

Dot looked at the wire, which ran in a neat circuit from the girl's mouth to her own small ear.  It was a comfort I had invented for Fred after we were dead, when there was all the time in the world to say it.  I had been so busy toward the end that I hadn't turned around. 

I had spent a good part of my life studying the physics of staying up, and now I wanted to understand the physics of letting go.  Pulling had only made the wire finer and the pressure on those thin brown ankles more severe.  I asked Dot to tip the globe the other way, and in the instant before the girl settled back on her heels I slipped both points under the wire.  It came off whole, a little zero, too small to have sustained the weight I had put on it.  Nothing came to take its place, not even an absolution in Fred's dry voice, and after a while I stopped listening for one. 

Dot uncapped her pen to note the change in the catalogue, but I asked if I might do it myself.  Under LAST TRANSMISSION I wrote the words that had in fact gone out, the line of position, the promise to repeat the message.  Below that I left the rest of the card clean.  Dot dealt our hands, and as I picked mine up I saw the child in the globe rise lightly onto the balls of her feet.  The crows had long since passed out of sight, but she knew what to look for now, and the whole sky was hers to watch.
