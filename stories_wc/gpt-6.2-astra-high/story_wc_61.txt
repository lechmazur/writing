In the nebula garden, a new daughter was given half her mother's light.  On the underside of the glass hull, where the youngest beds hung over a thousand miles of rose-colored dust, this amounted to a courtesy rather than a dawn. 

Iven had come from a world without daughters, but he kept the little cards that announced each division, down to the faint blue thumbprints of the newborn.  He kept the envelopes too, because someone had gone to the trouble of licking them.  When the last child in the stack brought him her envelope in person, he very nearly filed it without looking inside.  There was no card, only a white bean with its first two leaves folded tight together like the hands of a polite guest. 

"She won't eat," the child said. 

As keeper of the mirrors, Iven could have summoned a lamp from stores, but he knew what the child would smell when its wick took fire.  The oil came from beans that had done better than hers.  Besides, she wanted light for the whole bed, not a bright place on a table where visitors might admire a single survivor. 

He went with her into the shaft that ran through all the beds, carrying the bean in the damp pocket where his people carried their dead until they sprouted.  Fog had come up from the cistern as usual before the daily transit of the guide star across the garden's only clear pane.  By sonar pinging, a trick of his soft palate, he could hear the angle of each little silver gate without having to see his way up the ladder.  Half open, half open, half open: the notes of a lesson repeated perfectly by pupils who could no longer remember the question. 

At the top an old woman was getting ready to catch the scented petals that her trees let fall as soon as the star touched them.  Her sheet was embroidered with a family tree whose last blue leaf still had a needle stuck through it.  He showed her the bean, and she showed him the calculating machine gear that her own mother had cut from a silver breakfast plate.  It sat in its socket like a pet in the hollow of a knee, clicking once for every birth and setting another gate halfway shut. 

"It approximates infinity," she said, repeating the boast engraved around the rim.  "You can always give half of anything away." 

Iven put the white bean on the plate, where it lay across the tiny word infinity.  From somewhere underneath their feet came three small, precise clicks.  Iven pinged again and heard the bottom gate swing flat to catch everything, then the two above swing clear as the child climbed. 

"She's asking what happens if we let it all go through," he said. 

The old woman picked the bean up with her lips, having no free hand once she had taken hold of the gear.  Iven thought she meant to keep it from turning, until he saw the thin black crescent opening between the plate and its socket.  Together they lifted it out, and the counterweights took the silver gates up against the walls, all but the last one, where the child had tied her envelope string. 

For once the light went down in a piece, and from under the fog came the soft racket of a thousand pairs of leaves coming unstuck.  The old woman spat the bean into her hand and began to cry, quite silently, for the scent that ought by now to have been rising from her sheet.  Iven would have liked to promise her the same harvest under the arrangement he could already hear the women below calling out to one another.  One bed at a time, one whole minute apiece, measured by the ticking of the garden clock: even the oldest trees would have to make do. 

Instead he helped her spread the sheet under the bean and waited with her until the women called their minute.  He held the gate flat while she cupped the bean in the hollow where the sun fell on the cloth.  There was just time, across the quiet sigh of falling petals, to see the two white leaves let go of each other. 

Afterward he sewed the gear into the sheet where the family tree began, at the old woman's request, using a double thickness of thread.  Neither of them wanted anybody setting it back in its socket, and neither could bear to throw her mother's work away.
