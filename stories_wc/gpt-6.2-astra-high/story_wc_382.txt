On the last night the valley had a postal address, the Perseids were falling, and Miss Pell was trying to interrogate a pear tree.  She wanted to know whether its roots had gone through the bottom of the tub into the roof.  If they had, she said, we ought to leave it rather than let it die of the amputation.  She spoke with some authority, having been dead since March.  Our new keys had arrived in February, and Miss Pell had been buried with hers at her own insistence. 

I had come up to our rooftop garden with the antique desk seal from her study and a teacup full of sugar.  Beyond the parapet, down where the river went through the new dam, a single light burned in the gatehouse.  The gates would close in the morning; we had weeks before the water reached this high, but the last removal van would not wait for water. 

Miss Pell had been a dead language tutor even when she was alive.  I had gone to her ostensibly to study the extinct tongue in which the valley deeds were written, actually to learn the language of old buildings.  At seventeen I could tell when a house was settling, but not what it was settling into, and the difference seemed worth a lifetime of study. 

Too shy to announce this ambition, I began by borrowing sugar.  That turned into weekly tea sessions, since I always brought the sugar back and she always said we might as well use it.  Neither of us took sugar; after a while we dispensed even with the pretense of tipping any out of the cup.  Now I set it down on the potting bench, where the wood had two pale rings and a third, smaller one for the debt. 

Miss Pell's fingers, patiently nimble, worked their way around the felt she had packed between the pear tub and the slates against the frost.  A meteor threw her shadow sharp against the beans, or rather threw the shadow of the beans and left hers out. 

"Will you please ask the house what I should take?" I said.  All summer I had been carrying things across the ridge to our concrete boxes, waiting for one of them to sound familiar when I put the key in.  The brass knocker had not done it, nor had the loose tile that clicked under a caller's heel a second before the knock.  I could live without those sounds, but I could not bear to leave her answering a door at the bottom of a lake. 

She lifted the tub a finger's breadth and slid the felt free.  Underneath, in a little paste of earth, was the impression of the rose on her desk seal.  I remembered pressing it there the day we planted the pear, to certify, as we solemnly informed each other, that this was an orchard.  The tree had obliged us with precisely one pear in fourteen years, which we had divided with a ruler. 

"So it isn't rooted," I said. 

"Oh, it's rooted," she said, looking at the mark. 

Below us the men loading the van called up to ask if there was anything more, and I nearly shouted that there was an orchard.  Then I understood that a great part of what I thought of as home consisted of such agreed upon lies, and that agreement, unlike timber, took two.  I had been trying to get the new place to speak by furnishing both sides of the conversation myself. 

I dipped the seal in the wet ring under the sugar cup and stamped the back of my hand.  "Delivery for the orchard," I called down, and heard the men swear affectionately as they started back up the stairs.  Above the ridge three white strokes appeared almost together, like the marks Miss Pell used to put beside a particularly promising error. 

"There's a great deal I don't know about the new address," I told her.  "Could I come round on Thursday and borrow some sugar?" 

She pocketed the seal and told the men to mind the branches, as the crop was already heavily bespoken.  On Thursday, in a corridor of identical doors, I found hers by a little click under my heel, answered from within by the rattle of cups.
