In the hush before the library came down, Nell could hear her father worrying a loose tooth with his tongue.  Of the six men in orange coats outside the door, she knew him by that small, wet click.  Once he set his teeth and put his whistle between them, the cables would tighten.  She had until then to find out how her mother used to put weather on a wall. 

The library was a single chestnut trunk, hollowed by a scholars' guild when the river still ran on the other side of town.  Its grain rose around her in folds like the skirts of people standing too close together.  Now there was a split she could put her arm through, and white fungus as soft as bread inside the split.  Nobody, least of all a carpenter's daughter, could call the verdict cruel. 

Nell was face blind, a useful thing in a portrait artist insofar as it made her slow to assume that a likeness was finished.  She had learned to craft a mouth around the particular thing it was trying not to say.  Her mother, who had kept this library forty years, had sat for her only once, after the stroke, and had fallen asleep.  The portrait hung opposite the bed in a room that looked out on a brick wall.  Nell had gone there yesterday and found her mother trying with her good hand to turn the picture to the wall.  She did not want to look at herself all day; who would? 

When Nell was little and the shutters were drawn for the midday rest, her mother could make whole processions of rooks sail across the plaster above the catalogue desk.  Each bird pulled its feet up into its belly as it went, a detail Nell remembered because no one drawing a bird ever bothered with the feet.  If she could learn that trick, she might yet mirror the sky inside walls her mother would never leave. 

High on a shelf facing the desk stood the one volume nobody had claimed, an atlas her mother had always said was too expensive to lend.  It would not come down, but the cover opened on hinges, disclosing a secret book safe with a single silver coin wedged upright in its back.  Nell worked it free with her thumbnail, and a thread of daylight from outside laid itself along her wrist.  She stopped the split with her coat, shut the door on her father's orange shoulder, and waited for her eyes. 

In the plaster oval where her mother used to conjure there appeared a smear of blue.  On the centuries-old coin her thumb found two faint indentations, one inside the other, where somebody had pressed first a punch and then a needle into the king's left eye.  Through the very bottom of the second dent she could see a point of blue as clean as a pinhead.  She slid the king back into his groove.  The smear gathered itself into sky. 

Across the plaster stretched a black elm, upside down and fractally precise, each twig dividing as the limb that bore it had divided, until even the smallest fork held its scrap of light.  A man's hand came up between the twigs, huge and blurred because it was so close, and made the beak of a bird.  She heard the click again, and this time understood how her mother had contrived, even in winter, to call up birds at will. 

She had been about to pocket the coin when she remembered the brick wall.  There would be no elm there, no wedge of sky for a pierced king to spend on plaster, however faithfully she copied the little machine.  All it could give you was what stood outside.  Nell took the coin anyway. 

She opened the door and showed her father the two dents, laying his hard thumb over them as her mother must once have laid hers.  Since the stroke he had visited the sickroom only in his Sunday suit, to sit with his hat clenched between his useless hands. 

"Could you do that against a reading lamp?" 

He made the beak again, and with his other hand tucked two impossibly delicate feet against its breast.  She had tried for years to get those feet right with a pencil.  On the bare wall of the sickroom, with the portrait taken down, there would be room enough for a bird to cross and cross.  Her father waited until she had stepped over the slack cable before he set the whistle to his lips.
