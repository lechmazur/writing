The moonlit ridge path was just wide enough for Ruth to walk beside her father, provided neither of them minded getting a shoulder wet from the broom.  At seventy he still moved like a forest firefighter, testing the earth before he put his weight on it, and she let him take the side that fell away.  They had until the moon went dark to get the microphones in place. 

Down at the observatory a camera was already taking one picture every thirty seconds of the earth's shadow eating into the white disk.  Ruth had been commissioned to make the music for that film, the first to be shown in the rebuilt hall where her mother had taught piano.  She had promised the committee something that would need no subtitles in a town that picked its apples and buried its dead in seven languages. 

All the way up the hill a cracked porcelain box kept time against the brass buckle of her satchel, until her father could stand it no longer and slipped his wool cap between them.  His hands were ridged with scars, yet he could ease the cap into that narrow space without costing her a step.  She remembered the same delicacy in the way he used to lift a sliver out with a sewing needle, talking all the while about something of no consequence. 

At the foundation of the old hall she set the box on what was left of the sill.  It had held the pennies children earned for practicing, and when you struck its lid with a penny it had once rung as sweetly as a bicycle bell.  Now it made a short wooden sound, like a knuckle against a door.  Ruth handed him a penny and went to set the levels. 

He had supposed she wanted him along to carry things.  On the night the hall burned he had carried Ruth through this window, tearing both hands on the wire in the safety glass.  On the first night in her grandmother's house he had told her she was only a child and could not have known who was knocking.  She had said he couldn't have known either, what with the engine and his one bad ear.  After that the price of being believed was to believe him, and each had watched the other grow old on those terms. 

He laid the penny on the lid and watched the last clean shaving of light come off the moon.  In all the years since, he had thought about those knocks only long enough to stop thinking about them.  This time he stayed with them, past the first three blows that always woke him, into the little interval before the fourth.  It was in that interval that his wife used to take a breath when she knocked on the bathroom door to hurry Ruth along to school. 

And in that interval, with his boots already on the grass and Ruth still on his hip, he had felt the child draw breath to answer.  The knocking came from just above them, from a practice room whose windows opened only at the top.  He had put his palm across her mouth because if the woman inside heard her child she would stay at that window instead of trying for the stairs.  He didn't know whether he had thought of the stairs then or invented them afterward.  He had run for a ladder, and by the time he found one there was nothing for its top to rest against. 

Ruth came back from the microphones with her headphones around her neck.  Instead of telling her what he remembered, he played her the three strokes and the fourth, with room enough between for a little girl's lungs to fill.  She looked at his hand as if at something fetched up by a plow after a long winter underground.  Then she took another penny from her pocket.  On the second take, in the gap he left her, she rapped the sill twice with the edge of the coin. 

They listened together as the moon began its slow business of giving back the light.  Against the huge hush in the headphones the two small sounds were distinct, one asking where the other was and the other saying here.  Nothing in the answer said that anybody was forgiven. 

At the screening, when the shadow touched the rim of the moon, children all over the hall took their hands out of their parents' hands to knock back.
