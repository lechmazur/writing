At the celestial mapping observatory, a steady star could take a sleeping cartographer back to a place, but not to a particular night.  For that we went through variable stars, whose lights kept such different hours that two of them together made a serviceable date stamp.  I was the lucid dream chronicler, the one who went along awake inside the dream and came home with the words. 

The night my father was buried, my mother brought me the daylily-pressed telegram he had sent his sisters from the island: BOTH SAFE MARRIED THIS MORNING PLEASE SEND SHOES.  The lily had come from her bouquet, and under its ghost on the yellow paper you could just make out the date.  She had come to decode the temple inscriptions he had copied onto the back, the same two lines he had asked to have cut into his stone. 

"I promised him I'd do what it says," she told me, "and your aunts keep bringing casseroles and looking at me and not going."  By then the stroke had left him just one good finger to tap the paper with, and he had tapped until she promised. 

I put the kettle on the red lamp we used to warm our hands between observations.  "You could ask them," I said. 

"I could also sit down naked in the market and save everybody the trouble of trying to see through my clothes." 

She had been a mainland girl, shipwrecked with my father on his way home, married in a borrowed dress in a language she never learned.  He used to tell the story as if the ship had gone down expressly to get her into that dress. 

I set the two dials for the last dark hour before the wedding, when the temple bell would have called the neighbors in to witness.  In the narrow cot under the telescope she put her cold feet against my shins, as I used to do to her. 

We woke inside the sound of the bell.  There was my father on the temple step, picking black tar out of his toenails with a pin, and beside him a skinny girl with salt sores on her mouth.  My mother walked past them to the lintel where the letters ran in two deep grooves, blue paint still wet from somebody's hasty brush.  With my pencil I could catch the writing, but that got us no further than the stone.  My father had taught me the island words for cleaning fish and comforting babies, but he had never taught me my letters. 

"Wait for somebody to say it," I whispered. 

A woman climbed onto a chair and laid her finger under the first blue line, and my father spoke slowly, as if even the children in the back must understand.  "Who will carry what these two cannot?" 

The girl in the dress opened her mouth, but nobody waited for her to answer.  All along the benches people got to their feet, and somebody put a hand under an old man's elbow to help him up too.  As the finger traveled under the second line they said, "As long as one of us is standing, we will." 

Behind me my mother was crying with the peculiar restraint of somebody who does not want to wake a house.  "All those years," she said, "I thought that was the bit where I was supposed to say I'd manage." 

When understanding dawns in a dream the light does not change; it was still that blue hour, and the boy with the pin was still a boy.  I wanted to stay until he looked up, but my mother's feet had begun to shake against mine.  I brought her back to the little room where our kettle was singing itself dry. 

"It isn't an instruction for you," I said, and felt her legs go quiet.  "It's a question you're allowed to ask." 

She drank her tea with both hands around the cup while I rang my aunts down in the valley.  Neither had gone to bed; we could hear the squeak of a cupboard hinge as one put away whatever dish she had been getting ready to bring.  My mother took the receiver and read the first line off the page I had filled in my sleep.  There was a little scuffle at the other end as two old women who knew the answer by heart got to their feet.
