After the flood, the only thing still on time in the municipal butterfly house was the truck coming to take its heater away.  It was due on Friday, and by then June had promised her father that every living thing worth keeping would be out of his greenhouse.  He had objected to her use of the word worth. 

June was a neutron star researcher, a woman paid to strip needless drama from the sky.  Particles struck a detector, a satellite rolled into sunlight, and there went another afternoon explaining why a dead star had not, in fact, hiccupped.  Her father liked to tell visitors that his daughter preprocesses reality, which sounded better than saying she threw most of it away.  Now here she was with a clipboard, doing it to him. 

Together they carried out the mesh cages of caterpillars and cut down the passion vines for the striped little mouths that hadn't stopped eating even when the river came through.  The county zoo had offered a warm room, provided nothing came with it that was liable to decay.  That ruled out most of the woodwork, including the slatted bench on which her father used to set saucers of soft banana for the owl butterflies.  Not one owl had turned up in their sweep of the storm-damaged greenhouse. 

On Wednesday her father asked her to wash the three surviving panes behind the bench, although they were due to come out with everything else.  She got as far as wetting the rag before it occurred to her that he might want a last look at something other than mud. 

"Just the bottom halves," he said, drawing a line with a thumb that no longer bent. 

Above his line, in the slant light, she could see a sort of tarnish made of overlapping commas, like a school of fish turning.  Below it the wet glass took the print of her hand and then came clean. 

By Thursday morning there was one bronze comma on the clean side of the line.  He let her take the first look through his linen tester: dust from a butterfly wing, each scale ribbed like the roof of a freight car. 

"One left," she said, and took down a net. 

"At least one pass at a window," he said. 

She felt the small agreeable sting of hearing her own voice at a seminar come out of somebody else's mouth.  It was through accumulated dust patterns that he had learned where to set his fruit, he told her, back when all he'd had was a sack of bruised bananas and no money for the books.  An owl at dusk would follow the light along the glass, brushing it at each upright until it found a way around.  Move the saucer a pane a night and eventually you could get the old ladies who came with walkers a ringside seat.  She had always thought they sat there for his company. 

That evening she sat in their place with her net across her knees while the heater ticked and the odor of river mud came up out of the floor.  At the first change in the light a brown wing unfolded under the bench, then folded again around its great painted eye.  She knelt, expecting to see the insect on a slat, and found it hanging from its empty case on the underside of the lowest rail.  There were six other cases, five the color of old leaves and one gone clear enough to show the packed silk of a wing.  Had she been alone she would have reached for the saw straightaway, shaking the rail just when the wet wings needed stillness.  Instead she brought her father his stool. 

Before the truck came they had three owls in a net cage with a saucer between them, and five neat chips of sound wood, each bearing a silk button from which a brown case hung.  Her father had steadied the chisel with his stiff thumb while she supplied, at his nod, each little increment of force. 

At the zoo the keeper asked how many more butterflies she could expect, and June nearly said five.  Instead she showed her the clear patch to watch for, and how to leave room below each case for a pair of wings to fill.  Then she got her father a chair by the cage and, after a moment's consideration, dragged another over for herself.
