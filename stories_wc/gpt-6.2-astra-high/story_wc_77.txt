After the resuscitation, every photograph taken of Nell showed somebody still working on her.  At her wedding there were two wet hands between the rows of pearls on her dress, pressing hard enough to dent her breastbone.  By the time her daughter was six, the hands had begun to appear on the child.  One morning Lucy came down to breakfast with ten plum-colored marks exactly where the school portrait said they ought to be. 

Nell took the bus back to the abandoned carnival funhouse where, at thirteen, she had fallen through a rotten landing into the sump.  She took along her mother's opera cloak clasp, two little silver hands that hooked together by the thumbs.  Her mother had worked the funhouse as Madame Somnus, interpreter of dreams, in a velvet cloak salvaged from the municipal opera.  It was all Nell remembered of being hauled out: those cold hands tapping at her teeth as her mother bent to give her breath.  She had kept the clasp because it was easier than keeping the woman, who had never afterward let her so much as run for a bus.  Even dead, her mother served as whispering advisor at the soft hinge of Nell's jaw: mind the glass, darling, mind the step. 

Inside, the walls still sweated the pink syrup of decomposing insulation, and Nell felt a disgraceful impulse to put out her tongue.  Behind the souvenir camera lay the sample portrait the automatic shutter had taken that night, when the flash caught her mother straddling her.  The mouth in the picture was a black purse full of small bright coins; she could almost smell the violets her mother sucked to sweeten her breath.  She turned the portrait facedown, but its lingering luminance shone through the cardboard, especially at the hands.  For twenty years, then, somebody had been kneeling here, doing a job from which nobody had thought to relieve her. 

Madame Somnus had left instructions for the photographically cursed in the only place she could be certain the management would never look for them.  She conducted her real business through paradoxical notes in a dream dictionary, penciled so lightly you had to tilt the page away from the light to read them.  Under HANDS Nell found: To dream of being rescued is to be needed.  To be rid of a faithful servant, enter her service. 

She began to unbosom herself of twenty years of anger, telling her mother exactly what it cost a child to have such hands forever upon her.  Then she set Lucy's school portrait on the counter beside the old one and hooked the clasp at her own throat.  She would have to trick fear into revealing its weakness, and she knew only one trick, the one Lucy used when Nell insisted on tying her shoes.  Show me how, the child would say, and by the time Nell discovered she was no longer necessary the knot was tied. 

She laid two fingers on the little glossy collar of Lucy's uniform.  "Am I doing it right, Mama?" 

The light bunched under her fingertips, warm and horribly yielding, like a gum about to break a tooth.  Not there, darling, said the whisper, and in both pictures the wet hands lifted to move hers.  In the space they left, the child's white collar rose a fraction and fell again; the girl on the boards spat out a long bright thread.  Nell had expected a fight and what she felt instead, traveling up her arms from those terrible fingers, was the almost voluptuous ache of an exhausted woman offered a chair.  That was the weakness of the thing that had followed her home from the sump: more than it wanted to hold on, it wanted permission to let go. 

"I've got her," Nell said, and for the first time in either picture her mother looked up.  Her eyes were quite ordinary behind the smears her wet thumbs had left on her glasses.  Then the light went out, leaving only the girl on the landing coughing and Lucy looking stiffly past the photographer at whoever came next in line. 

At home that evening Nell found Lucy before the bathroom mirror, turning her head this way and that to see the last yellow traces of the bruises.  She wanted to feel the small ribs, to satisfy herself that nothing underneath was broken.  She unhooked the two silver hands instead and laid them in the soap dish, where Lucy could reach them if she liked.  "Does it hurt?" she asked, and waited for her daughter's answer.
