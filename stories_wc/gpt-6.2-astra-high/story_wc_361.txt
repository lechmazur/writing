On the aurora-lit ridgeway, where a walker might meet somebody born four hundred winters later, Len carried a box of spoons.  He had counted them so often on the climb that his thumb kept ticking them off against his fingers even after he had shut the lid.  His first delivery without Ada was supposed to be an easy one: six spoons from the avalanche hospice, requested by a museum not yet built.  Ada had eaten with those spoons, as had Len, as had anybody caught on the wrong side of the pass after the warning bell.  It tickled him that one day somebody would put on gloves to touch the spoon with which he had most recently dispatched a lump of gristle.  That was why he had wanted to be a relic courier, before he knew how it felt to make the climb alone.  He liked what the work did to an ordinary Tuesday supper. 

At the summit he drew the dragonfly-wing pendant from beneath his scarf and waited for the green bars overhead to swing into line with the road.  Somewhere up ahead in time another courier would be wearing the same wing on the same length of chain, or whatever chain had replaced it.  By vibrating sympathetically with its older self the wing could extend a brief encounter across the lights from the duration of a sneeze to nearly a minute.  Ada could pack a great deal into a minute, and Len had been counting on another ten years of watching her do it. 

The wing stirred against his breastbone, and a girl in an oilskin apron stood before him holding out a folded towel for the spoons.  He tipped them out like six little fish.  Five went into the towel and one landed in the snow, and when they both bent to get it the girl whispered a question. 

"Which one makes the soup?" 

She meant, she explained, the spoon of Saint Add, who had fed a hundred stranded travelers by stirring snow until it thickened into broth.  It took Len a few precious seconds to recognize in that name the woman whose winter underwear he had recently given away. 

"None of them," he said, a little sharply, and folded the sixth spoon into the towel himself. 

The girl's face closed up, and he recognized that look too, from the day a senior courier had explained to him that relic meant leftover.  He had gone to Ada in some distress, and she had made him sort beans for supper, picking out the handsomest and putting them in his pocket.  Only when he saw the little hooks of green coming through the soil that spring did he understand what a leftover could be.  Now he undid the flap of his pocket, where a few dry beans from last night's sorting still knocked together when his leg shook. 

"If you had any beans in your pack, she asked for one before you put them in the pot," he said.  "In summer the whole south face of the hospice looked like a quilt hung out to air, no two vines quite the same." 

The girl set down the towel and opened both hands, and he poured out seven beans into palms so cold that he could feel them through his gloves. 

"How do I make them germinate?" 

The minute was almost up, but it turned out he could get a fair bit into a minute himself if he didn't stop to count.  He had time to tell her about soaking the beans and waiting for warm ground, and even to specify which of the six spoons gave the most comfortable mouthful.  Then the road was empty except for a shallow print where the towel had lain. 

In the catalogue of a museum that would not be built for four hundred winters, the six spoons eventually occupied a single line.  The accession card for the beans had to be replaced every autumn as the girl in the oilskin apron took down the names of people who brought back seed.  People still told the story about the saint who stirred snow into soup; people like that sort of story, particularly in winter.  But on soup day in the museum courtyard the six spoons came down from their hooks, and nobody had to put on gloves.
