The last time the old button cutters gathered, they asked June not to make them listen to another tape of running water.  Everybody who made a film about the drowned town seemed to think that was what a drowned town sounded like.  June asked what they would rather hear, and her mother said, almost crossly, "Us getting up." 

For the fiftieth anniversary the reservoir museum gave June the use of its glass spire by the frozen lake, from midnight until sunrise.  The cutters had always kept a vigil on the night the gates were closed, though nowadays some fell asleep before the coffee came round.  June promised them a dawn before they ran out of dark. 

Her regular job was writing fortunes for the cookies her uncle sold by the gross, little riddles pitched somewhere between a promise and a dare.  She made each cutter a cookie and slipped inside it a direction that would mean nothing to anybody else.  To the woman who had lived above the butcher she gave: Teach the steel to sing again.  To the twins from Orchard Street: Quarrel over one good shoe.  That earned her a telephone call; there had been two good shoes and the quarrel had been over whose turn it was to polish them.  She corrected the score. 

By mapping childhood memories of her paper route she worked out in what order the town should wake, beginning with the butcher sharpening his knives.  As a girl she had longed to be the conductor of those unheard symphonies, whole streets playing together that you could only ever hear one doorway at a time.  She still lived within a mile of where she was born and was homesick almost continuously; she had simply never found a way to explain that what she missed was a when. 

At three, her mother unwrapped the one thing she had been asked to bring: a segment of nautilus shell with six round holes punched out of the pearly flank.  At five, June tried drawing a thimble over the holes to get the six descending notes her father used to play coming off nights.  All she got was a dry rattle.  Her mother watched her try three times before she said, "Honey, that was him whistling."  "He wasn't making a flute out of it; he was making you look at his hands so you wouldn't see his mouth." 

Beyond the glass the first thin light had made a mirrored horizon, a strip of rose under the black far shore as well as over it.  June could see how easily an eye, given two mornings, might go looking for the wrong one.  She put down the shell and asked her mother what she had been doing while her father fooled her. 

"Trying to get my stockings on without waking you up." 

From the chairs came a noise like grass stirring as forty women reached down and ran their palms up their shins.  Nobody needed a cue for the little grunt it took to straighten a seam.  June let the sound die away, raised her hands, and called it up again.  This time she brought in the butcher against it, then the twins with their stiff brushes, and the hiss and knock of an iron being set on a stove.  She stopped trying to take them down the streets in order and began to listen for who needed whom.  The iron wanted the spit that tested it; the shoes wanted feet on the stairs. 

When the women began to flag she let the town grow quiet enough for one man coming home from work.  Her mother put two fingers between her teeth and whistled six notes, clear and falling, into the hollow spire.  On the second pass June whistled with her, and found she had her father's knack of coming in just flat.  All along the glass the faces tipped up to follow the sound, and stayed that way after it was gone. 

By the time June lowered her hands, there was daylight enough to see the darn in her mother's stocking.  Her mother was asleep with the punched shell in her lap, her thumb resting in the space where a button used to be.
