By the last week on Pel, nearly everybody had brought the island reality checker something that would not fit on a boat.  A baker brought the warm yeasty breath of his father's kitchen in the hour before the ovens were lit.  Two sisters brought the difference between their mother's knock and their aunt's.  Mara took down each claim in her ledger, leaving the column headed Evidence discreetly blank. 

She was ordinarily a woman of exact if peculiar experiments.  To test a report that the town fountain ran backward on birthdays, she had stood in it with a cork duck and six children of independently verified ages.  But how did you test what people would remember of Pel when there was no Pel to go back to? 

The sea had eaten the soft cliffs clean up to the ancient ridge, which was in bloom for what the harbor surveyors judged the last time.  When the monsoon came, the ships would have to be gone.  Under the white bells of the last knowledge fruit tree, Mara dug up the seed her mother had tucked there against just such a departure.  An awakened seed could be planted anywhere, and whoever ate of its first crop would taste the life of the place it came from.  But her mother had died before she could teach Mara how to waken it.  All the ledger said, in the cramped hand preceding hers, was: Through interpreted dreams. 

That night people slept along the ridge with the seed passed from pillow to pillow like a baby nobody could settle.  At first light the baker said he had dreamed of a little lame horse coming down a wooden road.  One sister had seen a square of linen rise and fall as if something beneath it breathed.  The other had heard rain falling indoors, although in the dream the sun was out. 

Mara knew the old correspondences: a horse meant a journey, linen a marriage, rain a change in fortune.  She entered all three interpretations and waited for the husk to split.  It sat in her palm as impenetrable as a peach stone.  From the harbor came the squeak of the capstan taking up the slack on the first ship's cable. 

The baker was tapping his lame horse's gait on the ledger, trying to make Mara hear the catch in it.  Both sisters turned at the sound, and the elder said, quite casually, "Come in, Auntie."  Then she put her hand over her mouth.  Their aunt had kept one finger stiff since a break, so there was always that extra beat where the knuckle hit the wood.  The baker listened to her do it, then asked to hear their mother's knock as well, so he could tell them apart. 

Over the open ledger, in the cup of Mara's palm, a white hair curled out of the seed.  Mara crossed out Journey so hard that the point of her pen went through the page.  She asked the elder sister to show them with her hands how the linen had moved.  Watching those hands, the baker went quite pink.  He had been five when his father let him draw back the cloth from the dough and feel the warm air rising out of the folds.  When he showed the woman how to lift without letting the cloth stick, the filament put out two green ears. 

It's the dreamer's job to carry it, Mara thought, and somebody else's to know what it is.  But nobody on the ridge could say what the rain meant.  Her pen hovered over the blank until the younger sister took it gently from her fingers and wrote the sound down as best she could. 

Below them the gangplanks were sliding out onto the mud, and the white bells overhead had begun to shed their petals in earnest.  Mara scooped black ridge earth into her hat and bedded the green ears in it for the crossing.  To the captain, who wanted to know if the operation had succeeded, she showed the two green ears and did her best imitation of the rain.  All the way to the mainland he went about his ship making a soft uncertain noise against his teeth, asking one passenger and then another, like a man learning a song.
