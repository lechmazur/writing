In the stormlit observatory above the clouds, Sal slept under the cobalt glass portico because it was the one roof that did not leak.  It turned lightning into the blue of a clean gas flame, a color she had not seen in the mines since childhood.  By day she drew weather charts on the glass and let the old chart scanner send them to the workings below.  At night she sometimes drew a chair opposite hers, with a coat thrown over it as if somebody had just stepped out. 

The mines had not had a dawn since the ash came, and Sal wanted to give them one worth getting out of bed for.  Her grandmother had left her the ancient shadows, a troupe of jointed leather beasts worn smooth by the thumbs of a hundred generations.  She laid the smallest on the glass before her shift and made him unfold his ears in three successive scans.  Down in the breakfast halls, the chart machines spat out three pictures of a hare listening.  Then came the weather. 

Static off the slag heaps meant the pictures traveled through fragmented radio frequencies, a few lines to a band, and nobody had a set that caught them all.  In Copper the hare lacked a foreleg; in Salt he had a hole where a heart might usefully go.  The cooks filled the gaps with grease pencil, giving Copper a fist and Salt a fat little pocket for putting things by. 

Sal knew none of this until the morning she sent the hare out to speak for the first time.  She had heard a tremor through the iron feet of the portico, a sound like a spoon going round an empty pot.  Tomorrow the hill will want its breakfast, she wrote beneath his feet, and drew the east shaft shut.  Nobody went down that shaft the next day, not even the pit ponies, and by dinner it was a hole full of hill. 

By winter the hare was a prophet, and Sal had mail enough to stuff a mattress.  Children declared their allegiance to one variant or the other by wearing a mitten or sewing a pocket over the ribs.  At first she loved the letters most that asked what he would do next; then she began to dread them.  The Copper men had stopped sounding the rock before a shift, and the women at Salt no longer counted their sacks of emergency meal.  Why bother, with those long ears cocked on everybody's behalf? 

Then a letter came in two hands from a pair of sisters who worked the pumps between pits.  Each dawn Copper waited for the fist to tell them to dig, Salt for the pocket to tell them to save, while water seeped through the wall between them.  Which animal, the sisters wanted to know, was going to tell them to run? 

Sal took the hare apart that night, drilling out the rivets with a bit she usually used on her teeth.  With his ears in her palm he looked small and skinned, a thing you might buy cheap for the stewpot.  She wanted badly to put him back together and have him say the one right thing.  Instead she sent his pieces down on every band with the sisters' warning and a last line: Tomorrow let the mountain hear with ears of flesh. 

In Copper the cooks fitted the loose ears to the heads of the pump sisters; in Salt somebody drew them sticking out of a telephone.  The Salt cook rang the pump room and got the Copper cook on the same wire, and between them the sisters at last got a word in.  The safe way out of Copper went through Salt, whose heavy flood door had rusted shut from sheer disuse.  The boy in the biggest mitten brought a hoarded crock of grease; the cook with the biggest pocket knocked the hinge pins straight.  When the dividing wall began to bulge, the sisters banged a pan and two hundred people walked through the door before the wall gave way. 

That spring Sal fitted the pump room with a second scanner, and the pits took turns making dawn.  Sal kept the portico, but on clear mornings children came up for lessons in how a picture got made.  She had to scrub the grease off the glass and buy a second chair.
