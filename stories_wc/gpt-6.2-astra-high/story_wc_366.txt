When the lamp flickered out for good, the other light took up its station, a hard white wink from the buoy beyond the reef.  At the foot of the lighthouse steps Kit Vale found three stones stacked with the little one in the middle.  Somebody had an answer but did not want to be seen giving it.  Kit went back to her supply boat and made considerable business of finding a clean handkerchief.  When she went ashore again the stones were gone and a twist of paper lay in their place. 

In thirty years of taking oil and onions out to the lighthouse, Kit had never stayed long enough for anyone to ask about the ferry.  She did notice things, like the keeper who smoothed out each orange wrapper for his little girl's doll dresses; ever after, his oranges came double wrapped.  It was generally agreed that she was a good person to give a letter to, especially if you hadn't signed it. 

The first letter had gone round the bay when word came about the buoy, tucked into the handles of baskets or slipped beneath a saucer at the cafe.  What do you remember of the night the school ferry sank, after they got us up into the light?  Add one thing and pass it on, the letter said, and leave off the names, since we got enough of our names in the papers.  Where the road gave out, there were the old children's signals on the tide steps: smallest stone on top meant come and get it; smallest in the middle meant give me a minute.  By April the letter had grown so fat that Kit had put it in a preserving jar and started the next sheet on its way. 

Now she unscrewed the lid and added the final scrap.  She let herself into the tower that nobody needed supplies for anymore.  Above her, in the lantern room, a woman's boots went round and round the cold lamp, pausing every few steps for the same small, sickening sound.  It was a match being struck and failing to catch.  Miss Pike had been dead forty years and in all that time no keeper had let the room stay dark for very long. 

It had been Kit's idea to put tomorrow's sunshine in a bottle, as Miss Pike had once promised the shivering ferry children she could do.  What Kit really wanted to bottle was the feeling that came over you when an adult took off her only dry sweater and proceeded on the assumption that everything was going to be fine.  She thought it might protect the dead as well as the living, and after tonight there wouldn't be a keeper to spare. 

Kit set the jar on the iron sill, where each pass of the buoy light dipped the pages in milk.  The boots went on walking.  Having brought all these voices so far without having to use her own, she had not reckoned on this last stretch of water.  She took out the first sheet and began. 

Somebody remembered how Miss Pike had given them each a keeper's duty, even the smallest boy, who had been appointed to keep the raisins in the buns.  Somebody remembered the salt going white on his shoes as he sat with his feet in her lap.  By the time Kit got to the last scrap, the boots had stopped beside her.  It was blank except for a piece of sea glass folded inside, green as the bank under a breaking wave.  Miss Pike had given it to them to pass around whenever the lamp went out, so nobody need put a hand into the dark without another hand there to receive it.  The only child who hadn't taken it was the one who sat under the stairs and wouldn't speak.  Kit closed her fist on it now. 

"I was warm under there," she said, in case the woman had spent all this time thinking there was one she hadn't reached.  "Your skirt kept brushing my knees." 

A match slid back into its box.  Kit stayed where she was until the buoy paled and the jar on the sill filled up, quite ordinarily, with sunshine.
