Everybody who spent the week of the island fire underwater remembered the swallows, though nobody could tell me where they had slept or what they had eaten.  My father, who had kept the children down there while their parents fought the fire, had left me one burnt feather in an envelope marked WING.  It was brass, thin enough to take the print of my thumb without springing back. 

When the islanders bought the forgotten research facility of the underwater city for scrap, I asked to go down with the salvage crew.  They gave me until sunrise, when the tug would come for the copper light shaft that ran from the old nursery to the marsh above.  I was a landscape astrologer, somebody people paid to show them where a particular star would touch a particular hill on the night their child was born.  I could put a sky back together for strangers; I wanted to know how my father had made one for children he scarcely knew, when he had been so silent with his own. 

They sent along Vi Bell, who had been six the summer of the fire and had volunteered, she said, for first pick of the plumbing.  She wanted a deep sink for washing her dogs.  Under our feet as we descended, lake light moved in the seams between the stair treads, something like the light that shows between fingers held over closed eyes. 

The nursery had twenty iron beds and a ceiling of white sailcloth, furred with mildew but still tight as a drum.  Under a glass plate at the bottom of the shaft we found a softly ingenious little theater: two jointed tin swallows riding on the second hand of a kitchen clock.  There was a notch in one wing where my feather fitted, down to the crooked solder mark. 

I wound the clock and aimed my torch through the plate, expecting the old enchantment to captivate even Vi, who was measuring a sink with her apron strings.  Two enormous birds went round and round on the ceiling, one forever chasing the tail of the other.  Vi looked up once. 

"They didn't do that," she said. 

I tried the torch from every angle, until she took it from me and laid it facedown on a bed.  By then the last of the daylight was coming down the shaft, and as our eyes adjusted I saw a stand of reeds trembling upside down on the sailcloth.  Not a light shaft after all, but a lens somebody had ground by hand and a mirror they had tilted with exquisite care.  Vi lay down on the bed beside the torch and patted the mattress for me to join her. 

"You watched the reeds," she said, "because if you watched the birds you knew it was only a clock." 

In that week of smoke there had been a little green left offshore, and my father had sent his swallows flying through it.  I lay quite still, trying to learn the rhythm of belonging to a memory that wasn't mine, while the dark came up around the pale square of marsh.  The birds disappeared first; then the reeds ran together like the teeth of a comb seen edgewise.  Something kindled low in the square, went out, and kindled again a little higher, answered this time by a spark so close to the first that for a moment they made one light. 

Vi and I let out our breath together, spotting the first fireflies of summer the way people on the island must have spotted them for a hundred years before either of us came along.  It was such a small, luminous respite that I understood at last why he had never tried to describe it over the clatter of our supper plates. 

"He lay here too," said Vi, taking my hand and setting it against the iron rail where a hundred small fists had polished away the paint.  "Scared as we were." 

They took the shaft at sunrise, as agreed, but Vi got her sink and I got the clock and optics, which took me the rest of the summer to install at home.  When Vi brings her grandchildren over we lie on the floor together, and if the little ones get restless I let them wind the clock.
