For the six weeks of the butterfly migration, Ada kept supper on a trestle in the ancient greenhouse, where the mill hands came at dusk once they had finished showing strangers through their homes.  Ten weavers had gone down with the dye room floor in April; the company had paid for ten graves and shut the mill.  Now it wanted a single stone to stand in the courtyard when the estate was sold.  Ada, who had cut the headstones, had accepted the commission because she wanted to discover the shape of collective grief, and because the fee would get her out of town. 

She had discovered so far that it was not a wreath.  Whenever she drew one, her father appeared in the dusk glass behind her shoulder and made the little sucking noise that in his workshop had generally preceded demolition.  He had been dead eleven years, but his thumbnail still had its black split from teaching her to hold a hammer.  She waited for him to speak with the fiercely meditative patience he had taught her, the patience that could wear through a whole inch of granite without spoiling an edge.  All he said was, "Who gets a key?" 

That evening her boy brought out a woven ribbon roll, the last of the cream binding his other mother had brought home from the dye room in her apron pocket.  He wanted a strip for his pocket to take away with him, but cutting into it seemed to him like killing something.  Since he could not eat, Ada proposed the family custom of trading favorite poems at dinner: you gave somebody the one you loved best, and for that evening theirs was yours to say. 

He gave the binding to old Kit to hold while he spoke his mother's four lines about a goose that wouldn't fly south because it owed a frog sixpence.  Halfway through, his voice went, and Kit, who had worked beside her thirty years, supplied the indignant answer of the frog.  In return Kit gave him a verse about the sea so short he could have written it on his thumb.  He asked her to write it on the binding instead.  The thunder of the falls came through the broken panes, a steady sound that let nobody hear the boy crying except the woman who bent her head to write.  Others held out their hands for a length, not of the plain stuff but of the stuff her hand had written on.  Ada watched her son take up the scissors. 

That night her father made her retrace the lettering on the glass where, as a restless apprentice waiting for her supper, she had once scratched her name.  His finger met hers from the other side.  To him the letters ran backward, as they did on the seals they used to cut for merchants when nobody obliged them by dying.  She laughed, and for the first time in eleven years she saw him take pleasure in having been understood. 

By the time the butterflies hung in brown folds from the last orange tree, she had spent her advance on a cylinder of close grained limestone and left ten names standing proud around it, in reverse.  The agent took one look at the thing in its wooden cradle and said it was not what the company understood by a permanent memorial.  She let him keep the balance, which meant she and the boy would have to go third class, sitting up. 

On the last evening, with gold wings flickering high above the glass like scraps of their own departing light, Kit inked the stone and Ada's son turned the handle.  The roll unwound between his hands, taking the names in order and then taking them again, not one of them wearing away by so much as a hair.  People cut themselves enough to line a hatband or tie up a plait, and when the binding ran out they brought strips of shirts and the broad white tape from somebody's wedding petticoat. 

At supper the boy said the verse about the sea without any prompting, and Kit solemnly undertook to pay the goose's debt.  Ada looked for her father in the glass, but all she saw was the table repeated into the dark, every bowed head at it touching its bright opposite.  She packed the stone between two blankets; wherever they got off the train, they could start again with anybody who had a scrap to spare.
