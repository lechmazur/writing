By noon on the winter solstice, the cathedral bell rope was supposed to be a handrail for the icy steps.  The old bell could stay where it was, being too dear to take down, but the parish had found a use for everything else, including the keeper, who was going to cook at the school.  The only thing wanting was a signature on a piece of county paper. 

Isa Vale tested storm sirens for the county, a position generally beneath the notice of anyone whose siren worked.  She had come up through a mile of cloud to the windblown sanctuary, where the sun laid a thin gold ruler along the sill and the keeper was gluing envelopes shut.  Inside each was a different order for sounding the bell and the new electric siren down at the tram depot, with one minute left blank.  Once the glue dried they would take one at random and telephone the order to the depot, too late for anyone there to coach the listeners. 

It had taken Isa most of November to get the county to agree to that blank minute.  When she asked the quarry foreman what the siren sounded like in the cutting, he had said, quite honestly, that it sounded like all the men around him taking off their caps to hear it better.  She had laughed with him and then asked what the last man to take his cap off heard.  He had not laughed at that, but had gone to fetch the man. 

Since then Isa had been tracking microclimate variations with two borrowed thermometers and the help of people who emptied ash buckets before breakfast.  On her map of the valley, mornings when smoke lay flat against the quarry lip were mornings when the depot siren went missing down in the cut.  The answer from the county had been to turn up the motor until tea things walked on the depot shelves. 

It was ten minutes to what the transfer papers called zero hour when the keeper put a dry envelope in Isa's hand.  Below the sill there was nothing but white, but the foreman had telephoned to say the smoke from the watchman's stove lay flat along the lip.  Isa slit the flap with her nail.  Silence, siren, bell. 

The keeper called it down, then stood with her fingers loose about the woollen bulge in the rope.  In the first minute Isa heard ice slipping off a buttress in the sun; in the second she felt a faint tickling at her breastbone.  In the third the keeper pulled, and the rope lifted her heels clear of the stones as if the cathedral meant to keep one piece of furniture for itself. 

No one was taking off caps for the benefit of anyone else this time; the quarrymen had agreed to chalk what they heard on the backs of their own shovels.  While they waited for the telephone, the keeper showed Isa the blood blisters under her wedding ring.  She said she was looking forward to handling nothing heavier than a ladle for a change. 

The passengers reported nothing, siren, nothing.  From below the quarry lip came eleven shovels marked only with a cross for the bell, and one marked with a question beside it.  That last man had been running the drill.  He worked the lowest bench, where storm water gathered first and where the ladders were longest. 

At zero hour Isa did not sign the transfer or ask the keeper to stay.  She used the telephone to ask the drill man what it would take to reach him, and listened until she understood that no amount of noise was going to do it.  Switch the current off and on again in the string of lamps along the cut, he said.  He could attend to the other eleven himself. 

Until the depot got control of the lamps, the foreman undertook to fetch the drill man whenever the keeper rang, and Isa undertook to be the keeper.  By the time they laid the cable she had worn blisters of her own.  She was glad to help string the rope along the steps, and gladder still, on her way down, to hear the drill stop in the middle of a perfectly quiet test.
