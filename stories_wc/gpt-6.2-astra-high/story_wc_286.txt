Every map of the scorched country put the mountains somewhere different, but all of them agreed on the abandoned beekeeping oasis and its one full trough.  That was where the council meant to move the children when the town well failed, which it would do in eleven days.  They sent Osa the potter ahead because she had made the well gauge, and because her own son would have to go with the others.  She wanted to understand why some places resisted change before she trusted one with a boy who still slept with his fist in his mouth. 

She found her way by following sun patterns, not landmarks: wherever the histories of the desert parted, a stone cast as many shadows as it had possible afternoons.  Where they joined, the shadows lay neatly one inside another, like spoons.  Walking the seams brought her at last to a row of clay skeps with their mouths plugged by blown sand.  The trough held enough clear water to bathe a baby.  Above it hung a square of faded lantern glass in a frame without a wick or a door.  A little red fish of light swam on the bottom. 

She dipped out a cup, waited for the level to settle, and scratched a line there with her awl.  At dusk the smell of burning tamarisk made her turn.  A hut stood behind her where there had been nothing, halfway through the ordinary business of an evening: a fire kindled, a shirt coming off a line.  An old beekeeper came out with a pail, walked through Osa as through a patch of warmth, and filled the trough to its lip.  The skeps began to thrum.  Then she was alone with her cup and a wet line above the scratch. 

She could already hear the council cheering.  In the kiln yard where she had learned to sit zazen, her teacher used to tap her wrist when she reached to steady a wobbling bowl.  Let it show you which way it wants to fall. 

She sat beside the trough another day without touching it.  Toward sunset the red fish split into dozens, some darting along the lip and others swimming over the dry ground beyond it.  Each marked a place where the lantern hung in some other history; the glass made the fractured timelines visible where the tangled shadows failed.  As chimney smoke flattened across a dozen skies, she saw a dozen keepers, some walking past the trough and some turning toward it, pails in hand.  Only the histories in which the keepers stopped to pour bent close enough for her to hear the water.  The place was phototropic to kindness, turning toward it as the sunflowers in her teacher's yard had turned their blunt faces to the sun. 

Then in the nearest doorway a girl appeared, holding out a cooking pot for what was left in the pail.  The keeper tipped it; the girl shook the pot and listened, and he would not look at her. 

On the coast they still boiled fresh water out of the sea, but going there meant leaving all their handsome heavy things behind, her wheel among them.  For two days now she had been furnishing an imaginary workshop in the shade of the skeps. 

She went to the nearest doorway with the council's measuring jar full to its painted brim.  She did not try to catch the girl by the shoulder; nobody who came up behind a child ought to do that, in any world.  She let a few drops strike the bottom of the pot.  The girl turned, the doorway sharpened around her, and for the time it took to pour, Osa could smell onions and the wool of a shirt just brought indoors.  She left enough in the trough for bees and carried jar after jar through the red dusk until the girl told her, quite sharply, that they had enough too. 

When Osa got home, they wanted her measurements.  She gave them: so many jars to a trough, so many troughs to a town, one child's supper in every jar.  Nobody cheered, but in the morning people began taking the doors off their houses to make sledges for the coast road.  Her son found her sitting at the wheel, making cups that fitted one inside another so that even a boy could carry six.  She moved over on the bench and let him feel how little pressure it took to turn a lip outward, toward the mouth that would come for water.
