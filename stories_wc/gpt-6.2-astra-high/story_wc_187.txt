After the fog became solid, you could walk from our stormlit observatory to the tips of the tallest pines, provided you did not mind knowing how much tree there was beneath your feet.  Above the clouds we had a fine view of things nobody had ever wanted to see, including the weathercock on the steeple where my mother used to sing.  Below us lay all the streets by which a person might once have explained who she was. 

That was why the council wanted a figure carved at the head of the observatory stairs: the woman in the yellow coat who had counted the children as they came up.  As summer-quill archivist I was to supply her face.  I had got the job through a habit my mother called being preemptively nostalgic; even at supper I would write down what she said about the onions, against the day I had no onions and no mother.  Now I had onions enough for a winter and the private conviction that the yellow coat had been hers.  She had sewn a blue button at the throat of just such a coat, and out of a hundred depositions six mentioned the button.  None mentioned a face. 

On my last afternoon before the carving, rain rattled the copper dome so hard the masons stopped work to listen for a split.  Then the noise moved off across the treetops and I went out with my quill to the crystal forecourt, which only whispered after rain.  In dry weather our common paving kept to itself whatever it had caught of our lives; wet, it gave back a little sound and, if you knelt, a little light. 

I put the hollow nib of my summer goose feather to a joint and heard a child say seven.  Beside my knee the shadow of the monument to the first astronomer ran out like a black stocking, though the astronomer himself lay in pieces in the masons' shed.  I knew the child had climbed in the morning, for we used to tell the hours by examining shadow lengths at monuments, a valley trick older than clocks.  Farther along the joint the same small voice said eight, but here the astronomer's shadow barely covered his plinth.  No child took half a day to cross two flags.  I could only conjecture that the child had not been passing through but standing watch, which was no kind of work for a child that day. 

At the next joint I saw what the people coming up the stairs could not have seen against the sun: two shadows, one tall, one small, going through the motions of taking off a coat.  The taller one knelt to turn the cuffs back.  My mother always did that twice, once wide and once narrow, so the lining would not scratch.  Across the stone ran the dry little rasp of wool on wool, twice.  I followed the seam until I found what the child had said besides the numbers.  "Now you do me," she whispered, and another child started again at one. 

By dusk I had heard enough to understand what the coat was for: not warmth, but a spot of yellow you could keep looking at when you thought you could not climb another step.  Whoever could stand the sight of that stair a little longer put it on, and whoever had to go and look for somebody gave it up.  I sat there until the water left in the joints had nothing more to say. 

In the shed the masons had modeled my mother's nose in clay from a sketch I had let them see, rather too early in the proceedings.  I took it between my finger and thumb, and for a minute I had the wicked pleasure of pinching her nose just as she used to pinch mine.  Then I smoothed it out and asked them for a hollow coat, with room inside for somebody short to stand and see the stair.  All I asked them to keep of her was the turn of the cuffs. 

To this day children on the way home go in there in pairs; one disappears and the other climbs the last few steps to see what it was like to be expected.  From my desk I can hear their boots, then their whispers, then the quiet in which the one inside holds out a hand.
