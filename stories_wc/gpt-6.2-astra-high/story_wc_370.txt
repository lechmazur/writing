The public library in the children's hospital closed at six, but the private one, which occupied precisely the same shelves, kept the hours that children kept when they were sick.  Any parent could borrow a story; to borrow the other book that grew inside its covers you had to be shown the trick by another parent.  The Society of Endpapers had no other initiation.  An envelope behind the date slip held questions people could not bear to ask on the wards, and a second envelope held the replies. 

For nineteen years Edith Vale had written every reply.  She lived across the service alley, alone, in rooms she seldom left except to make her rounds when the corridors were empty.  People who mistook this for a dislike of company had never watched her read a question.  Even the weight of the pencil marks could tell her when a brisk request for information was an attempt to hide terror, and when the kindest thing was simply to supply the bus fare to the clinic. 

On Thursday she found a crumpled invitation in the mouse book, the one her own boy had liked because the mouse made a terrific mess and nobody died.  Somebody had flattened the paper against a knee to write on it: Please come to the shelves tonight at five to twelve and see what you did. 

Edith knew what she had done.  Last week, working by touch when a bulb failed, she had slipped an unanswered question into the envelope for replies.  She had been round twice since, hoping to retrieve it, but the mouse book was always out.  Now the invitation was all either envelope held.  She might have sent an apology by the night porter, except that she wanted to know how it had felt to open an answer and find a question there instead.  It seemed worth crossing the alley while people were still awake to learn that one thing. 

Within arm's reach of midnight the children's floor was as quiet as a field, though behind each door a little weather went on.  Two women sat on the carpet by the shelves; on the low table stood a kitchen timer with four minutes left.  The younger woman took the missing slip from her pocket and Edith recognized the green ink before she could read the words. 

"Does anyone else ever go into the linen cupboard just to be somewhere nobody needs you?" 

"I wrote that," said the older woman, "and she thought it was the answer to hers." 

"It was the answer to mine," said the younger one. 

Her question had been whether a good mother could feel glad when the nurses put her boy to sleep for a scan.  Edith would have written something about exhaustion, accurately and tenderly, and neither woman would ever have known about the other. 

"How did you find her?" 

"Green ink," said the mother, pointing to the felt tip behind the other woman's ear. 

The older woman cupped the timer in her palm and caught the bell on the first stroke, almost without sound.  They had been spelling each other in ten minute turns, she explained, so that each could get clear of the bedside without having to leave a child alone.  A father in carpet slippers appeared from the door of the recovery ward and the two women got up to relieve him. 

"We wanted you to have a turn," the mother said, and Edith nearly followed her before she understood which turn they meant. 

She sat down where they had been sitting, and the father sat beside her without asking which child was hers.  She had been staying with her boy all these years by leaving her flat each night, but she had also been leaving him by staying so busy with everybody else's grief.  These things fitted one inside the other like the little boxes the mouse had emptied all over the floor. 

On the back of the invitation she wrote the question she had brought home from the hospital nineteen years ago. 

"Did anyone else laugh with their child on the last day?" 

She put it in the envelope for answers, deliberately this time, and opened the book to the glorious spill of boxes.  At first the father took her dry, rusty sound for coughing; then she pointed out the mouse with a box stuck on each foot.  When the women came back they found the two of them still there, the book lying open between them in the wide quiet, taking up no more room than a child across their knees.
