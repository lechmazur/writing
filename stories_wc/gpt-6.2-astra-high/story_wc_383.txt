At twilight the velvet garden looked almost alive, except that the poppies kept the dents people's knees had made in them.  Families knelt here to speak to their dead, and the thick nap kept one family's questions from trespassing on another's grief.  I tested memorials for the city and had never once been tempted to kneel. 

Then the whispering seashell half in bed seventeen said, "Scratch my nose, would you?"  I got down and scratched the little ridge of pearl where a nose might reasonably have been.  "Now the other nostril." 

It was the first time a quarantined memory had made me laugh, and I felt in my pocket for the knot my predecessor had tied there against enchantment.  "It's not a charm that works against good manners," said the shell.  "Your predecessor always went for it too." 

"I thought the hood kept you from seeing out," I said. 

"I can hear a hand go into a cheap pocket." 

So much for the knot; I was charmed and stayed a skeptic, which is a perfectly serviceable condition for a public servant.  The woman whose last winter was lodged in the shell had tuned pianos under the name of Agnes Bell.  Her son had asked for the quarantine after she interrupted his wedding by begging a guest to take her home in his hat.  He had withdrawn his visiting privileges along with everybody else's, and now his lease on the bed was up.  "When the last star fades into morning light," the deed said, with a landlord's delicacy about the eviction of the dead.  My job was to archive the contents before the gardeners came to strip the bed. 

"Did you hurt his feelings?" I asked. 

"He had a right to get married without me there, and I had a right not to be there." 

I lifted the hood.  She was tragically radiant, as the expiring often are, the worn lip of her shell so thin that the moon shone pink through it.  In the archive she would never wear out because nothing there was allowed to answer back.  That was the bargain we offered the bereaved: permanence in exchange for the last word. 

"Would you settle for a piano instead of a hat?" 

"That depends on who gets to play." 

My archive cart carried a little upright piano, each wire good for one impression, to be unwound at the depot and sealed in glass.  Memories travel through resonance structures, a voice crossing into a wire the way the note sung into one corner of a church wakes in another.  I had always struck a key, held it till the shell went dark, and let the damper fall.  It had never occurred to me to ask what happened if you let the voice itself choose the wire. 

"Take the hammers out first," Agnes said.  "Otherwise you'll never get anybody to keep their fingers off me." 

We worked till the stars paled, she calling out which screws to turn and me laying the hammers on the velvet in a row like so many extracted teeth.  At her direction I wedged her shell against the soundboard and took my hands away.  The first note was faint and rusty as a hinge that had forgotten about doors.  Then every string began to tremble, not with her last words but with a tune that had certainly never been played at a wedding.  It had the halting gait of somebody going downstairs alone for the first time after a long illness. 

By sunrise the shell was empty and I had a whole piano to account for.  At the depot I archived the wire on which she chose to leave her name and trade, and entered the rest of the instrument as municipal property loaned to a working musician. 

Her son comes sometimes to the station concourse where she plays, and sits with his hat between his knees.  Yesterday he asked for the waltz they hadn't finished at his wedding.  For a long time there was only the sound of people going places, and then, as he stood up to leave, she played him the last eight bars.
