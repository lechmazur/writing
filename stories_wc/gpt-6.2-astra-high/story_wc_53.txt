The carousel had been still for nine minutes when Ruth found the black thumbwheel from the disposable camera in the silt under Horse Six.  She had known precisely where it lay for seventy years and had never yet found the drum still long enough to risk her fingers.  She knew the chewed place on its rim; she had been a child who tested everything, even glass, with her teeth.  She put it in her cheek for safekeeping and went to negotiate with her ghost. 

The lagoon was still going round, as lagoons did up here, driven by the warm air shouldering up through the clouds.  Rafts and pans of green water slid past the stranded horses in such profound quiet that Ruth could hear a woman on a passing kitchen raft beating an egg.  Three strokes, a silence to pick out shell, three strokes.  Once each circuit the kitchen door came opposite a blue door painted on the carousel drum, and for a second there was a whole house. 

That blue door had belonged to the school below, before the sea took the valley and the survivors came upstairs to live.  At nineteen Ruth had undertaken to paint the entire valley on a continuous strip that would unroll forever behind the horses.  At thirty she had settled for the main street; at fifty she was touching up the same six doors with progressively thinner washes.  Lately she lived by stripping brass off abandoned rafts, though she still woke some mornings late for school and others frantic to buy milk for a son who was sixty. 

Today the drum bearing had finally split, and there was a winter's worth of good dry planking in the thing if its maker would release it.  Under lagoon law that meant both the maker as she stood now and the shade she had deposited, full of intentions, when she signed for the wood.  Across the motionless saddle of Six sat a girl with paint in her hair, holding one hand shut. 

Ruth had collected fragments of overheard stories all the way around the lagoon that morning, meaning to offer evidence that nobody needed the painted street anymore.  She told the girl about a boy who knew the school door only as the place to spit from a moving horse.  She repeated what the egg woman had said to her husband about fitting their three children into two bunks.  She saved the best for last: a carpenter had promised his wife a table long enough that nobody would have to lift a plate to let somebody out. 

"They tell better stories about what they'll make of it than about what's on it," she said. 

"What color was the inside of that door?" the girl asked. 

Ruth could not remember which side she was standing on when her mother let go of her hand, or whether that happened before or after she put her own boy into a teacher's keeping.  The regret that pierced her was not for having failed to paint a valley but for all the mornings she had hurried the boy past his own door to come and paint this one. 

"I came to get lumber," she said at last, "not to prove you wasted it." 

The girl opened her fist on the miniature panorama Ruth had built inside a gutted disposable camera to persuade the raft council to give her the wood.  A curl of paper stuck out of the slot where the winding wheel belonged.  Ruth took the wheel out of her mouth, pressed it onto the little spindle and worked it with her sore thumb.  Across the empty lens hole went six crooked doors and then a long white stretch she had always intended to fill. 

"Give me the insides," the girl said.  "Let whoever takes a board paint something on the back first, where it won't have to be good." 

Ruth had driven harder bargains for a single hinge, but never one in which she got to keep so much.  She spat into her palm, and the girl touched it with a hand as cold as the brass Ruth fished up for a living.  When the kitchen came round again Ruth called for a pry bar and asked the woman the color of the door on the inside. 

"Which door?" the woman called. 

"Yours," Ruth said, and steadied the raft while the woman went in to look.
