The piano hung a foot above the flood, and from each of its three pedals a thin brown thread of water spun slowly back into the pit.  All over the dark opera house lanterns flickered as the salvage crew waited for Ada to tell them where to swing it.  For thirty years she had played viola in that pit, which apparently qualified her to say what lay under six feet of filthy water.  Her mother could have told them, but her mother had cleaned the place for forty years and been dead for two. 

Ada pointed toward the prompt side, then felt the cold familiar pressure of a thumb in the hollow of her wrist.  She closed her hand on the winchman's sleeve before he could throw the switch. 

The first time her mother had touched her like that since the funeral was a month ago, when the roof began to leak.  Ada had been upstairs boxing the little exhibition of theater relics that nobody had thought to move until the rain began.  At the touch she had looked down and seen, on an antique opera program damp along one edge, the delicate brown likeness of a flight of steps.  The leak had spoiled a beautiful engraving of a soprano to make that little staircase, but Ada had packed the program inside her shirt.  Since then every wetting had brought another picture, and the succession of them had lately inspired her to take up her pencil again after two years of playing nothing she had not been paid to play.  She had thought the code her mother's ghost had left her might be music, the steps a scale and the long pale bars that followed it so many measures of rest. 

Now, amid the hush of unstruck chords, she heard through the winchman's sleeve a little knocking, as if his very bones were asking to come in.  She let go, and it stopped; she touched the iron rail against which he leaned, and there it was again.  The crew had finished searching the house yesterday.  Still she unfolded the program, though one more immersion would surely cost the lady what remained of her face.  She dipped the paper as carefully as her mother used to dip a wrist to test a baby's bath.  The soprano's fine black eyebrows ran together into a ladder between two pale uprights, and this time Ada saw what was missing from her score. 

Nobody so far had bothered to play the spaces. 

She laid the wet sheet over the winchman's plan of the pit and turned it until the steps lay over the steps down from the street.  The two bars were the old foundation wall and the newer wall that held back the earth beneath the stalls.  As a child she had squeezed between them after her mother to hear the orchestra tune up through a grille, for the price of keeping very still.  Where the ladder ended, the piano was about to be set down on the only dry stretch of floor.  Ada drew a cross there with her finger, then took the man's broad hand and set it on the rail.  One knock and then two close together traveled through their joined hands. 

Ada had not wanted to disrupt the lift on the say-so of a ghost; the winchman only needed to feel those three small blows once.  He locked the drum and went down on his knees with a crowbar where her wet cross said to go.  They got the grille up, and a small hand came out of the dark and fastened rather painfully on Ada's braid.  Behind the hand came a boy in his stocking feet, so exhausted that even in the lantern light he kept climbing her like a ladder.  Later he would draw her a picture of the street door banging shut behind him and the river coming down those steps faster than a boy could climb. 

Now Ada sat with his head against her ribs and waited for the pressure on her wrist to ease.  On the boards beside her the program lay open, with just enough of the singer's face left to suggest that she was listening too.  Ada tapped out the boy's three knocks on the hollow of her wrist, and felt, before the cold thumb lifted, the rest that made them music.
