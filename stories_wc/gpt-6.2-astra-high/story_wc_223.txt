On the chessboard the commissioners had made of the flood, the white king was the hospital and the black king was the jail.  They had a piece for every building on the drowned square, but the board was missing all its pawns.  I asked for them and was told that nobody proposed to reconstruct the movements of individuals.  Above us, in the cheap seats of the whispering gallery, somebody said very softly, "Then you won't need the boats."  The curve of the dome delivered each syllable to the commissioners as neatly as a grape put into a mouth. 

During an interregnum the commission could take evidence only from servants of the crown; everyone else had been admitted merely as a spectator.  My peculiar qualification was that I had drowned for the crown every summer since I was twelve.  At the annual flood exercise we floated on cork backs, our hair combed out to catch picturesquely on the willows.  When the embankment actually broke, on an ordinary Tuesday, my cork back was in a cupboard four streets from the river. 

What the commissioners wanted to know was who had diverted the hospital boats to the jail, and what I wanted was to become the historian of small gestures.  Up to that Tuesday I would have thought it a pitiful ambition. 

From the roof of the jail laundry I had watched the shadow of the rescue balloon wander across the water.  That nomadic penumbra was familiar to anyone who had ever played a corpse: inside it, at noon, you were almost directly under the eyes of the man who sent the boats.  But twice it had slipped over my roof and twice it had gone on toward the hospital.  I had got onto that roof by standing on hands thrust through the bars of a window that was already half under water. 

I stepped out onto the floor beneath the dome and asked the people in the cheap seats to watch my feet.  I walked a long upright, retraced half of it, walked sideways, then made another upright parallel to the first.  An old voice overhead read out the letter H.  By the time I had finished the P, half the gallery had joined in, just as children used to do at the exercises. 

"The pilot read HELP," said the chairman, reaching for his pen.  "Why didn't you say so in the first place?" 

"Because I never finished it," I said. 

On the roof I had managed the H and the E before the slates came away under my feet.  It was somebody in the hospital, across the square, who had seen me fall and walked the L.  Another patient, a woman with her arm strapped to her chest, had made the P around him, because by then he was on his knees.  In the balloon it must have looked like a single word that had come unstitched in the middle.  Then every ambulatory soul on that hospital roof had pointed across the water, and kept pointing, even when the boats came close enough to take them off.  Nobody in the room could tell us who had given the order; what we had was forty people who had declined to put their hands down. 

"Were there deaths at the hospital?" the chairman asked. 

"Three," said the voice that had mentioned the boats.  "My husband was the L." 

Our chairman had a reputation for being benevolently ruthless: he would cheerfully bankrupt a parish to put a good roof on its school.  I saw him look at the little black king, which he could knock over with a finger, and then at the rows of people whose evidence he could not hear. 

"We have paid for a player's time," he said at last, "and I intend to get our money's worth." 

He took the knife he used for sharpening pencils and began to unbind the register in which my single deposition was to have occupied the first page.  He passed the loose sheets up among the benches and asked each person to write me a part.  That is how I got my archive, and why its first entry, in a widow's blunt pencil, gives neither a date nor a name. 

"When he got to the corner of the L he squared it off beautifully, the way he used to turn the earth around our beds of pinks."
