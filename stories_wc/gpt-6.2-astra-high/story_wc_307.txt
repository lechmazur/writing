The flat stone my mother threw that night went through the roof of a house that had been gone for forty years.  Only the three of us on the floating rock meditation platform saw it go through, because only we had entered the survey trance.  Outside it there was simply a pond in a magnetic grotto under starlight, the platform riding a hand's breadth above the water on the repulsion of stone for stone.  Inside, each widening ring of water lifted a section of the drowned valley briefly into view, complete and in miniature.  A roof came up in slices, thatch by thatch, and the magistrate beside me put a tick on his map. 

He had come to count the households displaced by the dam before the last of the compensation money went back into the treasury.  My mother had made his map, back when her hair was white and I had to pull on her sleeve to get a word in.  Four decades of research in the grotto had made her hands small enough to slip through the cuffs of my coat.  An hour spent looking into the past took a month off the wrong end of her life, and she had spent a great many hours looking.  The magistrate did not trouble to lower his voice when he asked me if the little girl understood why she was here. 

"I understand why my daughter is here," she said, "which I don't think you do." 

She had brought me for my trade, sailmaking, though it was not clear to me what use a needlewoman could be among all these substantial roofs.  At the outer edge of the pond the rings went under a bank of sycamores and the whole shining town went out. 

"Thirty households," he said, and reached for the bell that would wake us.  My mother caught the clapper in her fist. 

"On my survey I wrote thirty roofs," she said.  "Somebody in your office made the change to households, and I signed it because I was anxious to be asked back." 

She let that stand in the sycamore hush, without the excuse I could have supplied about a widow with a child to feed.  She had taught me that you weave truth as carefully as you weave sailcloth, since somebody you may never meet is going to hang his weight on it.  I had not known who had taught her. 

Where the rings came back from the bank I saw little pale triangles and took them for the undersides of leaves.  Then one turned edgewise and I recognized the belly of a wet shirt, pegged by its tails so the shoulders would dry without stretching.  We hung shirts that way at sea, where you couldn't go buying a new one every time the neck gave out. 

"Washing," I said, and had just enough of my mother's patience to withhold the word families until the next ring came.  Beneath each line of washing a low hull swung out from the shadows, turned on its rope, and swung back under the trees.  On the outward journey the wave had shown us only the leaves that roofed them; coming back it looked under the eaves.  My mother did not tell the magistrate what to write, though her own father had lived under those branches and gone to his grave renting half a shed. 

"Eight hulls," he said at last, and this time he kept the point of his pencil on the paper.  "How shall we find out who slept in them?" 

I gave him the name of the woman who still took in washing down at the ferry and who could tell one family's linen from another's by the darns.  My mother opened her hand to let him ring us home, but he wanted to wait for one more ripple so he could draw the moorings right.  She had gone into research, she used to say, to teach vanishing to appear, a thing I once thought meant making the water give up its dead.  Now I sat with her small cold hand inside mine and watched a busy man wait for a shirt to fill with light.
