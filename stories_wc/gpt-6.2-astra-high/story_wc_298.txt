In the catacomb orchard, where tree roots snake between bones, Ada kept a stall for people who had nothing left to sell but what had not happened yet.  She took no commission on bartered destinies, which was how her father had come to own a coat with pockets cut from an apron.  He used to put his hands in them and say he could feel what they were having for supper.  Now he put his hands in them and waited for somebody to tell him where he lived. 

On the evening he forgot her name, Ada went down with him to open the stall within the glow of half-lit corridors.  The lamplighter worked from west to east; by the time the last wick caught, every bargain struck that evening would be binding.  Until then you could trammel the pale feeder roots with a net and lift the two slips of bark back out of the earth.  Her father had made the net. 

She settled him beside the brazier with a burned tarot card he liked to handle, the Sun, saved from the fire that had taken their first house.  Only the little naked rider on the white horse had escaped the scorch.  As a child Ada had penciled reins into his hands because she hated to think of him having no say about where he went.  She was still at it, inlaying meaning everywhere, filling the cracks in the stall counter with scraps of brass that spelled out things worth waiting for. 

A woman in a porter's harness ran her thumb across the word tomorrow.  Her son had fallen from a loading stage and lay upstairs in the hospice, looking past her as if she were another peg for hanging clothes.  She had a fine future to offer: somebody she loved would sit beside her all night, the night she died.  All she wanted was an hour of being known again. 

Ada had just such an hour coming to her, a red bud on the branch that bore her name.  She looked at her father gravely trotting the little rider up and down his knee.  The boy might not last till morning; her father might last years.  She cut the two slips and pressed them into the earth before she could do anything so sensible as ask his leave.  The porter went up the stairs two at a time. 

People said Ada could patch holes in the soul, and until that evening she had been able to hear that without wondering whose skin she was using.  Her father had turned the card over and was rubbing the blank back with his thumb. 

"I was keeping the other side clean for a picture of my girl," he said.  "Do you suppose she is grown by now?" 

Three lamps still to light.  Ada fetched the net and cast it, weighting one edge with her knee the way he had taught her, and felt the roots go taut.  Up through the grating came a boy's indignant voice asking his mother what on earth she had done to her hair.  The laugh that answered was fiercely gentle, all the force of it pushed through a closed mouth so as not to frighten him away.  She let the net go slack. 

"She is grown," Ada said, "and she is a woman who does things in a hurry and is sometimes sorry afterward." 

Her father considered this without alarm.  "Then draw her sitting down," he said, and handed her the card. 

She got out the brass shears and the glue and made him a woman sitting on a stool with her two hands still in her lap.  While the last wick took, he picked a shaving off the counter and laid it across the woman's bent head for the comb he remembered buying her.  She had forgotten that comb altogether. 

He slipped the picture into his apron pocket, and when she held his coat for him he asked if she knew the way to his girl's house.  She said she did, and this time had the sense to let him finish buttoning it himself.
