Rain left Ivory Harbor the color of a peeled almond, except under the eaves, where the old salt still hung in gray beards.  The first people home after the exile ended touched those beards with wet fingers and heard their own doors shut twenty years ago.  Iona waited until the little shocks of recognition had passed before she set her ladder against the customs house.  She was the ocean-spray archivist, and she had come to take down the morning the children went away. 

Salt kept what it heard in the instant it set, and gave it up just once, in water.  Under a deep ledge a summer might accumulate in layers so thin you could read a newspaper through a week.  Iona lifted the layers onto slides with a razor and numbered them backward to the day the governor put the children on three separate ferries.  The orders survived, with destinations, but the lists of passengers had gone into the stove that heated the governor's bath.  The children had gone inland to be brought up speaking correctly, while their parents went to the islands to cut peat.  Now there were grown men and women in three inland towns who knew no other fact about themselves than that they had once crossed water.  Iona's sister had been four, old enough to know her name but not to say it the way a clerk would spell it. 

On the slides from all three landings the herald's trumpet blew without ceasing, making one long brass shaving of the morning.  Iona played six grains from six different ledges before she let herself sit down on the wet bottom rung.  It had always been her particular talent to see what might be done only after something had been done wrong.  Her sister used to tear a hole in paper and demand a drawing that needed exactly that much sky.  Now she sat looking at the space between her thumb and finger where a grain had been.  No one, she thought, could blow that long without taking a breath.  She asked if the town still had the horn. 

It had been beaten flat for a saucepan, but the herald's trumpet valve had been too thick to hammer and his widow used it for a bobbin.  They unwound forty yards of black thread in a kitchen fragrant with toast, the first commonplace kindness of that day.  The widow showed her how the herald had worked the thing against its bad spring, drawing it up by hand each time he changed the call.  The valve clicked going down and clicked twice coming up.  He used to brace the bell between his knees to do it, the widow said, demonstrating with the saucepan itself.  For as long as it took him to wrench the valve up, the children on the landings could make themselves heard. 

Iona carried her slides to the bait shed, which had a long counter divided into stalls like the landings outside.  She put a woman with a needle and a saucer of water at each stall and gave each the salt from its corresponding landing.  They played the layers at staggered intervals so that no two stalls sounded together, and at the first click each woman began to wet the salt grain by grain.  Out of the spaces where the trumpet wasn't came small sounds that had been going on all the time.  A boy at the north landing was trying to hearten his little brother with an account of everything a peat cutter could buy with his first wages.  At the east a girl was saying goodbye to the harbor cats in alphabetical order.  Iona knew that girl; half the people in the shed knew her, though no clerk would have thought to put the cats on a list.  They wrote the names in columns, leaving space for what the people inland might remember when they got these pages in the post. 

At the west landing a child said nothing at all but tore something with a small dry rip, over and over.  Iona kept her pencil still, though the others looked at her and one touched her sleeve.  Anybody could tear paper.  Under the column for the west she wrote, "Did you give somebody a hole to draw around?"  Then she tore a little square out of the page, so that if the woman who opened the envelope had forgotten the words she might still know what to do with the sky.
