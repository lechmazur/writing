In the winter quarters of the circus, a woman could get a postcard made of the last time anybody waved to her.  You went to the lion tamer's widow in the loft over the print shop, and you took the coat you had been wearing at the time.  Good wool caught the little crystals that shook loose at a parting; cotton generally let them through.  Most customers were mothers whose children had climbed into wagons without noticing the women on the ground were getting old.  Ida knew about that from both ends now. 

That morning her daughter Nell had laid a green coat on the bench beneath the rain-beaded skylight, beside an ash wood carving blank.  From downstairs came the occasional soft concussion of a trunk being tipped on end.  The wagons were going south with or without Nell, and for the second season running it looked like without.  The advance wagon had a press bolted to its floor and a bunk that was still called Nell's, although anybody used it now. 

"I want to know what he was doing with his hand," Nell said.  He had been too weak to come down to the yard the last time she left, so she had looked up at this very window.  Her father had lifted one hand and crooked the fingers just as the wagon passed under the arch.  By the time a telegram brought her back he had been in the ground three days. 

Ida teased a grain out of the collar and laid it on the copper lid of the kettle.  You could look through crystallized time only while the kettle warmed; once the water boiled, the grain went white as a blind eye.  Usually she spent those minutes cutting the chief lines of a likeness for a print she could finish later from memory.  She liked to harvest something serviceable from a loss, even if it was only a nose a grandchild might someday recognize in the mirror.  She put the kettle on and set her gouge against the ash. 

First came the window, bright enough to make them both squint; then the face behind it, much younger than the sick face Ida had put to bed that night.  Her first cut took out the white of his cuff. 

"There," said Nell, and caught her wrist. 

The fingers folded toward the palm and opened again.  It was the signal he had used in the ring to call a lion to him, and both women knew it as well as their own names.  Ida felt the girl's grip tighten until the tool began to hurt her.  Then, so briefly that an eye following the hand would have missed it, a second face came up beside his shoulder.  It was Ida's own, and she saw her own lips say something into his ear.  Memory gave her the words entire, with the smell of his flannel and the weight of his elbow in her hand. 

"I asked him if he wanted me to run down and fetch you," she said. 

The man in the window shook his head.  The kettle began its little subterranean knocking, and Ida had just time to cut the hollow between his thumb and forefinger before his face went white. 

"He did want me back," Nell said. 

"Terribly," said Ida. 

"Then why did he shake his head?" 

Ida took the kettle off before it could spit all over the wood.  "It's possible to want a thing and not ask it of somebody." 

Nell stood for a while in the rusty light that came through the leaves plastered flat against the skylight.  Presently she picked up the gouge and began clearing wood all around the shape of the hand.  She was a far better cutter than her mother when she let herself work, and by the time the trunks stopped thumping they had pulled a proof in burnt sienna.  Without a face to explain it the hand might be beckoning or taking leave; the small card was big enough to contain both. 

"You'll want the address of the next stand," Nell said, and turned it over.  Ida watched her write in the hauntingly practical hand her father had taught her, every letter distinct enough for a stranger to read in bad light.  That was where he was now, here and gone in the way she crossed a t. 

When Nell looked up from the yard, Ida put both hands against the pane and left them there until the wagon passed under the arch.
