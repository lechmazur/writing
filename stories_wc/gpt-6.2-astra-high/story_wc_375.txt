After the last echo of laughter faded, Nell took the broken combination lock off her father's coffin and put it in her apron.  All afternoon people had come down into the crystal cave to tell stories about the Great Vale, who had finally got into a box he couldn't get out of.  It was his own joke, written into his instructions along with the request that nobody mend the lock.  Even dead, he knew where the laugh would come. 

Now the undertakers had carried him up the stone steps, leaving Nell and her mother to dismantle the little underground theater.  The work lamps swung on their cables, and veils of green and rose went traveling through the long quartz points above the empty seats.  At the foot of the steps stood the easel where Nell had spent twenty years sketching portraits of people waiting to see her father disappear.  Her charcoal-fingered hand went up of its own accord to measure the distance between her mother's bent head and the moving light.  She could have drawn it tomorrow; she could still draw the first face she had sold for a quarter, down to the pockmark caught in the curve of the boy's nostril.  But the people who paid her wanted themselves, not the light that had gone across them, and long ago she had quit trying to put that in. 

Her mother was struggling with the big painting of the Great Vale ascending through clouds of unfastened chains.  "Help me turn him around, will you?" 

They tipped the canvas forward, and behind it, fitted into the cave wall, was a shallow cupboard full of paper figures jointed with thread.  Nell lifted out a girl in a yellow dress with a little black square for a mouth.  The girl's skirt was made of numbered wheels, so delicately painted that Nell recognized the combination her father had spun for luck before each performance.  It was her mother's birthday. 

From the apron she took the old lock, whose shackle had snapped in a fall the night before her father's first engagement in the cave.  She knew the family story: her mother had stood behind a sheet with a lamp and kept the restless crowd amused until a locksmith came.  What she hadn't known was what they saw on the sheet. 

"Turn her over," her mother said. 

On the back in pencil it said, The Girl Who Swallowed the Moon.  Inside the girl's paper belly a silver disk slid on a thread, drawing a scalloped tide up through the wheels of her skirt. 

"Why didn't you ever do it again?" 

"Who says I didn't?" 

Farther back in the cupboard stood a hundred little plays, each folded into its own stiff paper cover like a manila pay envelope.  All these years Nell had thought that soft scratching on the other side of her bedroom wall was mice.  Her mother put the girl away with hands that shook so much the moon rose twice. 

"I can see them all when I'm lying in bed," she said, "but I can't cut another one.  Now what am I supposed to do with the ones that keep coming?" 

Nell looked at the easel, at the stack of her father's publicity photographs that she used for backing boards.  On the blank side of his famous underwater escape she drew him as he had looked going up the stairs just now, except that she left the coffin out.  He floated on his back, hands folded, looking perfectly at ease as the crystal took him into its long green current. 

"No," said her mother softly, catching at the pencil to rectify the angle of a foot.  "He always pointed his toes." 

Nell found her knife and cut him free, leaving a hinge at the ankle for that small but necessary flourish.  They stretched the old sheet between the easel and the cupboard door, and Nell worked the figure while her mother tipped the lamp.  Across the cotton went the dead man, swimming up through colors that no paying customer had ever asked Nell to draw.  When he reached the hem she waited for her mother to tell her where he had gone.  Instead the old woman asked, in the practical voice she had always used for counting out the night's receipts, what else there was down in that water. 

Nell had never in her life been asked for a picture of something she couldn't remember.  She drew a fish made entirely of keys, a little deep-sea locksmith who wouldn't have to keep anybody waiting.  Her mother made room on the sheet.
