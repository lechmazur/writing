On the morning the path to the cemetery disappeared, the undertaker offered half price on anybody willing to wait.  My father, being already in his box, accepted by default. 

I had six hours to discover why some paths disappeared and others stayed obligingly solid under a pair of boots.  After that the tide would take the last foam off the quay, and with it the memory of everybody who had crossed yesterday.  As the town's sea-foam recollectionist, I had spent years trying to solve this nuisance with a saucer and a piece of crater glass.  The glass, black as burnt sugar from the heat of the fallen star that made it, gave the white bubbles a legible background.  Against it I could transliterate emotions, which the sea preserved as quivers, into the tap alphabet my deaf father had taught me. 

I skimmed up yesterday's crossing and tapped it into my own palm: mild disgust, pleasure, an itch of apprehension, pleasure again.  Where the path had failed there ought to have been terror, but nobody had been on it when it went.  I put the saucer on Father's box and asked the bearers for a little more time. 

Our town occupied the iridescent cave inside a giant crystal geode that the sea had bitten almost in half.  Between the inhabited shelves hung flights of crystal leaves, broad enough to walk on when extended and sharp enough to shave with when folded.  The missing pieces of our streets were all still there, tucked into the walls like the blades of so many pocketknives.  Nobody had seen one fold completely; every recorded loss had happened in the night, usually during a season of bad fishing.  Father used to conjecture that the stone got hungry too. 

He had been our bell ringer, a trade at which deafness is less of a drawback than people suppose.  What he loved was the shiver that went down the rope and through his good boots into his toes.  Now his bell stood muffled in black wool beside a dozen others, one for each boat that had not come home that winter.  All along the quay, the bereaved had tied up bells; even the buoy at the harbor mouth wore a sort of knitted sock. 

I might still be sitting on that box if a migrating bunting had not stopped to rob the undertaker's breakfast roll.  It flew across the gap to the cemetery with a beakful of crust, scattering poppy seeds as it went.  Most went straight into the drink, but three struck the rim of a folded step, making three quite distinct ticks.  At each tick the step stuck its lip out a little, as if it too fancied a bite.  I would never have noticed the movement without the seeds riding out on the lip. 

I knocked on the rim with my crater glass, and the first step opened all the way.  The next one stayed shut.  The open step began to fold again as its note died, so slowly that I only knew it by the creeping shadow of a seed. 

I went back to my saucer with a question I had never thought to ask of pleasure: did it have a rhythm?  Under my finger the bubbles of pleasure gave a double pulse, the second beat much weaker than the first.  Father always rang twice, once for the town and once, very gently, for himself.  The crossing feet had felt his greeting through the stone; even those in too much of a hurry to hear had felt it.  At dusk yesterday, after he died, I had wrapped his bell myself. 

I pulled the wool off now and gave the rope two good swings.  Across the gulf the crystal leaves sprang out in order, each one singing the next awake, right up to the cemetery gate.  Behind me somebody laughed, then apologized, which made the undertaker laugh too.  We carried Father over to the accompaniment of every bell on the quay, some admittedly a good deal better tuned than others. 

We keep the mourning wool, but nowadays we wind it round the bell ropes, where it does no harm and is quite a comfort on a raw morning.  I ring twice before I go down to gather foam, and under my good boots there is always an answer.
