My father bought fish at one end of the viaduct and sold it for six times as much at the other.  I kept his books until the winter he made me dock my aunt for the weight of the snow in their mouths.  Then I went to live with her in the basalt sanctuary by the frozen lake, where there was always room for one more person angry with my father.  She gave me the bed by the slit window and the job that went with it.  "If you're going to lie awake rehearsing speeches," she said, "you can keep an eye on the moon." 

The sanctuary had been built for people who crossed the lake after it froze over, before there were freight cars to lift a catch over the narrows and straight into the auction hall.  In the six weeks without a sunrise they had steered by tracking moon paths across a horizon that looked as flat as a shelf until you gave it your undivided attention.  There was a nick like a child's first lost tooth; there was a long shallow dip that would take a low moon half the night to clear.  On clear nights I drew what I saw on the whitewashed sill and on cloudy nights I copied it onto scraps of fish crate. 

"A vigilant horizon mapper," my father called me when he came looking for his bookkeeper.  "A thing we badly lacked."  He laid his brass compass on the sill beside my bits of wood.  I had walked under the magnetic viaduct after rain and seen the black iron sand combed into whiskers on its piers.  I asked him to take his compass down to the narrows and find a direction there besides up.  He put it back in his pocket and asked if my aunt would take thirteen cents a pound. 

By then I wanted nothing on earth so much as to drive a load of her char through the auction doors myself.  My aunt let me hitch the oxen before she asked who would drive the next load if I went through a soft patch on this one.  I started to unyoke them, furious at the waste of a bright night.  "Let somebody else take the reins," she said.  It took me a while to understand that she had set out to sculpt patience from impatience without wasting any of the original heat. 

That night the woman who gutted our fish drove us as far as the first headland, calling the tooth when she saw it bite a sliver out of the rising moon.  Her son drove us home.  On the next bright night we took the twins who mended nets, and one of them found a better mark for the return crossing: two black pines that became one when you were lined up with the landing.  The work was softly purposeful, like the sound of all those knives at the gutting table before the first story gets told.  Nobody made a speech; nobody even troubled to tell my father what price would keep us off the ice. 

When he came with his scales the last full moon of the long dark, there were six bare hooks where the harness hung and my aunt frying two char heads for supper.  I was eating with her.  That was the part he couldn't get over.  "You let them go without you?" 

I wanted to say something worthy of a patient revolution, something people might remember afterward, but the truth was I had worn a hole in my sock walking between the skillet and the window.  My aunt put a cheek on his plate, the sweetest morsel on a fish and the one for which his books had no separate entry.  We were picking the bones clean when the first sledge came home, then the others, runners singing on the hard blue road.  Father went outside with the money already counted into his hand; he still supposed there might be something left for him to buy.  What the drivers brought back was six empty boxes and a price chalked on each one, so even the children could read it.  My father put his money away and held the guttering lantern while the drivers unyoked, which was the only thing anybody needed him to do.
