As fog rolled across the harbor, Nell found her father's fishing rope laid out on the golden dock under snow so thin that the larch shone through like lamplight.  Somebody had put a black mitten over its cut end, the way you covered a face. 

Her father had left her his boat, which was worth less than its mooring fees, and a tube of fulgurite glass, which was worth whatever somebody would give for lightning that had once gone into sand.  She had come to sell both to pay his debts.  The rope was not in the inventory. 

Nell collected seeds for a living, and had consequently become something of a philosopher about what it meant for a thing to belong to somebody.  She took off the mitten and tipped three grains of wild shore barley into her palm.  They were plump, with the little beards rubbed away so they would not catch going down a narrow passage.  She knew the variety from one small envelope in the capital, labeled with the name of the man who had condemned the island where it grew. 

The islanders had been given passage to the mainland and told to leave behind their soil, their livestock, and anything else that might carry blight.  For years afterward barley had come up outside their kitchens in small, improbable squares of silver.  Her father, who had ferried those families over, used to put this down to the secret weavers, with a wink that made her think he meant spiders. 

She knelt where he had knelt to splice his lines, in two shallow hollows the snow had filled.  Under her fingers the knots came in sets of four, three tight and the fourth with a little give, like a tooth ready to come out.  She eased one loose; a pocket opened and barley spilled out.  But the grain that had fallen into her palm was last summer's grain, not some shriveled relic of the crossing. 

Farther down the dock the rope moved.  She followed it through the fog until she came to a girl sitting on a bait box, winding loose strands over her bare knuckles.  Her other hand wore the mate to the mitten. 

"He said you could work it out," the girl whispered.  "He said I could have the glass when you did." 

Nell drew the tube from her coat, and the girl set its narrow tip inside the open braid and began to coax the strands over it, turning the little thunderbolt until it sat snug.  The rough outside held the mouth open; down the slick bore went a pinch of grain.  Then came the three tight turns and the one that a thumbnail could undo. 

"My mother can do a whole washing line without getting out of bed," the girl said. 

Her father, who had once pitched a jammed concertina straight through a window, had nevertheless been infinitely patient about anything that could be untied.  She could picture him here night after night making room in a working rope for somebody else's field. 

On the mainland it was the custom now to give such a line to a couple setting up house, the girl explained; you strung it from door to fence post, and that first spring you knew what to do.  Her sister was getting married in the morning. 

Nell looked at the short stretch still to be filled, then at the child's red fingers.  In the capital she had written a paper arguing that the ancient methods of carrying seed ought to be preserved along with the seed itself.  She had meant drawings under glass, with arrows to show where the loose ends went.  Now she pulled the mitten back over the girl's hand and asked her to call the turns. 

They worked until the harbor bell sent its one soft note through the wool of the afternoon and the boat dealer came hunting for Nell.  He took the boat for the price of the mooring; she did not show him the glass.  That went home in a mitten, in the pocket of a coat that would be too small by spring. 

When the girl was gone Nell found one last grain caught in the seam of her skirt.  She knew a hundred fine dry words to write on an envelope, but she also knew, now, where to find a length of line.
