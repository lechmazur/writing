On the first morning after the ban lifted, the ferry families came on foot across the frozen lake, carrying their good shoes in their hands.  They put them on in the cobalt atrium, under the humming quartz colonnade where I had been hired to write the history of their disgrace.  Wind off the lake played those hollow pillars at a pitch just low enough to set the pools of lamplight trembling on the floor.  The visitors stood in that lantern-moth hush and looked for their names. 

I was a cloud-shadow poet, which on our shore meant that I could catch the shape of something moving and put it down in very few words.  That was what the council wanted, since the stonecutters charged by the letter, but what I wanted was to make the pillars beautiful.  My proofs, in lampblack on a ground of milk and chalk, went round each shaft in bands the exact width of a mourning ribbon.  On the landward row I had lettered the names of the passengers lost when the levy boat foundered; on the lakeward row, the names of the crew.  The inquest had found the crew guilty of abandoning the tiller, and our grandparents had voted to send their families across the water.  The crew's names had been forbidden here for thirty years, and putting them up had given me a tremor of certainty about my own courage.  I had always wanted to stride through boundaries once thought sacred, and in my imagination there had always been someone watching me do it. 

An old woman in red wedding slippers took my elbow and led me to the pillar that said the ferrymen had stayed at their posts.  She asked where I had got that.  I showed her the depositions I had been asked to paraphrase, eleven sheets much softened at the corners by my chronic indecision.  She turned straight to a sheet with a brown thumbprint, and I understood whose thumb it was before she fitted hers over it. 

"I said my father let go the tiller," she read, "and that girl off the farms put her hands where his had been."  "Nobody would write down what she did next because I couldn't tell them which girl." 

Behind her, a man whose sister's name was on the landward side set down the little hammer he had brought to cut the first letter.  The girl had held the boat across the wind, the old woman said, while her father made a bridge of his back between the deck and a shelf of ice.  "I walked on him," she said.  "He told me to take off my shoes because the nails were hurting him." 

Nothing in my education had prepared me for the nails, or for the small laugh she gave at the recollection of being scolded.  I could already see the lovely shape those two bodies would make in a border running underneath the names, and was ashamed to find myself pricing it. 

The man with the hammer asked whether there was anything he could cut that morning that she knew to be true.  "The bit about the shoes," she said at last. 

Her words wouldn't fit on either pillar, and while I was still counting letters she dipped the hem of her skirt in the pail of a woman washing the floor.  She offered me the wet corner as courteously as if it were a handkerchief and the trouble no more than a spot on my chin.  I washed off the bands, not the names.  Through the dissolving boundaries of my careful work the pale veins in the quartz showed like so many lines of unfinished script.  We started her sentence beside her father's name and let it pick up again across the aisle, where any one of the names might have belonged to the girl. 

We had money for three more sentences if I gave up my fee, four if the letters were small.  All winter the families came to wrangle over those four, and I went on caring how the letters looked, sanding each groove so it wouldn't catch a reading finger.  Even now, when the wind plays the pillars, there is a little rest after "hurting" while the reader makes the crossing to "him."
