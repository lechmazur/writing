After finding the old recordings, the reaper went up to the ancient observatory at dusk with a talking machine strapped to his back.  The last of the children his wife had saved was dying there, in the round room where the telescope had once stood.  Salt had begun to crystallize on the western window; through scattered light the color of apricots he saw an old woman sitting upright in bed, cutting teeth for a paper fish.  She had his wife's little habit of turning the paper instead of the scissors.  He had not been prepared for that. 

On the quilt lay an atlas page with sea monsters, each neatly deprived of a fin or a tail or an elaborate crest.  She was taking them apart to make new ones, as his wife had taught the children to do while they waited for the water to go down.  It had gone up instead, and when the coastmen came in the little cork boat there was room for the teacher or for the last three children.  The coastmen told the story every year with one fewer detail until all that was left of his wife was her goodness, which had never said much to him in bed.  He missed her cold toes and her rather dirty imitation of a bishop. 

The old woman looked at the machine and said she did not want to hear the hymn again. 

"No hymn this time," he said, and set the needle down. 

Out of the horn came the sandy scratching of scissors, then a child's voice announcing that the beast with eyes in its tail was called Afraid To Turn Around.  His wife had made the recordings during her first winter teaching up here, when it was too cold to take the children out to look at stars.  What she wanted, she told the school board, was to give names to nameless fears, so a child might ask for help without having to explain the whole dark.  The board had disallowed the expense of the blank cylinders.  She had bought them anyway, out of the money they had been saving for a carpet. 

From the bed came a little snort of recognition, and then the old woman held up her fish.  It had a mouth at either end, one smiling, one stretched wide in grief. 

"I never could get them both on the same face," she said. 

The trouble with being a reaper was that he echoed forgotten phrases, and by the time people came to him most of what they had ever heard was forgotten.  His mouth opened now and his wife said out of it, "You've got the giggles, haven't you?"  In eighty years of heartbreak he had imagined himself saying many things in this room, but not that. 

The old woman fitted the little fish between the blades as if she meant to cut its two faces apart. 

"We did laugh," she said, "in the boat.  She could still see us from up here and there we were shooting down the street where we usually had to walk in pairs, and somebody said we ought to stop at the grocer's for eggs.  We were laughing when the roof went." 

What hurt him, absurdly, was that it was just the sort of stupid joke his wife would have made, and all this time somebody else had had the use of it.  The woman said she had spent most of her life correcting that laugh whenever anybody asked about the flood.  She had given the town its hymn, in fact; she had written all six verses. 

"Is that what the fish is called," he asked her, "Afraid Somebody Heard?" 

She looked at it for a long time.  "Afraid It Means I Didn't Love Her," she said, and laid the scissors down without cutting. 

He could have told her what his wife would have thought, but he had been speaking for the dead long enough to know the difference between an echo and an answer.  Instead he asked her how fast they had gone in that boat.  Fast enough, she said, to reach the grocer's before the eggs got wet. 

She was the first soul he had ever carried out of the world laughing, and when he went back alone to fetch the machine he found he could remember the joke in her own voice.
