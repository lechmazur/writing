On the first morning of the excavation, Ada took the ferry home with her father's ashes on the seat he should have occupied the week before.  He had lasted almost to the end of the gangway in Naples.  In forty years abroad he had never quite believed the islanders would keep a place for him, and the invitation to watch them open his family tomb had finally persuaded him to go.  In his breast pocket she had found a second ticket, for the short bus ride from the landing to the orchard.  She walked that part, reluctant to arrive any sooner than grief absolutely required. 

Aboveground the quinces were still hard enough to knock together in the wind; below, their roots snaked between bones, sometimes wearing a knuckle like a ring.  The excavators were down on their knees in the family vault, scooping out the clay that had come through the roof in the winter floods.  Beyond their lamps stood three tiers of shelves, every one occupied.  "Well, Papa," Ada said, and could think of nothing that would not sound like crowing over a man for having been right. 

At noon the forewoman ate her egg on the sundial base outside, where a pale wedge in the lichen showed that the brass had gone missing fairly recently.  "Does everybody sit where you're sitting, or is that the privilege of rank?" Ada asked, offering the woman salt in a twist of ferry paper.  The woman laughed and made room.  "My grandmother sat here to take names, because people getting off the boat knew they could find her when the shadow touched twelve." 

"What did she do with them?" 

That was something the forewoman had never thought to ask. 

Each day after that, while the crew went deeper into the clay, Ada picked a different lane from the landing, asking wherever somebody had set a chair outside about the woman who took names.  She liked a question that got people out of their chairs to show her something.  A retired cobbler showed her his father's name scratched on the kitchen doorjamb, with the date he had sailed for Brazil and, much lower down, the date his little box had come back.  A schoolgirl took her under the orchard by another stair and showed her how to hold a lamp behind a quince root without touching it.  The root had seemed to be growing letters, but the writing was on the wall behind it: the cobbler's father's name with both dates, dozens of other names still with only one.  Ada went back to the family vault and looked for her father's name, but the wall where it ought to have been was still under clay. 

On Friday the excavators reached the old floor and found, beneath the lowest shelf, a row of small clay doors.  The forewoman let Ada brush the mud from one herself; somebody had drawn a ship in the wet clay and given it an absurdly tall smokestack, like the funnel on the ferry of her childhood.  Behind it lay a space no bigger than a bread tin.  By then Ada knew whose kitchen to visit with a drawing of the ship. 

The cobbler put down his coffee and pulled a chair up for her and said yes, he had made that door, in school, the year her father went away.  Every departing islander who gave a name at the dial got a door, though nobody liked to talk about the size of the opening.  He knew where, under the lip of the frame, she would find her father's name in pencil. 

"You might have told him," she said, with a sharpness that surprised them both. 

He said he was eight then, and what would you think, at eight, except that grown people told each other everything they needed to know? 

The next morning, while the crew cleared the last doors, she began the rounds again, this time to rebuild the roll of names that the old woman had carried in her head.  She took no straight way anywhere; one kitchen gave her a maiden name, another the spelling, a third an address for the living.  When the tomb was clean, she slid her father's tin behind the little ship and wrote to her sister exactly where to look, and which stair to take. 

At noon she spread the roll across the sundial base, weighting the corners with quinces, and waited there instead of taking the bus back down.  The forewoman set her finger where the brass had stood, and its narrow shadow fell on the first name.  Ada had a pencil for corrections, and a book of stamps so that nobody still alive would have to buy a ticket on faith alone.
