In the misty courtyard above the clouds, Nell waited for lightning with a pot of blue paint in one hand and her mother's flower press bolt plate in the other.  All round the courtyard ran the covered gallery of the convalescent home, its freshly scrubbed pillars innocent of the painted weeds that had grown there until yesterday.  The new matron had wanted everything nice for the patients. 

Each painted weed had been a message from a man who could not spare the breath to call downstairs, and the scrub brush had silenced a whole garden of them.  A dock leaf on a pillar meant a basin deep enough to wash your own feet in; a sprig of thyme meant somebody please come and write a letter.  Men with ruined lungs had little breath to waste explaining why these were not the same as a sponge bath and a postcard with boxes to tick.  Nell's mother had devised the flowers and built the weather eye that read them, each fresh exposure of its silver lid setting off a little tune downstairs in the duty room.  Even after her funeral the nurses could tell a basin from a letter by ear. 

What none of them could remember was the flower that called for Nell.  She was a seldom-heard balladeer, because a song in the lung ward could be a misery if you had not asked for it and the fellow singing had breath to spare.  Before she died, Nell's mother had sometimes rung her up to sing for a bed at the far end of the gallery, where nobody could see who had sent for her.  Tonight the matron had rung instead, her voice stripped of its starch: the weather eye was playing something she could not bear to listen to alone. 

Between lightning and thunder the instrument commonly gave one clear reading before the concussion jarred its delicately sprung lid shut.  Nell stood driftingly alert, as she used to stand at a bedside until a man's breathing gave her the time of his song.  White fire stitched the sky to the gallery rail.  From downstairs came three bright spoon taps and the little skid at the end that her mother's lame finger used to make on a dulcimer string.  Before her on the clean pillar a blue flower opened, entirely made of light.  Nell clapped the bolt plate over it, and through the four holes her mother had once tightened down on layers of blotting paper she dabbed four dots of paint.  Thunder punched the lid shut; the blossom vanished, but the dots stayed wet under her fingers. 

She knew now why a man's worst scrawl of a flower would set the eye singing, while her own careful copies had never once succeeded.  Her mother had used the old plate to space the petals exactly as the lens expected them; you could encode the whole garden just by leaving different holes empty.  Yet the eye had just thrown a flower back at the wall, something no arrangement of lenses and springs was supposed to do. 

Downstairs, the matron had her ear to the drum where the code was punched in tin.  "It's caught on something," she whispered, and Nell heard the lame finger trying over and over to finish its run.  There was her mother, as precise as ever: a ghost in code, with fifteen ways to ask for help and nobody left who understood the fifteenth.  Through the speaking tube above the drum she could hear the rain ticking on the sill of the very bed where her mother had always sent her to sing.  All those nights she had thought the old woman stayed downstairs out of indifference or tact, and all those nights she had been sitting here under the open tube. 

Nell took up the broken run exactly where it snagged and sang the rest of the silly ballad about the tinker who shod a duck.  On the third verse the tin drum gave one soft, conclusive plink.  In the morning she would help the matron paint the garden back, with a plate and a paint pot at every bedside so that nobody had to wait for a nurse to guess.  Now she sang another verse, an utterly unnecessary one about the duck demanding change, for the pleasure of the woman who had asked.
