In the first spring after dreams became shareable, my mother gave the library her dream of the night we spent under the bridge.  By then the library stood above the clouds, high enough for its apricots to flower while the drowned orchards below were still buttoned up in ice.  Each graft carried one sleeper's gift; you put your nose into a blossom and woke with pollen on your lip.  Children who had never seen an apricot asked if the fruit would be a bigger dream. 

I was the dusk noticer, appointed on account of my sharp eyes to tell the gardeners when a graft first cast a shadow of its own.  Until then a dream could still be pruned; after that it belonged to whoever breathed it in.  Mother's branch bloomed white, and in every throat of every flower lay the little black swallow I remembered on the concrete overhead.  Outside the bridge the water was taking our house apart with the softly deliberate movements of somebody unpicking a dress.  Inside, with a bicycle lamp and a river stone, my mother had made an egg hatch above my head.  I believed that bird had got us through the night.  When the gardeners asked what to keep, I told them to take the long muddled beginning off and leave the swallow whole.  Mother had been dead a week; I could no longer bear to see her fumble. 

On the evening before the branch was due to set, a boy came up on the provisions lift with his father, who grew our rootstock.  I let him try a flower.  He woke holding his thumb and finger in a ring, and looked into it as if down a well.  "I was making a way out for the water," he said.  "Why did it have to be a bird?" 

Already on the eastern wall I could see the tips of two forked feathers coming clear of the branch.  By morning every child who visited that night under the bridge would have precisely my consolation. 

In the dry collection I found the silent video a rescue worker had taken under our bridge to count the stranded before the boats came back.  Beside it I ran the equally silent footage of Mother's intake interview, forty years later, when a gardener asked to see how she had done it.  I stopped both films where the egg, which was only the shadow of the stone balanced on its end, began to split.  In each picture Mother tipped the stone into her apron and held the lamp quite still; in neither did her fingers flutter.  The fork in the swallow's tail was a split in my wet mitten; there it was when the camera turned on me.  At the interview it was the gardener's long hands that went up, and a hare unfolded ears big enough to catch the sound of oars.  Mother watched the gardener, not the wall.  All that fumbling I had cut away was the time it took another pair of hands to venture into the light. 

The prunings were still in their pail of meltwater, translucent as onion skins.  The gardener said we could bind the beginning back on, but not unless I pinched off the bird myself; there wasn't sap enough for both.  It hurt to take between finger and thumb what I had thought was the last living thing my mother made.  I wanted to preserve the shape of hope, and the only shape I knew was no bigger than the place where my fingers met.  I pinched, and felt the small resistance of a thread breaking in an old dress. 

While we bound the wet slips to the cut, the shadow on the wall came loose and slid across the floor.  It was a shadow unbound, with no obligation now to look like anything at all.  At the boy's feet it began to metamorphose, rounding to the mouth of a well, and from somewhere deep under the blossom came the chuckle of running water.  Through his little spillway a whole river was draining harmlessly into the sky. 

At dawn the graft set, not on the bird or the well but on the instant between the falling stone and the first brave finger.  I wish I had looked once, under the bridge, away from the bright concrete and into the dark where my mother sat watching me.  Now when a child leans into the bloom I know where to look.
