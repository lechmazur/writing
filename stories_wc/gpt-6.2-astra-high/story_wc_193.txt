When concepts began taking physical form, the first thing to gain weight in our district was the mortgage on the mushroom farm.  Every midnight it pressed the old submarine another inch into the mud, and the mushrooms shone a little bluer along the buckling seams.  The night the hatch jammed, there were six of us inside, including my sister Nell, who had not spoken to me in eleven months. 

I had sold her the share that put her there.  Six fresh growers under each grower, six under each of those, and presently the lake would not be wide enough to hold our prosperity.  I got out with my stake intact; Nell got a bunk beside a compost tank and a deed to one sixth of an inch of water.  Now the water was coming to collect her. 

Through the high observation port we could see dry reeds, each with a bead of moonlight in its elbow.  Beyond that glass was air; below it the lake was already blacking out the little round windows one by one.  Nell tried a hammer on the high port and knocked herself flat with the rebound.  I wanted then, more fiercely than when there was money in it, to translate pressure into diamonds.  One point hard enough to score the glass would do. 

The company had shown us a diamond the size of a plum and given us what the salesman called a quantum promise.  If one farm managed a harvest, every possible version of that farm would have access to the knowledge of how it was done.  Nobody had thought to ask what became of the farms that supplied the knowledge. 

I took my phone out and touched the little plum on the screen.  We got glitchy text messages from parallel dimensions in which our submarine lay at every imaginable angle in the mud.  Somewhere it was summer; a photograph came through of a mushroom bed speckled with drowned bees.  Somewhere Nell had my phone.  STOP WAI ING FOR FRUIT, she had typed, and underneath it, much later, the single word TEETH. 

All around us the caps stood on their stems like blue umbrellas after the people carrying them have stepped into shadow.  Nell turned one over.  Along its gills, fine as the stitching on a christening dress, ran a seam of hard white sparks.  Another message arrived, this one from a version of me who had stayed with the company long enough to learn its secrets.  PLUM IS PASTE, it said.  Small dots appeared as if she were trying to tell me more, and went on appearing till I put the phone away.  The mushrooms had been translating the pressure all along; it was the size of our hopes that had kept us from seeing what they made. 

I asked for a volunteer to hold me up to the high port, and nobody moved.  Once before I had stood in this room and asked who wanted to get in first.  I sat down in the rising water and laced my hands together for a step instead.  Nell put her boot in them. 

"Give us your lucky bolt," she said. 

I wore a submarine porthole bolt on a cord around my neck, the one I had quietly removed from the salesman's private observation booth.  Through the empty hole the night shift had passed a wire and enjoyed free electricity for a kettle all winter.  We packed its threads with luminous gills until Nell held what looked like a screw stolen from the machinery that turns the stars.  She drew a circle, slowly, the threads renewing their bite as each small tooth wore away and another took its place.  When the circle fell outward we heard frogs, and I felt her boot lift and missed its weight.  Four pairs of boots later her hand came back for mine. 

On the bank I took a picture of the bolt with its threads still shining and sent it back through the plum, marked FREE in case some other me got ideas.  The mushrooms were still shining through the uncut glass below us, lighting a staircase down which the dark went softly, step by step.  Nell did not forgive me then and there.  She asked where I kept my stake, and we sat dripping till the bank opened and I could answer her in something better than words.
