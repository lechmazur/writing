On the night the aurora came down to the latitude of Vancouver, the power company shed the waterfront to save its transformers.  In the towering labyrinth of shipping containers behind the fish market, eight hundred supper spoons paused, then went on scraping.  Rosa, who maintained the stairs and drains and knew everybody by their walk, heard her father coming up to the roof before she saw his torch.  Two quick heels and a pause: the old auctioneer still walked as if allowing time for one more bid. 

"You'll get your trial after all," he said. 

For months she had been trying to prove to the tenants' council, which he chaired, that a whisper could travel farther than a shout.  He had offered to test the proposition the next time the electric horns went out, reasonably confident that neither he nor the horns would live that long.  Now the geomagnetic storm had called his bluff, and the pump in the lowest court had stopped with the tide still rising. 

"Challenge stands," Rosa said, "but let's send something useful." 

They needed six people at the hand pump and no more; last time he called for help a whole stair had jammed solid.  From his waistcoat he pulled the zodiac coin he had carried since the fish exchange closed, twelve beasts around a hole once used to string the stall keys together.  Those same beasts, painted on the old fish stalls and salvaged along with their doors, now marked the twelve courts.  He spun it on his thumbnail and caught it against his palm: the rabbit for him, the opposite beast, the rooster, for her.  Three volunteers from each, equally distant across the roofs. 

His voice went out like a beautiful strong rope, and she almost wished it would hold.  Then the steel began throwing pieces of it back: three, three, pump, pump, pump. 

Rosa went down on one knee and struck the deck twice with the brass key presented to her at the opening of the estate.  A key with no apparent lock had proved an excellent way of asking leave to come in; below her a woman slid open a kitchen skylight.  Rosa whispered her request, passed down the key, and unfolded one panel of the map she gave every tenant when they moved in.  Drawn flat, the place took a bedsheet and looked like a railway accident.  On the folded maps each pleat was one level; pinch two pleats together and the kitchens that shared a service hatch met corner to corner.  She had worked it out delivering rent receipts in the rain, a private laziness now being put to its first public use. 

The little double knock went east, dropped a floor, went east again, and stopped where the laundry bridge had been taken down that morning.  She should have changed the maps then, instead of promising herself Sunday would do.  Already she could hear the handle falling hard against its stop: somebody had come, but not enough hands to keep both ends seesawing. 

Then from the landing below came two quick heels and the auctioneer's pause.  Her father had slipped away with the map; she could see his torch shining pink through its pleats as he brought two distant corners into contact. 

"Under my floor," he called up softly, surprised that his own kitchen lay on a route to anywhere.  He took the key from the stranded messenger and led her down through his flat, past the chair where he sat listening to recordings of his own auctions.  At the hatch beneath the chair he did the knocking himself. 

By the time Rosa reached the pump there were three women from Rooster Court on the uphill handle and one neighbor from halfway there hanging on the other.  "Thought he said sheep," the man explained, "but I thought I'd come and see." 

She and her father made six. 

When the tide turned, he took out his pencil and marked in the passage under his chair, bearing down hard on the map against his knee.  "Leave that one in the next edition," he said. 

She had expected to enjoy making him admit defeat; instead she found herself considering how often a man might like to hear a knock without having to call anybody first.
