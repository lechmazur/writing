After the beekeeper's last visit, I found his funeral money in the museum till, wrapped in the scrap of newsprint on which he had written his instructions.  He wanted his death announced in the valley script, in type big enough for his wife to read for herself.  She had read the announcements to him for sixty years; he thought she deserved to see what a distinction it was to get one's name in a box.  By the time the boy came up the steps smelling of the rosewater they used to wash the dead, I had counted the money twice.  It would buy the paper. 

I conducted historical tours of the grotto city, beginning at the edge of the spray, where a whole river dropped past our door without wetting the mat.  Behind that tremendous curtain the city sheltered three thousand displaced persons when the reservoir filled, including my mother, the beekeeper, and me.  I could tell visitors exactly when the valley press had ceased publication, down to the hour the compositors put the machinery on a barge.  It was chiefly to rescue dying arts that I had established my little museum, but so far I had rescued a great many dates. 

The handpress still worked, and by borrowing from the headings of old advertisements I could set all but one letter of the beekeeper's name.  His initial was missing, a sound no longer made in the language we spoke behind the falls.  Under my emerald-tinted opera glass lens, a relic of the more elegant employment I had once found for my eyes, the initial in his note resolved into three scratches and a grease spot.  Enlargement made the spot magnificent without making it a letter. 

My mother, who had set type as a girl, said she would know it if she felt it.  She could no more draw it in the air than she could climb a staircase by lying on her back and kicking.  I brought her the last lump of wedding dough out of the ice room and sat by her stove to let it thaw.  It had been a custom in our family to bake each guest a bread roll shaped like his initial, which made for some hard bargaining among the children at table.  The beekeeper had stood up for my father at his wedding; my mother must have made that letter at least once. 

I thought it would come to her if we did not talk about it, and lectured her instead on the antiquity of the wheat trade until she begged for mercy.  Then she rolled the dough into a rope and laid it out in a shape like a kneeling man.  I reached for my pencil.  She slapped my hand.  With two fingers she pinched the man's head and tucked it down between his knees, leaving a hole through which you could just pass a straw.  That was where the light came through, she said; without it we would have announced the death of a different man.  For the first time since the boy had come she began to cry, and for a little while we let the letter rise between us. 

I took a mirror and a block of end grain from the engraving drawer and cut what I saw in the glass.  At dusk we pulled a proof and carried it, still tacky, through streets where the lamps were coming on behind the green water.  Once again there was the smell of roses, and at the widow's door I let my mother go in first, as I had when I was small.  The widow put her thumb in the white space in the letter as if she meant to hold him down on earth a little longer.  Then she read his name aloud, with that soft catch of breath at the beginning that I had quite forgotten.  She had one correction: the man had kept bees for fifty-eight years, not sixty, because for two years after they came here neither of them could bear the thought of beginning again.  We made the change. 

These days I finish my tour at the press, where my mother and two apprentices print whatever the valley people will pay for, including bills which are a pleasure to nobody but me.  On the wall hangs that first proof, with its beautiful initial and its error of two years, the only interval in our history for which I can give a reason as well as a date.
