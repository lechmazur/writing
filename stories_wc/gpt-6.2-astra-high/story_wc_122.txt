My husband used to say that a drum sold better if the customer had just been made a fool of and could be brought to laugh about it.  Every autumn, during the faint rustle of turning seasons, people climbed to the drum maker's cavern to be fooled by Len and his clever wife.  I was the music teacher down in the valley, deaf since infancy, and the cleverness they paid to see was mostly his. 

We had six backward recorded songs on wax cylinders, all familiar tunes rendered strange, like writing on a steamed window seen from the street.  Someone drew a cylinder from a bag and fitted it to the machine while I stood blindfolded behind the biggest drum.  Before the song was halfway through, I would beat out its proper forward measure, then whip off the blindfold and conduct the customers in the chorus.  It can enthrall a person to be given back something he didn't know he'd lost, even when it's only the tune to Three Fat Geese. 

Len called our secret a filament of hush, which was a grand name for a length of his best waxed sinew running from his boot to my wrist.  He had only to wag his foot under the counter and send me the number of the song in tugs. 

Last autumn, the first without him, I put his boots on the counter and nobody touched them, though they were good boots and plainly marked for sale.  People spoke to my daughter in what she told me afterward were audibly muted voices, as if what had killed her father were upstairs sleeping.  This autumn I wanted the old noise back, not because I could hear it but because I thought I might feel something when the customers stamped.  For a year I'd been able to put food in my mouth, mark a pupil's progress, kiss my daughter goodnight, all with the same efficient hand. 

Nell agreed to wear the string if she could have whatever money came in from passing the hat.  I was so glad she wanted something that I didn't ask what it was. 

There were eleven customers for the first show, including a baby whose immense padded bottom Nell installed on the counter where the boots had been.  At the first pull on my wrist I lifted my sticks; at the second the whole line went slack.  Off came my blindfold.  Nell was standing with the broken phonograph needle on her palm and blood swelling out of her thumb.  She had tried to set the reproducer down with one hand while the baby, fascinated by the shine of the brass, reached with both.  The customers looked at me and I looked back at them, each of us politely waiting for the other to supply the appropriate face. 

Then Nell put her thumb in her mouth and wagged her foot.  One tug, two, then a long succession of uneven little jerks that meant no song I knew.  She wasn't looking at me but at the baby, whose bare heels were striking the hollow oak counter in just that pattern.  I gave it back on the drum.  The heels stopped and so did I; the baby tried one experimental thump, and of course I gave him one.  Somebody set a farthing spinning on the counter and Nell fed me its long diminishing shiver, so close to a roll that my sticks could scarcely divide the strokes.  By the time the coin fell flat I was laughing too hard to catch the next instruction.  It hurt across my ribs, precisely where the weight of Len's arm used to settle in bed. 

We sold three drums and Nell took enough in the hat to buy the boots, which she had been afraid to ask me for in case I had some other plan for them.  I very nearly told her they were hers for nothing, but she was counting out the money with her father's gravity and I held my tongue. 

All winter there were two kinds of lessons in the cavern, the kind where children learned the measure and the kind where they sent it down a string to me.  On wet days I felt Nell come in before I saw her, banging the mud off those boots on the plank floor.  It was her own knock, nothing like his, and after a while I stopped wishing it were.
