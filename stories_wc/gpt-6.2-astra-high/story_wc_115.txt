In the hush after her father said, "I was the first one out," Nell heard a drop of water fall from the roof of the abandoned train tunnel into his hat.  He had put it on the ballast, crown down, as if even now somebody might put money in it.  Forty years of testimonial dinners had bought him a bungalow and Nell a set of straight white teeth. 

On the table between them lay the object she had come to appraise for the diocesan museum: a beaded valve wheel, green at the hub and bright along the rim where desperate hands were said to have polished it.  His hands, according to the little cards that people still tucked under their pillows before an operation.  She had looked for this wheel the way she always looked for things in abandoned mills, by going in headfirst through an opening somebody more sensible had decided was too small.  Now blood was drying inside her sleeve, and her father had chosen this particular minute to stop being a saint. 

Outside, the dawn milk train went by on the new line; inside, under a yellow inspection lamp, the two of them might have been keeping a bedside watch.  His eyelids drooped, but his ear inclined toward the wheel.  She wanted more than the pleasure of catching God out, a pleasure that had rather palled since she was twelve.  She wanted to find the weight of a swallowed truth, to know what exactly had been taken off other shoulders and laid on his. 

Without breaking the quiet she fitted her headphones and thumbed the recorder in her pocket.  She had found the wheel by interpreting frantic calls to talk-radio psychics, the only people who had kept any record of what the women on the old bleaching floor called the ghost shift.  After the flood the women would wake at three with their arms describing circles and an ache in the small of the back that none of them felt during the day.  Some of them had been doing it for decades before cheap long distance let them seek help beyond the reach of a neighbor's ear.  One caller kept crying that she could see the moon below her feet, which had told Nell to look in the tunnel sump, where a round grating admitted daylight from the street. 

Now she listened past the sobs, past the soothing prescriptions of lavender oil, to the sound one woman made with her tongue against her teeth.  Four clicks, a breath, four clicks again.  With a finger Nell nudged one of the blue glass beads strung on wire between the spokes; it struck its neighbor with a little dental tick.  Four beads on each wire, four spokes, sixteen women saved when her father supposedly opened the washout valve and sent the flood down the freight incline instead of through the bleaching room.  She had thought the beads commemorated the rescue, but paint from the wheel itself lay in their little chipped mouths. 

On the tape a psychic was telling a woman that four was the number of the angels, and for the first time Nell heard what the woman kept trying to tell him over the soft incessant music.  Not four angels: four turns.  One woman remembered passing the rim to a hand missing a forefinger; on another station a caller complained of a phantom ache in the finger the mangle had taken when she was a girl.  The wheel had needed a hundredweight at the rim, said the brass plate on the hub; nobody, not even a saint, could have kept it moving very long alone.  And nobody asleep after forty years of doing her four turns every night could quite let go. 

Behind Nell's closed lids the whole factory ghost shift assembled, not in white gowns but in aprons bleached nearly colorless at the belly where the women leaned into things.  While the dinners went on upstairs in the parish hall, those women had dished up the peas and washed the platters.  Nell opened her eyes to see four beads creep uphill along their wire and come to rest against the hub. 

She took off the headphones and wrote on the appraisal form, under Value, that the iron was worth perhaps nine dollars and the wheel should on no account be separated from the voices.  Then she turned the form over and ruled sixteen lines on the back.  Her father had kept the payroll before he became a holy man; he could at least give them their names.  She slid the pencil across, and in the hat at his feet another penny of cold water fell.
