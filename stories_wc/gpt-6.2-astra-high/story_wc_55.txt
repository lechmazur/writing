In the hospice under the glacier, a slate hung at the foot of every bed, marked with the hour of the night watch's last visit.  If you woke alone at three, it was something to know that at a quarter to three somebody had stood beside you.  For Marta, the clock scribe, it was a way of keeping people company without imposing on them the necessity of talk.  She had been making those rounds for thirty years when she found one morning that her mother had rubbed out the time.  In its place she had drawn two narrow shoes, heel to heel. 

Marta shut the door with her hip and stood looking at the drawing until her mother turned it upside down and made the shoes dance on their toes.  At seventy, Ada still knew how to get a laugh out of a bad house.  She had been a dancer on the lake boats, which carried a piano and a square of maple flooring even when there was hardly room for the mail.  Marta had grown up under that piano, breathing the good wet smell of the passengers' boots and watching her mother's feet make extraordinary things look possible.  She had wanted nothing so much as to dance the crossing with Ada, one on either side of the pitching seam between the boards. 

But at their first rehearsal with passengers aboard, the semicircle of expectant faces had closed around her like a fist.  In her hurry to get ashore she had taken both pairs of shoes, and Ada had danced the return crossing in borrowed pumps that drew blood.  They could laugh about the pumps now, though never about what had happened before or after. 

Beyond the door the soundless sanctuary was beginning to lighten, not with sun but with the pale reflection of snow on the mountain opposite.  Rain had passed in the night, and through the open window Marta could hear the crystal forecourt whispering as water slipped between its plates.  It was the one thing you could hear distinctly up here in the glacial hush, and only after rain; it sounded remarkably like skirts brushing a maple floor.  Ada put one bare foot out of bed. 

Not the boats, Marta thought, not that whole business to live through again, and then she saw how carefully her mother was studying the distance to the floor.  She knelt and fitted the swollen feet into her own felt slippers.  Ada had scarcely enough wind left to cross the room, but she had kept the muscles in her arms, and between them they contrived to reach the forecourt. 

There were faces at three of the windows already.  Marta felt the old constriction, as intimate and unwelcome as a hand inside her dress.  She was about to seat Ada on the invalids' bench and step behind it when her mother tapped out on her wrist the four quick beats that gave the other dancer the lead.  All those years she had remembered Ada as the one who kept dancing and herself as the one who failed to follow.  She had forgotten that there came a point in every crossing when the younger dancer was supposed to bear the other's weight.  She slid one arm around her mother's ribs and felt the little cage working away under her palm. 

In the hush of a hidden sunrise, with pink going up the snowy wall across the valley, they began their crossing.  At first Marta was excruciatingly gentle, setting Ada down at each step as if she were a glass to be put away on a shelf.  Then Ada pinched her, quite hard, and she remembered that her mother had always liked a little speed on the turns.  She let the wet plates take her sliding weight and swung them both about, not prettily, but fast enough to fetch that old smell of mud and wool and morning out of the folds of their clothes. 

One turn was all they had in them.  Ada settled on the bench with her head against her daughter's hip, and Marta stood where the watchers could see her, not wanting an audience, not needing it to go away.  Later, when she had got her mother back to bed, she took up the chalk to mark the hour and found she had quite forgotten to look.  She left the shoes.
