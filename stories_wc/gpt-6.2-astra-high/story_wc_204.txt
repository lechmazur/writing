At the glass orchard library, which floated upon a swirling sea of clouds, we lent summers to people who had missed them through illness.  A summer took three minutes to read and left freckles.  This was some years after reality became negotiable, when the price of changing a fact was still another fact of equal weight.  For each summer that ripened on a branch, one of us had to give up a summer of our own. 

My father, the letterpress operator who printed our loan slips, could no longer remember me between the ages of six and nine.  When I asked if he minded the loss, he answered, as usual, with a question.  "Which teeth did you lose first?" 

I understood him better once I had a child of my own and learned how little of love consists of the big occasions.  My son lived eleven days, long enough to develop a preference for sleeping with his left fist tucked under his chin.  After we buried him I brought my father a commission: I wanted him to compress eternity into a moment, the last one my boy had known.  I would go into it and hold him, and if the price was all the time remaining to me, that seemed an even exchange. 

Into the sample socket above the press I screwed a bottled whisper, the goodnight I had breathed into the warm cup of his ear.  Father put his hand on the press lever. 

"Whose eternity?" 

I thought he was asking whether I knew what I stood to lose, and told him to go ahead.  He took his apron off and hung it on its nail, and I, who had watched him work since I could stand, set the type myself.  The press accepted my thumbprint and delivered a slip so heavy I had to drag it to the nursery shelves.  At its touch a clear pear filled with the cloudy blue of my son's eyelids.  I bit down and had him in my arms. 

For a while I did nothing but feel the little hinge of his jaw working against the heel of my hand.  Then came a noise like somebody looking through a drawer for a letter that used to be there.  It came again, and again, until I could distinguish in that rustling the awakenings of other people in the room.  I could not see them, but my father had taught me to read patterns in morning frost: every breath that touched cold glass left a fern pointing back toward its source.  On the skin of the pear above our heads I saw a whole white forest growing inward. 

A girl was waking to her first summer without crutches; an old man was waking beside his brother in the tent they had made from a tablecloth.  They would go on waking and never get as far as putting a foot to the floor.  To back my promise of forever, the press had seized every minute in the orchard, including the minutes already out on loan.  My father had not been asking a riddle. 

But the girl was getting angry, and each time she woke her breath came a little hotter against the glass.  At the crossing of two fern tips a bead of water formed.  I shifted my boy a little so I could put my mouth there, where the girl was breathing on the other side.  The word that came out of the bottle and through my lips was goodnight, and for the first time since he died I meant it.  His eyelids parted; he had my father's pale lashes, something I had forgotten in the terrible business of keeping him alive. 

The rupture began as a pinhole through which I heard the girl put a perfectly solid heel down on a perfectly solid floor.  Then the pear split and I was on my knees among the nursery shelves with juice running out of my sleeves.  Of the walk out to bury him I still have the gate and the grave, but not the bit between; something had to pay for the minute I kept. 

Father was sitting under his apron, his bare arms wrapped around each other.  I put my head against his knee, and after a while he asked me again which tooth I had lost first.  This time I understood that he really wanted to know.  I told him how he had cut open the apple to find my tooth, and how I had slipped the tooth under my pillow and planted an apple seed beneath his, so neither of us would have to do without.
