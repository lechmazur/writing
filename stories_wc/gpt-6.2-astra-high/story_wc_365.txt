Every year on her birthday Len's father had picked her up and guessed her weight, even the year she turned thirty and he had to do it sitting down.  He had never been off by more than a pound.  It was one of the things she wanted to tell the woman whose child he had taken.  It was also one of the things she could not imagine that woman wanting to hear. 

Len made sensory maps for a living, which meant she could take a crew through parallel universes on the evidence of a burnt smell and a change in the pitch of a drill.  Now she lay down alone in the abandoned spaceport repair bay where he had found her, among the pale rectangles left by lathes unbolted and sold for scrap.  The crossings here were cyclically spontaneous, an engineer's way of saying you could set your watch by the hour but not lay bets on what would open.  On the stroke of three, a thousand versions of the bay began to stir in the murmur of half-remembered dreams. 

She put the green glass shard from her father's tobacco tin against her teeth.  It had come from the observation window he had smashed to get a toddler out of a burning nursery on the other side.  He had told that story often, always ending with the little flourish of opening both hands to show how empty they had been of anything resembling a plan.  Only on his last night had he told her about the woman who came back to the window a month later, calling a name that wasn't Len.  He had drawn the blind.  He had been lonely, he said, as if it were an illness you could die of, and perhaps it was. 

Len tapped the shard with a fingernail and let the note travel up through her jaw.  Ordinary ears would have heard a single ping; hers heard all the windows that might have been struck, singing back in sympathy except for the one with a piece missing.  She followed that hole in the music, weaving between worlds that smelled variously of hot rubber, ripe pears and the nursery disinfectant she remembered as the taste of somebody's thumb. 

There she opened her eyes.  The window had been bricked up, but beside it somebody had done a very sensible thing and installed a bell.  When she rang, a woman appeared from behind a lathe with a sandwich in one hand. 

"I come on my lunch break," the woman said, and swallowed with difficulty.  "They told me you'd be either three or ninety, if you came at all." 

Len had expected to have to prove herself; she had not expected to be the one who would need proof.  Then the woman set down her sandwich and wiped a smear of mustard off Len's chin that wasn't there, and Len knew the thumb. 

"Did he ever see me?" the woman asked. 

It would have been easy to redirect the question toward the rescue, the birthdays, the thirty years of his hands holding steady under whatever weight she had attained.  She had come to discover the weight of a withheld truth; she hadn't understood until now that somebody else had been carrying it. 

"He saw you," she said. 

Her mother pulled up a stool and sat down hard.  "All this time I've been wishing I'd called louder." 

The hour began to turn, and through her shoes Len felt the first tremor of the bay in which the lathes had all been sold.  She took a pencil from her pocket and drew a map on the bricks: a green note, a turn at pears, the sharp descent to the smell of bare concrete.  Then she put the glass in her mother's hand and closed the fingers over it. 

"This way it doesn't have to be me who decides when," she said. 

Her mother studied the map with the grave attention of a mechanic being shown where somebody else's repair had failed.  Then she broke her sandwich in two, not quite evenly, and gave Len the bigger half for the trip.
