After seeing my own grave I drove up to the mountain ranger cabin to find something fit to say about Len.  He had bought a double stone and had my half lettered too, because the mason charged mileage and Len hated paying twice for anything.  There was my speech for Saturday, if only our daughter could have borne it. 

What Nell wanted was the proposal on birch bark, the one she remembered us unrolling on the kitchen table when she was small.  I remembered the scroll but not the proposal, and at the funeral home I had been too much of a coward to make the distinction.  Len and I had met at the cabin and separated there, which ought to have cut the field of research down considerably. 

I let myself in with the key the district office had lent me for the afternoon.  The government green on the cupboards had survived six administrations and a grease fire.  By four I had emptied them all and found nothing more personal than a mouse drowned in a measuring cup.  I lay down on the bunk and set my watch to wake me before the last ferry. 

For twenty years I had been the university sleep lab's resident lucid dream chronicler, paid to go to sleep with a question and come back with a reasonably legible account of what happened to it.  You could distill chaos into narrative that way, though you could not, as I had repeatedly advised my employers, distill it into evidence.  Since Len died my dreams had been chronologically confused, so I used the mean little trick that had never yet failed to land me in a particular year.  I got jealous of my past selves until one of them became solid enough to follow. 

I wanted the woman at the kitchen table, but the one I envied most that afternoon was the one who got to leave him.  She had no idea what a luxury it was to slam a door on somebody and know he was still on the other side.  There she went down the switchbacks with a pillowcase full of underwear and our good pepper mill sticking out of her pocket.  Behind her the cabin shook itself free of its footings and came loping after her, trailing its stovepipe like a length of snapped chain.  I knew that beast, the feral nostalgia that takes up residence at a funeral and has to be fed exclusively on the best cuts.  Already the pillowcase was turning into a bridal veil. 

I let the hot updraft off the shale lift me and began to soar, an old professional pleasure quite undiminished by the knowledge that I was lying on a rubber mattress with a government inventory number.  From above I could see what the running cabin left exposed: four concrete piers and the little galvanized box where we kept the winter key. 

I woke before the buzzer, with the taste of the pepper mill's brass knob in my mouth, as if I had carried it in my teeth.  Outside, I got down in the mud and reached along the lip of the nearest pier.  It took two tries to find the right one.  The key was gone, but the birch bark scroll lay snug in its tobacco tin, held shut by an elastic that fell to black crumbs on my palm. 

In my hand, at the top, was a list of things I wanted brought down to the ferry, beginning with Nell's vaccination card.  In his hand below it was a question about the pepper mill, which had belonged to his mother.  Our separate pencils had gone back and forth across that strip of bark all summer, getting smaller and smaller as the space ran out.  Toward the bottom Len asked if the baby still laughed when she sneezed.  Underneath I had written that the ferry ran both ways. 

There wasn't a yes on it anywhere, but I could see how a child might have understood the thing better than her parents.  On Saturday I told them about the stone and the mileage, and Nell gave a sudden wet snort that would have pleased her father immensely.  Then I unrolled the bark and held one curling end while she held the other, just as she remembered, so we could read it without breaking it.
