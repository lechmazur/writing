At the meeting of the three rivers, anyone licensed to write fiction was also licensed to solemnize a marriage, since both trades required somebody willing to answer for an improbable future.  The advantage of hiring a novelist, rather than the cheaper sort of magistrate, was that a novelist could invite the dead.  They came through ephemeral cameo appearances in the wedding book, and only the couple and the writer ever saw them.  Everything happened inside the whisper of closing pages; by the time the covers met, you had your ordinary life in your hands again. 

When Ada's mother asked her to write the book for her wedding to Ruth, she put a spiral seashell fragment on the kitchen table.  Ada knew it by the nick that made it whistle crookedly when you blew across it.  Her father had used it to call the ferry when her mother kept the landing, before the three rivers took the roof off their house with him still on it.  "I want him there," her mother said, and Ruth, who had pulled forty people out of the water that night but not the forty-first, said nothing. 

Ada had become a novelist to collect the shapes of different hopes, the way other children went along the tide line collecting bits of colored glass.  She could see what her mother hoped for, and she thought she knew what Ruth wanted, but the thing she herself wanted shamed her.  She wanted her father to turn up late and wet and put a stop to the wedding simply by needing a dry shirt.  All week she wrote him coming in, and all week she tore the wet man out.  At last she wrote only the landing as she remembered it, down to the fare box with its little tongue of rust, and shut the shell between the leaves. 

They were married on the hump of gravel where the landing had stood, a feral nexus in mist, loud with invisible wings until the women began their vows.  Then even the birds seemed to wheel without beating the air, and in that swirling hush Ada opened the book and let the covers fall.  Ruth's grandmother was there long enough to spit on her thumb and rub a smut off Ruth's cheek; then she was gone.  The little brother Ada's mother had buried came barreling down the gangplank behind a hoop and was gone before it fell. 

Beyond them Ada saw her father in his Sunday shirt, perfectly dry, sitting on the bench where passengers waited to be taken across.  He was looking not at the bride who had been his wife but at the woman who had left him on the roof.  Ruth stood lucidly watchful, as Ada supposed she must have stood at the tiller, reading the awful arithmetic of a boat already loaded to the rim. 

"Don't make him forgive me," she said. 

"I didn't bring him for that," said Ada's mother. 

She held the shell out to him, and he blew the cracked note, and his wife's hand went up as automatically as it had every evening of their married life.  It meant she had heard, that somebody would come for him, that he needn't waste his breath blowing twice.  Ada had forgotten that he always blew twice anyway, just to see the hand go up again.  When he did it she laughed, and he was gone, and for once it wasn't the roof she saw when she tried to picture him. 

The covers met with a little clap, far too small a sound to have had so much inside it.  Her mother had tears on her good collar, and Ruth had a smudge on her cheek that the old woman's spit had only made worse.  Ada signed the book and, where it asked the fee, put down one passage against the current to the village where the three of them now lived.  She had come down alone in the morning rather than ride with them.  Going home she took her mother's oar and pulled with Ruth, learning the queer little hitch in the older woman's stroke until they made one sound instead of two.
