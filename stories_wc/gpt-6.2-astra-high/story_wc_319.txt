In the country where I was born, distance could be crossed by scent, and my mother was never farther away than the smell of wet wool.  Here I could smell her on my own back and walk for years without getting any closer.  That is what I mean when I say I was dimensionally homesick. 

The librarians called me their feral guardian and let me sleep under the stove in exchange for keeping the weather in the books.  It was honest work, especially in those years when every congregation that foresaw the end of the world wanted its bit of the world put away.  The librarians called their method interiorizing exteriors, but all it took was a lamp and somebody willing to walk the boundaries of its light.  Everything inside the circuit went into a book, down to the worm in the pear and the weather in which the pear was ripening. 

Nobody ever asked about getting things out until Ada came through the veil of sleep, trailing a yellow quilt that was still tucked in around her somewhere else.  She had a cultist's pendant between her teeth because, as she explained, her hands let go of things when she fell asleep.  I knew the little brass pear at once and led her to the book where her grandmother had banked a garden against the coming fire.  It opened to a frozen garden in mist; for thirty library years a drop had hung like a glass ear from the ear of a hare. 

"Did the world end, then?" I asked her. 

"Gran did," she said, "and the last tree she grafted outside the garden came down on our henhouse yesterday." 

What she wanted was a slip of wood with two good buds on it, to graft onto a common thorn and so keep their little melting pears in the world.  She had no use for my suggestion that she visit every night and look at the perfectly preserved trees. 

"You might as well look at my quilt if you get cold," she said. 

I put a paw inside the book and held a twig down while she bit it off, leaving her pendant swinging by its chain.  The mist came nosing along the twig after us, and behind the mist I heard the soft stampede of thirty winters wanting someplace to go.  Already there was frost on the quilt, running in yellow channels toward the bed where a child lay asleep with her mouth open.  I put my foot on the quilt and told her to spit out the twig, but she was looking at the way the frost stopped where my shadow fell. 

"Gran used the lamp to put things in," she said around the wood, "so why have you got it lit to take things out?" 

I had never once let the library go dark; I had been found by that lamp, a pup inside a ring of flattened grass.  As long as it burned I knew exactly where my mother had lost me, which was the last thing I knew for certain about her.  I took the brass pear in my teeth and cupped its hollow underside over the wick. 

Darkness is big enough to hold almost any quantity of weather, if you have the nerve to wait in it.  We heard the hare shake itself, then the quick patter of drops from all the trees at once, as though somebody had dropped a great sack of peas.  Ada's hand moved through the fur on my neck, and when I could no longer feel it I found I could still smell it.  Had the twig gone home with her, or had she woken with nothing in her mouth but the taste of bark? 

I set the pear down and put one foot after another into that small warm smell.  It was not my mother's, but it was going somewhere, and for once it pleased me not to know where until I got there. 

A hen pecked my nose just after sunrise.  Beyond the broken coop Ada was binding a dark twig to a white thorn with a strip torn from a yellow quilt.  She didn't look up to see if I would stay, and I didn't nose the buds to see if they were alive.  There would be breakfast first, and after that a day in which almost anything might grow.
