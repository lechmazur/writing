All the years I hunted the eel herder, I carried a tin spoon, because the last thing my mother had said to me was that we would eat together again.  It was a small promise to have survived the end of friction, when even the hills could not keep their soil.  Our village lived on lashed boats now, and wherever we put down a mooring pin, the seabed peeled away like custard under a skin.  Only the tidal bluff remained, one long tooth of bedrock with the aurora snagged on its tip. 

I saw her there at low water, placing painted rocks in mathematical sequences in the old votive niches: one blue, two white, three blue, five white.  By the time I hooked my elbows over the lowest ledge she had gone, but a little train of silver eels still hung in the air behind her.  They swam in a clear vein of seawater that climbed the bluff instead of falling from it.  When I was little I had wanted to understand the sacred patterns so badly that I had chipped a fleck of blue off a shrine.  My spoon had leaned toward that fleck for a week; now it pointed up the vein.  Whatever the saints were supposed to have painted with, Mother had used collapsed star dust, which could make even down a matter of opinion. 

With my spoon for a compass I followed her into a cleft scarcely wider than my hips.  Inside, under a pale green light that made it feel as though we had borrowed dawn from a kinder latitude, she was taking the little bells off her eels. 

"You came with something to eat from," she said without turning, and I knew the curse had not lifted.  She had always heard what people would say to her a minute before they said it; by the time their mouths moved she could hear only what came next.  I had forgotten the particular loneliness of being with somebody for whom you had already spoken. 

"I brought my spoon," I said, and laid it against her wrist so there would be one thing that happened to us at the same time. 

Beyond her shoulder the village was climbing too.  Two hundred hulls had been tethered to the bluff, and the rising sea was lifting them toward the flooded gate in a bunch like cherries on a string.  She meant to bring them through to the salt lake inside the bluff, where they could drift for a hundred years without being lost to the open sea.  She could harness the pull of the dust to lift a ribbon of water, but if she widened it every hull would come up together and stove boats would rain down on the lake. 

The eels were her sounding lines; I could see by their bright backs exactly where the current ran.  At the mouth of the gate it split, and there, on opposite walls, the last two niches stood empty.  The next number was eight, and she had only eight stones left.  Beyond one wall lay the lake; beyond the other lay the round pool where she had kept her eels as a girl.  Eight was not a command to put eight in one place; it was a sum to be divided between a way through and a place to wait. 

I tucked five into the niche above the pool and dropped the other three into my spoon, which she lashed to the head of her biggest eel.  At her whistle it shot across to the lake wall, towing a thin vein of water and the first boat with it.  Then back to the pool for another, a silver needle stitching a seam that would hold long after the thread was drawn out.  Five stayed to make the water circle; three swam close enough under each keel in turn to coax it out of the circle.  I called out whenever a boat came round with its bow toward the gate; a minute earlier, on the strength of my voice, she had sent the eel to meet it. 

By nightfall the spoon was mine again, full of the thin onion broth somebody on the last boat had managed not to spill.  She took the first mouthful and passed it back.  I had meant to ask if she would stay, but saw her face change at the question coming, and a minute later I let it come.  She nodded twice, once for the girl who was going to ask and once for the woman who did.
