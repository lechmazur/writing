After the GPS fails, the ferry sounds three long notes, and Ada hears a hundred spoons go quiet against a hundred bowls.  The soup supper is being held under her whale, sixty feet of driftwood ribs suspended in the storm-tossed archway of the old ferry hall.  On its chains the whale rolls a little, as if delighted to have found the sea again.  Beyond the glass the tidal turbines keep up their low contented note; they have no opinion about a ferry caught outside the reef. 

Her brother is bringing that ferry home, with the night shift aboard and his daughter asleep, if luck holds, in the dent his coat makes beside the wheel.  He can steer the cut blind once he's in it, but from outside, one piece of white water looks much like the next.  Already the harbor master is on the radio telling him to hold off, and already he is answering that he can't. 

"We used to have leading lights," says somebody behind Ada, in the dreamy voice people use for things they think are gone for good.  Ada has made a profession of fishing such things out of the tide.  She climbs onto the soup table, flings open the little door in the whale's belly, and takes down her grandfather's magic lantern.  Every supper ends with his pictures of the harbor, projected on a bedsheet between the whale's jaws; she could run the show in her sleep.  Tonight she picks out the magic lantern slide he never showed, the boring one of two white dots on a field of rinsed blue. 

At eight she asked him why he kept a picture of practically nothing.  At forty she is still learning to ask what the nothing is for.  She slips the glass into its slot, and there on the sheet, scratched into the blue, shine the outlines of the two stone piers, one tall, one squat.  At the tip of each, where a pin has lifted away the last skin of blue, light shines through.  Seen from the water, the piers must look like a single pillar when the boat is in the cut; the two lights would stand one above the other. 

"It's not a picture of the harbor," she tells the harbor master.  "It's a picture of where to be." 

The squat pier still carries a lamp, a red one now, put there to keep children from riding bicycles off the end.  The tall pier is under their feet.  Through the floorboards its stone carries the song of the turbines into the soles of Ada's shoes.  She finds the old lamp bracket where she remembers finding it as a girl, before she had a whale to hang between it and the sea.  She had thought it a hook for a fisherman's coat; probably it had been that too. 

While the harbor master fixes the emergency floodlight to the bracket, Ada gets her saw.  Where the beam ought to fall on the water, it prints a pale coin on wood.  Somebody offers to lower the whale, but they all know how long it took to winch her up.  Her grandfather's skiff is in those ribs, the silver strakes of it, wood he had bent under steam and she had learned to bend again after the water gave it back.  For a moment her hand lies flat against his work inside her work.  Then, with the cheerful obscenity he taught her for a knot that won't give, she cuts. 

The ribs come away one by one into the hands of people standing on the tables, who pass them down as carefully as bowls of hot soup.  "White over red," the harbor master says into the radio, and across the humming water her brother answers, "I can do that."  A long shed of lit windows slips between the teeth of the reef, turns broadside, becomes a boat again.  Nobody cheers until they hear the little girl complain over the open channel that somebody has sat on her breakfast. 

In the morning Ada lays the ribs out in the hall, prepared to reimagine the whale as something smaller.  Her niece takes her instead to the squat pier and makes her look back through the gap she cut.  From here the whale has opened her mouth, and in it, even by daylight, you can see a small white star.  Ada leaves the ribs out.  At the next supper it is the child who shows the boring slide, and not one spoon moves until she says which light goes where.
