After the procedure I woke on the jetty with a socket behind my ear and the flood edition of the Clarion folded under my head.  The socket was for the wire that would bring back one minute of sound; the newspaper was how you steered.  Beyond my bare toes the underground lake shone with disturbed plankton, as green as the light on a radio console when somebody is listening. 

I had come down for a clean slate, the phrase my lawyer used as if he kept a stack of them in a drawer.  All I needed was the mayor's voice telling me to cut the stadium feed that night, and the board would give me back my pension. 

On my way down I had passed the usual school party in the museum, lined up to admire a baseball signed by a time traveler.  It was the only ball fished out of the stadium after the flood, and under the mud, in my handwriting, were my name and my prison number.  People found it more interesting that I would someday make the journey than that forty stranded people had spent their last evening in a dugout built for nine.  At the end of the tour the children got to cut a little cardboard wire, just like wicked Miss Vale.  My granddaughter had brought hers home and asked me for the proper scissors.  She was too young to remember all the calls thanking the station for a little decent music on the worst night of the year. 

I waded in holding the paper open, reading between the lines as the nurse had taught me.  The black type stayed put, but in the narrow white gutters the lake light stirred up all the news that might have gone to press instead.  There was the ferry captain refusing to turn back, there was the announcer refusing to play music over the cries, there was myself on the courthouse steps with my wrists gloriously bare.  Underwater the same choices branched and doubled back in glowing lanes, a labyrinth so delicately robust that the ripples from my knees could scramble its pattern without breaking a single connection. 

I followed the white space under MAYOR DENIES ORDER until I could smell the peppermint he sucked to keep his radio voice sweet.  One step more and I would be in his booth.  Then from a neighboring lane came the whack of leather on leather, two quick catches and a long wait for a third.  I knew that interval; it was the dead air I had been trained to fill and had filled, that night, with a waltz.  I had always supposed they were tossing the ball to keep the children quiet.  For once I let the silence run. 

"You've got time, Eddie," a child said at last, in the patient, slightly bossy tone my granddaughter used to coax me up steps.  I stepped sideways into the dugout, where a boy was rolling the ball along a bench to an old man whose hands shook too badly to catch.  Nobody was keeping the children quiet; the children were keeping the old folks company, each according to some private treaty about what the other could bear.  Eddie stopped the ball against his hip and grinned as if he had stolen second. 

"Sign it, radio lady," he said, pushing it at me, and I found the pencil that always rode behind my other ear.  There was nothing I could do about the water; yesterday allowed a visitor only what room the facts had left.  I signed the way I had signed for everything for eleven years, and instead of going back to get the mayor I opened the socket to the room.  For one whole minute the town would have to listen to people who had not known they were going to be anybody's victims. 

I got no pension.  But when the board played my minute in the museum, my granddaughter recognized the tune Eddie whistled through the gaps in his teeth, and whistled the next bar into the hush.  An elderly woman across the aisle finished it for her. 

"He couldn't ever get that high part," she said. 

Afterward my granddaughter walked her home at the pace she usually reserved for me, and left her cardboard wire lying uncut on the seat.
