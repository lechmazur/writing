Until she lifted the crown above her brother's head, Ada had never understood why their mother rubbed her temples with both thumbs.  The silver pads inside the band were set exactly that far apart.  Beyond them, fine silver threads ran up into the crystal caves and off along the seams, one to each of the valley's seven shelter doors.  As doomsday bunker inspector, Ada had tested every thread with a tuning fork before coming home to crown the next Listener.  She had been proud to get a pure note back from every door. 

Now Kit put one hand up, palm out, and in the heartbeat of her hesitation the cultivated pearl set in the crown went bright.  Not bright enough to cast a shadow; just the latent radiance of milk in a jug when you open the cupboard at night.  Into that little light she fell. 

She knew how the pearl had been raised, in the oyster bed below the caves, where the valley folk took their unwanted wishes.  They etched their confessions inside a seashell's spiral, in letters too small for anybody but the writer to read, and broke the shell for seed.  Into one oyster the pearl wife tucked a pinch of those splinters, and over thirty years their edges grew smooth together.  In this fashion the pearl acquired the valley's abandoned hopes, and the Listener, in recompense for giving up her own, got to wear them. 

Now Ada stood barefoot on cold tiles in the body of the baker who had bolted Door Four against the yellow rain.  Through the thick glass he could see a woman on the other side, beating on it with the heel of her shoe.  He wanted to stay with her till she stopped, but he had children to get down the stairs, and anyway, somebody was listening. 

Next Ada lay in a little girl's bunk with a sheet drawn up over her nose, wanting to go and keep her mother company at the listening post.  She knew the sheet by the place where her own teeth had worried the hem to lace.  She had carved that confession at eleven, just before she left the valley to learn what it took to make a door hold. 

Last she sat at the post itself, silver screwed against bone, while somebody at Door Four tapped out the first five notes of a song.  Her mother's thumbs moved on the pads, tapping the five notes back.  Of course the threads went both ways; what had never occurred to Ada was that her mother might have something to say.  She waited inside her mother to discover what hope she had surrendered, and felt a longing so modest that it nearly broke her.  Not that the door should open and let death in among the bunks.  Only that another pair of thumbs might take the answering part when hers grew tired. 

The light contracted to the size of a pearl, and Kit's hand was still coming up.  She had taken that gesture for refusal, but he only wanted to steady the band for her, as he had steadied their mother's cup toward the end.  Behind him, on the benches cut into the crystal, the people of the valley waited for their inspector to certify that they were safe. 

Ada lowered the crown, not onto Kit's head but into his upturned hand, and asked him to hold one pad against his ear.  The other she held out to the baker, who was old now and had to turn his good ear to it.  With the handle of her tuning fork she tapped on the thread from Four the little phrase their mother had sent along it.  After the fifth note the old man took the fork from her and played the rest.  All along the benches people bent their heads, straining after a sound so slight that any one of them breathing hard could have put an end to it. 

On the certificate, in the space left for the Listener's name, Ada ruled seven narrow columns, one for each night of the week.  She put her own name down for the first night and passed the paper along.  Kit signed beside her, not beneath, and passed it on again. 

Kit sat beside her at the post that evening, each with a silver pad pressed lightly to one ear.  The band itself rested on the bench between them.  They could hear the wind at all seven doors, trying the handles, finding them good.
