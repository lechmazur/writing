In the city of forgotten fountains, a sentence of forgetting ran from the pause of migrating geese until the same stars stood overhead a year later.  That was what the judges had offered the women who brought their drowned children out of the millrace: one year in which it would be possible to eat.  Afterward the water was to come on again, and each woman could drink at her own fountain and have her child back in the only way still possible.  Nine autumns later, scarcely anybody remembered what the dry stone bowls at the street corners were for. 

But Nell still had the light in her hands.  It was all that remained of a little boy who had believed, with the absolute conviction of the very young, that his mother could see in the dark.  She could read by her knuckles; what she could not do was believe the judges when they said that nothing of value had been withheld. 

Every autumn, when the geese settled without a sound on the reservoir, Nell took the polished brass cover off the inspection hole in her street.  Far below, a trickle went on running.  There had been bread on the water the morning they found the children, and although she did not know why she did it, she always crumbled a heel into the hole.  The ninth time she did this, somebody under the street said, very distinctly, "Rye." 

Nell fetched a second loaf and a ladder.  Other clockmakers teased her for drawing the insides of things she had never seen, but she always drew them to scale and always left room for the clockmaker.  The floor was a confusion of crossing gutters; only by following breadcrumbs could she keep to the one that ran beneath her house.  Beside its outlet stood a stained glass box and an old man fishing out her offerings with a tea strainer. 

"You'll gum up the works," he whispered, and then she saw that the works had already been gummed up quite deliberately.  A clot of black pitch held the star wheel fast, one silver tooth short of opening the nine little gates along the bottom of the box.  Inside its blue and amber panes, the light from her hands took on the shape of a boy bent over a buttered heel, biting it into the shape of a boat. 

"They said if you remembered the children you'd remember who chained the mill doors," the man said.  "I told them I'd stay down here and see that nobody did." 

He held his strainer like a man prepared to defend a border, although the rim trembled. 

"Was one of them yours?" 

He nodded at a green pane that had gone almost clear where his thumb had rubbed it.  Nell understood then what sort of bargain a father might strike for the privilege of sitting beside a certain square of glass.  She put the fresh loaf on his knee. 

"You needn't drink," she said. 

Among clockmakers they called it sidereal patience: you did not chase a star with the instrument but made a place for it and let the earth turn under you.  It was the hardest part of the job to teach an apprentice, the business of keeping your hands off what you wanted to move.  She sat with her palms cupped around the pitch, which needed only warmth to unstick the wheel, and waited for him to decide whether to stop her.  Overhead she heard the watch going round with iron rods, sounding the covers to make sure they were in place.  His lips moved; she thought he was going to call out, but he was counting the teeth as they slipped free. 

"Let me do the last," he said. 

In the street the water rose without a splash through nine thick socks of moss, and women in their thin night clothes came to look.  One went back inside; another knelt and drank and stayed on her knees for some time, steadying herself on the stone.  Nell drank, and what came was not the terrible door but the serious business of fitting a parsley stalk for a mast, and her own impatience because they would be late.  The door would come too, she knew, and when it did there would be work for her hands in broad daylight.  Under the street the old man was eating rye bread, and above him, with scarcely a sound, the women began telling one another their children's names.
