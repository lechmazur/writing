My mother sold tickets at the optical illusion gallery for forty years, and when she retired she moved upstairs so she could still hear people gasp.  By the time I came home, she could tell from the pitch of a gasp which picture had caused it, but not whether I had come home yesterday. 

I was a fever dream architect, employed by hospitals to build small, navigable versions of the rooms that sick children kept finding themselves inside at night.  A staircase built by weaving patterns of dark cord across two white screens could be climbed by the eye and dismantled by a step to the left.  The children called such things realistically imaginary, a distinction of considerable importance to anyone trying to get to sleep.  My mother, who had taught me the trick, called it rational enchantment and charged a quarter. 

Every evening now she asked why I had stayed away so long, and every evening I made the mistake of producing evidence.  There were the two cups in the drainer, the peel of the apple we had shared, curling in one unbroken strip because she liked to watch me do it.  She always apologized with the same composure, as if I were a customer whose change she had counted wrong.  I thought that what she needed was evidence she had made herself. 

In the last room downstairs I put up two frames, one behind the other, and brought out the waxed sinew thread she had used to mend the gallery's original screens.  It was flat and faintly translucent and held a knot even in fingers that could no longer draw it tight.  Each afternoon we added a single length, passing it back and forth between the frames while the winter light withdrew across the floor.  Seen from the doorway, our crossings made a little house with a lighted window, the light being a patch of bare plaster that I had taken care to leave uncovered.  From anywhere else it looked like a drying rack after a flood. 

On the twentieth afternoon I showed her how to stand in the doorway and count the courses in the walls.  One for each day together, I told her, laying my chin on her shoulder so that we could see precisely the same house.  She looked for a long time before asking, very quietly, what I had done with the woman who had lived through those days.  I felt a shudder erupt in her jaw and travel through the bone until my own teeth touched. 

Until then I had supposed that as memories alter, a person simply moves into the space they leave, like water settling in a cup.  I had built my mother a place from which she could look in at her own absence.  I went to take it down, but she had already stepped between the frames, where there was just room for a woman of her narrow build.  She plucked a strand and the wall under my hand gave a little kick. 

"Again," she said, though it was she who had done it.  I answered on the strand she had plucked, and after a moment she answered on another, setting me to feel along the frame for her reply.  We stayed down there past closing, two grown women trying to discover the texture of connection in a room where neither could see the other's face.  Toward the end we got so good that she could make me laugh merely by letting the slack accumulate when I expected a tug. 

Next afternoon she asked why I had stayed away so long.  I said I was here now and could use a second pair of hands downstairs if she had the time.  She put on her old change apron, which had a pocket exactly deep enough for the spool.  Sometimes customers stopped in the doorway and waited for the two attendants to get out of the way of the house.  My mother would tell them, with her old authority, that the view was considerably better from inside.
