The day after the prophet arrives is moving day, so by the time he came into the murmuring pavilion most of the town had already packed.  Only Esta, the half-masked line walker, still had a rug spread out, with two cups on it and her mother's little brass kettle.  The prophet was a boy of perhaps thirteen, and he stood beside the second cup until she remembered to invite him to sit.  "I thought that might be her place," he said.  Most people said her mother was still with her, and proceeded to sit wherever they liked; she found the boy strangely honest. 

He could see where the buried river meant to surface next; at dawn he would take them there.  Tonight his business was to consecrate one sound that the town could carry into its next settlement, intact as a coal bedded in ash.  Esta wanted the judge's last ruling, the one that had given every household a door that locked, including households that owed rent.  In three settlements now, landlords had tried to take the doors off when people fell behind.  Nobody could remember her mother's exact words, only that the landlords themselves had gone the next morning with pots of grease to ease the hinges. 

Esta took the boy outside, where the guy ropes of the pavilion ran on over the sand to stakes nobody living had driven.  She set one slipper on a rope and took his hand.  Beneath the dunes a kinetic parapet, built to keep sand out of a city long since ground to powder, still shifted its weight in answer to a footfall on the line.  As a girl she had learned to steer it through peripheral awareness, her mask cutting off the downward view except for a silver thread beside her left cheek.  Look straight down and the body's small corrections multiplied until the buried wall heaved like a sleeper trying to get comfortable. 

On either side of them conjured clearings came and went, patches of swept floor where an old sound could be heard entire.  She passed the spit of grease from a pan and the first squall of a baby arriving, and stopped at the dry click of her mother's knitting needles.  To their left lay a square of blue tile, hardly larger than the rug, on which the judge had heard all cases.  Esta let go of the boy and opened the little kettle to catch the ruling. 

Instead there came the sound of a man trying to say something with his mouth full of sand.  She shifted her heel to run the minutes forward, but the boy touched her elbow.  "It's all right," he whispered, as if it were she who could not speak.  Between one click and the next a small voice said that when there was no door his father made him sleep in his trousers.  It was not a man at all.  He wanted to take his trousers off because they were his good ones and he had to wear them to school.  Somebody began to explain what he meant, and the needles stopped, and whoever it was fell quiet. 

Esta knew that voice too: she had been her mother's clerk and had considered it a kindness to find the words that people lacked.  She remembered writing PRIVACY in the ledger; she did not remember the trousers.  Out on the blue tile her mother put down the knitting and asked the child if he would mind telling the gentlemen himself.  There followed a silence quite long enough for a kettle to go off the boil.  Then several pairs of heavy boots shuffled closer, at the boy's request, so he would not have to raise his voice. 

"That's what I want," Esta said, and shut the lid before the ruling came. 

"Are you sure?" the prophet asked. 

"You can get people to write down a law," she said.  "I don't know how you get them to come closer." 

In the pavilion he breathed across the spout, and the brass took the sound the way a new reed takes a tune.  Years later, when Esta sat on the blue tiles they had laid in another place, she used the kettle for tea like anybody else.  Only when she tipped it did the boots move softly over the floor, and by the time both cups were full the person opposite generally had something to say.
