When my mother died I had four hundred portraits to choose from for her coffin lid, and not one contained so much as her thumb.  She had been our village photographer for fifty years and knew exactly where the edges of a picture were.  The carpenter offered to carve a camera instead, which struck me as much like burying a plow and telling the children it was their grandfather. 

On the evening before the funeral I carried her tuning fork over the hush of winter-cracked fields to the orchard greenhouse.  Its panes were washed with silver on the inside, for the quinces that grew there needed endless night to ripen, and daylight spoiled an exposure.  Mother had planted a pip under each place where her tripod had stood, then transplanted the seedlings into long troughs of quicksilver.  Strike the fork against a trunk and the fruit would glow with whatever the pip remembered.  These resonant awakenings had paid for my schooling; people would pay twice what a wedding portrait cost to see the groom sneeze just before it. 

I started with my own wedding, reasoning that even a professional might set down her camera to kiss her daughter.  There I was with my mouth puckered, and there was the side of the camera, and between us slipped a stranger in an ashen veil.  She raised one gloved hand, and the quince went dark.  In the next tree she was a guest leaving a christening, and in the next a passerby at a cattle auction, quite ridiculously dressed for it.  I knew her then for the bride who was said to ghost the orchard whenever a customer asked for the impossible. 

"If this is about my mother," I said, "you might at least stand still long enough to tell me." 

I sounded two neighboring trunks together and caught her stepping out of one quince into another. 

"Oh, clever duck," she said in the lovingly venomous tones of an aunt extracting a splinter, "now suppose you try looking where I'm pointing." 

Her finger indicated a carved stone bead at her throat, a little pomegranate of black obsidian with one smooth cheek that took the light.  In its curve something white moved like a grub.  It was my mother's shirtsleeve. 

I ran down the aisle striking every trunk, and the bride passed from fruit to brightening fruit, turning her throat in each small scene so that the bead faced the lens. 

"Did she put you up to this?" I asked. 

"That vain creature?"  "She'd have had me reflect her good side." 

At the end of the row the bride paused inside a quince no bigger than a walnut, the first my mother had ever grown.  Behind her stood a boy in a burial suit, his hair still damp from the river that had taken him the day before their wedding. 

"Before your mother was your mother she painted miniatures," the bride said, "and before that she cut silhouettes, and in every life I sat for her and asked for him beside me." 

The boy put his hand into hers with the tentative delight of someone touching snow for the first time. 

"It took her three lifetimes to get his fingers right around mine, and you thought I'd let her go into the ground without a likeness?" 

I had brought a sensitized plate but nothing to enlarge that pinprick of reflected light.  Then I remembered my mother making mural prints by shining light backward through the bellows of her camera.  I fetched it, sounded the wedding tree once more, and fitted the bellows over the quince with the lens pointed at my waiting plate. 

What came up in the developing salts was not a face composed for posterity but a woman rushing in for a kiss, one eye shut against the brim of my hat.  There was even the notch in her front tooth where she used to snap the threads for my buttons. 

On the coffin lid next morning she looked absurdly likely to lift it, and the carpenter and I had to sit down on the shavings until we could stop laughing.  When we stood up he had two clean tracks through the sawdust on his cheeks, and so, he told me, had I.
