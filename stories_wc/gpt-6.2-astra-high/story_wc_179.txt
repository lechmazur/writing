Whenever the river changed its mind about where the town had been built, the sanctuary reappeared wherever somebody needed a door.  This time it came up in the courthouse, neatly dividing the bench from the dock, which was almost exactly where I had meant it to come.  I had filed a false petition to get Judge Venn out of his hilltop house and into the flood district before the change.  My sister wanted him dead; being a pacifist with few scruples of any other kind, I had settled for extortion.  All I wanted was his signature on a stay against selling our houses as abandoned every time the river put us on its other bank. 

Through the courthouse windows you could already see two sets of roofs laid over one another like badly shuffled cards.  Venn took the paper I offered him, read it, and reached for his pen as his carpet turned to reeds.  Then the sanctuary folded its door shut between us, leaving him outside. 

Inside, not even the hammering of his fist made a sound.  The walls were made of skin donated by generations of river people, who had wanted one place where a frightened child could sleep through anything.  I laid my palm against the door and felt my mother's resentment come up in welts between my fingers.  Venn had sold our house with her still in it, too sick to make the crossing that would have proved her legally alive.  I had expected to have to convince a judge, not a dead woman. 

Through the thin place that had been her wrist I saw him hold the signed stay against the door.  His mouth formed the word please, a courtesy he had not extended to us when he ordered her bed carried into the street.  The door grew thick enough to blot him out. 

The sanctuary knew things through cellular memory, the way a graft of apple wood remembers the precise tartness of its fruit.  There was no arguing with it except by giving it something else equally true to remember.  From my pocket I took the music box I had brought to swear on, our family having owned nothing else continuously through seven changes of address.  It had lived under Mother's pillow even on the pavement.  One pin on the brass cylinder had broken when the bailiffs set the bed down hard.  Ever afterward there had been a hitch in the waltz, just where one partner was supposed to let go and the other turn. 

I wound the spring and pressed the box to the door so that Mother's wrist could feel the teeth plucked and the one tooth skipped.  Nothing happened until I let the door take hold of my other hand.  Then I was back on the pavement trying to lift my mother, while she clung so hard to the iron bedstead I was afraid her fingers would snap.  What I had forgotten was who had wound the box that day: my sister, who couldn't lift anything yet but could still give us the count.  On the missing beat Mother had let go of the rail and put her arms around my neck. 

That small shifting grace, from iron to flesh, was all I wanted her resentment to learn again.  It needn't love the judge or forget the pavement; it only had to let a living body through. 

The wall thinned, and I saw that Venn was still holding the stay above water although he had nothing to hold on to himself.  The box came round to the broken pin again and a little space opened, barely the width of a sheet of paper.  He pushed the stay through, and I pushed it back and put my fingers through instead.  To take them he had to let the paper go.  The door gave with his weight and we went down together on the soft floor, his wet collar in my mouth. 

By morning the courthouse stood on our bank, the sanctuary was gone, and the ink on the stay was somewhere out at sea.  Venn asked for another sheet before I could think of anything to threaten him with.  My sister came to witness his signature, bringing a dry shirt which she handed to me rather than to him.  I held it open and he put his arms through.
