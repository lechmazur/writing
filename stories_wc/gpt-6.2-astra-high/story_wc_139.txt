On the day I laid my father out, I brought his creditors into the prep room one at a time, the way other families came in to identify their dead.  I had promised them sight of the body, since at least one of his previous deaths had been reported quite convincingly in the local paper.  Nobody had any trouble identifying Sidney Bell.  Even with his mouth stitched shut he looked about to offer somebody a chair that belonged to somebody else. 

On the instrument trolley stood the last of his portable property, a vintage ink pot shaped like a crouching lion, with a hinged mane and two green glass eyes.  It had once graced the treaty room of the old republic, a fact my father could prove and the point at which his dealings with the truth generally ceased.  He had set it on a desk beneath our chapel and charged exiles for certificates stating that their prison records had been examined and authenticated by a consul.  Since the coup there had been no consul.  When people asked me about the lion I always gave them its exact provenance, down to the auction lot.  I was known as Sidney's honest child, and until the first letter from an immigration judge arrived, I believed a true answer had to be an honest one. 

An antiques dealer had offered six hundred for the pot; my list showed twelve people who had paid fifty apiece.  I thought I had come upon the mathematics of a fair trade, and it was almost a pleasure to draw twelve little boxes on a sheet of chapel stationery.  The first eleven needed money and signed beside their boxes for a postal order to follow.  The twelfth, a woman named Adela, put both hands on the steel table as if to keep my father from rolling away.  Her prison record was genuine, but the judge hearing her claim for political asylum wanted to know why, in that case, she had bought a forgery to support it.  She could be sent back to the men whose signatures appeared on the genuine article. 

"What did you tell me about the lion?" she asked. 

I recited its history again, with the date and the lot number. 

"No, what did I ask you?" 

I had been peeling a pear for my father that afternoon; I remembered how the strip of green skin had swung between us while she waited for my answer.  She had asked if it was safe to give him the only paper she had carried out of prison. 

I pulled the stool out from under the sink for her.  In my trade you learn to wait with your hands occupied, so the person working up to speech need not also work at being watched.  I trimmed the loose threads at my father's cuffs and washed the pearly glue from one corner of his mouth.  Adela took a small tape recorder from her pocket and set it between his shoes.  Her lawyer wanted a statement, but she wanted me to hear the question first, the one I had answered without answering. 

"You understood me, didn't you?" 

There went the hard distinction I had lived by, between a lie and the careful rationing of truth; it ran like the glue under my wet thumb.  "I understood you," I said, and reached across my father's feet to press the red button so I could say it again where it might cost me something.  I gave the address of my business and described how the room beneath the chapel came to be available to a man who paid no rent.  Then I told her lawyer exactly what she had asked and exactly what I had said.  My father used to call it a breach of confidence when either of us supplied a detail the customer hadn't asked for.  I supplied one now: he always sent people upstairs to me when they had begun to doubt him. 

Adela stopped the tape.  She signed for her fifty just like everybody else; she would have to miss a shift at the cannery for the hearing.  Before she left she asked if he had suffered, and I said no, not because he deserved the courtesy of a lie but because he hadn't. 

Downstairs the dealer was waiting for his lion.  I carried it down with both hands, and when he had counted out the money I counted it again, having learned nothing against arithmetic except where it ends.
