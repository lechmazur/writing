The first cart glider was a freight barrow called Dot, who discovered that if she took the hump in the foundry floor fast enough, for half a second she belonged to nobody.  She spent that half second looking down at the handles where people's hands usually went.  An interesting vacancy, she thought. 

Dot lived in a gears-and-cogs workshop floating on an acid sea, where the penalty for skimping on a rivet was a sudden intimate acquaintance with chemistry.  Every machine aboard had a bell by which a human being could summon it.  Dot wanted to ring a bell that worked the other way.  Nobody had heard that bell yet, although the men occasionally claimed that a lathe had called them an ass. 

Her maker, Ida, had given Dot a yearning algorithm: whatever motion an operator began but could not finish, Dot was to try to complete.  That was why a slight lift of the handles sent her sailing over a sill, and why she had eventually taken to sailing for the hell of it.  Ida had since installed versions of the same mechanism in everything from the rivet press to the little foot pump by the privy.  Throughout the evolution of the device Dot remained steadily curious about what happened to an unfinished motion when the hands that began it were gone.  She had felt one in herself ever since Ida died. 

On the morning the yard was to be sold for scrap, she found the pump tapping at the boards long after the last man had buttoned his trousers.  She fetched Ida's kaleidoscope, the one with iron filings instead of beads.  Set against a gearbox, it made the faintest shiver visible six times over; Ida could tell a stripped tooth from a bent shaft through the images in that ridiculous jeweled cathedral.  Dot saw a black fern unfold, lose a leaf, and unfold again.  She had seen that fern in the kaleidoscope the day Ida, too ill to lift the handles, had tried to turn her toward the open door.  Not lift, then, but turn. 

Dot tried the other gearboxes before the breakers came with their hammers.  The press had a splendid stammering star that meant lift; the lathe drew the fine tight bud of hold.  She could transliterate those shapes into the notches Ida had used on brass instruction strips, if she could find something harder than brass to write with.  From the odds drawer she took a hunter's arrow tip of black flint, older than any metal thing afloat, still lashed to a finger's length of wood.  Ida had used it to scratch her name on every machine she made.  Working it between her handles, Dot cut three strips and fed each machine its own word. 

The lathe held its jaws shut, the press lifted its empty bed, and the pump wrenched itself sideways with a squeal.  Of course, thought Dot, with the particular disgust of a clever being who has just wasted a morning inventing a handle.  The pump could no more turn its own casing than she could take herself by the handles.  Those were not orders they wanted to obey. 

She backed up to the hump, charged it, and hit the suspended brass cover of the master drive with both wheels.  It sounded remarkably like the end of the world taking off its hat in church.  The breakers came running.  Dot laid the strips in the palm of their forewoman, who had served her time under Ida and could read an instruction strip with her thumb. 

The woman turned the pump as directed, and its whole working heart came loose in her hand, neatly as the meat of a walnut.  They had been going to smash the casting to get at that.  The press wanted its bed lifted clear; the lathe wanted its two halves held together while its spine was unbolted.  Each knew how to come apart alive. 

By evening the breakers had a deck full of working guts instead of so much brass by weight, and a distinct respect for the bell.  Every time it rang some enormous fellow laid down his hammer to ask a piece of plumbing its professional opinion.  Only Dot had no strip, but when the forewoman reached for her handles she tipped them ever so slightly toward the open door.  Outside was the loading ramp, the long gap over green water, the low deck of the salvage scow.  Ida had never got to see what happened after that half second.  They took it at a run, and for considerably more than half a second neither of them belonged to anybody.
