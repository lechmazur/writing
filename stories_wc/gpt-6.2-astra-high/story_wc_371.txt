After the birds flew south, Iona could hear the cups touching in the palace courtyard from halfway down the lane.  In summer the swifts had covered that small sound, as the creeper covered the breach in the wall where a person could turn sideways and slip through.  On the other side, under what remained of the royal laundry roof, her sister Nell kept a tea house with no sign and seven mismatched chairs. 

That morning there were six. 

Iona had a gift for noticing the little subtractions that people learned, almost at once, to call the way things were.  She still put out a hand for the rail on the laundry steps, sold for iron two winters ago.  Once she had earned her living making just such losses look natural on paper.  Where a family business no longer paid the palace ground rent, she had been instructed to enter a zero in the column headed Occupants.  The zero beside her own father's bakery had cost her an afternoon of looking out the office window before she could close the loop. 

Yesterday the masons had pulled the oven down; today Nell had carried the seventh chair to the gap to bar their way.  On the table lay the last loaf their father had lifted clear of the bricks.  He had gone to stay with a cousin who had room for him but none for his daughters.  Nell cut the loaf in half and held out the knife. 

"Bless it, then," she said. 

Since childhood Iona had tried to find in the ordinary things of a meal whatever it was her neighbors found in church.  She usually got closest washing up, feeling with a thumb for the smear of egg the dishcloth missed.  Now all she could think was that her father had turned this loaf once in the oven, because the right side always ran hot.  She said that, and Nell turned the bread over and looked, and for a minute they could eat together. 

Beyond the gap a mason hammered a notice into the mortar: clearance of unoccupied palace grounds would commence on Monday. 

By habit Iona began with the gentlest test she knew: what could they try that someone else could undo with one hand?  She took a trowel out to the lane and lifted a plug of earth from the strip the palace claimed on either side.  Into it she tucked a tuft of mint from the laundry doorstep.  Mint went into every pot Nell brewed, and half the town knew where to go when a baby's belly needed settling.  By dusk there was a green dotted line down the lane, stopping one plant short of the breach.  She would not uncloak the entrance without asking the woman who would have to stand in it. 

Nell went out with the chair and a mint root in a chipped blue cup.  She planted the root where the line broke off and sat in the chair where anyone passing could see her. 

On Sunday their customers came with spades instead of cups.  They did not storm the rent office or ask permission to dig up the hard verge in front of it.  They simply kept dividing the mint until the line ran all the way from the office door to the chair, each tuft watered from a kettle. 

When the masons followed it on Monday they found seven chairs in the lane and a queue of people holding cuttings.  On his sheet the foreman had a zero, which entitled him to begin without serving notice on anybody.  Iona recognized the figure, not as her own but as the work of the boy she had taught to take her place.  She handed him a cup and asked which of them he would like to put down as nobody.  With some irritation he wrote Occupied on his sheet, which gave them until the next rent day and not a minute longer. 

Nell wrapped the heel of the loaf for Iona to take on the train to their father.  "Don't tell him we saved anything," she said, brushing crumbs into her palm.  "Tell him we are still here." 

It was not quite forgiveness, but it was an errand Nell could have given to somebody else.  Iona took the parcel in both hands so as not to break the crust.
