Nell kept the town's puzzles, including three thousand crosswords and a marriage proposal in the form of a maze whose intended recipient had penciled in a shortcut.  Her professional gift was knowing when to leave the pencil marks alone. 

When the ribbon mill closed, Nell rescued forty years of panoramic picnic photographs from a skip with three inches of rain in the bottom.  They fitted the dove gray map case her mother used to carry between looms, full of patterns for ribbons wide enough to sash a mayor or bonnet a very ambitious baby.  Her mother had lingered a year after the closing, long enough to see the looms leave by lorry and not long enough to receive the pension she had been promised.  Nell wanted to make something so beautiful out of what was left that people would have to linger, too. 

At the old picnic ground, a lavender field tucked behind rusted factory ruins, she spread the dry prints on the trestles where the pension committee had lately been dispensing onions.  In each black-and-white photograph the workers were skipping, a mill tradition that had begun with some spoiled grosgrain and two foremen willing to make fools of themselves.  Arranged by year, the pictures showed a workforce growing thinner; arranged by the arcs of the ribbon, they did a queer and lovely thing.  Nell made little copies, and under her flicking thumb a girl jumped at nineteen and landed a grandmother. 

She borrowed a carpenter's shed in the field and built a wheel to flip the pictures past a window when you turned a crank.  As the bean vines climbed the shed, a green calendar ticking off the weeks until picnic day, she refined the movement until the ribbon never faltered.  Her mother flew forever in her good blouse with its scandalous second button undone. 

The first person Nell invited to look was Ada, who had stood next to her mother at the looms for thirty years.  Ada turned the crank twice and put her hand over the window.  "You cut my husband off." 

Nell, who could tell the difference between a silence that wanted filling and one that was still at work, sat quietly among the beans. 

"His boots," Ada finally said, and Nell unbuckled the case and unrolled the year in question.  At the far right, where Nell had trimmed the print to keep the jumping figure central, a man in his socks was turning the ribbon.  On the feet of the woman in midair were two enormous boots. 

"Your mam came straight from chapel in heels," Ada said.  She put one finger on the boots and laughed so hard the trestle shook, then sat holding it steady long after it had stopped. 

Nell looked through the other uncropped prints and saw the procession of people whose hands had kept her mother in the air.  It took her all night to admit that what she had considered the masterpiece was the clever bit in the middle.  By morning she had a question pinned to the shed door: Who was at the ends? 

People came carrying their own prints, because the official photographer had been no better than Nell about the edges.  One pensioner recognized his own rolled sleeves and was obliged to explain why he had been wearing his wife's blouse.  "Laundry," he maintained, against considerable testimony.  A woman who had stopped coming for onions brought a picture of her son turning the ribbon from his chair, and stayed to help shell beans.  Together they built two side wheels, each with its own crank, to show the turners in whatever fragments survived: a quick wrist, a supporting knee, a whole beloved face. 

On picnic day three people had to cooperate or the ribbon acquired a hump in the middle and somebody's grandmother appeared to be jumping a boa constrictor.  The pension had not come through, and they still needed the onions, but for once nobody had to invent an errand to spend an afternoon in company.  Ada beckoned two children to the handles and took the middle one herself.  When the big boots rose she let them hang a moment, and the children, watching her instead of the pictures, knew enough to wait.
