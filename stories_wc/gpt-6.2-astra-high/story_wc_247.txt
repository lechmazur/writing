When no one remembers you, the harbor gives you one night back, with the municipal band at your disposal.  I came up the ladder with my old plate camera knocking against my hip and a very particular piece of music in mind.  Behind the bandstand, the moon laid a white road across the water, and each incoming wave broke in a green fire that lit the undersides of the musicians' chins. 

The whispering advisor put his lips to my ear.  Every bandmaster had one, an old player who knew the repertory and could suggest something tactful when the dead asked for the impossible.  I signed that I didn't want Home Again, and he signed it back as a question, with the particular inland accent that makes a house look like a steeple.  I want my mother's sewing room, I told him.  Not the machines, but the gaps. 

Upstairs at the sail loft twelve women used to sit at twelve machines, six facing six, piecing together sails that would go to Spain without them.  They used sign language because half of them were deaf and none of them was foolish enough to compete with the clatter of twelve shuttles.  When somebody had news, first one machine would stop, then three, then perhaps the whole floor would go still while a woman with pins in her mouth delivered a devastating account of her husband's new mustache.  I had wanted all my life to translate those silences into music, but all I had ever managed was to photograph the women laughing. 

The advisor whispered into the bandmaster's ear, and the band struck up the Sailmakers' Hymn, which nobody in the loft could stand.  Over the years it had acquired an extra verse about the dignity of toil; my mother would have asked what the dignity paid per yard.  To disrupt a hymn in this town takes nerve even if you are already dead.  I climbed onto the bass drum and conducted a cut with both hands, and the startled cymbal player caught his brass against his belly. 

The advisor, a quietly charismatic little fellow, only had to start shifting chairs and everyone fell to helping him, including the bandmaster, who generally did not even carry his own stick.  I set them six facing six and opened the hood of my camera.  It was an antique even when I bought it, and there was no film inside, but on the ground glass the two rows shone small enough to hold in one hand.  That was how I had learned the shape of a joke as a girl too poor to waste exposures, by mapping silence on the glass with a greasy forefinger.  I showed the advisor the path my finger remembered, from the corner where my mother sat to the far end where her sister always got the news last.  Together we worked out the stops, his pencil marking on each player's part the measure where the joke reached her chair. 

For the running machines the bandmaster chose a quick figure out of the hymn itself, four notes suddenly light on their feet, like a parson caught dancing in his socks.  I stood in my mother's place and signed the story of my father's mustache, the ends of which had required sugar water and the protection of a little tent at night.  As the horns dropped out, people on the quay began turning to neighbors who knew sign language, and I could see the joke going out in rings beyond the reach of any camera.  At last there was just one oboe going stubbornly on, my aunt to the life, and when it stopped the silence came down whole. 

The oboist lowered his instrument and signed, as my aunt had signed a thousand times, What did I miss?  I had forgotten that part until he gave it back to me.  For a moment I had my elbows on her table again, sweeping the pins aside to make a clean place for my cheek.  It hurt to lift my head. 

Going down the ladder in the first gray light, I heard them take it from the top, but they had to begin twice because the man who played my aunt was still laughing.
