After the survey the numbers on our doors stopped telling the postman where we lived and started telling us how deep the water would be in our kitchens.  At the mill they called me the borrowed-time timekeeper, since the crew had each signed over an hour of severance to keep me on after the last delivery of wheat.  They wanted me to fetch the town time capsule from the emerald silo, whose green tiles had guided three generations of truckers home through the fog.  My father had put it there, and the crew believed he had kept something from each of their families. 

In the scale room I found his tobacco tin squashed flat under a fallen length of pipe.  Inside were little wads of gray pulp and one curved scrap of blue china that I could have picked out blindfolded.  It was a fragment of my brother's cremation urn, the one Dad had dropped on the funeral parlor steps and mended except for that thumb-sized bite.  For years afterward, whenever he took change out of his pocket, the blue bit came up too.  Now its broken lip felt as smooth as the glazed side.  All my working life I had entered hours to the quarter, and here was the first bit of time I had ever actually put my finger on.  I wanted to understand how that happened, how long a thing stayed sharp and whether you felt it change. 

I teased a wet slip open with my pencil until I could read six words in Dad's block capitals: ASK TO SEE THE OTHER ONE.  I knew the game, though I hadn't played since my brother died.  Every autumn Dad used to slip messages into the descriptions he wrote for the mill garage sale, little errands that kept us kids busy while our parents haggled.  "One left boot, hardly worn," meant you went looking for the right boot, and whoever had it generally had a nickel for you too. 

The crew had given me thirty-one hours; I spent the next six setting type for the clearance sale that would empty the mill.  "Blue china, incomplete," I wrote under the listing for the scale room, "owner wishes to see the other piece."  I set the type upside down, as he had for the hard clues, and waited to see which of us children would rediscover the knack. 

Instead of a child it was my mother who came through the door with the urn in her shopping bag.  I had packed it in newspaper myself, and I started to tell her it wasn't what I was asking for.  She took my hand and ran my thumb around the bite, which was every bit as smooth as the rim of a teacup.  "He had what fell out," she said, "and I had what stayed home." 

She knew what had been on the slips; for every tool in the mill Dad had asked its operator to name the place at home where the day's work showed.  The hand that couldn't close around a beer glass, the cuffs that came out of the wash permanently white with flour: you needed both halves to get the whole shift down.  His own job all those years, besides weighing wheat, had been getting through the day without a boy who used to bring him lunch. 

With the big clock already sold, the scale room was suddenly timeless; nobody punched out and nobody sent my mother home.  We went down the sale list together, embedding a question in each description, and by Sunday the green tiles were sending people in our direction again.  "Platform scales, accurate to an ounce; what did you carry home, Ed?"  Ed brought in his wedding ring, sawn through because the knuckle he'd smashed under the platform kept growing bigger even after he retired.  His wife slipped it on her own finger and showed us how she pinched the gap shut.  I entered that in my ledger, where once I would have put an eight. 

By the time the county truck arrived we had filled eleven pages, and my mother was winding the urn in newspaper for the journey up the hill.  I offered her the chip to put inside, but she pushed it back across the desk.  On the road out of town I kept finding it among my change, and each time I took a little longer to let it go.
