At twilight the engineers came down to the exile's cottage for the chessboard, not for her.  They wanted something flat on which to sign the order drowning Saint Orra, and every table in the relay house was jumping.  The house stood at the magnetic nexus of three ore seams; whenever the tide turned the dynamos, even the spoons stood up to dance.  Ida took the board there herself, with a man's felt slipper tied under each corner. 

She had been a thoughtfully precise chess tutor, which was how she had come to be an exiled one.  When the governor published his daughter's championship games, Ida had corrected one small error in the proofs: the name of the winner.  It was a pump boy from Saint Orra who had beaten the girl, and the girl herself had tipped her king and shaken hands.  Only the adults had found the outcome difficult.  That had been Ida's reason for teaching in the first place, to discover beauty in the difficult, though she had never expected to pay quite so much rent for the privilege. 

Through the relay window she could see the pump lamp blinking across the estuary, eight white flashes and a rest, the old assurance that the pumps were running.  At the next phase of the tide the engineers would open the sea gates and give the river back its bed, which included the streets of Saint Orra.  All afternoon the last train had stood on the siding here, its green carriages empty.  According to the governor's order, this meant there was no one left to bring across.  According to the engine driver, it meant there had been no order to go. 

Ida put her hand in her pocket and settled her thumb in the dent of her polished basalt worry stone.  It had been the pump boy's black pawn, one of sixteen beach pebbles he had chipped so his blind father could tell the armies apart.  She had kept it after the match; in exile she had worn away everything that made it a pawn except the little hollow.  Now she pressed it into the fourth square of the first rank, where a flash had failed to come.  In the following group the sixth was missing.  She set her finger on the sixth rank and looked at the square where stone and finger met.  Then she asked for the survey map. 

She had taught the boy by lamplight between their windows when the fever closed the school, each learning to read the other's moves by translating silence.  All he could do from the pump room was pass his hand across the beam; all she could do at home was shutter a candle.  It took the driver to correlate the squares with the survey grid, since the boy was looking upriver and they were looking down.  Three times he sent the square for the schoolhouse, and then, with beautiful economy, the square for the siding. 

The chief engineer looked at the unsigned order, then at the eclipse carved into the board's rim, a black disk between two open hands.  Every school in the estuary had owned such a board once, a gift to mark the day both armies stopped firing to watch the sun go out and somehow let the barges of children through.  The terms of that eclipsed covenant were cut into the rim in letters so small the engineer had to bend down to read them: Safe passage, either way.  Ida turned the board over to the blank side.  On it the engineer wrote out an order for the train to cross before the next phase, and signed his own good job away. 

By moonrise there were forty children in the relay house, taking turns letting go of spoons, and an old blind man feeling over the chessboard for a game.  His son came last, smelling of hot lamp oil, with thirty-one beach pebbles in a flour sack slung over his heart.  So he too had kept a place for her.  Across the water a whole street of windows caught the moon before the sea went over them.  Ida gave the old man his choice of colors, and when he chose black she set her thumb for the last time in the shallow dent and let him have the stone.
