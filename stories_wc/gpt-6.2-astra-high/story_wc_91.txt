On the black sand beach below Mount Ivo, where red stones occasionally came skipping down to hiss in the surf, my aunt sold futures for a penny.  She had once owned the ferry and the three canneries whose silver roofs now lay under thirty feet of cooling rock.  The ferry she had sold to buy the tents in which the cannery hands and their families slept.  She herself slept in the ticket booth, which could accommodate all of her except her feet. 

There was to be an eclipse, and people had crossed an ocean to spend four minutes in the dark on our particular strip of sand.  Aunt Lida proposed to separate these people from their money with the help of my bottled lightning bug collection.  All summer I had been trying to learn whether a bug felt different in the instant it went from being an ordinary beetle to being a light.  I wanted to understand the texture of that transformation; a scientist in a book would have told me its temperature, which was not the same thing.  Every jar had a muslin lid and a twig for the occupant to climb before I let it go. 

Lida tied a paper fortune around each neck and laid out a route from the ticket booth to the tents.  Each customer would carry a living lantern into the moon's shadow and release the light wherever the fortune said to stop.  "A handsome man will put silver in your hand" led to Paolo, who had hammered spoons out of the wreck of a refrigeration tank.  "You will keep company with royalty" meant our one remaining rooster, Prince, whose owner sold drawings of him looking insufferable.  Nobody orchestrates coincidences like a woman who knows what everybody has left to sell. 

She had made no stop at the tent of her old foreman, my father, although he could draw anything except a full breath and badly wanted work.  "He won't take anything from me," she said, and that was true ever since she had bought us our tent without asking. 

While the moon ate its long measured way into the sun, the volcano sent a runnel of sour yellow water across the path to Paolo's stall.  Nothing dangerous, but enough to stain the white shoes of our visitors, who stood there looking remarkably like people who had just discovered the limits of money.  I made them detour through our tent, holding the flaps apart so they could go out the back. 

The first man stopped in front of a charcoal sketch my father had made on the canvas itself, of my mother's hands splitting a fish.  In the whispering hush that came over the beach as the last bead of sun went out, I heard him say he knew those hands.  His mother had split cod in Norway and had lost the tip of the same finger.  He held his jar close, and the light swelled green along the charcoal knuckles and went away, and then another customer held up hers. 

"Could you do my hands?" the man asked, and somebody tore the blank back off a book of tide tables.  By the time the sun came back, Father had a dozen commissions, including one from the owner of the rooster, who wanted a family portrait.  He had also acquired an agent; Lida was at the flap taking names and telling people with fidgety children exactly how long they could go away for. 

Father paid her six silver pennies and closed her fingers on them when she would have opened them again.  The slip on the jar she was still holding promised silver from a handsome man, and she looked at her little brother as if inclined to dispute the adjective.  Then the rooster jumped onto his shoulder to sit for its portrait, fulfilling the other prophecy quite by accident, and she laughed until the tent shook. 

That night she paid us a penny for a bed long enough for her feet.  I lay beside her with the last bug walking on my wrist, and when it lit I felt only the little hooks that had been there all along.  The five pennies slipped from Lida's sleeping hand; I put my thumb in the warm hollows before they could disappear.
