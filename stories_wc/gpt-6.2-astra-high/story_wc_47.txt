After my confession went viral, people kept mailing me things for the miners' memorial garden.  They sent sunflower seeds, little brass suns, a photograph of a baby named Sol on account of what my father had supposedly said from the bottom of the mine.  Apparently you could watch a woman admit she had invented her father's last words and still want a piece of the invention.  I kept the parcels under the potting bench in the abandoned data center where I tended the moss garden. 

On the last night of the rescue I had been seventeen, old enough to sit at the field telephone and young enough to believe there had to be something I could give my little brother.  What I actually heard was a man trying to clear his throat.  What I told Kit was that Dad had said the sun was down there with him.  I put my amber pendant against Kit's eye and cupped my hands around his face so he could see how even the dark had gold in it.  By morning the sentence was on the radio.  I had finally told the truth because his daughter came home with a worksheet asking what her grandfather meant by the sun. 

Kit came to the garden the first Sunday of the thaw with his nine-year-old daughter and one of the brass plaques the town had screwed to every bench along Quarry Street.  He had got permission to take them down, he said, but not the money for new ones.  We stood where the server racks had stood, in the honeyed hush that fell over the moss beds when the sun came through the old yellow skylights.  His daughter Evie put the plaque down among the feathermoss, face up.  She had never seen our father but she knew his handwriting, or thought she did; the town had paid a designer to make the words look like script. 

I had always been a watchful dreamer, the kid who could spend a whole meal noticing a nick in a plate and furnishing it for a family of invisible people.  Now I was watching Evie run her finger over the curl on the S, already dreaming up something better to set in brass.  I could proffer a real sentence this time, one of his awful jokes, the name he had for the skin on boiled milk. 

Kit turned the plaque over.  "Can we have somewhere to miss him without anybody telling us how?" 

All winter the roof vents had stood open to give the moss its cold season, and icicles had grown like little organ pipes along their lips.  Where they dripped, the soft floor between the beds kept a pattern of small dark craters, each with a grain of white perlite at the bottom.  In the widest aisle the pattern made an almost perfect rectangle the size of our old kitchen table.  That night, after the two of them went home, I rolled the low steel sorting table into the aisle and lined its edges up with the dimples.  I set blank slate plant markers around its rim, each under a drip, with a tin of chalk in the dry middle.  Then I wrote the word TEST on one and stayed till the T lost its cap and looked like a little figure walking away. 

On Sunday I took the phone out of my apron and locked it in the seed safe before I let Kit and Evie in.  Evie wrote GRANDAD, watched the letters run and wrote it again in bigger letters, delighted to find herself on familiar terms with somebody dead.  Kit wrote with his shoulders hunched, and I went to water the haircaps instead of looking over them.  When he called me back there was nothing on his slate but the wet track a snail might leave. 

He asked if he could borrow the pendant.  I slipped it over my head, expecting him to cup his hands around Evie's face the way I once had around his.  Instead he held it to his own eye, and for a moment I was afraid he was going to ask me what Dad had really said.  He only wanted to know if he could keep it.  I did the clasp up for him, lifting the gray hair off his neck. 

The next time I walked down Quarry Street, every bench had two bright screw holes in it, and on one somebody had set a paper cup of daffodils.  Nobody had thought to fasten them down.
