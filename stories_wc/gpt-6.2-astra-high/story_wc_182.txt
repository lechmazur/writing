The time traveler arrived at the returns desk with a botanical diary and a claim ticket dated thirty years hence.  She was the girl who had gone down with the first rescue party that morning, now a woman with white roots in her black braid.  Her father knew the tiny scar at the parting, where his scissors had slipped when she was six.  "Don't send the second party," she told him. 

All around them the mountain bookstore was making room for the volunteers.  Balconies swung out from cliffs of dictionaries; ranks of reading lamps inclined their green heads as another stair unfolded through the floor.  The shop occupied the entire mountain, and its charter promised every customer an aisle of his own.  As its arcane linguist, Iven had spent his working life keeping that promise legible to stone. 

"Every time somebody comes to get us, the people beside us get farther away," his daughter said.  She had come back by buying a secondhand hour, the only copy of this morning she had found in thirty years.  Iven turned the sign to CLOSED and heard a hundred boots come reluctantly to rest. 

For years Iven had meant to find the Library of Unlearned Lessons, the department where the mountain kept proposed amendments to its charter.  When a boy vanished between Botany and Horticulture only yesterday he had resolved to renegotiate that generous, murderous clause.  Instead he had stayed at his desk translating instructions for the rescuers, and sent his daughter to deliver them. 

"Did you find the boy, Mina?" 

"I watched him grow a beard across a crack I couldn't get my hand through." 

She opened the diary.  It was the boy's, passed through that crack a sheet at a time, the petioles of pressed leaves serving as pins to stitch it back together.  He had been making a survey of the small blind plants that grew wherever two departments met.  On every sheet there were gaps in his neat columns of specimens, always at the same depth below the summit.  Iven took the transparent wrappers off seven unsold maps and traced one year's omissions on each, rather than risk marking the boy's originals.  Held against a lamp, the gaps described a level line that never moved, however wildly the neighboring aisles had wandered. 

"Nothing grows where no two things meet," Mina said. 

There was one department the mountain had never opened to a customer, because nobody had ever come to buy a lesson not yet learned.  They went down together in the dumbwaiter, counted off the fathoms with the knots on its rope, and stopped at a wall without a seam.  Through the stone Iven felt the slow, concerted lift of a thousand shelves waiting for his order.  His fist was raised to knock when Mina put her palm under it. 

"Do you want an aisle to yourself?" 

Instead of the knuckled imperative he taught new clerks, he set their joined hands against the stone in the old inflection for a question.  "What would you like in exchange for letting us walk together?" 

The wall rolled back on crowded pigeonholes, each holding a petition in the cramped script of a mountain obliged to write to itself.  Mina pulled out the oldest, and under her fingers the mineral ink pulsed like the throat of something too tired to sing. 

"An hour closed at noon," Iven read.  He thought of all the lunchtimes he had sat eating at his desk, taking the tremor under his cup for the reassuring evidence of trade.  "Two hours," he offered, "if you'll let people choose their company, starting with the ones downstairs." 

Against their palms came a pressure so light it might have been his daughter's pulse, except that overhead every lamp bowed.  Long before they saw the boy they felt his footsteps running up through the rope, with the heavier tread of the rescuers coming behind.  He was still a child, of course, and Mina had to get down on one knee to give him back his book.  His younger daughter stood behind the boy, touching the roots of her braid. 

Iven let the noon bell go unrung.  He brought three chairs to the returns desk and sat between his daughters while Mina told them what the boy had been like at twenty, at thirty, at forty.  Under their feet an entire mountain lay still to listen too.
