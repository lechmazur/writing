Six months after receiving the donor heart, I took a job washing cups at the floating rock tea house, where nobody knew me well enough to put a hand on my chest.  At home I had become a kind of municipal clock; even the butcher felt entitled to find out for himself that I was still going.  I missed the days when he used to lean over his scales and tell me who was buying cheap cuts for an expensive wedding. 

Up here there was Sella, a watchful dye master who rented the sunny end of the counter to match her skeins against customers' scraps.  I wanted to learn why some people magnetize stories, and I had never seen anybody draw them the way Sella did, sitting there doing nothing more attractive than wetting bits of wool with spit.  She found our wandering establishment each morning by tracking sun trails, the bright tracks our copper roof sent sliding across the valley mist.  Whichever ridge the light climbed, she would be waiting on its landing with her basket when our rock came softly grating in. 

One morning she brought a blue morpho wing between two slips of glass, and a girl in a black pinafore followed it aboard as intently as a cat follows cream.  "That's what I ordered," the girl said, "but you've only got enough for one ear." 

Sella tilted the glass until the blue went out and all we had was a shred of brown leaf.  She tilted it back, and a piece of sky opened on the counter.  The girl wanted twenty yards of ribbon that would do that, for the hats of the twenty women who sorted slate at the quarry.  From the countinghouse windows their hats must look as sober as the company required; across the sorting table each woman ought to see a blaze of kingfisher blue.  It was a year since the slip that killed the other shift, and by company order the survivors still wore mourning for the benefit of visitors. 

All this came out sidelong, with the iridescent caution of the wing itself; turn your full attention on the girl and all you got was a price per yard.  Sella went on licking wool.  I thought I had her secret then, and began polishing a perfectly clean cup.  But the girl shut her purse with a snap; we'd be coming round to the quarry landing soon, and nobody had told her whether the thing could be done.  I could see the high gray windows already, and below them the long shed with its low door where the sorters had to stoop. 

"Looking down is different from looking across," I said, and took off my apron.  That sounds obvious until you've spent half a year arranging a collar to hide a scar from people who are taller than you.  I pinned a twist of blue yarn under the brim of the girl's hat and climbed onto the counter to look.  Nothing but black straw, even when she lifted her chin.  Sella crouched where the woman opposite would sit, and the girl's smile was enough to illuminate the old dyer's face before the yarn came into view. 

Then the girl told us what her mother, who had died in the slip, used to say the first time she bent to go into that door each morning.  "Nice of them to build a place where we can all kiss our own arses goodbye." 

The laugh that came out of me was a quietly explosive thing, like a chestnut bursting in the ashes after everybody has gone to bed.  Nobody came running to see if I was all right. 

Sella cut the ribbon, charging for nineteen yards on account of the girl having done the design work, and we let her off at the low door.  When I asked Sella what she would have done if I hadn't taken off my apron, she said she'd have lost an order.  She hadn't been keeping a tactful silence at all; she simply hadn't known how to do the job, and had been willing to sit there not knowing. 

On our next circuit I looked down and saw twenty black hats moving along the sorting table.  I had to take it on trust about the blue, which was rather the point.  That evening I stopped at the butcher's and asked for something cheap that I could pass off as dear, because I had invited a lady to supper.  He drew me behind the bacon, where nobody else would hear what he was going to tell me.
