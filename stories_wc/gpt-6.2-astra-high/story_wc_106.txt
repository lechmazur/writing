At Emerald Station the setting sun developed portraits on the west windows, one for every passenger who had failed to come home.  The town owned these illuminated moments by ordinance and charged a penny to let a widow sit beside one until dark. 

Nell stole them. 

She was a quietly savvy thief, the sort who bought her ticket of admission and always asked for a receipt.  Nobody ever searched a woman with an expense to justify.  Under a fleeting golden sunset she could slide a sheet of black paper against a pane and lift the likeness whole, like the skin off cooling milk.  The pane went blank for good, but a family got somebody back to put above the clock.  On the back of each receipt she penciled the address where the portrait had gone. 

Orders came in flower arrangements left beneath the station clock, using the old porters' code from the days when an inspector might open a letter but hated getting pollen on his cuffs.  Stems gave the window number; a sprig of mint meant home, a twist of rosemary the burial ground. 

One September evening Nell found three carnations thrust through the handle of a little glass jar.  Three was her father's window, and the jar held neither mint nor rosemary but a cold light that lapped at the lid.  She knew a jar of captured moonbeams when she saw one; her father had been the last lampman on the night line.  Her mother stood by the ticket scales with a thumb wrapped in linen, where the silver rim of the moon net had bitten. 

"I'm not paying another penny to look at him," she said.  "But look at what he's holding before you take him down." 

In twenty years Nell had never looked much below the familiar place where one eyebrow broke like a pencil mark rubbed out too soon.  Against his lamp he held a fist of tiny white bells, with the cut stems sticking up.  Lilies of the valley meant passengers aboard; held upside down, they meant passengers waiting below. 

Below the station was the platform they had bricked up after the night line vanished, leaving only its iron door.  As a girl you went down on a dare, to press your ear against destiny's door and hear, they said, the name of the one you would marry.  Nell had heard nothing, but then she had been listening for a name. 

She pressed the black paper to the glass just as the last gold drained from the sill.  Instead of rolling her father up she carried him flat on both hands down the unlit steps, with her mother following close behind.  At the door she laid her cheek to the iron and heard a noise so familiar it was almost beneath hearing.  Scissors, she thought at first, and then her childhood taught her to reinterpret the sound: a ticket punch clipping its way through a queue. 

There was no handle on their side, only a round socket with three brass contacts crusted green.  Nell had spent enough evenings cleaning her father's lamp to know where it belonged.  She fitted the paper over the socket, lining up the pictured lamp with the little ring of tarnish. 

"I thought if we could give him a light," her mother began, but her hands were shaking too hard to unscrew the lid. 

Nell took the jar and tipped a trickle down the paper; the black drank deep, and behind the glass of the lamp a small moon rose.  Her father's eyes moved first, sliding sideways to see what idiot had filled his lamp without asking his leave.  Then his mouth made the particular O it always made when that idiot turned out to be his daughter. 

With a clack the iron folded back against the wall, and there stood Emerald Station by moonlight, the same green tiles but not a crack in any of them.  The passengers were standing in an orderly line with their pennies ready, unaware that anything beyond the usual delay had detained them.  Her father put out a hand to steady an elderly lady stepping across and found an elderly lady taking it from this side too. 

"Look at you," he said to his wife, with such astonished delight that Nell looked away. 

She had a sudden wish for a portrait of that instant, the two old hands and the one still young, each helping somebody somewhere.  But there were people coming through who would need to know which houses to go to, which clocks to look above.  She let the light fall where it would and went to fetch her book of receipts.
