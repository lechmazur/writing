The first breath Nell held underwater tasted of onions; Pip had eaten his supper over the bellows intake again.  The second hung in a leather bladder by her cheek, and between those breaths the listening tank allowed her one long visit to the dead.  Beyond its glass walls the gears and cogs of her floating workshop turned with a limp she could feel through her teeth.  Outside, the acid sea was eating the last of the copper pontoons. 

At dawn a barge would carry them inland, where Pip meant to go on introducing himself as the son of the man who had saved the workshop.  Nell had taught him to say it that way, with nothing about the six houses cut loose to accomplish the saving.  Now he was old enough to ask if the houses had agreed. 

The old ferrymen had kept no ledgers; they made their contracts with a hand on the iron saint bolted beside the tow winch.  Whatever the iron heard stayed in it, but when the tow parted the saint had fallen against the capstan and broken into pieces.  Nell had the pieces on her knees in a cradle of gold wire, and on her wrist the key ring charm Pip had lent her.  It was a little tin saint with a polished nose where Pip rubbed it to get to sleep.  Pip's father had cut it on the reducing lathe the winter before he died, faithfully copying what three centuries of acid spray had done to his patron. 

With the broken edges fretted away, Nell could only place the fragments by matching the pockmarks on the centuries-old statue to their tiny counterparts under her thumb.  Here was the cheek stippled like a strawberry; here, fitting no closer than the width of her little finger, was the curled lip of the ear.  An alchemist certain of her art might have filled that gap with iron and heard a father's voice go on speaking as if it had never stopped.  Nell had done it once with her mother's jawbone and got back, in her mother's exact impatient contralto, the words she herself had wanted to hear.  She had not been certain of much since. 

From the wire she coaxed a bead of gold and drew it shining through the water, along the empty place between cheek and ear.  Gold could carry a silence without making it speak.  That was why her teacher had made her mend bowls before he let her touch a bone. 

Through the cheek she heard Pip's father shouting that the houses were dragging the workshop down; through the ear came a sound like a spoon tapped on a cup.  She knew that sound from the years when she too had lived in a house on the tow and eaten with a hole in her table for a soup cup.  In a gale no shout would carry, so they struck their cups against the rail, one ring from each kitchen for permission to cut.  She set the chin in place and the rings came separately, each with a little trailing burr as a hand failed to damp the metal. 

Five kitchens answered. 

She tried three times to fit the last scrap of forehead where it would give her the sound of a sixth, but it belonged over the eye.  From there it gave her Pip's father saying, very quietly, "Bess, love, I have to," and the whine of the rope giving way strand by strand.  Bess was what he had called Nell's sister; to Pip she had always been the woman in the little oval frame above his bed.  She could give the boy five answers and a place to set his mother's silence without having to decide what it meant.  She let the last of the gold run there. 

The second breath tasted of onions too, and of blood where she had bitten her tongue.  When Pip hauled her up he had the slate ready, with his father's name at the top and space below for her to narrate the grand deed.  She turned it sideways and drew six squares, like the blocks of the quilt her sister had pieced from the worn aprons of those kitchens.  Into five of them she put a ring, and as she told him what the ringing meant he touched each circle once with his wet finger.  In the sixth he wrote his mother's name and after a while asked Nell what she had cooked.  That was how the story they took inland came to begin with a recipe for onion soup.
