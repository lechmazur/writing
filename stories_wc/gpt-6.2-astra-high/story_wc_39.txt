At dawn's earliest whistle, Ned Pike held a jar to the crack beneath his door and caught the sound of the inland train.  Once a vagrant penny poet who went wherever that train went, he now lived at the end of the buried line, in a feral loft beneath the dunes.  Beach grass stitched the roof boards together, and foxes had polished the banister with their bellies.  The only part of the station still above sand was the brass ball on the flagpole, a little sunrise even in rain. 

Ned was labeling his catch when a girl slid down the banister and set a copper on his table.  "My dad says you wrote him a letter from his mother and there wasn't a word of her in it."  "If that's what a penny buys, I'd like my money spent on something else." 

She had May Bell's trick of pressing the coin flat with one finger, as if even money might get up and leave.  May had stayed to nurse her bedridden father when the last children were sent inland, a journey everybody expected would last a winter.  The winter had lasted, in her case, the rest of her life. 

"She kept stopping to cry," Ned said, "and I thought I ought to sweeten it a bit." 

"You made her say she liked the quiet." 

He remembered being particularly pleased with that line.  The girl wanted one thing her father could believe, and she had heard what Ned could do with jars.  Capturing sounds in glass jars had been his livelihood before the railway went under; for sixpence a passenger could take away the sea.  All he had left were the spoiled takes, surf with somebody coughing over it, gulls interrupted by the clatter of cups. 

He showed her how to ease a lid sideways; a sound let out all at once would never go back in.  They listened until the light came thin and green through the grass roots and the girl, whose name was Esther, put her cold hands under her arms.  In three jars now they had heard the same few notes behind the breakers, followed by a noise like a boot sucked out of mud. 

"Dad does that with his cheek," Esther said, and made the noise exactly. 

Then Ned knew where to look.  In the old lost property cupboard, underneath a felt hat gone white with salt, they found the forgotten music box from the platform nursery.  May had minded children there while their mothers loaded trunks, and her boy had always got to wind the key.  Esther turned it; Ned put his ear to the wood to hear the little comb give up its tune one rusty tooth at a time.  The last tooth was missing, had always been missing, and into that expectant silence Esther blew the fat wet kiss her father had taught her.  It was so exactly May that Ned had to sit down on the stairs. 

"Now we want the morning after," he said, and pulled the bottom drawer out so far it landed on his feet.  He had gone on bottling breakers for the first week, saving even the ones so quiet you could hear the sand drink.  Here was one with the tune and no kiss after it, only a woman saying, almost merrily, "Come on, slowcoach," and then the kiss, twice.  She had played both parts.  Esther sat there holding the lid askew until the sound had gone around again, and this time she came in on cue. 

"Don't tell him she was sad," Ned said, then bit his tongue; he was at his old trade again.  Esther put the penny back in her pocket and wrapped the jar in her scarf. 

"Come and tell him which bit you made up," she said. 

Ned had once imagined himself the translator between generations, a grand fellow in a good coat finding words where other people failed.  It hadn't occurred to him that the job might consist chiefly of carrying the box and keeping his mouth shut until he was asked. 

They found her father waiting on the ridge where the new rails shone, his hands tucked up his sleeves exactly like his daughter's.  "She never said she liked the quiet," Ned told him. 

Esther eased the lid sideways, and when the woman called for her slowcoach, the man took his hands out of his sleeves to answer.  It carried a surprisingly long way, that ridiculous noise, over the shining flats where once the line had run.
