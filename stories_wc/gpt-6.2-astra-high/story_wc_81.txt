During the blackout, Mara took the evacuation map down from its hooks and carried it to the woman who had refused to sign its label.  The dedication was at dawn, power or no power; her father had already shaved the patch under his chin that he generally missed.  He had been a boy when he drew the map, and for sixty years people had thanked him for getting their families out.  The woman had written just six words across the proof: We were already on the hill. 

As the municipal indexer, Mara assigned pages not just subjects but logical relations: a warning was one thing, an account of a warning another.  Her father had given her that knack for distinctions, along with the habit of licking her thumb before turning a page.  Now he was asleep with his good shoes beside him, and all she wanted was to trace the map to an origin he could honestly stand beside. 

The woman, Nell, lived on a scow above the sunken city, where the old library clock still told the height of the tide by how many numerals showed.  Nobody remembered the library itself; they called the bit above water the clock rock.  Nell unwrapped the map and put a square brown thumbnail on the red cross by the ropewalk.  "Our house backed onto it," she said, and began to readjust the scow's moorings to bring its bow against the clock.  Moonlight through the numerals laid a broken ladder on the wall inside; they climbed through the service hatch into what had been the children's room. 

While the water was still rising, Nell said, the children had run messages between this high room and the camp on the hill.  She tugged a swollen drawer open and took out the librarian's antique ink stamp, a cross with one arm nicked.  By moonlight Mara recognized the nick in what the label called rescue stations: ropewalk, salt market, school, fish quay.  Inside the drawer were date slips from books, each stamped at the bottom when the book came back.  An alternative hypothesis came to her with something of the old schoolroom pleasure, before she remembered whose shoes were waiting.  Suppose the crosses had meant the same on the map as on the slips: not go here, but back safe.  She asked Nell what her father had done about a house where some came back and some did not. 

"Turn it over," said Nell. 

The linen backing had come loose at one corner; inside the fold of paper it concealed were four names in her father's small hand.  Beside Nell's brother's name he had penciled NOT AT ROPEWALK, and underneath, in ink this time, WITH AUNT LILY, SPOKE TO HIM MYSELF. 

"My mother was about to swim for him," said Nell.  "We had to sit on her till your dad got there with his piece of paper." 

Outside, where the electric pumps generally kept up their fretful knocking, water slipped in and out of the empty stacks.  In the reclaimed stillness Mara heard Nell's little catch of breath and understood that the old woman was still feeling her mother's weight buck under her.  She put away her pencil; the map could bear another minute without a label. 

When they got back up the hill her father was sitting in the museum doorway, one trouser cuff caught in his sock.  He listened without once attempting to put it right, though ordinarily such a thing would have bothered him. 

"It got so hard to tell them I hadn't been in a boat," he said at last. 

She knelt and freed the cuff, then handed him the proof with Nell's six words across it.  By candlelight he wrote below them: The children kept this register so nobody would go back into the water looking for somebody already found.  Nell signed first, holding the paper down for him when his turn came. 

The linen came away under Mara's patient thumb, taking scarcely a fiber of the old paper with it.  At dawn, with the lights still out, they propped the map between two panes in the east window so people could walk around it and read both sides.
