After the leash broke, Pip followed my daughter up the gangplank, and the ferry took them both.  From the stern Nell held up the small astonished dog, and I waved the brass clip as if this were the arrangement we had made.  On the farther shore she was to sing the opening verse at the vigil in twilight, when the net boats came home for the winter and the names of the missing were read.  Last year my husband's name had been the last one, and I had heard it through half a mile of rain.  This year I had promised Nell she would not have to look for me across the water.  By noon I had talked us both into believing that Pip would howl if I left him and spoil the singing if he came. 

The guides, Ruth and Ada, were sisters so old that each called the other Granny when she wanted to start something.  They kept a little canvas boat for people who missed the ferry or could not pay, and it was their custom never to distinguish between the two.  Ruth found me in the ticket shed, looking in the biscuit tin where the unclaimed returns were kept.  The ticket punch with its star stamp made a round hole for a crossing taken and a little raised star for a crossing paid for but not taken.  Four paper stars lay under my thumb, one for each time Nell had invited me over to hear her rehearse.  I suppose I had come to find where unused courage waits, as if somebody could keep it for you like the change from a fare. 

"Don't fancy sitting out there with that in your hand all evening," Ruth said, meaning the broken leash.  She took one ticket and left me the other three. 

To traverse the veiled ridgeway behind the shed we had to go single file between nets hung to dry, fine meshes silvered with scales that caught in our hair.  As a child I had loved going through there with my mouth open to taste the salt that drifted down.  Halfway along I caught myself doing it again.  Down in the gut behind the shingle Ada had her oars shipped and a folded coat on the middle thwart to keep my skirt dry. 

The sisters got their most reluctant passengers across through indirect routes, staying inside the arms of the old stone fish traps as long as the tide allowed.  Here the water hardly lifted us, and the little slap under the boards was like somebody patting a baby's back.  At the end of the last wall Ada rested her oars.  Beyond it lay the stretch where my husband's net boat had turned over within hailing distance of houses with their kitchen lights on. 

"I could hear the spoons hitting the bowls that night," I told them.  What I had never told Nell was that I had kept setting the table while the men outside were calling for a rope.  There had been hands enough without mine, but a person still had to live with the knowledge of what her hands had done. 

Ruth put one of the oars in my lap.  "You can take us back if you like," she said. 

From the far landing came a gently persistent whine and then above it my daughter's voice singing the verse she had been asked to sing alone.  She got as far as the word pillow, and Pip let out a howl so tuneful that I heard the whole assembly laugh.  I wanted the rest of that song unsmudged by distance.  Ada took the starboard oar and showed me how to follow her shoulder, and between us we pulled my ridiculous cargo of three spare crossings and half a leash into port. 

Nobody interrupted the reading of the names when we came in.  Nell just shifted on the bollard to leave room for me and passed me the dog, who settled against both our knees with one long sigh.  In the morning I would tell her about the spoons.  For now, when the last name had gone out over the dark water, there was the verse about the pillow to sing again, and this time anybody could join in.
