When they came to buy the Sea, the first thing the engineers asked me was where it ended.  I was a corridor echo cartographer, like my mother before me, and ought to have been able to put a chalk line around a sound.  Twice a day in our ivy-veiled passage, three hundred miles inland, you could shut your eyes and hear a long green breaker turn itself inside out on shingle.  After the climate shifts, children were born in our resettlement blocks who knew the sea only as something that happened between two brick walls. 

The engineers wanted to take the passage apart and install it in the new inland capital, where considerably more of those children lived.  My mother's name would go above it in brass, and my share would pay for the hip that kept me awake until dawn.  All they wanted was my signature on a drawing of what to take. 

My mother had taught me to hear the difference between a cupboard with a coat in it and one with a frightened child.  She had earned her living finding people in the dark, but she used to say she went to work to discover what walls dream about.  I took my quilt into the passage that night because I had never heard what ours did between performances.  At four I woke to a little hitch in the long withdrawing wash, the unmistakable hesitation of somebody changing hands on something heavy.  At four in the morning nobody had any business making the Sea.  I knocked once, and the water stopped. 

Most of what I knew of the first winter inland had come to me through half-burned diaries found in a fireplace when we cleared my mother's rooms.  I had skipped the accounts, which were what I wanted now.  Beside six months of entries for the public laundry, my mother had drawn a little ear.  A film negative strip folded into the binding showed, when I held it against the engineers' white drawing, a row of upturned faces I had always taken for spectators.  Now I could glimpse what their raised arms supported: one long shallow tray, slung on ropes like a scaffold for washing windows. 

The laundry had been divided into flats before I was born.  I went around the block and knocked on the basement door, and an old man let me in without asking what I wanted.  His kitchen ceiling was a sloping skin of copper, and in place of a table he had a lever worn to the color of butter.  He pulled it down and I heard a dry, quite paltry rattle overhead.  Brick did the rest. 

"Your mother wanted the little ones to sleep," he said.  "Mine thought of putting the beach stones in a laundry tray." 

He had been practicing with his left hand because his right thumb would no longer close.  I asked why he had never knocked on my door in all the years I had been accepting compliments and passing him on the stairs. 

"Well, I thought you knew," he said, which was harder to bear than an accusation. 

I showed him the drawing, and where the black line sheared his kitchen in half he put his good thumb down.  "I'd like the table back," he said. 

I had been ready for him to tell me we must save the passage, because that was what I would have said if anybody had thought to ask me yesterday.  Instead we spent the morning taking measurements, including the length of the lever that had been right for a young woman and wrong for her aging son.  The engineers bought our plans for rather less than they would have paid for the bricks, but enough for two operations if we shared a room.  He wanted his thumb for cabinetmaking, and I wanted to dance at least once on my new hip. 

In the capital the children work the tray themselves, six to a side, under a photograph of the women whose names we are still collecting.  Here the ivy has taken the passage entirely, and some evenings we sit at his new table with the kitchen door open and let the walls sleep.
