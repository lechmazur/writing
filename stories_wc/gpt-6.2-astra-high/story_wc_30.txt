After tomorrow disappeared, people still had to get up in the dark and gut the herring.  They could set out a clean knife before bed, but they could no longer say whose hand would close around it.  Down in the tidal atrium, where winter sunlight lay in long bars across the moving floor, Eda could see exactly what had been lost.  She was the titan-gear librarian, small enough to stand inside a brass letter on the rim of the wheel she tended.  Between the shelves marked YESTERDAY and TODAY there had always been a third shelf, just wide enough for a row of names laid out to meet their owners.  Now she could put her arm through the place where it had been and wet her fingers in the sea. 

The rate at which a self passes from body to body fluctuates with moon phases; in the thin weeks a potter might wake with nothing new but a liking for salt.  Under a full moon Eda had seen a whole marriage cross the street, the new wife going straight to the new husband's collar to turn it down.  The wheel did not cause these migrations; it printed the harbor roll so that a child could find the right lap in the evening.  Without the shelf for incoming names the great press had begun to print just the bodies, the one thing that stayed put. 

That morning, according to the roll, the man who brought Eda her dinner was still her father.  He set his fisherman's bamboo creel on the floor and stood looking at the lid, evidently in some distress.  "I know there ought to be something in there besides bread," he said.  From the queue at the wheel a girl with cropped hair called, "Feel underneath the hinge."  His fingers found a cockle shell on a thread, its two halves opening on a pearl of butter.  Her father had been packing her dinner for thirty years and had never put the butter in the same place twice.  The man who had carried it down to her was blinking back tears, for he too remembered packing it, although he had forgotten how. 

Eda divided the bread in three and let the sun melt the butter into it while they ate.  She had been trying to think how to build another shelf, as if she could bring back tomorrow by giving it somewhere to sit.  Yet here was a dinner no one person could have given her, and here were the two people who had. 

She asked to borrow the creel and hung it by its strap beneath the empty space.  As the tide lifted the wheel, little copper names that should have fallen into tomorrow fell tinkling into the basket instead; the water ran out between the strips of bamboo.  Eda took them round to TODAY, where the sockets were already full.  Her father, in the girl's clear voice, suggested taking a chisel to the old names.  Eda looked at the crumbs on the man's coat and shook her head. 

She kept a bin of green ore to smelt for replacement type, and a little charcoal furnace that drew its breath from the wheel.  While the ore cooked, she cut a forked channel in the casting sand, one foot to fit a socket and two heads to carry names.  By moonrise she had cast a dozen forks, and the three of them were setting the first double line of type.  Against the address of the girl they set her own name and the father's; against the father's address they left his name standing.  In all its years of keeping order the library had never done it by accepting contradictions before.  People stood reading in the blue dusk, some with fingers on two lines at once, until the lamps along the quay came on. 

Next morning the light returned to its usual place, inching up the wall as if nothing in the world had ever been lost.  The man brought bread wrapped in a napkin, since the basket was still on duty, and waited while his daughter looked for the butter.  This time it was in the little brass thimble he wore to mend his nets, and his surprise was as good as hers.
