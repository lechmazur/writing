By the time the last human dream was dreamt, the children of the estuary already had a word for the black between lying down and getting up: the rent.  It was what you paid for another day in your body.  Nell had paid it for seventy years and still felt cheated every morning. 

She was the odd historian employed by the reclaimed silo library, the one who went around collecting the stories walls had heard.  Other historians interviewed the oldest resident; Nell asked to see where he had slept, how far his bed had been from the next, whether a whisper would have carried.  She knew the difference between a silence that fell when somebody left and one that fell when somebody was listening. 

On the morning the fitters came to straighten the library's night wheel, she heard the second kind.  A small boy had his ear against the blistered door of the old infirmary, halfway up the silo wall.  He wanted to know why the brass label on it said SEA WINDOW when there was no window and the sea had taken his town.  Nell, who had written the label on the authority of an invoice for blue glass, fetched her screwdriver and took it off.  The boy followed her downstairs. 

The wheel was a hooded lantern on a turntable, left over from the years when the library had sheltered children from the drowned parishes.  In its beam two little brass dragons swam, exact copies of the beasts on a fragment of medieval tapestry the librarians had saved when the tide came into the diocesan museum.  Once a night the keeper used to wind the wheel and send those beasts swimming round the circular wall above the cots.  Tomorrow the custom would begin again for the newest arrivals. 

But the wheel had a displaced axis: its spindle stood a good yard off the center marked on the floor plan.  Set it right, said the head fitter, and every child would get a dragon of precisely the same size.  Nell nearly signed his work slip.  Then the boy asked what size the children behind the door would get. 

To probe that question she went through the half-burned diaries found in the fireplace when the infirmary was opened for repairs.  She had indexed them herself under Fuel, having taken the penciled columns of times and distances for a record of wood used and wood fetched.  This time she let the boy read across the ragged gutters while she held the leaves flat.  He made out a heading she had missed because it ran over two pages: How far inland tonight.  Beside the greatest distance somebody had drawn two horns and the square notch of a missing tooth. 

Nell took the tapestry down from its hook.  Between the jaws of the larger dragon a single stitch of silk had rotted away, and the brass cutter had faithfully copied the gap. 

They waited until dusk, the boy inside the infirmary, Nell at the spindle, the fitters standing with their tool bags in their hands.  The wheel unwound into a bronze quiet, one soft click coming along after another like the cooling of a bell.  As the lantern turned toward the far wall its dragons swelled until their bellies brushed the floor and their horns went over the infirmary door.  From the other side came a cry of disgust.  "All I get is a tooth!" 

Nell stopped the wheel and climbed up to look.  Through a blue transom black with dust except for one small pane, a tooth of light fell across the whitewashed wall behind the iron bed.  She put her thumb in its path and the boy put his fist there, and on the opposite wall a rock rose out of blue water and a seal hauled itself ashore.  Move the spindle back to its proper place and that traveling sea would never rise high enough to enter the room.  How far inland tonight, she thought, and understood what the keeper had been measuring as he tried the wheel in one place and then another. 

Downstairs the fitter was waiting for her signature.  She asked him instead to clean the high blue panes, and on the back of the slip began to draw a seal for a new label.  Then she heard the boy calling through the door to somebody on the stairs, telling them where to stand to make the tide come in.  She put the pencil away and went to fetch another blanket; there was room on that bed for two.
