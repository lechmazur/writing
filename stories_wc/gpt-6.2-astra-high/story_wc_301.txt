The week after she stopped the pain medication, Nell rode the river bus and for once stayed awake to pull the cord for her stop.  She got off at the disused railway station, where ivy had shouldered the clock face out of its iron collar and left it hanging like a button.  On her lap all the way down had been the accordion she had promised to bring back.  Her wrist could take its weight now; she could no longer put the journey off on that account. 

Nell was the county's river chronicler, paid by the library to keep a record of what the water gave and what it took.  There had been six households under the railway arches, all of them washed out of rented rooms by two winters of floods.  She had put their stories in her atlas without their names, and with the six precise map references that made all her work so useful.  The county land agent had thanked her in writing. 

Her wrist had gone under Bea's wardrobe when she tried to get it out ahead of the clearance crew.  As acts of courage went, it had been of considerable assistance to the wardrobe. 

From the platform came three notes on a mouth organ, a tune left hungry for its fourth.  Bea used to play those notes to call her boys in when the river began taking bites out of the mud.  Nell followed the sound into the ladies' room, the one place the crew had left alone because it had a roof full of asbestos.  Bea sat on the attendant's counter with her heels against the little cupboard where the good soap used to be locked away. 

She put the mouth organ in her pocket and reached for the accordion without getting down.  Nell had patched the bellows with tape where the wardrobe hinge had bitten through three pleats.  Bea eased them open; the tape made three little clicks, like a dog coming across a kitchen floor. 

"Does it hold air?" 

"Try it," said Nell, who wanted credit even for that. 

Bea played the fourth note and held it until Nell stopped waiting for praise. 

"I came to ask what I can print in the correction," Nell said.  She had hoped for a sentence of forgiveness that she could take home and use privately whenever she needed it. 

Bea looked past her at the wisdom accumulated on the tiles in everything from eyebrow pencil to tar.  Among the prices and propositions some wag had lettered a commandment in green: GIVE DIRECTIONS TO THE SUPPER, NEVER TO THE BED.  Bea had only to tip her chin to allude to the six references and the six naked rectangles of dirt along the river.  Nell read the line again, this time as a set of instructions. 

At the library she took her atlas apart, page by numbered page, while the director reminded her whose signature they both needed on their paychecks.  She left the stories whose tellers still wanted them told and pulled every sheet of coordinates, including the transparent overlays in the children's edition.  The map she drew instead gave the references for the county supper kitchen, the appeal office, and the records room where any citizen could ask to see the clearance order.  Against that last square she printed the land agent's letter of thanks and signed her full name underneath.  Then she went after the copies already out in the world. 

It took eleven weeks and one trip across the river in a poultry van to exchange the last one she could trace.  She lost the county salary but kept the habit of checking a thing against the ground.  On Thursdays she walked the supper route before dark, penciling in the gates that stuck and the steps where the concrete had gone to meal. 

One evening she heard three notes from inside the kitchen and stood stupidly waiting for the fourth.  When it came she could hear the little dry clicks behind it.  She went in and ate what was put in front of her, the first time in months she had taken a chair without first looking to see who thought she deserved it.
