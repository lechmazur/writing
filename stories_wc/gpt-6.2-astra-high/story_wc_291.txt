When the river took the police basement, it took the boxes for eleven cold cases, but it left Ada Venn's lie perfectly dry.  She could still go to the library and read what she had said at fourteen, under a picture of the shadowed quarry in mist: I saw Mr Bell take Lucy down the steps.  Nobody had ever printed what the sergeant asked her next, which was whether the man had his mask on or off.  Off, she had said, because it was off. 

Ada earned her living now painting portraits, a peculiar trade for somebody face blind from birth, though her sitters seldom noticed.  She worked from the collar out, dividing the head into squares with a thread grid, painting exactly what each square contained and never asking a nose to remind her of anybody.  People said she got the set of a mouth particularly well.  She knew what work went into holding one still. 

After the flood the library commissioned a traveling show about the missing, to go first to the places where they had last been seen.  For Lucy's panel Ada asked both Lucy's mother and Martin Bell's widow to sit, without telling either about the other.  She had an artist's clever notion of unifying opposites: two profiles cut from one sheet of black paper, each woman's face shaped by the space the other left.  She wanted people who had spent thirty years taking sides to feel something for the woman on the side they hadn't taken.  She did not propose to put herself in the picture. 

They met under the judges' awning on the quarry rim, where the stone steps went down to the level floor once used for the masquerade.  Mist had filled the cut to within six treads of the top.  Neither woman made a scene; they simply set their chairs so far apart that Ada could not hold both profiles in her grid.  Lucy's mother had brought a masquerade mask ribbon fragment, still sewn to the card on which her daughter had collected scraps from every costume she made.  Blue for the knight, she had written beneath it in the small ruled hand of a girl who meant to send out bills one day. 

Ada stood on her chair to lance a low pocket in the awning with her scissors before the wind could empty it over the card.  When she sat down again both faces fitted easily inside her grid.  Mrs Bell put a finger on the card. 

"He paid her double because she made his helmet fit over his hearing aid." 

"I never knew that," Lucy's mother said.  "Did you see him put it on?" 

"I helped him take it off to dance.  He left the lance with me, too, and I stood it in the umbrella barrel." 

Down in the white cut a stone came loose, and all three listened until it stopped.  Ada had recognized the knight by his lance, a broomstick wound with the same blue ribbon Lucy had used for the mask.  She had seen a bare face above it; that was the whole extent of what she had seen.  At fourteen it had seemed impossible to explain that she could see a face as plainly as anybody and a minute later not know it from the face on a coin.  At forty-six what seemed impossible to explain was letting a man go to his grave with children shouting after him in the street. 

Ada laid the black sheet across her knees and wrote on it with her white marking pencil.  Then she told them what she had actually recognized on the steps. 

"You let me feed you," Lucy's mother said at last.  For two years after Lucy vanished she had packed Ada a second sandwich, because grief had made her own hands go on packing two. 

"Yes," Ada said. 

She took the sheet to the library that afternoon and asked the librarian to record her correction in both women's presence.  That sheet, with three signatures at the bottom, became Lucy's panel.  Then Mrs Bell asked for a photograph of the ribbon, to send to the other dancers with a question nobody had thought worth asking while the boxes were dry: who had carried the lance after Martin?  Lucy's mother held the curling card flat for the camera, taking care that her thumbs did not hide her daughter's writing.
