All winter Ada had known the steps to the terra-cotta arbor on the ridge by the different sounds her stick drew from them.  On the night she missed the hollow one and spilled six people's supper, she sat in the warm barley and finally admitted she could no longer see the steps at all.  The sound of a spoon turning slowly on the tiles went on long after the pot had stopped rocking. 

During the sanctions period, with their pensions caught on the wrong side of the water, the island exiles had lived quite literally from hand to hand.  Their elected head, Ada, kept them fed through midnight food runs timed to the change of shift at the packing sheds.  Under the arbor hung the antique silver bell that had called three generations of island children in from recess.  Ada had slung it mouth upward in a net and lined it with a square of felt so that nobody could tell, by listening, a penny from a crown.  The anonymous donations bought grain; the packers brought fish too small to fillet, and Ada made her rounds with a pot that smelled of both shores.  She alone knew where to knock. 

The boy who came up the steps to help her was Emil, a packer whose grandmother had once sent back a bowl of stew because he was in the room.  He set the pot upright and began scraping barley off the tiles with his pay envelope. 

"I can take it tomorrow," he said, and she could hear in his careful voice that he knew precisely why she would refuse. 

"If I give you six doors," she said, "by morning there will be six old people who won't open them." 

"Seven," he said, so softly that what she heard first was the crease he made in the paper.  "My room is right over yours and it comes up through the boards, that smell." 

She had counted on those boys to fill the bell and had never once asked what they ate on the walk back down. 

"Come make it with me tomorrow," she said.  She could be gently persistent, a quality he remembered from learning his times tables, and by the time they reached her door he had agreed to peel the onions. 

The next evening, when his grandmother came looking for him, he was crying copiously into the onions and there was only one chair in the kitchen. 

"Sit and taste," he told her, exactly as she used to tell him when his head barely cleared her stove.  She tasted, reached for the pepper, and tasted again; by the time she put down the spoon there was nothing in her saucer but a little spine of dill.  Ada felt the clean saucer and understood what sort of invitation she had been failing to issue. 

"Could we feed a whole shift up at the arbor?" she asked. 

"Not with what they throw you," the old woman said, and took his thumb and guided it to the seam where he could get two good cheeks off every head. 

On Friday Ada asked the six to come up and teach the packers what to do with the parts a packing shed didn't want.  Five came; the sixth sent his iron skillet with instructions to bring it back full, which made Ada laugh until her stick rattled on the tile.  Ada waited until the last bowl was full to declare the school open, and somebody asked whether scraping the skillet would count toward graduation.  They settled on bread for the next Friday, the sour island kind that took two pairs of hands to stretch across a table. 

A girl who had never seen the island asked why a school bell hung wrong end up, and Emil's grandmother reached into the net to show her the clapper.  Under her hand the felt slipped and a single coin fell against the silver.  There was the thin, familiar note of a child being called indoors, so clear that for an instant Ada could see all the steps down.  Then someone put the warm skillet in her free hand, and someone else took her elbow, and together the three of them went down the ridge.
