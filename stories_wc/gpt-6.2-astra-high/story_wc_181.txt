Between the first and last page of the succession ledger, the astral orchard had time to bloom exactly once.  Its roots enclosed a lonely asteroid, and when the bewildered curator lifted the cover, white flowers switched on in rings that raced each other to the horizon.  June had expected the light, but not the smell of the cheap lemon soap her father had used to wash the orchard dead.  Nor had she expected the ledger to list him as his own heir. 

Every other bequest was simple: a voice went to a niece, the trick of sleeping upright to a grandson not yet conceived.  Only the orchard keeper's gift, the knowledge of how to wake a body after its heart had stopped, went round and round.  June had come out here to inquire where that knowledge had gone while her father lay beautifully washed and quite dead in the next room.  To open a bequest before its recipient claimed it was forbidden, but that was why she had taken the curator's oath instead of the keeper's.  One oath came with a key. 

From the probate drawer she took the pistachio shell cameo blank, smooth cream over green, on which she was to cut the claimant's profile.  A face was an address; even identical twins had never broken their noses in precisely the same way.  Before attempting her own she held the shell to the keeper's branch, and heard a newborn crying with the thin, indignant squeak of a sticky drawer.  June knew that noise from the recording her father had played on her birthdays, the one that began with two minutes of silence he would never let her skip. 

She set the shell down and went to the bathroom.  She had a history of having philosophical breakthroughs in bathrooms, beginning at six, when the lock had taught her the difference between solitude and abandonment.  At thirty, cleaning her father's dentures, she had discovered that disgust could coexist quite comfortably with devotion.  Now, in the two hinged mirrors over the sink, she saw something she had forgotten about the birthday recording: her father's face reflected in the delivery room glass.  She had always thought he looked frightened because she wasn't breathing, but he had been looking straight at the glass, his head turned to show his profile.  He had been posing. 

Nonlinear inheritance was simply complicated, as he used to say of any job you could do if you could only bear to do it.  The books made it sound like a miracle of transport, but standing by the sink she understood it as a species of trust.  There was a man in a delivery room waiting for a woman who did not yet exist to decide what he should know. 

Outside, petals began to fall up, escaping the asteroid in a glitter that turned the black sky almost hospitable.  The ledger was turning its pages faster now; his body in the next room would still be warm.  June went in and tried to turn his head to the angle she needed, but the soft old cheek slid under her hand.  It wasn't the right face anyhow; the nose in the glass had still been straight, unbroken by the little girl who would jump into his arms a year too soon for such a game.  If she cut her own face, perhaps he would wake and they could argue like sensible people about whether she had ever been that girl.  She wanted the argument almost worse than she wanted him alive. 

Instead she took the little knife and uncovered his straight nose in green, leaving the cream of the outer shell for the glass between them.  When she fitted it to the branch, the crying stopped, and for two minutes there was nothing, not even the papery tick of the turning pages.  Then came the squeak, followed by a sound he must have trimmed off the birthday recording, a young man's furious, gasping sobs of relief.  She let him cry as long as he liked.  By the time the last page settled, the orchard was dark again, except for one shell swinging on a twig, its pale face catching what little light there was.
