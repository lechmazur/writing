The first time Nell crawled under Bell Head, she came out backward with a stranger's boot in her fist, having tried to pull herself through him.  It was an awkward thing for a woman who mapped caves for a living to have to explain to a man hopping on one foot.  Now he held her coat while she waited for the ebb to uncover the mouth again. 

Above them a crane was set to tip concrete into the old cable shafts as soon as the spring tides were over.  Nobody had remembered the research station inside the headland until the crane driver asked how much empty space he was supposed to fill.  Nobody, that is, except the women whose bus still stopped outside the library every Thursday. 

They had tested the joints in the first undersea telephone cables by listening in pairs, one woman reading into a handset and another writing down what came through.  The company history called this a test with a simulated human voice.  Nell had been putting up the library exhibition when one of the women took the label out of her hand.  She spoke in a rich contralto that made the tin blinds buzz.  "Which part of that do you suppose they simulated?" 

They had kept a book, she said, a big stitched codex made for them by a girl whose father bound missals.  In its margins the listeners had drawn what they heard, a little bestiary of errors saved from the wastebasket where their daily sheets went.  When the joints were good again and the words came back intact, they used gold leaf from the instrument shop to light up the pictures.  Their supervisor had locked it away for an example of what happened when you left women to their own devices.  "Mind you get my badger," the contralto had said. 

Sea struck rock and fell so abruptly quiet that Nell could hear the crane driver's watch ticking inside his cuff.  She had forty minutes before the water turned.  She slid in feet first this time, keeping the dwindling patch of daylight before her until her shoulders cleared the pinch.  Then she did what she did best in the dark, which was to track things nobody could see.  At each fork she breathed on the cold blade of her penknife and watched the silver bloom of water dim and vanish.  In the branch where it vanished first there was moving air; somewhere beyond that branch was the station with its shafts open to the sky.  Condense, evaporate, turn left; it gave her something to do with her breath besides wonder how much of it she had left. 

She came up through a grate into a room where two chairs stood back to back so the listeners could not read lips.  The cupboard above them hung open, its bottom eaten away by rust, and the floor was a mattress of fallen plaster.  Her lamp found nothing until she took it off her helmet and laid it on the floor, as if to look for a dropped pin.  One gold fleck answered from under a chair.  Working out from that point with her fingertips, she uncovered a badger with a shining tooth, then a margin, then the sodden bulk of the book.  She could not lift it without tearing the page away from its stitches. 

The sea hit the outer wall with a sound like a great drawer slamming shut.  Her fingers closed on the page as they had closed on the boot, and she heard the first stitch give.  "Stand by," she said aloud, the words the women had used while the engineers went down the line looking for a fault.  She opened her hand.  The tin seat of one chair came loose with two twists, and on that she carried the book level through the grate and down to the pinch.  "Stand by," she called again, and a hand reached through the crack to take the weight before she tried to follow. 

At the library the following Thursday the book lay drying under fans, open to the only page that could yet be shown.  Nell had left the label blank.  The old woman considered the badger for a long time before she picked up the pencil.  "That's mine all right," she said, and wrote two names, her own and that of the girl who had spoken at the other end.  Then she passed the pencil down the line.
