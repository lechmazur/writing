On the day the harbor gate finally rusted shut, the gatekeeper was pensioned off and his daughter inherited a room that smelled of oranges and wet iron.  She also inherited the view he had spent thirty years facing away from.  The city rode on the shells of eleven gargantuan turtles, and from any other window there was always a laundry line or a temple dome between you and the silent watchers on the horizon.  From this one you could count their elbows. 

There had been six elbows when her mother sailed to question them; now there were eight.  Nobody liked the arithmetic, least of all her father.  A boat could go out on the white current that wound through the salt banks, but only a turtle had the muscle to come back against it.  He came by every morning to peel an orange in her window and ask if she wouldn't rather have his room uphill. 

Anet was a spiral-shell cartographer, one of the people who could put a whole voyage on a whelk small enough to carry in a pocket.  A flat chart told you where water had been yesterday; around a spiral you could record where it went and how long it took to come back.  For a year she entered the watchers' postures on a shell of her own, a little row of bent pins following the whorl.  Her father thought she was making a comb.  She let him. 

At the end of the year she ran her thumb along the pins and felt, with the skin instead of the eye, the awful familiarity of the tune.  Her people spoke across the gaps between turtles through pattern languages, in which it was the space between strokes, not the strokes themselves, that carried meaning.  A reed whistle served in fog, a square of red cloth in clear weather.  The watchers were not singing, as she had first thought, but patiently spelling out a harbor pilot's question: What is your draft? 

She went uphill and found her father cutting an orange into quarters for the first grandchild of a neighbor.  How deep, she asked him, did a turtle swim?  He dropped the knife so straight that it stood up in the table.  His wife had been a pilot, and here was her daughter asking the very question she had asked before she took six sounders out to the watchers and never came back. 

He said, "If you want a boat you'll have to wait for me to die." 

She put the knife away in the drawer before she said, "I don't want a boat; I want the city." 

By noon divers had measured the draft of eleven turtles and red petticoats were going up on eleven domes.  What she could never have done by persuasion her father did by putting on his old cap and telling people it was an inspection.  Only when the flags had given their answer did he take her hand, and then he squeezed so hard she heard a knuckle crack. 

The fourth watcher lowered one arm until its finger pointed straight down at a seam of water so blue it looked cut with a diamond.  The other three inclined their arms toward it; from his pocket her father drew the reed whistle that had once called boats in through the gate.  He could still blow the low note that made the lead turtle turn its blunt, scarred head. 

All afternoon they sailed into their own widening shadow, between banks of salt on which lay the white keels of boats, bright and clean as teeth.  The watchers proved to be derricks worked by hand, each on its own salt island, each with a ladder running up its spine.  In their lee were terraced fields no one aboard had ever been high enough to see.  On the fourth ladder stood a woman with her mother's elbows and a dress pieced from flour sacks, waving both hands as if conducting a particularly stupid choir. 

Anet had meant to ask what became of the boats, and how the lost expedition had raised a fourth derrick, and whether there were graves.  Instead she caught the rope the woman threw and felt her father take the strain behind her. 

"Up or down?" she called, for one of them would have to climb the blind iron face of the gate. 

Her mother considered the eleven crowded domes, then looked back toward the three other islands, where bits of red cloth were breaking out like flowers. 

"Down first," she said, "and bring the oranges; there's people here who've never had one."
