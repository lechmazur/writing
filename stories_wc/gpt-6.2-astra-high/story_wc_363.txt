At the rummage sale Nell bought a box for the noise it made when she tipped it against her ear.  For forty years she had been a spinner at the long communal loom, and she knew the little wooden clatter of bobbins, but this was a noise she had spent considerably longer missing.  Inside were the edges of a jigsaw puzzle, each cut from the lid of a pear crate by her father's fretsaw.  One still bore the red half moon where, aged six, she had tried the varnish with a thumbnail. 

The woman minding the stall said the pieces had come out of a magpie's nest when the county cleared the new road through the bamboo.  It was the first thing in all those years to come out of the forest bearing so much as a scratch from home.  She took the afternoon off, a liberty so unlike her that the other spinners stood up to watch her go. 

Before the bamboo there had been an orchard, and before the orchard was forgotten there had been a house under its oldest tree.  Nell remembered the puzzle spread on a quilt and her father asking her to fetch a pear for their lunch.  She had stepped into a raveling silence where first the bees went quiet, then her shoes on the grass, then the very sound she made inside her head when she said her name.  Strangers found her on the county road with a brown pear in her fist, a hundred and twelve years after her father reported her missing.  All that was left of him in the county office was his signature, slanting uphill as if the nib had fought him. 

Deep in the bamboo forest the new road ended in a cliff of roots, but beyond it she saw the white froth of pear blossom.  She crawled the last few yards on her stomach like a child slipping under a tent.  There lay the quilt, printed with little anchors, and there sat her father, hardly old enough to be trusted with the care of a child.  Between them hung something fine and stoically sparkling, like a web that has borne more dew than it was ever meant to hold.  Her mouth made no dent in it.  When she put out her hand, her bracelet caught, and its one bead, ancient tree sap hardened into amber, dropped through onto the quilt. 

Her father picked up the bead he had polished for her sixth birthday and looked with such naked expectation toward the trees that she nearly ran.  If she could have sent her face across, she would have had to send the wrinkles too, and how could he be made to understand them?  Then he turned the bead over and rapped it twice against the side of the pear crate.  Their old game had been to build the border blind, his eyes shut and her small fingers guiding his to the next joint with one tap for hot and two for cold.  He shut his eyes now, and she knew he wanted something better than a face to tell him who was there. 

Nell opened the snips at her belt and scratched a letter on the back of each piece, following the old familiar order of the joints.  She sent them across in a heap and let him grope among them, one tap with the snips for hot, two for cold.  His tongue came out between his teeth, and she could feel her own tongue doing the same as they bent together over the work.  Last of all he fitted the piece with the red half moon, and now on the quilt there stood a crooked wooden frame around the empty place where she had been. 

He opened his eyes.  Along its upper edge he read I GREW UP, and down its side he read I HAD A GIRL.  At the bottom she had cut the five letters of her daughter's name, which was also her mother's.  The last edge read GO HOME PAPA, though what her hand had wanted to cut, and God knew it, was PLEASE COME.  Through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry she heard, quite distinctly, his breath recommence.  He ran his thumb along those last four letters.  Then he put the bead in the middle of the frame, where the heart would go if the space inside it were a little girl.  By the time she got to her knees there was only a very old tree in a shower of white petals, and on her tongue the precise remembered grit of a pear.
