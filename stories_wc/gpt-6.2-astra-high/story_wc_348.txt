The empty chrysalis was still in its mustard jar, with the words WHAT THE MILL DID in my mother's handwriting on the lid.  She had carried it into the first compensation hearing and I, a polymer chemist employed by the mill, had been invited to identify the substance inside.  There was no substance inside, I explained; the neat split down the back meant that a perfectly good butterfly had got away.  Somebody laughed, and the chairman moved on to the matter of whose wells would be tested at the company's expense.  My mother put the jar back in her shopping bag with the slow care you give a glass when you discover your hands are shaking. 

Now that she was dead, the jar belonged to me, along with a house on the wrong side of the water.  In our braided ravine the east channel ran past the mill and the west past the school, and between them lay enough willow scrub to hide a county fair.  The mill had found its acrylic binder in the east channel after a tank spill, but nothing in the west channel in samples taken at the same hour.  I had signed both sheets; I had not volunteered that the hour was my choice. 

On the morning we were due to divide the money for a last round of tests, I took the jar down to the old tank pad.  I thought I might bury the thing there and go home without having to see anybody.  From the pad I could look straight down on a brown shelf of foam peeling away from the bank, each scallop picking up the morning light.  Most of it sailed east, but one scallop took a divergent path into what I had always read on the survey as an island.  I could follow it through gaps in the willow tops, first alongside the main current and then almost directly back toward me.  It came out below the school steps long after the rest had passed the east gauge.  Nothing in that foam identified a chemical; what I had been given, tracing its course with my finger on the damp lid, was a second clock. 

My mother, who played viola, used to call me symphonically minded because even as a girl I could keep my place through forty bars of rest.  It had never occurred to either of us that a talent for knowing when to come in also meant knowing how long you could keep quiet. 

At the school the east bank people sat on the little folding chairs and the west bank people stood along the wall in their coats.  On the blackboard was the sum available, enough for six sediment cores, all six penciled in beside the east bank pump.  My mother's chair by the door was holding a tray of supermarket cookies.  I had wanted to atone for the laugh, which was a pleasant small ambition compared with what I found myself having to do.  I set the jar on the map and showed them where the two clocks started and where they stopped.  I told them I had sent the sampling crew home rather than pay for a second shift; that entry was still in the ledger, if anybody cared to look. 

The woman who ran the east pump pushed three of her six brass map pins across the blue pencil line.  "Don't put them where the foam went today," she said.  "Find out how high the water was then." 

That was how the work began, not with anybody forgiving anybody, but with the school janitor going downstairs for the book where he marked the spring floods against the bottom step.  While the pieces fell into place on the map, a small boy unscrewed the jar and asked if he could have the skin for the nature table.  I nearly told him it was evidence, which would have given my mother a laugh worth hearing.  Instead I helped him carry it across the room; it weighed so little that between the two of us we had to be careful not to tip it out.
