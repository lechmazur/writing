To draw an echo on open water you laid soot on the surface and watched for the place where the soot parted.  Mara had made her living as a natural history illustrator with that trick, which was also how she had come to publish a lie.  In her plate of a female with three young, the young were just three ripples from the prow of her father's boat.  He had told her so before he died, and she had let the second edition go to press anyway.  Now a boy at the canal school had written to ask what the young ate, and she found she could not answer him with another ripple. 

She set out to find the place where echoes are born with a jar of soot and her father's oldest captive, a lock echo that still knew his knock.  Two quick taps, a rest, one slow: it had once been the signal for Mara to put down her pencils and open the door.  The rooftop canal system lay slack in its stone troughs, weaving between towers on the back of the sleeping giant.  His shoulders came level when he slept, and until he woke she could go as far as she liked without ever stopping for a lock. 

At every junction she would uncage the echo just long enough to see which way it stirred the soot, then scoop it up in its little brass mesh box.  She had expected it to head for one of the deep sounding wells on which the towers were built.  Instead it led her to the narrow gutter under a footbridge, where generations of children had leaned over to shout their names.  She tied up, set the cage on the planks and stretched out beside it, her ear to a knothole.  The old echo knocked; from below came two quick taps, but the last slow tap never fell.  Reaching under, she felt hundreds of little pits, each about the size of the dimple a pencil makes in a thumb.  The interrupted cadence, a tiny draught against her wrist, and once again she had arrived in time to draw only what something left behind. 

She took off the infinity symbol pendant her father had made for her when her hands were still too slow to draw a diving tern.  It was a tool of his trade, for a lock keeper on a moving mountain sculpts time itself, holding a gate open longer than the instant its two sills are level.  The trick worked by borrowing time, and the crossed silver loops showed exactly where it went: what you added to this moment came out of the next.  She might miss the giant stirring; she might have to winter on the wrong shoulder.  She settled herself in the prow where she could look up into the pits and slid a finger through the silver.  With her free hand she opened the cage for good. 

This time the two taps came as far apart as hammer blows from opposite banks.  In the long rest something soft came nosing out of the pit, a membrane so thin she saw the whorls of her own finger through it.  The old echo laid its last tap in that open mouth and the membrane folded over it like a dumpling skin.  It drifted free and tapped once against the hull, quite distinctly, from the outside.  All down the plank mouths waited, and the old echo went from one to the next like a lamplighter.  She put the pencil to paper and for once left a little white between one drawing and the next.  There were things you could not truthfully join up with a line. 

Then she was on her knees in a dry gutter with stars between the planks and somebody on the upper shoulder ringing the supper bell.  A drop of water trembled in the mesh box; nothing knocked when she shut the lid.  The giant had rolled onto his side; she was wintering here, it seemed. 

At the canal school she found a light still burning and the offending plate pinned up beside the door.  She wrote on it what her father had told her and signed her name.  She knocked twice and was waiting to give the last tap when the boy opened the door.  He had a pencil behind his ear, and when she showed him the drawings the first thing he wanted to know was whether the floating skin ever came back to its pit.  Mara pulled up a chair beside him and said she hadn't seen.
