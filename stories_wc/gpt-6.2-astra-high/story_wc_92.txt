Even during the blackout, nobody could have called Fort Wick dark.  At twilight its windows shone the particular green of a cat considering a misdemeanor.  The electric fence was dead, the telephones were dumb, and the commandant had taken the back off his desk clock without learning anything useful about the failure.  But the beetles in the lamp room went on being beetles. 

Mara could see them from her post at the canal lock, where for nineteen years she had inspected every lantern going out and every empty lantern coming back.  Not a single female had passed her in all that time.  "It's a distinction," her daughter had remarked once, "that I should hesitate to put on my tombstone." 

The fort rented out males, which died in a fortnight; the females stayed in the long moss trough called the cradle of sparks.  When a household could not pay, supper ended at sunset, and so did reading and the fine work of taking stitches out of a wound.  Mara's daughter, a seamstress with ambitions in that last direction, had got half the canal district to plant moss in their window boxes.  Getting the moss inhabited was another matter. 

From the lock Mara could also see the half-painted sundial on the nursery wall, a civic improvement which the nursery girls had contrived not to finish for three summers.  They worked at its gold rays in the old code of the lockmen, who used to signal with two poles when the spillway drowned their voices.  Today two fresh strokes made the narrow angle for a passenger waiting on the upper landing.  A stipple of gold between them meant the passenger was expecting. 

Well, somebody had a high opinion of the lock sentinel.  Mara buttoned her coat and went to collect a pregnant lady. 

On the landing sat a bronze quill holder, the commandant's concession to the fashion for writing with actual feathers.  Somebody had stuffed the little holes with wet moss; through one of them a green light came and went like a thought.  From below came the slap of oars, then the voice of the commandant asking if darkness had been understood to mean a general holiday. 

Mara had meant to slip the holder into her daughter's shopping basket.  Instead she took it down to her desk, shook the moss from one hole, and planted a feather in it.  Then she set out the emergency ledger, a handsome volume whose pages had never suffered the indignity of ink.  Without the telephones, she informed the commandant, she would require his signature for every lantern that passed; would he prefer to stand, or should she fetch a cushion? 

He eyed the queue of boats backing up to the bend.  Surely one signature would do for the entire evening? 

Mara considered this with the pained attention she usually reserved for discovering a cracked chimney on an otherwise excellent lantern.  At length she wrote that all lights passing the lock that evening were going free by his authority, and turned the book for him to sign.  He took the feather, made his flourish, and saw what was climbing the shaft toward his knuckle. 

They both looked at the fat, wingless creature, and then at the boats, where people who could not afford a fortnight's light were watching him by its glow.  Behind his shoulder the nursery windows were filling up with faces, each lit from below by a pair of cupped hands.  Until that instant Mara had thought the girls had trusted her with their only passenger.  She saw now what a small ambition it had been, to get one lady past a sentinel. 

Nobody said a word to help the commandant out of his difficulty.  Finally he addressed himself to the beetle.  "I take it you know where you're going?" 

Mara's daughter held up the shopping basket, and he tipped the feather into it with the air of a gentleman yielding the pavement.  By the time the telephones began ringing again, every boat had its passenger, and the girls had painted the two gold rays wide apart: channel clear. 

In the years that followed, the district got into the habit of putting a light in the window whenever the young surgeon was wanted.  Mara, who still noticed everything, learned not to count them.
