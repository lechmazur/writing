The night after the energy beings arrived, every nail in the harmonic caves worked itself halfway out of the wood.  Doors slumped on their hinges; at the ferry landing a plank tipped and sent three caulkers sliding down the chute that carried broken ice into the mountain.  By the time Senn reached the landing, men had lowered lamps through each of the three grates where the chute might have delivered its cargo.  All anybody could see below was a gently luminous mist, the color of milk in a green bottle. 

Each grate weighed as much as a house wall, and there was one hoist, on wheels, with a lame axle.  At high water the sumps beneath the grates would fill to the lids.  They looked to Senn, the ripple-mark mapkeeper, who could tell from a finger's breadth of ridged sand which way water had run in the dark.  On her map the middle sump took the strongest current, but a man sliding on his back was not a grain of sand. 

She laid an ear against the first grate and got such a blow of sound that she tasted the penny she had swallowed at five.  The newcomers were still pouring in from the storm outside, little knots of green fire that took the sea wind's pitch and held it.  From every vault that note came back a little changed, as if a thousand hammers were falling on anvils of a thousand different weights. 

Senn put her hand into her apron and closed it around the echo chamber stone.  Her husband had used its hollow face to catch the shipyard whistle, then turned its smooth face toward their sleeping baby to conjure a bloom of hush just wide enough for a cradle.  A stone could hold one sound, could give it back or take it out of the air.  What hers held now was her husband calling that baby by the absurd name of Mister Boots.  The boy himself, a grave young man with his father's chin, stood at the hoist waiting for his mother to name a grate. 

She made herself look away from him, down at the sill of iron on which all three grates rested.  Spray had frozen there in a long white welt.  Examining crack patterns in ice was how she mapped the shallows in winter, when there wasn't a handspan of naked sand to read.  Here there were stars and broken ladders, but along the very lip of the sill ran a single set of breaks, regular as the teeth of a comb.  Whatever clamor beat against it, the iron itself was singing one note.  Here was where to find the quiet in the storm, not by stopping a thousand mouths but by stopping the one against her ear. 

She wanted to put the hollow to her ear once more before she spent what was in it.  Instead she put it to the iron.  Something tickled her palm, like whiskers moving with a smile, and then all she felt was the steady draw of a bow across a string.  She turned the stone and laid her cheek beside it.  In a little circle of utter repose she heard, under the first grate, water lapping; under the second, the soft collapse of rotten ice.  Under the third somebody was tapping the old caulker's measure, two for oakum, one for pitch.  Senn tapped it back before she lifted her head; it mattered that whoever had the hammer should know somebody had heard. 

Her boy wedged a boat hook under the bad wheel and walked it forward while the others heaved, until the chain hung plumb above her head.  When the lid came up, three heads floated like apples in a tub; the hammer, still held aloft, was the only dry thing about them.  They got the people out first and then, at the eldest caulker's furious insistence, the hammer. 

By morning the little green fires had blown inland, leaving a great deal of joinery to be done in blessed ordinary noise.  Senn was setting the nails back in her door when her son came to ask if she wanted him to say the name into the stone.  She considered this with a nail between her lips and shook her head.  "But you can hold the door for me, Mister Boots," she said, and when he laughed she had the sense to lay the hammer down.
