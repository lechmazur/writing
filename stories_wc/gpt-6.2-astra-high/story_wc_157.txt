After thoughts became taxable, an hour spent talking was free and an hour spent listening was free, but an hour of silence cost sixpence unless you could produce the other party to the conversation.  I had lost my speech to a stroke, and at sixpence an hour I was about to lose my greenhouse too. 

The assessor came in out of the sleet and set her little foreclosure flag in the warm dirt between two sleeping bumblebee queens.  She was the first visitor in thirty years to enter an entomologist's greenhouse without asking if anything stung.  I liked her for that and wished she had come on some other business. 

My advocate, Silas Bell, took her wet hat and sold her a raffle ticket before she could get her gloves off.  He had been professor of dead languages until somebody noticed that two of them had never lived.  Asked what the raffle was for, he was candidly cryptic: "A roof, madam, but whose depends on you." 

He laid my appeal on the potting bench.  For every hour since my stroke that I had spent looking out over the municipal allotments, he claimed a listening exemption.  The other party was a secret language club of eighty gardeners, of whom I was the oldest and, in his unreliable opinion, the wittiest. 

We had started long before the tax, joining community gardens to plant message patterns because my husband, going deaf across the valley, could still read a bed of marigolds at half a mile.  After he died I kept up my end, mostly gossip and weather but occasionally the sort of remark about a mayor for which a tenant might lose a nice south-facing plot. 

The assessor looked down the terraces, where the sleet was turning the town's handwriting white.  "Give me a dictionary and the names," she said, "and I can backdate the exemption." 

Silas had already drawn a dictionary from his sleeve when I knocked it into the watering trough.  I watched its leaves warp and darken.  He looked as if I had slapped him, which in fairness was what I had first intended. 

"I thought you wanted to keep the place," he said. 

There was a sentence in there I still sometimes said to my husband, and they could sell the glass from over my head before I would let them have it.  I took from my pocket the gourd rattle seed my husband had shaken loose on the day he invented our question mark.  A whole dry gourd on its stick meant listen; give it a shake and it meant answer.  One seed in a pill tin could not summon the valley, but it could summon Silas to the sort of mischief at which he excelled. 

I rattled it and pointed at the assessor's hat.  Understanding dawned on him in its usual form, a quite untrustworthy smile. 

"Choose something you'd like done to it," he told her, "something nobody could possibly guess." 

"I should like," she whispered in my good ear, "a fried egg in it, sunny side up." 

I had no word for fried.  I shoved the trays of winter lettuce against the glass in the pattern for a hen's wages, put calendulas on top for the sun, then held the hat beneath the lot and rattled my tin.  The nearest gardener squinted, tipped a barrow upright for a relay board and began hanging bunches of kale from its handles.  Below him barrows went up like startled hares.  A shed door slammed, somebody ran with a skillet, a dog pursued somebody pursuing a hen, and up through the sleet came three boys fighting to keep a plate level.  Behind them came forty people who wanted to see what sort of idiot wore an egg. 

Silas caught the plate, the assessor held out the hat, and a yolk as round as a sixpence and worth rather more slid into the crown.  Nobody spoke until she began to laugh. 

On the appeal she wrote that she had conversed with the entire valley and that the vocabulary in use was immaterial to the case.  She pulled up the flag.  Silas, who had hoped to publish the dictionary, spent the raffle money on a new hat instead. 

The next morning I put my husband's sentence in the window, and Silas, who knew what it said, sat beside me drinking tea.  For a whole lovely untaxed hour he let me have the last word.
