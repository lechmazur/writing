Decades later, when my father stopped setting a place for my brother, I finally noticed that none of us could remember what Kit had liked to eat.  We could lay his table from muscle memory: blue plate, pewter mug, spoon turned over to show the little fox stamped underneath.  Once the plate stayed on the dresser, there was just a boy's name and a clean rectangle of oilcloth. 

I made my living as a folklorist, the hands-on sort who will learn to darn a heel rather than photograph a stocking.  Chronically curious, my father called me, as though the chemist might have an ointment for it.  Now he watched me put Kit's spoon in my pocket and said, quite sharply, that a habit ought to be allowed to die. 

When habits break, the old women on our coast say, you can see what has been living off them.  I had written down that saying in six kitchens, always from women who had lost somebody in the great tide that drowned the lower town.  This time I went back with a blue plate and asked each woman to set it for the person who hadn't come home.  They all turned the spoon over, even those whose spoons had nothing on the back.  One of them caught my wrist and said that if I meant to undo the work I had better take boots. 

At low water she walked me out along the ribs of a collier to the sunken orchard greenhouse, ringed by shipwrecks the tide had deposited as neatly as shoes outside a door.  Inside the cracked dome, pear trees stood up to their forks in black water.  She had picked pears here with my mother; after the tide they had come back together to plant something else. 

That winter, she said, children had lain awake listening to their parents cry, and in the morning nobody could get up and light a stove.  The women had brought a fairy ring mushroom from the headland and crumbled its cap into the soil around each trunk.  Set a place, turn a spoon, and whatever you remembered at that moment went underground instead of through you. 

"You let it take ours too," I said. 

She looked at my wet boots and said they hadn't known how to tell a child's grief from a child's breakfast. 

She showed me how to detect a living ring by studying broken constellations in the water between the trees.  Over ordinary mud the stars lay whole, but where the white threads fed, a star would go out and come back like the pulse in a baby's temple.  Orion lay across the flooded aisle with his belt intact; the long handle of the Dipper was down to one nail.  I waded towards it, the fox on the spoon deep in the pad of my thumb.  Under the water my fingers found a stem as cool and firm as the neck of a pear. 

"Pull that and we'll all have it back," she said, and her voice held nothing that I could mistake for thanks. 

I had come to recover stolen memories, not to put a drowned husband back in a woman's bed against her will.  Yet I could feel my brother at the other end of that stem, as distinctly as if we were pulling a wishbone.  I let go, turned the spoon face up for the first time in forty years, and slipped it under the cap.  Something settled in the bowl, lighter than salt.  I put it on my tongue. 

Kit had liked the buttery skin off hot milk, that disgusting yellow glove which he lifted with two fingers while I made extravagant retching noises.  He had been missing a front tooth; I had been the one who knocked it out, swinging a pail.  I was laughing so hard my boots filled. 

I took the spoon home empty and drew my father a map, with the collier for a landmark and a little picture of which way the spoon should go.  He folded it into the sugar tin, where he kept things he might want but did not propose to be nagged about. 

Three mornings later he came in with his trouser cuffs wet and asked whether I had ever apologized about that tooth.  I told him I had, though I hadn't, and for a lovely ordinary minute I was in trouble about it again.
