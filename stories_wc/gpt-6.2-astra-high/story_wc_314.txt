In the village of Orra it was no disgrace to become a bird, but it was considered bad manners to do it indoors.  So when her mother started shedding little black feathers into the bread dough, Linn put on her boots.  The auburn-haired alchemist had delivered half the valley out of its clothes and into the air, and everybody knew what she carried in her bag.  Nobody knew what she carried in her pockets.  Secretly sentimental, she had kept the bent pins, thimbles, and copper buttons that otherwise would have lain under the trees until they turned green.  Her mother took nothing except the loaf, which would not be fit for company but which somebody ought to eat. 

Behind the kaleidoscopic waterfall lay the hidden orchard where the people of Orra had always gone to change.  Every apple there was descended from a pip spat out by some old fellow waiting for his wings.  As they edged behind the sheet of water, colors traveled over her mother's face, and Linn could see how thin the bones had grown.  She picked an ancient tree with a hollow in its trunk and set the loaf inside, out of the spray. 

From her bag Linn took a frozen dew crystal and slipped it under her mother's tongue.  It was good for an hour before the final transformation, time enough in ordinary weather for the new bird to get its bearings with a human mind.  Wherever it found itself when the crystal melted would seem to it the proper place to live.  But rain high on the mountain had thickened the falls, and where there should have been a dozen slits of clear daylight there was solid, roaring pearl.  Linn lit her lamp and held it up; the flame stood straight, without a tremor to betray a passage. 

Her mother had already lost her mouth, and could only point with one claw at the blunt end of the orchard, where the cliff rose sheer.  Against that dark wall a flock of starlings was folding and unfolding like a length of cloth being measured by a merchant.  Perhaps they too had come in before the flood and could not get out. 

Linn would gladly have pocketed her mother and walked home, but she had seen what became of birds carried home in an apron.  They sat beside the salt at meals and waited to be asked to pass it.  Her mother had been the one to carry those sad creatures back here, sometimes too late to do them any good.  Even now she was tugging at Linn's cuff, trying to lead her heavy daughter through the grass.  Linn let herself be led. 

Through the patterns in a flock of starlings her mother had once taught her to read the shape of a hawk too distant to be seen.  Look for the bit that's missing, she had said, drawing a wing with her floury finger on the table.  Now what was missing was a long slantwise streak, never twice in quite the same place.  Linn scrambled onto the lowest bough of an apple tree and held the lamp in that blank stroke of sky.  The flame lay down flat.  Somewhere in the rock was a chimney, and from it poured a wind too strong for any small bird to enter.  The birds were not looking for the chimney; they were waiting for it to draw breath. 

Her mother fluttered onto her palm, and Linn climbed until the rock was close enough to smell, wet and sharp as a cut apple.  She counted the beats between gusts against her wrist, the way she timed a cooling metal, and felt the touch of wonder when the flock tilted just ahead of her count.  Neither pulse alone would have persuaded her to open her hand.  A cold drop fell on her thumb, and in the little black beak she saw the last bright grain.  This time, as the flock leaned and her own blood gave the beat, she pitched her mother straight at the stone.  For one hideous instant the small body tumbled like a button thrown away.  Then a crack in the cliff took in the whole long ribbon of birds, and an apple blossom sailing after them stuck to the wet rock where they had gone. 

Coming home above the falls, Linn saw a starling perched on the milestone with a dab of dough still clinging to its foot.  She put down the loaf and broke it, and they ate together without either one having to pass anything at all.
