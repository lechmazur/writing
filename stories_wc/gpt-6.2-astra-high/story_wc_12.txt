At the dimensional anchor, where the drowned town and the dry town shared one room, Father Bell decorated with silence.  He laid each name of the drowned on his loop pedal and left a little space on either side, the way his mother had hung the good plates.  The names came round under the floorboards, distinct but soft as spoons heard through a kitchen wall.  It was the only work for which either town still paid a priest. 

On the day his daughter sat for possession, Bell brought out his wife's palette.  Its hollows had held six colors when Ada painted shop signs, but only one color could register on both sides of the anchor: the blue she had used for shadows on snow.  Kit balanced it on her knee and dipped the brush, which shook until the first syllable of Ada's name came round.  Then her hand steadied into that familiar slant which had made even the word LAUNDRY look as if it were going somewhere.  She began the blue ring on the floor around her chair. 

Once the circle closed, Ada would have Kit's body, and until the anchor opened next winter Kit would have her mother's room beneath the water.  Kit had turned eighteen on Tuesday and had been asking to do this since she was nine.  Bell had kept the rules even when she wept, and now that she was old enough he meant to keep them still. 

"Do you want your mother back?" 

He caught her yes on the pedal and sent it down to Ada, as he had sent a hundred other offers down.  The brush went on traveling, its small dry hiss their customary proof of acceptance.  Yet where the floorboards met, it did a curious thing: it lifted clear and left a gap.  Kit went back to fill it and again the brush rose, as gently as a finger laid across a lip.  When Bell cut the names, what he heard in the unraveled stillness was one girl breathing hard and one woman trying to set a paintbrush down. 

Under the rules he had written, he had until the circle closed to plumb the will on either side.  He had always done it with a question that contained its answer, a kindness, he had thought, to people too moved to speak at length.  Now he tried the old pulpit trick of turning a sentence inside out to see what fell from its pockets. 

"You would give your mother a year, Kit; would your mother take a year from you?" 

The girl stared at him, and in that instant of inattention the brush fell out of her fingers. 

"You never asked it like that," she said. 

"I never had to sit in the chair," he said. 

His heel was on the recorded yes; all he need do was rock back to make the room ring with it again.  Instead he looped Ada's name alone with the silence on either side, a summons without instructions about what the summoned woman was to do. 

On the palette in Kit's lap a line appeared in the wet blue, as if somebody had drawn a nail through it.  Another line crossed it, then a third, until there stood the little gate Ada used to draw beside a finished sign on an invoice: account closed, nothing owing.  Kit touched a bar of the gate and got paint on her finger. 

"I thought if she didn't come up it meant she didn't miss us," she said. 

Bell had thought so too, and had made of that thought a law under which a hundred people had gone down into the dark to let somebody else have daylight.  He took his heel off the pedal and let the yes go where sounds go when nobody saves them. 

Kit stepped over the broken circle with the palette held level in both hands.  At the door she asked if next winter she might bring some more blue, not for a ring but for a sign they could paint together.  This time he knew whom to ask, and how long to leave after the question.
