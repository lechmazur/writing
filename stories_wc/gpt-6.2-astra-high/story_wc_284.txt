At the tilt of the earth toward spring, Nell came up Zephyr Bluff in mist, carrying the engraved pillbox that had once been all her daughter could be trusted to carry.  Money she lost, mittens she traded, but the little silver trout on the lid had pleased her, and she liked the snap it made between her palms.  When Nell had sent her inland with the other dockyard children, she had been allowed one piece of luggage and had chosen, sensibly, the cat. 

That was nine years ago, when Nell was a watchmaker with a useful view of the naval basin and had not yet seen her photograph pasted up at every station.  A burned spy found few customers even after a peace, since nobody liked to think about what else she might be noticing while she set a watch.  She had spent a winter trying to find where unused courage waits when it is no longer wanted for its old work.  Apparently it took some to write to a schoolgirl, because by the time she managed it the girl was apprenticed to a joiner. 

The answer, in the big square hand that marked timber, had specified no house, only Saturday morning and the bluff road and a question: Did she remember the benches?  Nell remembered the codes in accumulated snow that had got the dockyard children the last mile to their winter billets without putting a list of addresses into anybody's pocket.  Each doorstep had its bench for taking off boots; one seat swept clear meant room for one, two meant room for a pair that must not be parted.  It was a prudently daring invitation, the sort a girl might issue who did not know whether she would be glad or sorry when her mother got off the bus.  Until she took a broom to the snow she could still change her mind. 

All the way up, in a silver hush broken only by water ticking in the downpipes, Nell looked for one narrow seat.  She reached the bus shelter at the summit without finding it and sat down hard on a bench cleared from end to end.  There were two little heaps of snow, one at either elbow.  She moved over. 

Almost immediately somebody came round from behind the shelter, carrying a broom, and sat in the place she had made.  The girl was wearing a man's reefer jacket with both cuffs turned back to let her hands come through.  On the near hand a thumbnail had gone black, and Nell had to sit on her own hands not to take it up and kiss it.  Nobody had warned her that the enormous child would contain the little one entire. 

"I can't come back with you," said Ada. 

Nell had rehearsed a speech about what the courts would vouchsafe a mother who had not willingly abandoned her child, and about the lovely south window in the room above the shop.  From where Ada sat, that window must look about big enough for a cat to get out of. 

"I've brought your fish," she said instead, and put the box on the bench.  Ada pressed the tail as she had always done, but the lid came off in her hand. 

"It's had rather a lot of being opened," Nell admitted.  She did not say how often, on nights when she had lain in a rented bed with the curtains drawn, she had listened for that small familiar report. 

Ada examined the empty knuckles of the hinge, then worked a brass pin out of the fold in her cuff.  "Hold it like it wants to go," she said, with the authority of somebody who had been told this a hundred times and had finally found out what it meant.  Nell held the two halves steady while Ada threaded the pin and bent its sharp point safely under.  The sound it made was the right sound. 

Down in the mist the bus was struggling round the hairpin bend, changing gear where it always had, giving them perhaps another minute. 

"Next Saturday there won't be snow," Nell said. 

Ada put the box in her pocket and stood, and for a moment Nell thought that even this much hope had been an imposition.  Then the long cuff came down over Ada's black thumbnail as she lifted her arm to flag the bus. 

"Third house past the pump," she said, as plainly as if it had never been a dangerous thing to tell somebody where you lived.
