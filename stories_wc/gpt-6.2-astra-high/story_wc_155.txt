Three hundred years of thick fog had reduced the Orchard District to a smell of wet lime and, in a good November, one pear bumping a roof on its way down.  Yet at noon, in the fold of a daydream held in common by the scattered families, you could still walk its ruined lanes without falling through a floor.  You might fall, but somebody who remembered a floor would lend you one. 

Minta came in wearing her vermilion overalls, with a camera under one arm and a bit of wall in her pocket.  She was the mural artist who painted with light, a trade her father had described as whitewashing without the bucket, the brush, or any discernible benefit to the house.  Since his death she had done forty commissions in shades of respectable blue and had not once been able to think of a rejoinder.  Today she meant to display something on the blind gable of the old packing shed that might have made him stay and look. 

He had kept the fresco fragment on his kitchen sill, propping it against the salt so that its painted yellow curve stood upright.  Whenever a visitor mistook it for a piece of the famous Golden Pear, he had quietly turned it upside down.  Then he and the visitor would perform some obscure business behind a napkin, from which both emerged pink about the eyes.  As a girl Minta had considered this an intolerable waste of a clean napkin.  Now she would have given all forty blue walls to be let behind it. 

She set the camera facing the gable and passed a question through the district's old knowledge network, which worked on the principle that nobody knew anything but everybody knew somebody.  Who remembered a yellow curve?  Within a moment she had offers of a banana, a jaundiced moon, and treatment for a disorder of the thumbnail.  She tried again, asking this time about the napkin.  A woman who still folded linen the way the packers had folded fruit wrappers sent her to a boy whose grandfather could whistle through his ears.  By this somewhat roundabout route she arrived at the information that what went on behind the napkin required two noses. 

The boy volunteered his.  He crouched beside the shed and felt along the damp plaster until he found two holes at the height of a seated packer's head.  The fragment fitted beneath them, yellow curve down: a mouth wide enough to accommodate two people who could agree on nothing else.  "Put your noses through," the boy instructed from behind the wall, "and the pear can smell itself."  He poked his nose out, an excellent nose, slightly luminous at the tip as things belonging to other dreamers often were. 

Until then Minta had planned to spend the noon minute drawing a perfect pear around the fragment with her amber torch.  With the shutter open, each passage of her hand would leave a line on the film even after the fog had swallowed the light.  A perfect pear, it now appeared, would be a shocking waste of two perfectly serviceable nose holes in a wall.  She traced a fat lopsided fruit, gave it the boy's ears for leaves, and went behind the wall to put her face beside his.  The brick was cold enough to make her snort, and the boy, who had been waiting for just this, supplied a ripe descending whistle.  Of course that was the noise a pear would make on discovering what it smelled like.  She laughed so hard she bumped the plaster and felt the fragment settle against her nose like a finger laid along it. 

Her camera clicked shut.  The boy wanted to see, but exposed film keeps its own counsel until the dark and the developer have had their say.  She put the roll in her little tank and shook it as her father used to shake a parcel to guess his birthday present.  A minute in a daydream has room for such things, even for the interminable business of waiting for a negative to dry.  By the time she slipped the strip into her lantern, word had gone back along the network and the linen woman had brought a napkin. 

On the gable the pear appeared, black where it ought to shine, with two pale twitchy smudges and a grin of absolutely no discernible benefit to the house.  The woman unfolded the napkin and drew a great yellow mouth on it with her thumb.  Minta ducked behind it and pressed her nose beside the other nose against the linen, and somebody out in front began to laugh before she even had time to whistle.
