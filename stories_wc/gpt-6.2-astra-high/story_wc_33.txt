At the day's lone mail drop, Lio took off his cap to both portraits before turning them to face the wall.  A disputed territory mailman could be dismissed for disrespect to either governor, but neither postal code said which way a portrait had to look.  With their painted eyes averted, no one in either capital would see what passed across his counter. 

Twilight came through the roof of the cobalt arcade, laying six blue panes across the counter and a seventh, clear as water, where the glass had broken.  He put the parcel in that patch so Nell could see exactly what her father had left her.  Inside the bottle a black thunderstorm turned on itself like a dog trying to lie down in too small a basket.  Once in a while its glass stopper clicked against the wire.  The people waiting for mail could hear that click from the far end of the arcade. 

"Tuition," Nell said, reading the single word on the tag. 

Lio had agreed to foster her for the six weeks her father expected to spend in prison, and kept her for the eleven years it took him to die there.  Every birthday her father had promised that she would study medicine across the river, and every birthday Lio had let her believe the promise was his to make. 

The river had become a border on account of the smuggling drones; before the first collectors flew, nobody had thought of clouds as something a country owned.  Now there were vineyards on the rich bank where it rained to the minute and villages on the poor bank where a whole generation had grown up washing out of cups.  One good thunderstorm would fetch the three years of fees in full. 

Nell did not put out her hands.  "Which village?" she asked. 

Lio had spent all winter trying to find where broken promises heal, on the theory that a man might at least take a girl there.  He brought out the two things that had finally seemed to him more useful than an apology: his old delivery book and her father's flight calendar.  The authorities had looked for matches between flights and sales, but Lio, accustomed to knowing who was sick by whose shutters stayed shut, had worked by linking absences.  On five dates the official calendar showed no flight, though Lio had delivered the usual invoices for the collectors' fuel.  On those same five dates the rain gauge readings in Lio's book stopped short of the arcade; beyond it he had carried his letters through dust. 

Nell ran her nail down the column to the fifth date, the one scratched into the bottom of the bottle.  "Here," she said, and looked at the people in line. 

No one called her a thief or offered to buy the thing, although the vineyard agent stood close enough to lay his silver on the counter.  Then the woman who cleaned the arcade took a chipped enamel cup from her pail and set it under the broken pane. 

"That won't do," Nell said.  She knew the ground outside was baked so hard the first rush would go straight down the streets and take the topsoil with it.  At her direction the waiting people lifted the iron covers off the old cistern mouths, careful even now not to let them clang.  Lio watched her measure the distance between the mouths with the practiced eye of somebody who had been dividing water all her life. 

"If I sign for it first," she asked him, "does that make it mine to give?" 

"It makes it yours to lose," he said, and, for once, did not promise to make up the difference. 

She signed the book, slipped the wire, and eased the stopper out as far as her thumb would let it go.  Rain struck the blue glass from below, fell back into the open mouths, and went on falling until the counter shook with the weight of water moving underfoot.  When the thunder came it was only a little sound, the last coin rattling in an empty purse. 

Nell stood with her head bent until the woman with the cup brought it to her full.  "I'm still going," she told Lio, and he said he knew, though neither of them did.  Then she drank half the water and handed him the cup.
