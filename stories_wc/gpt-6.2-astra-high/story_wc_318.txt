Until my wife died, the funniest thing in our house was the line on my tax return that said leaf blower symphony conductor.  Ellen would read it aloud each April with the dignity of a judge pronouncing a capital sentence.  Then she would hand me a spoon for a baton and stand back to let me conduct the steam off the potatoes.  After the funeral our daughters tried this once, and from the register under the table came the sound of somebody trying to get a spoonful of potatoes down.  When we stopped laughing, it stopped choking. 

It was not a sound we could mistake.  For six months I had had to macerate pears for Ellen and strain out the little knots of flesh that even a ripe pear keeps around its seeds.  At the end we could no longer afford to make her laugh while she ate, and she could hardly do anything except eat or try to eat.  Now there were three of us at that table keeping our mouths as straight as three cuts in wood.  By October even the youngest could pass a smile for a sneeze. 

Our house had once belonged to the sound engineer at the radio station, whose echo chamber tunnel ran the length of our street.  He had put in the register so he could hear the local choir rehearse without getting out of his bath.  I used the tunnel myself to rehearse the parks crew for the November concert, six men in ear defenders making the beeches sing once their leaves were gone.  All I wanted that autumn was to have my girls laugh without having to pay for it.  I thought if I could get Ellen to follow the music out through the far end of the tunnel, we might have our kitchen back. 

I told the crew to stay home and enlisted the children, who knew what music she might follow.  We would call her the way she used to call them back from the ragged edge of sleep, via half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind.  The little one was mournfully eager, already wearing the red mittens her mother used to make her wear for waving to me at concerts.  The big one brought the three-minute hourglass we had used to time the spaces between spoonfuls.  She set it on the brick ledge below our register and turned it over.  It seemed that even underground we could not begin anything until after the final grain slipped through the hourglass. 

Then I pulled the starter, and the girls leaned into the wind with their song about a blackbird who had borrowed a coat.  The tune broke up against the arched bricks, and in the gaps between notes we heard the terrible wet drag.  I walked backward toward the street, drawing it after me, until my eldest reached over and killed the motor.  She had seen something I had not: the pull cord handle kept jumping out of its socket in strict time, three short strokes and a grand one.  With the motor dead it went on jumping. 

The girls had spent so long tending to the unspoken, reading in the twitch of an eyelid a wish for water or for company to go home.  My eldest took a spoon from her pocket.  She had evidently come prepared to try something quite different from an eviction.  She laid it across the jumping handle, and up it went, flashing its little signals to an orchestra of three.  Now that nobody was eating, now that there was nothing in the world a laugh could choke off, I saw how very funny I must have looked to my wife all those years.  The little one was the first to break, and this time when the choking started the big one put her arm around her sister and let her go on.  We let the sound go on too, past the point where any of us had ever let it go, until it turned the corner into Ellen's old smoker's cackle.  It had been so long since we had heard her finish a laugh. 

I had meant to lead her out into the street; instead I carried the machine home with the spoon balanced on it like a very full mouthful.  At supper the girls told their mother all the things they had been saving up that nobody else would think were funny.  She is still there under the table, and sometimes it is terrible to feel the draft on our ankles and know how little of her is left.  But we have stopped taking the spoon away.
