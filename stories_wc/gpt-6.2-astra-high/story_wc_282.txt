Every autumn my mother put me to bed with a stone tree, and every spring I woke with my thumb in its branches.  It was an elm leaf fossil, but to a child waking blind from five months of hibernation the raised veins made a tree of just the right size.  By the time my eyes cleared, I generally had enough of myself back to put it down. 

Mother had a factually mystical turn of mind: she believed the dead could bring you out of the earth, provided somebody kept their recordings dry.  For thirty years she had been the voice that guided our town through the coastal cave network from the winter beds to the fishing beach.  The spring after we buried her, she was still on all the speakers, telling us where to stoop and when we ought to smell kelp. 

I was the sensory map maker, responsible for everything on her chart except the gray stretches marked UNKNOWN, where the sea had lately cut new passages.  I wanted to become the voice of the unknown, not the daughter who went about replacing batteries in the known.  I kept that ambition quiet until the morning Mother's voice said to step down and my sounding stick went down six feet without touching anything. 

Behind me two hundred people in various stages of sight were filing out of bed, dutifully sniffing for kelp.  I cut the speaker before anybody could obey her.  My little brother Nat walked into my back and asked why I had switched her off in the middle of a word.  I had no answer he would like, so I gave him the stone tree. 

Beyond the drop, a ribbon of daylight shivered on a ceiling where my chart showed solid rock.  I stripped, tied on my sounding line, and eased myself over the lip while Nat paid out the knots between his fingers.  The winter storms had taken out a wall; from below I could see the beach through a low arch, and hear pebbles rolling in the wash.  A person could float through on the ebb without so much as kicking, if a person could bear to lie still.  That last condition was why we made sensory maps instead of simply measuring the depth of things. 

Back on the ledge I unrolled the wax cloth we used for heat-mapping emotions, its dye so sensitive that even a cold fingertip left a red print.  I scratched the passage into it and described the crossing as it passed from hand to hand; each traveler was to press hardest wherever my words frightened them most.  I expected a crimson drop or a bloody arch; the darkest prints instead came just past the lip, at the place where my nail had skipped.  Nat laid my finger against the little blank and said, "That's where you have to let go and there's nothing yet to hold you." 

I thought of all the springs I had supposed the stone helped me find my way by giving me something like a map.  It had never mattered where the branches went.  What mattered was that I could take one world with me until I felt the next one underfoot. 

I asked Nat to go first and made him a sling from the sounding line, tying the fossil into the loop he would hold against his chest.  I took the free end through the arch and up the beach; behind me the first pale face came drifting out, chin high, both hands around the stone.  When Nat felt gravel under his heels he let go, and the people on the ledge hauled the loop back for the next pair of hands.  All that ebb I stood knee deep in the wash and called, not directions, just the names of the people as they came within sight. 

In autumn I went back with a microphone to make the new recording, which plays each spring just where my mother's voice cuts out.  I made it floating on my back, talking my way from rock to water in the plainest words I could find, a stone in my fist as if I were still a child.  You can tell the very instant my heels touch sand because that is where I stop trying to sound like my mother and start to laugh.
