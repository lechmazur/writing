On the night I finally met the locksmith, I had been postmistress at the junction of all possible lives for thirty years.  Nobody ever came there in person, which was why I was in my slippers with my good teeth in a cup.  He put a little brass plate on the counter and asked if I had anything for a man who could no longer remember his mother's voice.  On the plate, in the space where a tradesman usually put his name, he had engraved the single word Locksmith. 

With my hand over my mouth I asked which mother, in which world.  He said it didn't have to be his. 

That was precisely the idea I had kept to myself for thirty years, for fear of what it might make me if I tried it and was wrong.  Behind me, in pigeonholes that went on almost as far as grief, lay the letters that could not be delivered.  Most were from people who believed, quite rightly, that somewhere their dead must still be alive.  What they forgot was that somewhere was not an address. 

Sometimes an envelope came unstuck and I saw what people would have been ashamed to put on postcards: how to get the lumps out of gravy, or which shoulder to carry a sleepy baby on.  Such small enveloped secrets, and such a waste that nobody should ever get the good of them.  But a letter was not mine to give away just because the gum had failed. 

Without asking leave, but with an air of merely straightening something crooked, the locksmith turned my counter sign from Closed to Open.  "We could start with one of yours," he said. 

I took him behind the counter, where the roof ended and the sorting aisles radiated under starlight like the spokes of a black umbrella.  To disorient thieves, the aisles slowly changed places, and after so many years I could not have found my own world's pigeonhole by looking for its number.  The trick was to remember the first star you had seen at twilight as a child, when you were small enough to stand under a grown woman's arm without making her lift it.  Mine had trembled red above a gasworks chimney, and now a red spark winked through the slats of aisle nine. 

By that hint I found the blue envelope I had posted the day before I took the job.  It was addressed to my son in whatever life he had got to be a barber.  Inside was a lock of hair tied with ribbon, the only straight cut he had managed to make before his hands began to shake.  I had written that I still went about with one side shorter than the other, in case he wanted to finish. 

I read it aloud, and in that shadowed place my voice sounded absurdly brisk, the voice you use to get a sick child interested in his breakfast.  The locksmith took the ribbon between finger and thumb and rubbed it the way my boy used to rub the satin edge of his blanket.  For a moment I wanted to ask him where he had grown up and what he had meant to be before he took to opening locks.  Then he asked me to read it again, and this time I heard the courtesy in his asking, the room he left for me to say no.  My son would have said, Again, and tugged my sleeve.  I found that I wanted to read it anyway. 

At dawn he turned his brass plate over and lent me his graver; it was a fine tool, worn smooth where the doubtful thumb would rest.  On the blank side I scratched an offer to every sender whose letter had come to rest in my care.  If you wish, I wrote, somebody else could use what you meant to give.  We set the plate in the slot for postal notices and heard its small metallic echo go out across the hush from world to world.  Some said no, and those people's envelopes are still where I put them. 

The locksmith comes on Thursdays now, and while I read to him he oils the cabinets or files a key for some drawer swollen shut with rain.  Once he brought scissors, good ones, and I bent my head and let a stranger make the two sides even.
