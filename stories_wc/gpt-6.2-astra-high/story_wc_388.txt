After the liquidation of Bell Pears, the pickers bought the orchard and inherited everything nobody wanted, including the diamond that had killed nine men.  They brought it to Nell in a matchbox, along with a hammer for use if she found the charge justified. 

Nell made her living debunking conspiracies for the valley papers; it was largely on her word that the pickers had bought.  When people said Bell had cooked the radiation figures, she had obtained the original badge films and held them up to the light herself.  Clear as Sunday gin, she had written.  Outside her window a radioactive dust storm was taking the paint off the windward shutters, and on the lee side pears knocked against the wall like customers too polite to come in.  There wasn't another green thing for forty miles. 

The men had all been photographed wearing the diamond, a tiepin with a black feather trapped inside it.  All nine had gone to the city hospital and come home in boxes.  What bothered Nell about the photographs was not the identical feather but the identical tie, with a run in the silk like a snail track.  Even the knot had not been undone.  These were not portraits of nine successive owners; somebody had slipped that loop over nine bowed heads. 

She turned the matchbox over and saw that its padding was the same pink paper the company doctor had used for his correspondence.  At the dispensary she found two sacks of it under the empty medicine shelves, shredded fine for packing ampoules against the long rail journey.  She spent the storm reassembling shredded letters on the floor, following the rusty tracks that the doctor's chipped type wheel left through every small e. 

What emerged first was an account with a city pawnbroker: nine advances against one feathered diamond, each exactly the price of a hospital deposit.  The doctor had redeemed the stone out of his wages every time.  Payday was monthly, and the train would not wait.  The photographs were receipts for the families, proof that each man had left with something worth more than the shirt he would be buried in. 

Then came pieces of a letter to the inspector, never sent, asking him to come when the pears were ripe enough to fall.  Below that ran half a sentence about a place where the fruit lay exactly as it fell.  Nell had picked here as a girl; she could still intuit the shape of the wind from the way a branch had healed around a break. 

When the shutters stopped rattling she went out past the straight commercial rows to the old spiral of seedling trees that served them as a windbreak.  Even in the lull the loose pears rolled underfoot, nudged along by little persistent fingers of air, until she reached a hollow where they lay in soft heaps around their parent trunks.  Here was the hidden stillpoint the doctor had wanted somebody to see.  In the deep lee of those interlocking boughs not even the fine red dust had settled on the white door of a little instrument hut. 

Inside hung sixty brass clips, each stamped with a payroll number, each just wide enough for a film badge.  On the bench somebody had left two films in the developer's drying rack, both bearing the same number, one clear and the other black as the doctor's ribbon.  She had held sixty honest pictures of the inside of a cupboard up to the light. 

On the way back she found herself composing the easy column, the one about a shabby doctor's private charity, and it frightened her how good it was going to be.  People would cut it out and keep it in their Bibles. 

Instead she brought the pickers to the hollow and let them find their numbers.  Nobody thanked her, least of all the woman who had put her house up against the price of the packing shed.  They voted to keep the harvest on the branches until both the pears and the people who gathered them could be tested, and to sell the diamond to pay for the tests. 

Nell took the hammer back to the dispensary, where she used it to hang a board for the results on the outside wall.  Before there was a single figure to post, she tacked up her old column, with her name still on it and room underneath.
