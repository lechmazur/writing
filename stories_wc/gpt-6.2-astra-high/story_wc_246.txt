After we planted dye flax instead of bread wheat, my father started setting the table with the plates upside down.  He said it kept the dust off, but I think he couldn't stand the sight of an empty circle.  In the blue fields below our kitchen, a hundred other families were growing enough color for the trousers of the known universe.  As the district's celestial signal interpreter, I was expected to go up to the lens and get us back our wheat. 

The lens was a gravitational anomaly about the size of a pig, suspended in a clean room above the seed exchange.  Starlight that passed through it sometimes reached our valley before the stars had actually given it off.  We had learned to send a little news upstream on that light, although we could never address it to a particular person.  Editing history was a trade conducted entirely through contingent relationships: if a woman looked up, if she recognized a signal, if she chose to wake her husband.  People said I was confidently uncertain, which meant they liked me at the instrument and disliked me at meetings. 

Father came upstairs with his guarantee pen, the very one with which he had signed for the wrong sacks without opening them.  It was a dried-up fountain pen with an exotic nib, twin splinters of blue diamond set slightly out of true, so that every stroke cast a little shadow of itself.  Even on smoked glass, where ink was unnecessary, you knew his name among a thousand names.  He wanted me to throw his signature across the exchange floor on the morning of delivery, under the single word INSPECT. 

I let him watch me unbend the brass fingers that held the old exposures rolled tight, and together we went hunting for that morning.  Nothing but cloud from the thaw to the first blue flowers, a close white wool that it was almost pleasant to run one's fingers through.  Father sat down on the floor and asked if there was any way to send an apology.  It was the first time since the flax came up that he had thought of anything to say besides how to fix it. 

I asked him who else might deserve one.  He thought a while and then he said the dyers at Salt End, who must have slit their sacks expecting flax and found our wheat.  He had never mentioned them at any of the meetings. 

Salt End lay across a tidal flat which a loaded barge could cross only on the two great tides of the year.  The autumn tide had gone out three weeks ago.  It mortified me that I, who had gone into this trade to thresh meaning from experience, had been willing to stop at the edge of a cloud.  I found that autumn night on the roll, and there in the exposure was a blue square fluttering from the roof of a barge at Salt End.  I nearly passed it by, since people hang their laundry anywhere there is a breeze.  But Father recognized the square as a freight flag, the kind flown by a captain who was heading home with an empty hold.  Somebody had even inked our valley's number on the cloth, a faint black figure I had taken for a darn. 

On the smoked glass he scratched WILL BUY WHEAT WITH FLAX, and underneath he signed the old double name.  I slid the glass into the lens and laid his bright promise along the quay where the dyers would be coming to work before the tide turned.  They could still refuse, I told him, and he said they had every right. 

That was the only transmission I ever made for him.  The lens did not ring or flare; a bill for towage simply began to poke out of his shirt pocket, and downstairs somebody sneezed in the flour.  I remembered carrying those sacks up from the landing, and I remembered turning my plate over to see if a crumb had stuck.  Both memories had weight, and I could feel them settling against each other like two sleepers in a rather narrow bed.  Father wanted to know which one he ought to keep, and for once I had a definite answer. 

At supper we set the plates upside down, then turned them over when the bread came, which is how our family sets a table to this day.
