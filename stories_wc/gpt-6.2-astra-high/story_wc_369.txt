At the moment a pin was heard in the silent corridor, Nell put her thumb on the brass counter and stopped the night's accumulation of sorrow.  It was the map pin that held up Mulberry Row; the weight of the little tin numbers threaded on it had finally pulled it out.  One hundred and six hours, she wrote on her cuff, having nowhere left on the map to write. 

The city paid Nell to maintain the labyrinth, a job that consisted mostly of oiling hinges and putting people's dreams back where they belonged.  Dreams came down the tower drains like scraps of wet paper, and a bad one carried on its margin the exact length of time the sleeper had spent alone with it.  By the time a slip had washed through all the turnings, the happy parts had dissolved, leaving only what needed someone's attention.  Every hour earned an hour of company from somebody on the municipal visiting list.  Children liked the visitors because they brought dominoes, and old men sometimes put on ties. 

Nobody lived on Mulberry Row.  Until his death Nell's father had taken every shift there, sitting under the trees with his lunch pail between his boots.  She used to pass him on her way to work and feel ashamed that her father got paid to eat pears in a public place.  Now the accounts office wanted his wages back, and she wanted to know what the trees had witnessed that he had been willing to sit with for so long. 

She took the astronomer's broken sextant off the shelf where everyone put instruments too good to throw away.  The old man had left a label on its split mirror: Heals in moonlight; do not glue.  Upstairs in the stormlit tower she opened the shutters, and between two bouts of hail the moon laid a white stitch across the glass.  By moonlight she could just read the scale. 

She went downstairs to deconstruct the city's map, easing out the pins one at a time so as not to tear the dreams impaled beneath their little tin hours.  She had been taught to file by the address visible in a dream, never by the place from which that address could be seen.  Now she set each scrap against the sextant mirror and measured the angle between the hospital chimney and its reflected tip in the canal.  By mapping scattered dreams this way she found twelve points of view, each exactly the height of a pollarded mulberry.  She pinned the scraps in overlapping strips around the tower lantern and turned the wick up slowly. 

On the whitewashed wall a man was carrying a girl whose bare foot kept knocking the buckle of his belt.  A woman followed with the other shoe.  In twelve successive views the hospital door closed on them, and that was all the trees knew of the matter.  Nell had been that girl; she still had a red crescent on her heel where the buckle had taken off the skin.  Patients who went home went out by the level door at the back, where a chair could roll straight into the yard.  She remembered her father tapping his spoon on his pail when she passed, and how grudgingly she had sometimes turned and waved.  Look, he'd been saying to those twelve anxious creatures, this one grew up. 

In the morning she took the lantern to the accounts office, where the clerk watched the bare foot swing until he reached out to steady it.  He canceled the demand for repayment, but when she asked him for a visitor he showed her the list, already full on both sides.  They spent his dinner hour hanging two looking glasses at the turn of the hospital wall, using the sextant to get the angles right.  From the row you could see a boy in a wheeled chair being scolded for working the brake himself, and a woman going home in slippers through the puddles.  The mirrors also showed a porter taking flowers out to the bins, and there wasn't anything Nell could do about that. 

That night the mulberries sent down six minutes, which Nell entered without rounding off.  She took those minutes herself the next day and ate a pear on her father's bench, holding it by the stem as he had done to spare his cuffs.
