At dawn the cobalt lighthouse was the only blue thing left in a world the color of wet flour.  The heat wave had broken in the night, and where cold rain met the baked stone of the breakwater, fog stood up like steam off bread.  Alma, the assistant lighthouse keeper, could hear her father downstairs trying to shout a boat home through the brass throat in the wall.  Each time he stopped for breath, the throat gave back a little slap of water, the sound of his voice falling short.  Out beyond the bar her sister Nell had six passengers aboard the mail launch and a bad spring tide under her.  She had been due at the breakfast table an hour ago, with a paper full of cherries for their father's birthday. 

There was a second socket in the wall, scarcely wider than a nostril, which the Board of Lights had ordered plugged after their mother died.  She had been the last keeper licensed to work the scent light, a mercy in fog so thick that even sound came apart in it.  All winter Alma had petitioned the Board for one trial, to prove that whispers travel farther than shouts.  It was not the softness that mattered, her mother used to say, but what a person could bring herself to say softly. 

From the sewing drawer Alma took a vial cork, greenish and furred from its years in the neck of a bottle of night stock.  She picked out the official wax with a darning needle and fitted the cork into the socket, where her mother had put it a thousand times.  Downstairs her father was bellowing Nell's name now, not even giving her a bearing, just the one syllable with the skin torn off it. 

Alma put her mouth to the cork and tried to beckon her sister by the scent of a garden at night.  She whispered the names of the flowers they had deadheaded by moonlight for a penny a row, stock and pinks and great white trumpets of tobacco.  Nothing went out but the dry smell of a drawer.  Under her lip she felt the vibrations of intent, two distinct pulses where there should have been one.  One went with the beat in her neck; the other had the prim little rhythm of a pen tapping a desk at the Board.  The cork purifies intentions, said the faded label on the bottle, as if the process were as painless as straining tea.  It hurt like fury to admit she wanted two things: her sister safe and six witnesses to say who had done the saving. 

Through the soles of her feet came a long grind, followed by the tinkle of a spoon falling off the breakfast table below.  The rain had brought down the scorched outer face of the breakwater; she could hear loose blocks knocking together in the fairway like bad teeth.  Her father could not hear them over the noise of his own immense and useless love. 

Their mother's garden had not been here at the light but three miles east, behind the old quarantine pier where nobody ever tied up anymore.  Nobody there to see a thing, thought Alma, and felt the second pulse go quiet under her lip. 

"Go steal us some flowers," she whispered, which was what Nell used to say when they had filled their baskets and earned their pennies. 

The cork shook so hard it split and drove a splinter into her lip, and the tower filled with the warm peppery sweetness of pinks.  Her father broke off in the middle of a cry and came upstairs with his hands held out as if somebody were about to give him a baby. 

They waited until the telephone rang, an instrument so seldom used that at first they both looked up at the clock.  Then Nell said, from the quarantine shed, that there were seven wet fools here wanting breakfast, and did anybody have a cart?  Alma's father bent his great red face to the receiver, his mouth working around all the terrible endearments he had used up on the fog.  In the smallest voice Alma had ever heard him use, he asked his girl if she had remembered his cherries.  Across three miles of dripping wire came the crackle of a paper bag held tenderly up to the phone.
