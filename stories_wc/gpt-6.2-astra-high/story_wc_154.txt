After the anesthetic took hold, the surgeon handed Ina two halves of a plaster cast and reminded her of the promise she had made its owner.  "Read it to both of them," Tavi had said, "while nobody can ask me what I meant." 

Ina found the women in the mist-clad atrium, where twilight had turned the high glass roof the color of a mussel shell.  Each had taken one end of the same bench, leaving room between them for a good-sized boy. 

Ina had spent the last island war translating across counters like the one that still stood here, in what had been the Pacific ferry terminal before it became a hospital.  Indeed she had translated at this very counter the instruction that children went aboard first, which nobody then understood to mean that there would be no second boat.  Tavi had been three then, and as far as his mother Sela knew, he had stayed three for six years.  The woman at the other end of the bench had had the ordinary privilege of watching him get big enough to fall out of a breadfruit tree.  It was the appeal for money to have his badly set elbow straightened that had brought his photograph back across the strait. 

Sela had come over on Tuesday with a brain coral fragment the size of an apricot, the only toy of his she still had.  Since Tuesday Ina had gone between the women with reports of what the doctor said and never once with anything either woman said herself. 

Now she fitted the plaster halves together on her knee.  Children in the ward signed one another's casts, but these smudged pencil notes were all in Tavi's square school hand, which gave the same generous allowance of space to every letter except a miserably narrow O.  It took her a while to unknot his system: you followed the writing around the arm, turning it like a cob of corn, and where a word was rubbed away you could sometimes read its impression in the softer plaster.  The language was the one he'd grown up in, except for the first line, taken down by ear from Sela and almost lost in the cut the surgeon had made.  It said, very formally, "When you had to sleep in that big boat, what did you do for your poor fingers?" 

Ina looked up; Sela had been worrying the coral in her lap, but she laid it now on the vacant stretch of bench.  One whole ridge had been worn smooth by a little finger traveling it to get to sleep, Sela said, and traced the route for them.  At the end there was a pit where the finger could curl and spend the night. 

The answer, halfway down the cast, was a drawing of a fist with a thumb sticking out between two fingers.  Ruth, the woman who had raised him, made the shape with her own hand; on the crossing he had slept with his hand wound up in hers. 

"By the thumb," Ruth told Ina, who turned the plaster and found, tucked into the crease, a question in Tavi's own shrewdly innocent manner: "What if the one I ask for is on the toilet?" 

Ruth laughed first, shutting her mouth almost at once as if something precious might get out.  Ina could have told them that of all the things a boy might fear on waking, he had chosen the one he could bear to put into words.  But she'd had enough of telling people what other people meant; she'd come back to work in this building because she wanted the words she gave them here to do something besides divide families into those who went and those who stayed.  She put down the cast and asked if there was anything either wanted translated. 

"I don't know how to put him to bed," Sela said. 

"I don't know how to let him go to bed anywhere else," said Ruth. 

They sat with these two pieces of news while a cleaner drew a dry stripe across the streaming floor.  Then Ruth picked up the coral and tried the long smooth road with her forefinger, getting lost at a turn so slight that Sela had to take her hand to show her.  When the nurse came out to fetch them, they were still holding hands, though by now it was Ruth who was teaching Sela how to make the little berth in which a boy had crossed the strait.
