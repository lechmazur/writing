The day the plague ended, a pilot came down into my sewer and put a seafarer's chronometer cog on my table, which was also my door laid across two barrels.  She pushed it with one finger; it rolled until it came to the place where three teeth were missing, then sat down.  "That," she said, "is how far I got this morning." 

Beyond the mouth of the harbor there were nine days of open water to the islands that had promised us seed grain.  She could find her latitude by the sun, but for longitude she wanted a clock that kept the time of the port she had left.  I wanted to be a philosopher, a profession for which the sewer supplied both leisure and a fairly rigorous set of objections to the idea of providence.  My actual trade had been sweeping the floor of my father's clock shop.  She had come to the wrong member of the family, as I supposed she knew. 

According to my father all our fine wheels came in little blue envelopes from across the water; according to the women who swept upstairs, the envelopes went out full as often as they came in.  I had believed my father because he could mend a clock and because the alternative was to think about why I swept downstairs while our Nell swept upstairs.  The fever took them both the same afternoon, leaving me free to believe whichever of them required the least of me. 

The pilot put a blue envelope on the table, inside out, with my sister's sums on the seams.  I knew the writing; nobody else did division like a descending flight of geese.  The harbor master wanted proof of local manufacture before he would pay for local work, a distinction he could afford to make because he had a garden. 

The permit nailed to the sewer mouth forbade the disturbance of the sunken city ruins, on the excellent ground that a roof under water could still fall on your head.  It said nothing about what a man did in a sound brick sewer directly beneath that roof.  I took the door off the barrels and used it to cross the gap to the old shop drain.  Every bench in the building had emptied its sweepings down that drain so my father could catch the brass in a settling box and sell it twice. 

Working through sedimentary layers with a kitchen slice, I took off black plague muck, gray mortar from the collapse of the upper story, then a lovely green paste that stained my fingers.  In it lay a brass disk with one tooth cut too deep, another with two teeth run together, another as thin as a communion wafer.  These were not the leavings of a woman who only swept.  Neither were they instructions, and it cost me something to climb out and tell the pilot that. 

I asked who had taught Nell.  The pilot named a woman who sold boiled eggs at the quarantine gate and whom I had passed every day for eleven years.  I began to agonize over what I should say to her about my father, but the pilot took back her wheel and went to buy an egg. 

The bad blanks satisfied the harbor master; they did not satisfy the egg woman, who wanted to know what good a fee in the city's paper money would do her hens.  The pilot settled on her a share of the grain.  Only one point remained unsettled: the egg woman needed a pair of eyes that could still tell daylight between a tooth and its gauge.  "Yours will do," she told me, which remains the only flattering assessment ever made of my equipment for philosophy. 

She could supply a wheel in two days, she explained without heat, or in five days a wheel and somebody who could make another.  We put the choice to the pilot, whose ship it was and whose neck would be out there on the water.  She gave us five. 

For five days we divided circles the old way, stepping a pair of dividers round a smoked plate until the last black interval exactly matched the first.  On the fifth evening I delivered the wheel in a blue envelope, with the names of my teachers written on the outside where a name belongs.  I still live in the sewer, but I have bought a table; it was tiresome taking the door down every time a pupil knocked.
