After the nostalgia ban, people who woke with writing on their skin had until sundown to make it fledge.  A living memory commonly took the shape of the hand that had once tucked you in; a dead one wrote in that hand.  Mara could draw either kind off the body without drawing blood.  She had been the last tide dancer at the public baths, where, before there was anything to prohibit, people came for relief from the sheer weight of their dead.  She would set a basin rocking with her bare heel until the water ran in time with the small blue tide at a client's wrist.  Then the black script would lift, stroke by stroke, and gather into something with wings. 

Nobody had thought to ask where it went until her son came home with both arms black to the elbow.  Through the open door she saw the twilight garden under stars, and above the white stocks a flock so dense that she could measure its slow wheel only by observing gaps.  He had been standing out there with his sleeves rolled up. 

"They want to land," he said. 

He was seventeen, old enough to sign the book of elective exile at the eastern gate, not old enough to remember why the city had begun coaxing memories out of people.  It had begun with a widow who went on setting two plates; by the end whole families sat down to supper and did not know whose chair stood empty.  On his wrist Mara recognized her own mother's tiny capitals, the shopping lists that had always ended with a little extravagance squeezed in sideways.  PEARS IF RIPE, she read, and her mouth filled with water. 

"I thought you wanted a clean arm for your examination," she said.  As an apprentice skywriter he would have to pen a cosmic verse on mortal skin, one a pilot could read by touch in the dark to find the harbor.  Nobody had ever hired a pilot whose chart was spoiled by somebody's grocery bill. 

He put her finger on a word that had spontaneously anchored in the crook of his elbow, its downstroke already palpable as a thorn.  It was her husband's hand this time, the big backward slant of a man forever pushing a pen uphill. 

"Read it," the boy said, but she drew back. 

"I had a father too," he said. 

From his pocket he took the moonstone cabochon his father had worn at his throat when he went out with the harbor pilots.  He had ground a notch in its milky dome; on his thumb a speck of the dust gave off the cold light by which charts were pricked into skin. 

"What if the harbor isn't where you want to go?" 

Then she understood what he wanted from her, and what he did not.  He had no use for a clean arm; he needed the words moved just far enough apart that the stars could be set among them. 

"Which harbor?" she asked. 

"The cove where the exiles put in," he said, and she remembered that his father had gone out one winter night to sound its bar and never come back.  She bent again to the sentence she had been afraid would be a farewell; it said, almost crossly, Keep east of the bar in winter.  Stars to find the cove, his father's words to bring a keel safe over the bar: he wanted to pilot people in. 

She took the basin outside and worked barefoot in the falling dark, easing a little water first one way and then the other, keeping each black stroke afloat without letting it fly.  Her son pricked the stars into the water lanes between the words, using his father's stone and a thorn from a rose.  Now and again she felt the old professional itch to finish the job and send that beautiful black trouble streaming into the night.  Instead she let the water still. 

By morning there was a way into the cove that any blind hand could follow, and a boy with no legal way to stay at home.  He turned his arm over and on the soft inside she found two places where stars were missing. 

"I couldn't reach," he admitted. 

She took the thorn and set them in. 

At the eastern gate he signed first; she let him get as far as the boat before she signed below him.  Somebody there would have to teach the art of taking the weight off without taking everything, and somebody would have to buy pears.
