In Echo Gorge the sun rose twice, first as a light under the river and an hour later as a coin above the rim.  In the hush of that hidden sunrise, Ada sat on the ferry steps beside a baker whose small daughter had been dead eleven months.  He had brought a crocodile's tooth in a twist of paper, as carefully as if the tooth itself could still feel pain.  Every morning just before his ovens caught, he heard her say the same three words up the chimney: I spoiled it. 

As the district's lost soul collector, Ada had heard spectral confessions come up through drains and once out of a loaf when a knife broke its crust.  Mostly the dead stuck where the living stuck, at some small terrible piece of a day neither party could get past.  The river carried the rest of the day down here. 

She unwrapped the tooth, worn brown at the root from handling, and laid it on the sounding board of her harp.  Its neck had twisted in the damp so that every string sounded a shade lower than its neighbor expected.  Her predecessor had charged her to have it straightened; instead she had learned to pursue the sounds that slipped between its proper notes.  Once, behind a soldier's long apology to his widow, she had caught the unmistakable squeak of a bed.  The widow had laughed until she could breathe again, and Ada had been trying ever since to bring that mercy into her work. 

She plucked a string and the tooth danced sharp end down like a little white foot testing hot sand.  Out came the three words in a high, affronted voice.  Then through the distorted notes of the misaligned harp came a second noise, like somebody trying to start a reluctant pump.  The baker snatched for the tooth and missed.  Across the gorge something enormous began to wheeze, and the old iron ferry cable shook showers of rust into the water.  Ada was modestly fearless, which is to say she tied herself to the rail before she leaned out over the drop to listen.  Under the racket she could hear a woman saying, in the exhausted, particular tone of mothers everywhere, Give your father back his tooth. 

The baker sat down hard on the wet step.  It had been the last day the girl could sit up, he said, and they had paid a man to come with his camera.  She had wanted the three of them together, handsome as the family on the flour sacks, with their hands folded on their laps.  He had lost an incisor as a boy, and the crocodile fang was what he wore in the gap when she begged him to be the beast that ate bad buns.  Just as the man took the cap off the lens she had slipped the tooth under her lip, and her father had gone off like a pump.  On the glass there was a child between two blurs, already an orphan in the only picture they would ever have.  He had asked the man to scrape it clean. 

"I tell her I forgive her every morning," he said.  "She never seems to hear." 

Below them the light was draining out of the river as the real sun made its way up the cliff.  Ada wanted to tell him what to say, but she had heard enough dead people to know the sound of somebody else's words in a mouth.  She set the harp in his lap and let him feel the string that was still shaking with his own ridiculous noise.  He sat until the iron cable stopped its singing.  Then, very softly, he told the tooth he was sorry he had thrown their picture away. 

"Your mother moved too," he said, as if this had only just occurred to him. 

The sound that got out of him then was not the great undignified hee-haw that had spoiled a plate, but its small, recognizable descendant.  From the harp came an answering snort, horribly like his, and the tooth rolled quietly into his palm. 

Ada left him on the step to wrap it up or pitch it in as he pleased.  Halfway home she passed the bakehouse, where his wife was pulling the first batch out alone.  Above the chimney the air was clear and trembling, as it is above anything warm on a cold morning, and there was nothing in it anybody had to answer for.
