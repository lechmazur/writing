Until the railway went through, the sleepwalking constellations used to come down to drink and go home with mud on their paws.  Nobody minded a little mud falling out of the sky.  Then the trains began to whistle in the same two notes with which a bitch star calls her litter, and the little ones began to follow the night freight east.  By the third morning you could look straight through the ribs of the Great Dog at the dark where her milk used to be. 

"Change the whistle," Nell told the superintendent.  He took her into the yards and let her pull the cord on an engine cold since Tuesday.  Nothing came out of the whistle, but the rails sang both notes all the way down to the sea.  It was Nell who had sold the company the right to lay iron through her sound wave garden, where anything that sounded once took root and grew.  That money had put a watertight roof over her father's bed, and by God she would take it again.  But the sound was hers to weed. 

Before he lost the use of his hands, her father had fished that garden for wedding songs and words the dying had spent their last breath trying to finish.  From him Nell had learned the cool art of letting a line run even when what was on the other end was screaming. 

"You'll want their mother," he said when she laid the trouble before him.  He meant the goddess whose first litter had scattered burning milk across the dark and whose lullaby lay at the root of every song that put a beast to bed. 

To catch the echo of a goddess you went fishing at the turn of the tide, when patterns break themselves and even the oldest tunes come loose.  Nell bent a rail spike into a hook in the yard forge so that whatever she caught would run in iron as readily as the whistle did.  At slack water she stood among the reeds of old rain and cast into a thicket grown from the whimpering of pups.  Out along the tracks seven white muzzles swung east in their sleep. 

Something took the hook so gently that the first joint of her finger barely dipped.  A good fisher transliterates emotions from the script of the line into that of the mouth without changing so much as an accent.  Nell felt the long pull of love and the two short jerks of alarm and gave them back as a low croon broken twice by teeth.  The seven lifted their paws in time with her voice and marched on east.  She had caught their mother's fear of losing them, which was no different from a whistle if all you heard was the hurry. 

She let it run. 

Underneath the tugging there came a little interval of perfect ease, such as a child knows with its face buried in fur.  It was the part of the lullaby no one remembered because everyone who had ever heard it had been asleep.  Nell caught it by voicing silence the way her father did now when he could no longer lift a hand to soothe her: he opened his mouth against her hair and let her feel the warm dark of his breath. 

The spike came up heavy with that hush.  She drove it home with a stone just as the freight came through, and the hush went east ahead of the engine, taking the hurry out of the rails.  For the first time in weeks the pups began to zigzag, nosing after field mice made of sparks and snapping at one another's tails.  Nell watched them all the way to the water and sat there till the smallest had drunk its fill and scrambled up the dark. 

When she got home her father was asleep under his good roof, one crooked hand open on the quilt.  A little mud had come in through the window.
