In the hush of improbable beginnings, when the dark was just turning back into water, Linn heard a book come unstitched.  She knew the sound because she was the odd-hour librarian, the one they paid to sit with the atlas while other people slept.  Its pages held the places drowned in the whispering archipelago, each one kept above water by somebody's recurring dream.  Now there was a white rectangle where the island of Bell had been, and a smell of pears ripening in an empty room.  Linn put on her boots before she let herself know what this meant about her father. 

Downstairs his bed was still warm, though the hand beside his face was cold as the china dish that held his hearing aid.  She set the dish on the sill for the neighbors to see, the custom on these islands where a spoken word could follow the water for miles.  Then she took his watercolor palette and the library camera and went down to the slip.  To capture a runaway dream you had until the sun cleared the horizon; after that anybody might see it, and no two people would see the same thing. 

Her father had taught her to read the dreams of Bell through sunrise photography, which was how he had made the atlas page in the first place.  A dream paints with light, he used to say, but it needs a good dark box to paint the inside of.  Every exposure he had made showed the same green slope, the same pear trees, and his wife coming uphill through the grass with her shoes in one hand.  Linn had slept in a drawer in the evacuation boat; that barefoot woman was the whole of what she knew of her mother. 

From the slip she could see a green bar moving against the tide, already too far out to catch with oars.  She opened the tin palette on her knees.  Under each cake of paint her father had scratched a sign, his private cipher of longing: the fork of a tree under green, the slant of a roof under red.  As a child she had thought the little ladder under yellow meant a ladder, until he painted her the bars of sunlight falling through the slats of a shutter.  He had missed the afternoons more than he had missed the house. 

She wet her thumb and drew a yellow ladder on the white lid, then tipped it toward the water.  The green bar faltered, broke into trees, and came so close she could hear the soft successive thuds of pears dropping into grass.  Between two trunks appeared the woman with her shoes, but instead of climbing she was standing still, looking past Linn at the lit window above the slip.  Linn turned the camera on its tripod until she had the woman framed exactly as her father had always framed her. 

It would take one click to vitalize the empty page for another lifetime, and for the length of a breath she wanted nothing else.  Then the woman's hand went up, not in greeting but to shade her eyes, and Linn understood that from under those trees her mother had never seen this shore.  All her father had ever asked of the light was that it come to him. 

She stepped into the slip up to her knees, lifted the tripod over the bright seam in the water, and set it down on grass.  For one shutter beat she stood beside her mother and took the picture facing home. 

When she developed the plate there was no woman in it, no pears, only a narrow house with the light on and a little china dish like a moon caught on the sill.  At the bottom, where the sun had just reached the slip, two long shadows touched at the shoulders.  Linn stitched the print into the atlas and wrote Bell under it, not because it showed the island but because it could not have been taken from anywhere else.  By then the neighbors were downstairs with clean sheets and bread, speaking softly to the dead man whose deafness they no longer needed to remember.  She left the book open to dry and went down to let them feed her.
