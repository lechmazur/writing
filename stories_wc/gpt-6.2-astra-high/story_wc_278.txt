At the laboratory of lost sounds, Nell took off her coat but kept her tattoo needles in her pocket.  She was a constellation tattooist, the kind sailors once paid to put a scrap of home sky where a cuff could cover it.  Tonight she wanted the square of August dark that had hung above her brother's crib on the last night of his life. 

On the bench stood the nursery monitor, a little box that translated baby cries into words a tired person could act on.  Since Kit died its haunted firmware had acquired one more word than the maker listed in the manual.  At odd hours the screen lit up with NELL, even after she took the batteries out. 

She had been sixteen that August, up on the roof with a boy who knew nothing about stars and let her tell him everything.  Downstairs her mother had done alone what the hospice nurse had promised neither of them would have to do alone.  Nell had brought the box here to hush the dissonance of that old guilt, the thin note of her name against the silence she had sent back. 

Beside it stood the antique writing desk Nell's mother had used to change both her babies on.  Nell had found the first of the handwritten letters when a drawer stuck, and the rest by taking the desk apart.  All were in her mother's hand, on the backs of hospice bills, and all began with the same impossible salutation: Dear Older Nell.  They described an annual instant during the meteor shower when time folds in on itself and the drawer opens on the same room twice.  In the last letter her mother had asked her to send the sky down.  Not one mentioned the boy. 

Through the laboratory skylight a grain of light fell without disturbing the dust on the bench.  The drawer eased out, and out of the monitor came the dry hitch of a breath that Nell remembered better than any voice.  To tether the two rooms, she laid across the lip of the drawer the piece of amber her mother had worn at her throat.  Inside it a winged ant stood poised for its first flight, one wing still creased like a shirt from a suitcase.  Another piece of amber swung up from the dark below, and the two ants fitted wing to wing until there was only one. 

She could see her mother's chin now, the red groove the pendant cord had worn in the soft flesh beneath it.  The screen said NELL.  She bent close enough to see that Kit was asleep with his mouth open and that the sound the machine was translating came from the woman holding him. 

Her mother's other hand slid a hospice bill into the drawer.  On it she had written, "He can't get up to the roof, love." 

Nell laid the bill flat on the bench, and her hand stopped shaking as soon as the needle went in.  She knew the distances by heart, the little skew in the cross of Cygnus, how much paper to leave unpierced around the bright eye of the swan.  When she fed the sheet back through, her mother held it over the nightlight, and a scattering of faint stars climbed the plaster above the crib.  Kit never opened his eyes.  His mother looked up, though, and in the cold little field of light Nell saw the girl somebody might once have taken onto a roof. 

She wanted to say she was sorry, but there was no sense making her mother spend any of the time left telling her it was all right.  Instead she slipped her hand into the drawer and took the weight of her brother's head while her mother moved a cramped arm.  They stayed like that until the amber dropped onto the bench with the small ordinary sound of a button coming off a coat.  The monitor went dark.  She had never held him long enough before to have the weight of him stay in her arm after he was gone.
