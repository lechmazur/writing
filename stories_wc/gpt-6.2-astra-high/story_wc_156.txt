"Shall I mix the cement?" 

Tess held the question on the end of her tongue as if her apprentice might need it back.  In his palm lay the hobo nickel her grandfather had carved on a freight train, according to the story that had earned her this commission.  The nickel had started out with a buffalo on one side and a man's profile on the other; the buffalo remained, but the man now wore a cauliflower ear and a cap with a broken peak.  Just such a cap adorned the enormous sleeping tramp whose open hand Tess had spent the summer coaxing out from under green municipal paint.  The round hollow in that hand was exactly five cents wide. 

The sound wave garden had been laid out when freight still came through town.  Its curved brick walls threw a whisper from the lilac walk to the fishpond without troubling anyone on the path between.  Men waiting for a ride had covered those walls with pictures in exchange for supper, her grandfather's sleeper being the last picture you saw before the tracks.  Tess, a woman of violent orange braids and an almost surgical delicacy of touch, had made a game of finding the old listening spots.  Every morning she advanced one paving stone, announced the name of a pigment, and waited to hear which flower bed would say it back.  She could bewitch a whole class of schoolchildren into dead silence by making the sleeper murmur burnt umber through reflected sound.  Now the sleeper sent back the little tick the nickel had made against her apprentice's wedding ring. 

At that tick she woke on a bench with a newspaper over her knees.  The garden was open; the coin was fixed; a little girl was trying to prise it loose with a lolly stick.  Tess got up to stop her, and found she had grown so old in her sleep that the child had time to finish the job.  Out came the coin with a plug of painted plaster stuck to it like the flesh on a pulled tooth.  The child was crying, not because she was in trouble but because she had only wanted to turn the buffalo over and give him some air. 

Another tick, and Tess woke from the bench dream under her grandfather's worktable, where she used to fall asleep on the warm sack of sand he kept for scouring.  Up through a knothole she could see his good eye, enlarged by the lens he wore for carving nickels when the trains no longer ran.  His lips were moving and she knew what he would say because she had told it so often at council meetings: One day you'll put me back where I belong.  But the table threw his voice at the floor, which tossed it into her ear the way the lilacs tossed a whisper to the fishpond.  What she heard was the small sound he made when he didn't know what he was going to cut next, a whistle between the two teeth that didn't meet. 

She woke standing up with the last reflection of the tick still traveling among the bricks.  Her apprentice had not yet drawn breath after his question.  The nickel in his hand was the only thing in the garden she had never examined as a restorer, and now she saw what the family thumb had done to its edges.  They shone like the lip of the hollow; things put away for good did not get that shine.  Perhaps men had stood here trying their own nickels in the sleeper's palm, just to see how it felt to give instead of take.  Already she could hear herself telling that one at a meeting, moving the council ladies to tears, shutting the little door of the story with its satisfactory click. 

She wanted, quite suddenly, to be the person on the other side of that door, still wondering.  Her apprentice, who played the drums on his days off, was holding the nickel now the way he held a brush against a cymbal. 

"Leave the cement," she said, "and give me time to get to the lilacs before you play me whatever you're hearing."
