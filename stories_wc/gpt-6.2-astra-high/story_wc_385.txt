At the Foundlings' Vigil, I earned my supper by singing the first note for people too frightened to find it.  I was a canyon echo singer, which meant knowing the difference between a silence that wanted filling and one that had cost somebody years to make.  Yet I had never understood why some people echoed.  One mother would sing into the gorge and get back every hitch in her breathing; the next might as well have sung into wool. 

My own mother, who had taken me from the flood nursery as a baby, came every year to listen, never to sing.  This year she brought the brass key to the nursery register and a whispering seashell half on a string.  She had been keeper of the register for thirty years and had never before let that key outside her dress. 

"Tomorrow they pulp the book," she said, laying the cold shell against my ear.  "Tonight you can learn what it leaves out." 

Below us the gorge divided and doubled back until each branch lay cheek to cheek with another, a serpentine nexus under starlight.  Down there, while the universe hums its secret song, the stone sometimes takes up the tune; that is what they tell the children.  What I heard through the shell was a boy trying not to sneeze. 

My mother pushed aside a curtain of reeds and let me see his bare heels on a ledge below ours.  A woman called across the gorge in the flat, high voice of somebody calling chickens out of rain.  The boy called back, getting the chickens exactly right.  He had practiced. 

Over the next hour my mother gave me the gorge the way she had given me my history, one piece at a time, waiting to see what I did with each.  First the children on the ledges, some of them grown and gone gray; then the boatmen who taught them the calls their first mothers remembered making.  Last came the nursery matrons, who slipped the boatmen scraps of paper telling them which doors to knock on and which never to approach.  In a town otherwise remarkable for its inability to keep a surprise birthday supper secret, these webs of intrigue had held for thirty years. 

"Why not just give us the names?" I asked. 

She turned the shell so I could hear the woman calling chickens begin to sob, not prettily, but with the rude gulp of a pump drawing mud.  "Her husband thinks the child drowned," she said.  "The boy's fathers think he's out fishing." 

The flood judges had made quick, permanent work of placing hungry children; an empty cupboard counted as abandonment, even in a house without a roof.  Tomorrow, with the register gone, there would be no way left to find each other except through people who already knew.  And tomorrow my mother's hands, already too twisted to fit the key into its lock unaided, would have nothing left to guard.  I began to understand why she had brought me, and to wish I had been a little slower about understanding. 

From the lowest branch came three descending notes, reed-blown and melancholy, the little tune my mother used to cool my porridge, blowing between spoonfuls.  This time she did not turn the shell for me. 

"You learned it from her," I said. 

She nodded, then took a breath to answer the call herself.  How many years had she stood behind me answering for a child who hadn't even known enough to keep silent? 

"Don't," I said, and she stopped with her mouth still shaped around the note. 

Across the gorge the woman waited.  I could ensure that she went home comforted, but comfort had been arranged for all three of us long enough.  I sang the tune as I remembered it, with the impatient extra note I used to add when I wanted the spoon.  After a moment the new note came back, uncertain and a little sharp.  It was the first echo I had ever had. 

At dawn I unlocked the nursery and carried the register down to the boatmen before the pulping cart arrived.  Beside each name we would leave room for two marks; only where both people made theirs would we tell either where to go.  My mother sat beside me to turn the pages, wetting her crooked finger as she had done over every story she had ever read me.  Against my own name I made one mark and left the ink to dry.
