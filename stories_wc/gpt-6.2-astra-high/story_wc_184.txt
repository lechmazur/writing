On the day they buried her husband, a door opened in the university clock tower mechanism room after the hour for doors was past.  By treaty, at noon on a burial day the clock opened for one minute onto the place where the dead person's absence would be felt most keenly.  At noon it had opened onto Ruth's kitchen, where she had stood with her good black gloves on, holding a peeled onion she had no idea what to do with.  She had gone up afterward to sign the register, an archivist's last small service to a husband who had conducted his affairs largely on the backs of envelopes. 

Now it was twelve past, and through the gap between the great descending weights she could see thirty boys sitting down to soup.  She knew the bowls by their blue rims and the boys by their shaved heads; it had never occurred to her to wonder what the inside of the town jail looked like.  One boy put his hand up, the way you did in school, and asked if she had got the onion. 

Ruth was the university archivist, and of all the habits her husband had liked to tease her about, the one he had most admired was her way with a muddle.  She would put the kettle on, set out as many cups as there were competing accounts, and give each teller time to finish.  This time she sat on the oily floor so the boy could see her face and said that yes, she had it, but she had lost the instructions.  That made him smile, and presently she knew what to do with the onion and rather more about her marriage than she had at breakfast. 

Every Thursday for nineteen years, while she thought he was playing skittles, Daniel had taught the kitchen detail how to cook something they could make for a person they loved when they got out.  He had gone in the first time to see his own brother, who had asked him not to tell Ruth where he was.  The brother was long out and living respectably in Hull; the Thursday lessons had continued because there was always another boy.  Today they were making the soup Daniel had made for her on their wedding night, when all the shops were shut.  The boys had sent her an onion from their sack so she could sit down to the same meal. 

The gap was narrowing, and somebody at the back was asking the others to describe her before she disappeared.  In the archive there was a shelf of what the old clock keepers called fables of absence, stories which began not with a creature but with the hole it left.  In one, a hen left a hollow in the straw and a silence in the yard, and a fox who went looking for two hens went hungry.  Ruth had catalogued it under Arithmetic, for want of anywhere better.  Now she looked at the two loose ends of the centuries-old ceremonial rope that coupled the striking train to the doors, and saw what the fox had missed. 

The diplomat's wax seal impression on the treaty showed a rope between two mourners, but the engraver who copied it for the clock keepers' manual had tidied the rope into a border.  Ruth had handled the original; she knew where one mourner's strand crossed over and went into the other's hand.  She could close this irregular door, as the manual required, or she could follow the older drawing and merge the two places without giving either precedence.  She took off her gloves. 

Working from the ends inward, she loosened the stiff braids and passed an equal number of strands from each through the other, until the pull that had been coming in two successive tugs came all at once.  The weights settled; the oily floor gave way under her to the flags of her kitchen, which extended quite naturally under thirty pairs of dangling boots.  One boy made room for her at the bench and another passed the knife.  She cut the onion as instructed, very thin across the grain, and scattered the rings on the soup so that the first mouthful was sharp and the rest was sweet. 

After a minute the bench went away, but not before she had asked what time they ate on Thursdays, or before somebody had thought to ask whether she could teach them anything.  Well, she had said, there was always skittles.
