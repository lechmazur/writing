By the time words became unnecessary between Ada and Kit, they had been hauling things up the whispering bluff for forty years.  Below their house the clouds lay flat as a made bed, but air coming up through the split rock kept up a small domestic hiss.  That was where Ada tuned her bells, in the one place where a weak note had something to contend with.  Kit built the moving frames that carried them, and together they made what the people on the coast called symphonies of truth.  A widower brought them the worn tread of a stair, for instance, and Kit mounted it on rockers so that a visitor's weight rang the exact two notes of a wife running down to open the door.  Even her children had turned at the sound. 

The spring that Kit began to lose her sight, she sent a fisherman out for a piece of an iceberg, the bluest he could get.  The first time she saw Ada, they had been on opposite rails of a ferry when a berg rolled over, lifting into daylight a blue that brought everybody to the same rail.  The deck listed and they caught each other by the elbows.  For years that blue had been their private measure of the capacity of an ordinary Tuesday to astonish.  Kit wanted to keep the color, not a likeness of their two young faces; she pushed the photograph Ada offered back across the table.  Ada, mute as she was every spring when the yellow pines shed pollen, had no way to hum the chord she was considering.  She laid three tuning forks against Kit's wrist instead.  Kit withdrew her hand.  Anybody could give a color three pretty notes and expect a blind woman to take it on trust. 

The iceberg fragment arrived in wet sacking, a wedge as long as Ada's forearm and as colorless on the kitchen table as a lump of tallow.  Kit held it to the window endwise, making the light travel the length of the wedge, and the faintest wash of blue came into it.  Ada saw it glisten on the tip of her wife's nose before the weight dragged Kit's arm down and it was gone.  She took Kit's elbow, and there they were on the listing deck.  What they had to keep was the turn. 

They had until the ice melted to get the timing right.  Kit fetched the tilting cradle of an old ship's compass and Ada knocked out the pin that kept it from swinging too far.  On one end they lashed the ice; on the other Kit hung a copper tube, to which Ada added lead washers until the whole affair rocked under one finger.  On the sill it rolled the wedge from white to blue and back again, but Kit had to stoop so close to follow the change that her breath clouded the ice.  Ada fed a brass bead into the tube and capped each end with a bell, one felted, one bare.  As the cradle tipped the bead ran down the tube with a sound like all the small change in a hundred ferry passengers' pockets sliding to one side.  It struck the bare bell just as the blue showed through. 

Kit laughed, then shook her head and took a file to the bell herself.  She wanted less sweetness, more of the shock of a cold hand closing on an elbow.  Ada steadied the rim and let her work.  Between strokes they tested it against the dwindling blue until Kit stopped looking at the ice and reached for Ada at precisely the right instant. 

By supper there was nothing left of the ice but a puddle on the sill.  Kit balanced the cradle again with a smooth beach stone, and they set it on the porch where a caller looking for the knocker would have to give it a push instead.  In the years when Kit could no longer see even the white of the clouds, she would hear that brief bright upset and put her hand out across the table.  Before Ada went to see who it was, she let the table list a little under their elbows.
