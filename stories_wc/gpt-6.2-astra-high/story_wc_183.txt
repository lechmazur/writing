I went back to the Mercy for my mother's name, which I had sold for a quarter to a man with salt sores on his hands.  The abandoned carnival ship was drifting in a foggy bay, her paddle turning now and then as if somebody in the engine room were having a bad night.  I hooked my skiff to the old ticket window and climbed in where the money used to go.  My sign still hung above the change drawer: ADA VOSS, CHIMERA TWIN DISCOVERER. 

I had discovered precisely one twin, my own, when a clinic found that the blood in my veins and the skin of my cheek came from different fertilized eggs.  I was blissfully aberrant at first; I walked home from that appointment feeling that nobody who ever saw me eating supper alone would know the half of it.  It was my mother, who made the costumes on the Mercy, who thought other lonely people might pay a little for that feeling.  She stitched a curtain for my booth out of scraps from the lining of everybody's pockets, so a customer reaching through the slit had the pleasant sensation of finding something.  I would take that outstretched hand and whisper a name, the supposed name of the child whose little life had folded up inside theirs. 

I never used a name I knew until the last night, when the man with the sores put his hand through and I saw my mother's silver thimble on his thumb.  He had bought it at the auction of her effects that morning, but for one foolish instant I thought it was her hand.  As I said her name a green thread ran out of the curtain and wound itself around his wrist.  By the time he got ashore all I could remember of the word was the shape my mouth made letting it go. 

I had come to find the garden of forgotten names, which my mother said was somewhere on this ship and which I had always understood to mean the bar.  I brought an emerald swatch card from her workbox, the only sample with a notch bitten out of it by my own milk teeth.  It was the color of the thread I had seen go, and when I held it to the curtain the loose end lifted like a worm tasting rain.  I tugged and heard a man's voice saying that his sister would have had his hands but none of his nasty temper.  Here was a thing I hadn't bargained for: people had gone away and made up whole lives for the names I sold them. 

The only way through the curtain was through those narrative threads, following a voice as far as it went and picking up another where they crossed.  As memories alter in the telling, so the passages belowdecks changed; I stepped out of a sister's kitchen into a brother's long orchard, though the whole walk took less than a fathom of green silk.  Under the paddle box the orchard dwindled to a garden of dwarf pears, each fruit tied up in muslin against the wasps, each the size of a quarter.  Through a split seam I smelled the brown smell of my mother's iron and heard her spit sizzle on its heel.  I put my lips to the split, ready to suck back what was mine, and from the other side a dry thumb pressed against my mouth. 

He was out there somewhere, in his bunk perhaps, telling her what kind of day he'd had.  To recapture her name I had only to bite the silk as I had bitten the swatch, but then what would he have in all that salt dark?  For the first time I wondered what my mother had put in those pockets for herself.  She had never come to hear a name.  It had been enough for her to sit on the other side of the curtain, hemming it up where people's boots wore it away, listening to me earn our bread. 

I threaded the swatch through the split and worked it back and forth until the notch caught and held the edges shut.  When I climbed out, the fog was lifting; you could see the whole bay and the two arms of land that kept it a bay.  I could have gone anywhere inside those arms.  I knew where the name was now, and I let that unkindled solace keep me company on the long pull in.
