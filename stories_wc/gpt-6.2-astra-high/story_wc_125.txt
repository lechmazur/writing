After the energy beings arrived, people took to carrying worn things instead of photographs.  In the terra-cotta grotto by the old prison road, you could lay a thing in a hollow and hear again the minute that had worn it smooth.  The hollow had been a saint's ear before the stars began coming down at night to drink the heat stored in the clay.  Nobody called them stars anymore except me, and I had a professional interest in keeping a few mistakes alive. 

For twenty years I had drifted from one roadside stall to another, writing love poems for the price of lunch and apologies for twice that.  The second lunch went to the offended party; I had effected some tender reconciliations over a pork pie.  When my niece Kit found me, she set two pies on my folding table and put a frayed coil of rope between them.  "I don't know which of us owes the other," she said.  She had her mother's trick of pushing away her plate when what she wanted was to eat. 

Her mother and I had tried to escape together, which until that night I had believed was the only honest way a person could get free.  I went first with the baby buttoned inside my coat; halfway down I felt the rope begin to travel back up.  Above me a guard had hold of my sister and my sister had hold of the rope.  If I hung on, he would have all three of us; if I dropped, she would have to go back inside without her child.  I dropped, and presently the rope fell on us like a whole extra person whose weight was all wrong.  That was all Kit knew except that her mother had died inside, and it was all I knew except for one thing.  My sister had called something down to me, and for twenty years I had been free to decide what. 

Kit had brought the rope to hear her mother making it.  She had heard from the laundry women how we used to pattern the stolen hemp with bits of colored thread, so that uncoiled on the dormitory floor it looked like unfinished festival bunting.  While we worked my sister sang rude words to the festival hymn, invariably rhyming the governor's name with itself because it annoyed him to be mentioned twice. 

"You want that instead of what she said about you?" I asked. 

"I'd like one thing she didn't say about me." 

Under the starlight the ear was a dull red shell big enough to sleep in, polished at its lip by the things people had surrendered.  Whatever part you laid against it would sound, and then the light would travel through the rest and leave you something perfectly new, without a history anywhere to touch.  Kit gave me the coil because only my hands knew the work.  I wanted to discover the texture of time, not the pretty name for it I might have sold a stranger, and I set about it by uncovering layers.  Beneath the outer whiskers where the stone had scoured it, the hemp was slick from the dreadful heat of my descent.  I held that place a finger's breadth above the clay.  If the word had been go, here was the rest of my life for the taking; even stay would have given me somewhere to begin an apology. 

Kit did not so much as clear her throat.  I parted the strands until I felt the little hard nub of a knot my sister had tied with her teeth, and turned that to the ear instead.  What came out was not the song but the small wet noise of a woman getting a fiber off her tongue.  Then, very distinctly, she said, "The hell with the green, it tastes of feet." 

Light ran the length of the coil, laying down each whisker like a mother smoothing hair for church.  Kit laughed with her mouth full of the pie she had carried all that way in her pocket.  She broke the crust and held a piece to my lips without looking, still listening, although there was nothing left to hear.
