They hired Mara to appraise the stone arrowhead because she was the only person left who could make a stone sing without striking it.  The diggers had found it beneath the west window of the pilots' house, working down through centuries of layered dust to a sill worn hollow by two elbows.  They brought her the sill as well, having once incurred her considerable displeasure by bringing her a buckle without the bit of shoe that went with it. 

Below that window stood the towers of bone, the last upright ribs of the beasts that had come to the canyon to die.  In a canyon of endless echoes you did not shout for a pilot; your own grandfather might answer.  Instead you waited at the ferry steps for a harmonic stone skimmer, somebody who could skim a flake across the floodwater and hear, among all the other sounds, its particular note come back.  Mara had done it standing in the bow for forty years, feeling the shoals ahead of her before the poleman felt them under him.  Deep water handed the note from rib to rib; a mudbank took it like a thumb pressed to a string. 

Now even being carried down the steps made the broken ends of her pelvis grate.  She had spent the winter trying to learn to navigate without moving, with about as much success as a woman trying to sew by remembering a needle.  She meant to sell the house come spring. 

The arrowhead lay sweetly on her thumb, but the left barb was shorter than the right, as if the maker had flinched at the last stroke.  Across the hollowed sill ran a scratch with a crook in it precisely where that short barb caught when she drew the head back to throw.  Whoever had knapped it had meant to skim from that window, and whoever had skimmed there had done it often enough to wear away bone. 

She made the diggers carry both her and the sill up to the present west window, two floors above its buried predecessor.  There was the ferry waiting below, her grandson on the thwart with his pole between his knees, looking studiously away from the steps where she had fallen.  When she let the arrowhead go, it curved left off the sill, kissed the water once and vanished.  All she got back was the old confusion of market bells and mothers calling children who had been dead a hundred years.  The boy looked up; he had heard that one clear kiss down there, and he thought she had given him leave to start.  She lifted both hands to stop him, but already he was pushing out. 

Above the first rib a knot of cliff swallows opened like a fist.  Then the next knot opened, and the next, going left around a shoal she knew had drowned two polemen the summer she was born.  Her arrowhead was in the mud by now, but its note was out there still, a pulse left wandering from rib to rib along the open water.  From a boat she could never have seen more than one knot scatter; from the height of those vanished pilots she could see the shape of the whole channel. 

She leaned out and held her left arm level, the old sign for a long turn, and the boy swung his pole across the stern.  Halfway over, the birds lifted on both sides of a rib, and her right arm nearly went out from forty years of knowing which side ran deeper.  But the swallows on the right settled almost as soon as they rose; on the left the opening went clean through the flock and out the other side.  She kept her left arm out until the ferry grounded soft as a slipper on the farther bank. 

That evening the diggers came for the price they ought to put on their find, not yet aware that it lay under six feet of brown water.  Mara gave them the money she had saved to move and asked them to set the old sill into the new.  She spent the last of the light knapping heads, taking a careful chip from the left barb of each.  By the time the boy came home there were seven good crossings laid out on the sill, and room between her elbows for his.
