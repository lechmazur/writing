At the moment a sculptor's chisel first strikes marble, there is room inside the blow for a good listener to spend an hour.  It was in just such an hour, with a white chip hanging like a tooth in front of her father's mouth, that Iona made her case for keeping the sky. 

The settlement occupied an ice cavern under a long moraine; the sky it possessed was a slit about as wide as a coffin lid.  Down that slit came the northern lights, but down it also came a draft that took the skin off a pail of warm milk and folded it neatly against the rim.  Her father had been commissioned to cut a marble plug.  He had agreed to give her the interval of the first blow to get the winter council to certify wonder as essential, in which event he would cut a shutter instead.  Somebody would have to go up twice a day to work it, on steps that had cost him two front teeth. 

The people standing round the block could move neither hand nor foot, but their hearing was loose inside the sculpted hush, and Iona, the prism-song bell ringer, could move whatever she could hear.  Her mother used to call her synesthetically blessed, generally while trying to discover which perfectly inaudible shade of yellow was keeping the child awake.  She could hear the seven glass bells by their tints alone.  On the amber music sheet her mother had left her, seven staves converged on a pinprick through which you were supposed to sight the source of whatever light you meant to play. 

Iona lined up the slit and began.  Out came the stately green processional she played at every winter wedding, with its little heel kick of violet that made even widowers want to dance.  The sound went washing up the walls and slid back down to rest against their feet.  She waited for the council's answering hum, the assent that would pass through the chisel into her father's wrist.  All she got was the thin whistle he made through the gap in his teeth when something was going to be expensive. 

Then from beside his elbow came the small deliberate whistle of a child copying him exactly, expense and all.  It was her daughter, to whom that expertly rendered green meant a wedding, and violet meant it was nearly time for cake.  She had heard the tune so often she had no particular reason to look up.  Indeed Iona herself had only looked up long enough to get the slit inside the pinhole. 

She let the processional subside.  Up in that thread of sky a red so faint it barely troubled the ear was slowly coming apart into two reds, one rising and one staying still.  Neither belonged to any of her seven bells.  She tipped the amber sheet edgewise; the ruled lines disappeared and the narrow edge picked up the red like a wick taking fire.  What she had to unfetter was not the song but the listening, held all these years to seven tidy lines. 

Through the colors of the aurora she drew their hearing up out of the cavern, using the sheet as a sculptor uses a chisel, to follow a division already present in the grain.  One red made the sound of a finger circling a wet glass; the other was so nearly its twin that between them a third sound swelled and sank, swelled and sank.  Her daughter tried to whistle it, missed, and for once did not turn to Iona for the proper note.  She was listening for the next swell. 

Into the trough before it came her grandfather's whistle, then the baker's deep hum, then the four unlovely voices of the winter council naming, not without considerable haggling, the mornings each could take the steps.  The two reds went their ways; a chip of marble landed in the dust. 

Her father turned the block on its side and marked the sockets for the hinge, while Iona entered her own name on the roster for all the evenings.  She took the girl up with her that first evening, and when the child put out a hand for the amber sheet, Iona tucked it into her mitten.  They shut the shutter before the milk could wrinkle.  On the way down the girl whistled a little fray of silver that Iona, busy with the latch, had missed.
