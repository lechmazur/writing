In the seventh summer after the emancipation, a man brought an infantryman's button to the foster care file room and asked for the loan of a woman who could read.  Ruth Bell, archivist for the colored churches of the lower James, put on her spectacles.  She took the books off the visitor's chair, which was the one her husband had caned with strips of walnut the week before he drowned.  She had not wanted anybody sitting there and wearing out the work of his hands. 

It had been sewn into his cap when he was a boy, he said, not for ornament but so that his mother could describe him.  He had come back from laying track in Ohio because a cook there remembered seeing a notice for a boy with an eagle on his cap.  Did Ruth keep such notices? 

Ruth kept six hundred of them, scissored out of newspapers or lifted off church doors, and knew better than to ask what he meant to do if his mother was dead.  She said only that there were enough sons with eagles on their heads to make a search worth starting. 

The cook had not seen it on paper, he said as she opened the first book; he remembered how the thing had bellied out when a door opened.  Then she knew what he had seen. 

Before the churches could afford the price of advertisements, her husband had carried a length of sailcloth from one congregation to the next, adding a line whenever somebody fed him supper and told him a name.  Ruth had rather envied him his journeys until the spring freshet took the ferry out from under him.  After that she had envied people whose husbands stayed home and did nothing of any particular use. 

There had been three such sails, and she had packed none of them; the river people had brought his effects upstairs while she sat downstairs smelling the mud in his hair.  They searched until the light came level through the west window and the man went down on his knees.  Under a press that ought to have stood square against the wainscot he could see, through accumulated dust patterns, where one foot had traveled a little and the other not at all.  Someone had swung the heavy thing out like a gate.  When they swung it again they found three rolls of canvas in a stovepipe, with rag plugs at either end to keep out mice. 

He helped her unfurl the sails across the floor, holding down one end with the walnut chair.  In her husband's narrow hand they found what the cook had heard read out: an eagle button sewn over the place where a horse had bitten a piece out of a boy's cap.  The man turned his button over; a crescent of brown felt still clung to its shank.  There was his own name, Pompey, which he had not heard since the day of the sale, and there was a direction: care of the sexton at Little Bethel.  He sat down without being asked and for a minute Ruth let him have the whole room to be a boy in. 

When she returned with ink he was reading the cloth with his fingers, stopping at each place where a different hand had freshened letters gone pale from hanging in the sun. 

"I can take a letter down there," she said, "but it is six years, and I don't know what I'll bring back." 

That was not the loan he wanted.  He would walk to Little Bethel himself; what he wanted was for somebody to stand in the Ohio cookhouse on payday and read out every line.  The cook could tell you about a cap but couldn't tell you whose boy had worn it, and there were eighty men who ate there.  And after the reading somebody would have to know what to do with the answers. 

Ruth pictured her husband's thumb and forefinger working a needle through those hard green strips, quite unaware of the weight that chair would one day have to hold.  While he was gone to Little Bethel she made two copies, one for the cookhouse and one for the next place the track might go.  At the foot of each she wrote a direction for sending back word; when he came to the door she asked him what time the eighty men sat down to eat.
