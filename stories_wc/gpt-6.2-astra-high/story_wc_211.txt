After the foghorn fell silent, the women who had come to say goodbye to the orchard put their hands over their mouths.  That was how the bronze plaque at the elevator showed women taking leave, four generations of them, each with a hand pressed flat against her lips.  Sella had supplied the sculptor with his evidence, though not, she thought now, with anything he ought to have cast in bronze. 

The ancient orchard garden atop the Exchange skyscraper had once stood at sea level, behind the customs house where departing ships took on their water.  When salt got into the wells, the city had lifted the trees, soil and all, to a height at which engineers then thought the sea would never trouble them again.  It wasn't water that had come for them in the end but the weight of their own roots, which had begun to pull the roof apart.  At noon the first pear tree would be lowered onto a barge, bound for a country where there was still earth enough to bury something without first consulting an engineer. 

As the city's venerable oath scribe, Sella had to witness the transfer, but she had brought her recording horn for a private purpose.  She could speak six dead languages, all of them rich in provisions for relinquishing land, none with a word for what the mouth did just before relinquishing it.  It was to archive emotional patterns like that little hesitation that she had spent fifty winters listening to the cylinders made at the old customs house.  Her most frequently quoted finding was nine words long: At the moment of departure the women fall silent. 

A girl tugged her sleeve and held out a thin curl of wax, violet in the blue shade of the pears.  "If you put it back," the girl whispered, "will we hear what they said?"  She had found it clinging to the cuff of Sella's coat, where the shavings fell whenever she pared a used cylinder smooth for a new oath. 

As the girl wound it around her finger, Sella remembered something curious about a third-grade field trip to the recording booth, the year before the customs house came down.  Each child had been allowed to keep one wax cylinder phonograph shaving, on condition that they immerse their hands in cold water first so the delicate spiral would not melt.  She remembered the tin basin, the astonishing cold, and then what she had seen through the glass while the teacher was explaining the silence on the records.  A woman with her hand to her mouth had flung that hand out toward the ship, and a hundred hands along the quay had opened like hers.  They were blowing kisses. 

A clerk had turned the recording horn away from the quay at the sailing bell and stuffed a velvet bag into its throat to keep the dust out.  Only then had he lifted the needle from its last few turns of immaculate silence.  God knew what noises the women made.  The sculptor had frozen the kiss at its source, and here stood a whole garden of women afraid to lift their fingers, making an accidental prophecy out of nine words about a needle coming to rest. 

The crane took the strain; a pear fell and rolled against Sella's shoe, smelling so exactly of her mother's knife opening a lunchbox pear that she put her own hand to her mouth.  She let the hand fly.  Across the widening square of naked roof the girl caught the kiss, considered it gravely, and tossed it after the tree.  Somebody laughed, a low and nearly furtive sound, and somebody called after the departing pears to be good, which was what you said here to a child leaving the table. 

Sella had only to swing the horn around to take it all down, and for a moment her arm trembled with the wish to have this sound against the winter.  Instead she spoke into it herself, giving the hour and the weight of the tree and the name of the woman on the barge who had agreed to receive it.  In the space on the wax box where she usually described the feeling of the occasion, she wrote that the instrument had been facing her.
