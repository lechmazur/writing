The treetop village of Linn had no address except the smell of crushed fern where its ferry rope disappeared into the leaves.  Boatmen who tried to find it by daylight generally found an egret standing in a different tree.  Its herbalist, Ada, had never spent a night beyond that rope, though she intended to run the finest public glasshouse on the coast.  For three years the director downriver had invited her to come and present her work, and for three years she had sent a parcel instead.  It was easy to journalize the reasons: influenza in the upper boughs, a difficult birth, a late crop of feverfew.  She had never written down the smell of wet rope slipping fast through flesh, although it was what she remembered best about the day her father lost two fingers and almost lost Ada. 

This spring the last of the great carrion blooms in Linn's hanging gardens set a single viable fruit before its corm succumbed to rot.  Inside was a corpse flower seed the color of a peeled almond, already nudging out a root.  The director sent back her letter with a note promising a heated bench, not to the seed alone but to its keeper. 

Ada laid out damp moss and the last sheet of her father's brown wrapping paper.  In her family nobody had ever wrapped so much as an onion without first doodling something on the inside of the paper for the person who would open it.  Often it was an onion with trousers on; nobody had claimed this was art.  Her father had left a little fleet of boats along one margin, each with three strokes for a wake, like the print of his damaged hand.  She put an egret in the stern of the smallest and folded the paper loosely round the moss. 

They launched at the turning of the tide's thought, as the ferryman called the instant when a petal on the brown water stopped going inland but had not yet started out.  Small white asters brushed the ferry roof as it slipped from under the village, making a sound like skirts on stairs.  Ada was so olfactorily sensitive that she could sort dried mints blindfolded, and when the smell of running rope reached her she was eight again.  Her fingers closed on the parcel until she felt the thin shell of the seed give a little under her thumb.  She put it down between her feet.  It seemed a monstrous thing that to keep something safe you sometimes had to take your hands off it. 

She could still ask to go back; the ferryman had one eye on her and one hand on the tiller.  Instead she unfolded the paper across her knees, keeping the moss cupped in a fold, and fished a pencil from her sleeve.  What she wanted was not for the boat to stop but to cultivate serenity within motion, the way her father must have done to go on making those absurd little boats.  She drew what the shore offered: three reeds bent one way and a fourth bent the other, a willow apparently washing its hair.  Presently she noticed that the wakes her father drew were crooked in precisely the way the wake behind their ferry was crooked, one stroke longer than the others.  He had been drawing on his crossings too.  Here was the undercurrent of wisdom in all that family foolishness: you could give a frightened hand something small and exact to do. 

At the glasshouse she showed the director how to lay the seed sideways so the root need not spend itself turning, then asked for a chair beside the heated bench.  For whose use, he wanted to know.  For anybody who wanted to watch, she said, surprised to discover this mattered more to her than having her name over the door.  He found her a stool instead, and in the years before the first immense pleated flower opened, the seat wore smooth.  People remembered the stink, of course, but Ada kept the wrapping from that first crossing in the top drawer of her desk.  When a pupil complained of shaking hands she turned the paper over and showed where a tremor in the line had given a willow its leaves.
