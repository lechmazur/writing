At the alchemist's market, strung high above the city rooftops, my mother could still be seen dropping a cup.  It had been falling for thirty years in a painting no wider than my two hands.  Once, when I was small, I climbed up and put a cushion on the boards beneath it.  The stallholder let me leave it there until the rains came. 

Now I was the city's between-worlds translator, and the stallholder's son had sent for me to witness a sale.  The museum wanted the whole row of little paintings, each of which held the same instant of the evacuation, when the lighthouse shone red and the last ferry turned aside from the broken pier.  To fix the pictures under glass, the buyer needed the consent of the dead inside them. 

"Only a formality," the curator said, showing me the title already lettered on the mount: The Keeper's Warning. 

I had learned, between childhood and now, to attend to what my teachers called the anchors of intuition.  You know somebody is in the kitchen before you see her because the knife on the table rocks a little on its curved blade.  The dead, having no breath to rock a blade with, spoke through hidden details in paintings.  The trick was to catch yourself knowing something, and then to find out how you knew. 

I wanted my mother's name on that mount, though all she had done, in the history we taught our children, was serve tea to the keeper who had saved us.  I put my eye to the first picture and saw the keeper at his wheel, his wrists cocked to turn the red pane across the lamp.  In the next he stood the same way, but something about his hands made my own go slack.  I went down the row until I found it: in every painting a little white line showed between the wheel and his fingers.  He was letting go. 

Then who had turned the light red? 

The stallholder's son brought out the lighthouse lens fragment his father had used to burn the borders into the frames.  Through its stepped glass a single inch of paint became a staircase of inches, and at each step something elusively tangible brushed my cheek, like a sleeve in a crowd.  I asked him to unfasten the pictures from their separate hooks and set them edge to edge on the boards.  Looked at singly, each showed a woman somewhere in the background putting out her washing; together, the clotheslines made one continuous sag from the tenements to the tower. 

Not washing, either.  At the tower window, where all those women with their combined weight had hauled it, hung the huge red silk banner of the laundresses' burial club.  I could even read the words they still stitched on every shroud: Not One Alone.  The red pane lay in bits at the keeper's feet; it was the light through that banner the ferry captain had seen.  In the last picture my mother was dropping her cup to take the weight of the wet line as it swung out over the water. 

I could name her now, and the curator, who was not an unkind man, offered to put her on the mount beside the keeper.  But under the lens I saw what had touched my cheek: at each edge of each picture a painted sleeve reached into the next.  There were hands I had cut off with my own thumbs just holding a picture up to the light. 

"Ask them what they want," the stallholder's son said. 

They had been telling me; it was the one thing no one had ever done with those pictures until this morning. 

"One frame," I said, "and room below it for people to write what they know." 

The curator knelt on the boards and turned his handsome mount over to its blank side.  When he slid it under the row, the line tightened, the ferry leaned into its turn, and my mother's cup fell the rest of the way.  It made a small domestic sound, perfectly distinct above the cries of the market.  On the white card I wrote the name of the woman whose sleeve I knew by touch, and passed the pen to the stallholder's son.
