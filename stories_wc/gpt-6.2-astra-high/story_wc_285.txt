Before the bridge went in I was the gloom-hill coachman, driving the mail down to the fog-bound river crossing and bringing back whatever the tide had let through.  My boy Kit rode beside me on Fridays, a comfort to the mare, who liked to have the velvet inside her ear rubbed while we waited for the boat.  He was also, though I would not have put it so baldly then, a way to save a penny on a stamp. 

His mother lived across the water, and since our parting we had kept what she called our palimpsest promise on a piece of sailcloth folded in his woolen muff.  We were neither of us going to let our troubles cost the boy his Fridays with the mare or his Sundays in the loft above her bakery.  Everything after that was rubbed out in bread crumbs and written over in pencil as the flour deliveries shifted or a mail train changed its time.  There were places where a time long canceled showed through a new one, like a rib through the flank of a lean dog. 

One November Friday I found Kit on the box with his two small fists quite bare to the weather, although the wind was lifting little white feathers off the river.  I got his muff from under the seat and shook it, and an extraordinary quantity of paper came out.  There was the sailcloth, but also the scraps on which his mother and I had lately taken to explaining why this or that alteration was no fault of ours.  Kit had rolled each scrap up as tight as a spill for lighting a lamp.  Not one had been delivered. 

Across the slip his mother stood with a basket of seed rolls on her arm, waiting to take him home on the boat that had brought her.  She saw what I held and came as far as the mare's head and stopped. 

"Give me until the rain gutters stop ticking," Kit said, when she would have gathered up the papers. 

Rain had passed downriver, and the iron gutters of the packet shed were counting out its last bright drops into a row of empty mussel tubs.  Driving in that country you learn more through ambient awareness than by peering into the particular patch of whiteness in front of your nose.  I could tell by a slackening of leather that my mare was going to piss, yet I had never been so closely attentive to the creature sitting at my elbow.  Now I heard him drawing breath to speak each time a drop fell, then letting it go as if somebody else had spoken first.  His mother set her basket down and let the smell of caraway percolate through the damp wool smell of us. 

"I can remember all the times," he said at last, and gave them to us without a hitch, even the Thursday before Christmas when the mail went double.  "It's having to watch your faces while you read." 

His mother picked a yarn needle from the felt band inside her basket, where she kept it for stitching the mouths of flour sacks into hems for towels.  She asked him if she might sew up the pocket in the muff, leaving just the warm tunnel for his hands.  He put his hands in first to make sure she understood what must stay open, and she stitched the pocket shut against his knuckles with red darning wool.  I took our sailcloth to the window of the packet shed, where there was a post office no wider than its door, and bought a sheet of penny stamps. 

I used to think the point of revising an agreement was to distill complexity into essence, to arrive by slow degrees at something so short a child could carry it.  In fact our agreement got longer, running in the end to a biscuit tin apiece, and Kit never knew another word of it.  That evening as the ferry slipped its chain he stood beside his mother in the stern with both hands in the muff and his face turned up to catch the flying salt.  He did not look back to see how I was taking it, which was worth a penny of anybody's money.
