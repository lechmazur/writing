On the last night of the Rialto, my father asked me to find where unused love is stored.  He was standing in the projection booth with his hat on, as if he had only stopped by and hadn't spent forty years up there.  Below us the audience was trying to sing the palace anthem, which nobody had sung since the organist died.  They had the words on the screen, but they kept starting over in different keys.  While those forgotten lyrics searched for their tune, we had privacy; once the song was over, I had to go down and give the organist's eulogy.  My father had been asked first and had said he was only the man who changed the reels. 

I made my living as a crystal gazer in the Rialto lobby, telling people things about their futures that they were decently free to disregard.  For a psychometry reading, which concerns the past and is consequently a less forgiving business, I needed something that two people had touched.  Father gave me a film strip wound around a little sheaf of goodbye letters.  My dear Hal, the top one began, in the organist's beautiful upright hand that I knew from birthday cards. 

Six times, Father said, they had made a plan to part before somebody took it into his head to put a stone through our window.  Each time one had gone to the station, sat with his suitcase until the train pulled out, and come back to work. 

"All those years we could have had a house," he said, and put his finger on my crystal as if ringing for service. 

I wanted to tell him that a man who loved somebody ought at least to be able to stand up at his funeral.  Instead I cupped the letters against the warm lamp housing and let the two handprints incubate under mine.  Downstairs the bass pipes rumbled softly as the substitute at the organ felt with his feet for the floor of the song. 

I have always been spatially imaginative; give me the smell of a stair carpet and I can build you the landing it leads to, including the view.  A room came clear inside the ball, and for a moment I mistook it for the booth we stood in.  It had the same slant to its ceiling, but where the second projector should have been there was a narrow bed.  Where the fire shutter hung there was a window over a sink, and beyond the glass a pear tree leaned into view.  Father took off his hat. 

"That's the tree he wanted," he said. 

I turned the strip over and saw what I had taken for scratches: the organist had drawn that tree on fifteen frames of discarded leader, a pear dropping a little farther in each.  At the end a little girl caught it in a man's hat.  With a shock of professional embarrassment I recognized the little kitchen I had gazed into for years whenever a frightened customer asked me to find him somewhere safe.  I had thought I'd invented it. 

Now I remembered being four, perched on a film can with my feet in the organist's lap, watching a pear drop forever into my father's hat.  My mother had just died, and nobody at home could get me to eat, let alone laugh.  But in that booth I had eaten slices of pear off the blade of a pocketknife while two men argued companionably about the proportions of a kitchen window. 

"I didn't know he could draw people," I said. 

"Who do you think drew the girl," Father said, and for the first time that night he looked a little offended. 

Below us a single voice had found the interval everybody else was afraid to try, and the house went up with it like one enormous chest filling.  I held the crystal out to him, but he was already fitting the strip into the little hand viewer he had used to keep me quiet.  He asked if there was light enough down there to show somebody how it worked.  I said there was a lamp on the lectern, and he went downstairs ahead of me and waited for the singing to stop. 

Then my father took his place where the organist's name stood in white carnations and held the viewer up to the light. 

"This is our house," he said, and gave it to the first stranger in the front row.
