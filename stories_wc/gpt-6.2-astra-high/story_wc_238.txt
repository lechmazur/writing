The orchard got one last hour of quiet because my sister had put it in the contract.  At seven the county men would start cutting, whether or not she had managed to play the trees.  They sat on their truck beds eating hard pears with pocketknives while I climbed the outside stairs to the sheet music archive.  It was the first time in thirty years I had gone up those stairs without a question from a reader as my excuse. 

I was the gossip columnist who did exorcisms for the price of a Sunday paper.  People wrote to ask what to do about a dead husband who kept turning up at dances with somebody else's dead wife.  I advised through metaphors, the cheaper the better: Don't darn a sock there's no longer a foot to fill.  Sometimes the husband went away and sometimes the widow took up dancing, and I counted both among my imperfect miracles.  Kit had written just once, to ask me to stop calling the music she made on the orchard wires the singing of our dead mother.  I had printed the letter under the heading A JEALOUS DAUGHTER. 

Upstairs she had taken the quilts off the cabinets, and the slant light showed the little dunes of plaster grit that had settled along the spines of the scores.  The archive had begun as a lending library for the packinghouse band; you could still tell the popular marches by the greasy thumbprints.  On the desk was the score Kit had never lent anybody, ruled in six lines for the six steel wires that ran from the sill to the oldest pear tree.  On the fourth line, where the tune ran too high for any woman's throat, the paper was soft with erasures. 

She was trying to reconnect that fourth wire, fumbling the nut with fingers permanently bent from years of sorting fruit.  I held the wire taut, and after a moment she let me take the wrench too.  Under each wire sat a pickup from an electric guitar; through induced currents the shivers of the distant tree reached a little speaker in the room.  Already you could hear the dry creak of a ton of sick wood shifting in the sun. 

Kit held out her hand for the thing I had promised to bring.  It was our mother's antique perfume bottle, narrow at the neck and fat at the foot, with one side worn cloudy from the singing wire.  As girls we had taken turns drawing it along the steel, but only Kit had learned to make the notes bend like a voice about to break.  I had kept the bottle in my desk at the paper, where clients could put a dollar in the poor box to smell the last of the violets.  It ought to have been a glorious moment, Kit getting her instrument back, but she only wiped it on her skirt where my hand had been. 

I had rehearsed something about a pear tree grafted onto quince stock, how by the time it bore fruit it had forgotten whose roots were feeding it.  Kit set the glass against the wire and waited, not for me to speak but for me to get out of her light.  I moved. 

I said, "I knew it was you playing, and I made you out a liar because people would pay to hear her and not you."  "I'm sorry I stole your music and left you to face the people downstairs by yourself." 

"They'd open their mouths at me," she said, "and pretend somebody else was singing." 

I hadn't known that part, though it sounded exactly like something I would once have found space for on a Sunday.  She put my hand on the lowest wire, in the place I used to hold it to keep the bass from drowning out her small, high tune.  Down in the orchard a man folded his knife and another took off his cap, and what came out of the speaker sounded nothing like our mother.  It was a hard bright noise full of little catches where the glass stuck, and Kit kept every one of them in time.  At seven the first saw started and she lifted the bottle, but for a moment longer she let my hand stay beside hers on the sill. 

Afterward I asked if I could print her name, and she said yes, in the same size type as JEALOUS.  She went down to the pressroom with me to measure it.
