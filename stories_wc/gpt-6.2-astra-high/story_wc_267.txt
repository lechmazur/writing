My position was listed as silent-choir bellhop because the Grand Cobalt had no rate of pay for a man who carried trunks and interpreted the complaints of sixteen deaf girls to a hearing cook.  The girls paid for their winter board by singing with their hands for the diners on Sundays.  From the valley our hotel looked like a cobalt fortress under snow; from the attic it looked like what it was, a barracks with one staircase too few. 

After the laundry fire the inspector gave us until January to demonstrate that a sleeping girl in the attic could be roused by the new alarm.  Otherwise the choir must go back down to the county home, where even at meals they had to sit on their hands until addressed. 

I had burned my palms getting the laundry door open, and during nerve regeneration, which the doctor said would take months, I was to handle familiar objects with my eyes shut.  My favorite was a piano bench bolt with a square head and a little nick that my left thumb could find before my right.  The bench had gone for kindling the night we spent in the courtyard after the fire, and the bolt rode in my apron like the small hard seed of that miserable night. 

The new alarm was a hammer that beat on the steam riser, and all sixteen iron bedsteads had been clamped to the pipe.  Three nights running the manager tried it at a different hour and three times the girls came down in pairs, their boots neatly laced.  It was the boots that made me want to understand the cost of safety as the manager proposed to sell it to the county.  A child who has once fled a fire does not stop for the second boot, let alone the laces. 

I found my answer via the slightest discoloration on a dusty spiral staircase that wound from the attic down to the linen cupboard behind the manager's desk.  One tread, just where you could see his hand reach for the alarm lever, had a black crescent of fresh boot polish on its lip.  At midnight I took a blanket there and found little Agnes crouched in her shift, her chin bruised yellow where it rested on her knees.  She admitted they slept dressed down to their boots, and took turns watching the desk. 

"It works on the first bed," she signed, "but Nell in the first bed takes her fits if she loses sleep, so we let her alone." 

Upstairs I laid my bolt on the bare iron rail of the last bed and asked Agnes to pull the lever.  In my bad hand I felt nothing; in the moonlight the square head did not so much as rock against the rail.  Nell sat up, though, and the look she gave me said plainly enough that she knew what the others were doing for her. 

The manager had a seraphically mundane explanation: a night porter cost more in a winter than the choir brought in. 

"Then use the bellhop," I said. 

He pointed to my bandages and asked how many sleeping girls I supposed I could carry.  Agnes, who had followed me down, tugged his sleeve and held up two fingers, then walked them briskly along his ledger.  He looked to me for the words, but for once I made him look back at the person speaking. 

"You can walk," he guessed at last, and she tapped his nose as she did with particularly slow diners. 

It seems to me now that the unpardonable silence of that winter belonged to us who could hear the hammer three floors down and had never once asked a girl how it felt upstairs.  The inspector let them remain on condition that somebody with a key and a lantern sat watch on the landing every night.  I took the first watch and the manager the second; we left the trunks for the guests to carry themselves.  Just before dawn Agnes put a bare foot out from under her quilt, and I tucked it back with the hand that had begun to feel the cold again.
