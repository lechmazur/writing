In the frozen library above the clouds, even a turned page made a small, exact sound, as if a facet had been struck off a crystal.  Edda, its orderly records chief, laid felt under every buckle before she opened a case.  She was gently meticulous even with things that had no ears left to hurt. 

On the day they came to take down the library, she had one magic lantern slide still to file, and no felt left except the square inside her wedding ring box.  Everything else had gone down the mountain with the families whose luminous vows the glass contained.  Below the frost line the pictures would last for one showing; after that the children would have only what they could tell each other. 

The last case was marked with the name of her husband, who had kept the rope ferry until the night the river took it.  She ought to have put it in her pocket.  Instead she set the slide in its brass jaws and turned the lantern toward the ice.  In the cold a vow could be viewed a thousand times, which was precisely why she had never let herself look at this one.  Yet what if, down in the valley, she should waste her single sight of him looking at his hands when it was his mouth she meant to remember? 

The light came up on a bench wet with spray, an iron cleat, a coil of rope paying out into absolute dark.  There was no man in the picture at all.  Across the bench a vow ran in the pale upright script she knew from the notes he used to leave beside her breakfast: I will get you home. 

Under the words something glimmered like the green at the bottom of thick glass.  She took out the little comb of prisms with which she used to measure a vow by sampling frequencies along its length.  To the eye a promise shone white whether it was merely spoken or faithfully kept; through the comb you could see what had happened to it in the world.  What she expected was the clean break of a life cut short, a thing she had seen so often she could draw its spectrum blind. 

Instead the green divided into a ladder of colors, each beginning a little before the one below it ended.  She knew that pattern from the vows that went round a cradle at a naming, when each new pair of hands accepted the weight before the last let go.  She slipped the comb across the lens, letting just the lowest band of green pass through.  A hand appeared beside the cleat, her husband's hand with its familiar split thumbnail, offering the rope to somebody the white light had washed out.  As she tipped the comb from band to band, other hands closed on the rope, some so small they could not close all the way.  They had made a handrail of the sagging line, holding it above the water until the next person had a grip.  Each had taken up his words along with the wet hemp. 

For twenty years the people he had saved had come to her door on the anniversary with pears and loaves and jars of little river plums.  She had thanked them on the step because she could not bear to be told again how good he was.  It had never occurred to her to ask them what they themselves had done that night. 

Outside, the porters were waiting in the quartz hush, their sled loaded with empty cabinets.  Edda crossed out nothing on the case, but after her husband's name she made a comma, and after the comma she left room. 

That evening she set the lantern on her kitchen table, between a loaf gone hard and a jar of plums that had kept perfectly.  She had sent word, and the people came, bringing their children this time because she had asked them to.  The ice on the glass turned to water; the bright script trembled on the kitchen wall.  While there was still light enough to see by, Edda opened her book and began to take names.  But the children wanted to know how a rope that heavy had felt, and somebody fetched the clothesline, still damp with the dew.  Soon everybody was up from the table showing them, hand over hand.  By the time the wall went dark she had put her pencil down and was holding on with the rest.
