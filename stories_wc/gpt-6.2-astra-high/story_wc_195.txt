When the town commissioned a statue of her brother, Sir Linn went into the orchard to find out how tall he would have been.  She took the measuring cord the sculptor gave her, although it seemed a mean thing to put around a ghost.  The alchemical orchard occupied a greenhouse shrouded in endless night, since daylight fixed the silver in its pears and left them full of nothing but your own reflection.  Under the glass you could ask a tree what might have happened, and for the price of a little warmth it would let you walk among the answers. 

That was the factually mystical part of Linn's errand; the rest was a lie she had not quite found the courage to contradict.  Kit had not died carrying the town standard up the burning stairs.  He had gone back for his puppets.  She had lent him her standard to wrap them in, a detail too small, apparently, to survive a victory. 

For eleven years the withdrawn knight had received no visitors, but when the sculptor knocked she had let him show her his clay soldier, already taller than herself.  They wanted the man he would have become, the sculptor said, not another dead boy.  All she had been able to say was that she would check the height. 

Now she laid the cord along a bough and felt the bark begin to take the heat of her palm.  A man came ducking under the branches with Kit's way of ducking, too early and then not quite low enough.  He caught his hair and swore, and she shook with the exquisite cold of hearing a voice she had begun to forget.  She lifted the cord, but as reality shifted he was a stooped little fellow in a floury apron, then a stranger in a good coat who stood precisely as high as her heart.  Each put out a hand to steady the same swaying pear.  It was always the left hand, the one the sculptor had wrapped around a sword. 

Her fingers had grown so numb that she had to unsnap the throat of her tunic with her teeth.  Inside, against her skin, was the abandoned marionette theater program she had picked off an empty seat after they carried him out.  On the back Kit had printed the finger exercises for children who stayed to try the strings.  Without the paper she could not have recalled a single one, but her stiff hand went willingly through forgotten patterns: the goose drinking, the fish escaping, the soldier laying down his pack. 

Across from her the baker made the goose drink.  The fine gentleman let the fish go.  Then all along the aisle men of every height and circumstance were setting burdens down, and her teeth chattered with something perilously close to joy.  Here was a measurement that did not change.  She looped the cord between her fingers and marked in knots the distances that let the fish slip free.  What she wanted to capture was the shape of possibility, which turned out to be about six inches of slack. 

By then the frost had taken her boots to the ankle, and she knew why some people stayed under the glass until there was no difference between them and trees.  To the brother who might have baked her bread, to the one who might have grown rich and insufferable, she murmured the allegiance she had once shouted to a flag.  "Whatever comes, I will stand for you."  She pulled one foot free and then the other, and went to tell the sculptor what the standard had been for. 

They quarreled for a winter, not about the truth, which he accepted with an ease that made her eleven years of silence feel very expensive, but about bronze.  In the end he cast a fish in jointed sections, light enough for the smallest child to lift, and Linn herself cut the strings to length.  They hung it where the ticket window used to be, with the instructions from the program and a step for anybody who needed one.  On opening day she stood behind a girl who kept trying to hoist the fish like a flag, until at last she asked Linn what the trick was.  Linn bent to show her, and at the small cold tug of another hand on the strings the hair rose singing on her arms.  Together they gave it room to swim.
