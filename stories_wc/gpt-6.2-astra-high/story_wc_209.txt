In the amber pavilion that autumn, Nell painted a white lily as long as a child, and a woman old enough to be her grandmother spat on it.  She rubbed the spit into the wet lime with her thumb until the stem broke.  Then she sat down on a packing case, out of breath from what she had done. 

Nell was known for saints with beetroot faces and fish so blue they left an afterimage on the eye.  Nobody would have hired her to paint forty identical lilies except that her uncle had been one of the fever children who slept here, under the honey-colored roof, and never came home.  The lilies, according to the brass plaque beside the door, had flowered in the cathedral window opposite on each night a child died. 

"My bed was here," the woman said, tapping the case.  "If you want to know why flowers bloom at night, try lying down." 

From the floor Nell could see nothing through the remaining plane leaves but a patch of cathedral glass the dull purple of a sucked plum.  The woman opened her handbag and laid a collection of old postcards on Nell's stomach.  All had been addressed to the same little girl in the pavilion; all showed that window, photographed by day, with a flower drawn on it in pencil. 

"That's where to look," the woman said, "but I can't get up those stairs anymore." 

Nell left the broken stem to dry.  She had inherited her mother's way of pursuing a thing without ever appearing to give chase.  While the trustees argued about the lilies, she carried hot lunches to the cathedral glazier, who ate with both hands and let her turn the pages of his repair books.  By Christmas she knew that the purple was not one color but a sandwich of red and blue, installed after the fever to protect two skins of clear glass too thin to bear the wind.  By February he trusted her to help him take it apart. 

In lamplight a confusion of scratches on the hidden layers resolved into petals, each etched half on one skin and half on the other.  Looking straight through, Nell could not reckon a single whole flower.  She had to prop the panes a handbreadth apart, as the book prescribed, and sight along the slant to the little girl's bed before a daisy joined its bitten petals.  At another slant a buttercup opened.  Along the sill stood a row of shallow tin dishes, some black inside and some still bright beneath the dust. 

"The glass man had twins in there," the woman said when Nell brought her the tracing of the daisy.  "It was him that worked out how to send us a kiss without anybody catching anything." 

Each evening the families climbed to the gallery with their nightlights; each child knew where to look for a private scrap of fire.  The daisy had been hers, but it was not the one she wanted to see. 

"There was a boy beside me whose mother was in the ground and whose father wouldn't come.  Your uncle used to crawl in with me and we'd shove our heads together till he could see mine." 

Through the evenings it took for winter's last snow to melt, the glazier moved his lamp from dish to dish while Nell signaled from the pavilion with a bedsheet.  On the third evening a small bent iris came alight where the old woman said no one had ever held a candle.  Nell had never heard that about her grandfather, and had heard too much about him to wonder if it was true.  She took her coat off and made a pillow of it, and this time they lay down together. 

In the dawning hush the iris lost first its stalk and then its hood, so gently that neither could have said when it went.  That was how Nell painted it, not white on the wall among forty others, but just going out, in the blue space between two heads on a pillow.  Underneath she painted the daisy, its bitten petal mended where the two foreheads touched. 

At the unveiling the old woman showed her grandchildren how to see it, and one by one the trustees took off their good coats and got down.
