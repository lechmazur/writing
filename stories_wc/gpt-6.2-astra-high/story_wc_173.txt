In the silver courtyard under snow, the people of Bell took one breath and let it go together.  Their sigh lifted a skin of powder from the pavement, without disturbing the little heaps that lay upon their open eyes.  From her stool beside the Regent, Dove watched for the first corner of a mouth to rise.  Everything she meant to do must be done between sigh and smile. 

Dove was the last of the chaste courtesans, whom one hired for the pleasure of being seen by someone who would neither possess nor betray one.  She had a bodily gift for poetry: a lift of her shoulder could give a woman back the exact weight of the baby she had once carried there.  Such a woman often made a sound she had never heard herself make before.  In her heated silver walls these small disclosures lingered, audible only to an ear pressed close.  Then came the winter of the slides, when whole streets went under and the living dared not sleep for listening to the dead knock.  They were not all dead, which was the trouble. 

Dove had let the silversmiths take a harvest of echoes from her walls, on condition that it last one night.  They drew the fine vibrations out like wire and twisted them into a single string, on which Dove played the tune her mother used to hum against her skull.  Half of Bell had slept while the other half went digging, and in the morning nineteen people came up alive.  Ten winters later the Regent still put every public question to that lullaby, and the smile it elicited was entered in the minutes as consent.  Tonight the question was whether the rooms of heated silver should henceforth deliver their echoes directly to the state.  Dove could have managed one more smile of her own, but not on behalf of anyone who had ever sat with her. 

She slipped off her stool and stepped inside the prism sanctuary under starlight, where the string hung quivering from gable to floor.  The sanctuary had been cut to give the player time to tune: what passed outside as a beat of music lasted inside as long as the note held true.  Through its slanting walls she could see a snowflake standing on the pale lash of the Regent, who as a young man had always chewed his sleeve when frightened.  He too had sat with her. 

Starlight divided the shining cord into colors too delicate to see by day, a flesh pink beside a pearl pink beside the pink at the base of a nail.  With the tip of her nail she picked one filament loose and felt her jaw assume the angle of a boy trying not to weep.  She remembered him well enough to know that the next thing his jaw had done was jut.  When she let hers do likewise, the filament slid free.  The silversmiths who made the string had put no malice into it; they had only twisted until each voice was obliged to move its neighbor.  To unlock the chord she would have to stop conducting it. 

She set her fingers to untangling threads, letting her body be taken from her a joint at a time.  A washerwoman's crooked elbow rose in magnificent disdain; the Regent's front teeth caught the wool at her wrist.  She had forgotten how funny some of the noises were, and how much it hurt to be made to laugh with cracked lips.  The last strand was her mother's tune, which she had thought she could pick out of any tangle without thinking.  Only now did she hear how the last note went up, as if to ask a child still awake whether it wanted another verse.  She left it that way. 

The snowflake fell, and the people of Bell completed the movement into which their faces had been set.  For a terrible moment everyone was smiling.  Then a woman rubbed her cheeks with both hands and asked to hear the question again.  The Regent looked toward the empty stool, his cuff between his teeth.  Dove went out to him, for there was nothing in what she had done to forbid her ever offering comfort again.  She waited until he put out a hand before she took it, and together they listened to the unruly, unmusical business of counting the votes.
