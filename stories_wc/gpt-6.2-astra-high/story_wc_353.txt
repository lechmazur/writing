After the hedgehog uncurls, Linn sees what has kept it rolled in the path: a loop of wire tight around one hind foot.  She snips the loop with her little bronze cutters and waits until the foot takes weight.  Only then does she follow the wire into the siskin alder grove, winding it around her elbow as she goes so nothing else will be caught.  All morning people have been coming here to dismantle the work of her hands.  By dusk they intend to have the last wire down. 

Linn is a tree-song sculptor, as her mother was before her, although people smile at the word song in her title now that sound is gone.  Her mother got to hear an alder sing; Linn, born six months after the stilling, has only the tender place between thumb and wrist where a drawn wire leaves its shiver.  She has spent eight winters trying to make that shiver travel from tree to tree and so into other hands.  She is symphonically minded even in her sleep, dreaming of separate currents that lift a whole field of grain without breaking one stalk.  But in the grove her only way to reach the farthest tree has been to pull the wires tighter.  Yesterday her small son Iven laid his palm against the first wire and drew it back with a welt.  Today he sits on a stump feeding alder seeds to the siskins, using the hand that does not hurt. 

The loose length leads her under a bank of fern to a porcelain insulator the color of skimmed milk.  It is screwed to a wooden shoulder; when she clears the fern with her sleeve, three more shoulders emerge, each with the dark hollow of a lap beneath it.  She knows the long-forgotten sculpture from a drawing on the flyleaf of her mother's ledger: four seated women passing a skein from hand to hand.  She had thought it a pretty joke about the way the women of her family could talk across a roomful of working looms.  Now she wonders why a thing made merely to be looked at should have worn quite through the glaze. 

She kneels among the damp skirts and begins to read the stress cracks in the wood.  On the first figure they run from wrist to elbow, on the second from elbow to shoulder; in no two bodies does the strain fall in the same place.  The arms were cut along the grain to let them yield, each according to its own thickness and reach.  Even the porcelain neck is polished on both sides, where the line ran free instead of being tied.  Linn lays her cutters in the hollow of a lap and motions to the people taking down the wires.  Keep taking them down, she signs, but leave me one long piece. 

She runs it around the old insulator and brings both ends to Iven, who puts his sore hand behind his back.  For an instant she feels the familiar wish to take that hand and show him what he ought to feel.  Instead she sits beside him and spreads the slack across her knees.  When a siskin lights on the wire to peck a seed from his trousers, they both feel the exact small weight of it.  Iven looks at her and, with the heel of his good hand, leans a little weight back.  She lets the line slip until the pressure on her knees is no greater than the pressure of the bird.  Then, on the wire between them, she begins to strum. 

Down the length of the clearing people take up the loop where it lies, not to tighten it but to give it places to bend.  A woman with stiff fingers slips her forearm under it; the man beside her drops his wrist to meet the smaller reach, and Iven shifts his knee to make room for both.  Linn feels these accommodations coming toward her long before she lifts her head to see them.  Nothing in the grove makes a sound, not even the flock lifting out of the alders like a handful of seeds tossed high.  But when she stops, the phrase keeps going, altered a little at each pair of hands, and presently her son gives it back to her on the palm that hurt.
