The first star went out above the tin orchard, and nobody looked up except the boy whose job it was to polish the apples.  He reported it to Prince Hal with a certain satisfaction, since a patch of absolute dark meant seven fewer apples on which a highlight had to be maintained.  Hal had spent thirty years keeping the heavens in order and had not known until then that anybody had to polish a highlight. 

That night he walked through his father's suspended palace, which was anchored to giant stalactites by iron collars that groaned a little in their sleep.  Beyond his windows the constellations wheeled across the cavern roof, punctual as the trains that carried ore out of the dark below them.  He wanted somebody to look up without being paid for it. 

At breakfast the king found a bedraggled prince in his chair, dripping rust onto the velvet from a night spent hunting leaks in the projection room.  A drop on the lens might have swallowed the star, Hal said, or a moth might have died in just the right place.  Hypothesizing about everything was a habit left over from childhood, when his mother had let him assemble clocks beside her bed and tell her all the reasons they might not go.  She had generally found the missing piece in the blankets after he went to sleep.  The king pushed the marmalade across to him, her recipe, bitterly sweet with strips of pith. 

"Have you looked at the sky itself?" he asked. 

In the projection room the sky itself was what they called a copper globe no bigger than a melon, revolving round a bead of electric fire.  Hal took it off its spindle and found the missing star stopped up with a grain of polishing paste.  He could have picked it out with a pin, but by now he wanted to know who had decided where that pin should go in the first place.  The pattern had come down through nine generations of globes, each punched through the holes in its predecessor, all the way back to the thing in the landing museum. 

Under a bell of yellow glass stood the egg of the extinct lantern goose, big enough to have made breakfast for a boatload of children.  The label called it the only surviving record of the upper sky.  Through a magnifier the little holes looked ragged, some round and some triangular, and beside one was a scratch that might have been a letter.  The tremor of the ore trains traveled up the iron collars and into his wrist; the tiny letter danced as if it were still being written. 

He turned off the museum lamps and threaded a bulb through the hole where somebody long ago had blown out the yolk.  The thin shell glowed like a cupped hand, and under its ancient coat of varnish he could read the scratches all around the stars.  One said Ada, who could whistle with her teeth out.  One said The baby, before we knew what to call him. 

These were the stars the first arrivals had wanted over their beds, and nine generations of royal astronomers had faithfully reproduced the punctures and polished away the names.  Hal carried the lit egg to his father as carefully as if there were still some chance the bird might hatch. 

By evening the boy from the orchard was helping them take the copper globe apart along its soldered waist.  The king worked from the inside with a fine awl, his son holding the globe steady against the shudder of a passing train.  On a slip of paper for the new register, in the cramped hand in which he usually signed decrees, the king wrote Nell, who could find small things in the dark.  The boy took the register downstairs and began his rounds, explaining at each door why the sky would be late coming on. 

When they lit the sky again there was a crooked star low over the orchard, trembling whenever a truck of ore went by.  The boy offered to put its glint on seven apples for nothing, but Hal said to leave them be.  People were coming out among the dull tin branches with bits of paper in their hands, looking up to see where there was room.
