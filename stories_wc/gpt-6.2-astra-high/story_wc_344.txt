From the valley, the frozen library above the clouds looked like a chip of china caught in the blue glaze of the sky.  Up close it smelled of tar, because every shelf had been cut from the hull of a boat that no longer had a harbor.  Nell arrived under the soft hush of a distant morning, with the valley still in darkness and the western moon already bitten red.  The library's silver locks were cast from moonlight, which stood still long enough to take a key only when the earth came between it and the sun.  For thirteen years Nell had been a lunar-eclipse locksmith without ever once coming home to mend this particular lock. 

Beyond it stood the hall of mirrors, where a borrower could turn a crank and watch one ordinary day in any of the drowned towns.  Through the little window Nell saw what the last borrower had left behind: her father on their step, forever putting the same curl of apple peel into his mouth.  She had been that borrower, and the crank had broken in her hand as she tried to turn it backward.  Children were growing up below who had never seen a gull walk, or understood what a washboard was for.  One of them had written asking whether the sea really wrinkled your toes, and it was to this child, rather than the trustees, that Nell had promised a working hall. 

The key went in, but when she turned it the peel jumped out of his mouth and the door kicked shut against her knee.  The lock and crank shared a spindle; she would have to learn the rhythm of repair from the man who had built them both.  Her father had distrusted instruction books, on the grounds that a person who needed one generally had both hands full.  He preferred prophecy, which in their town meant things jotted down by sleepers and discovered to be useful some embarrassing number of years afterward.  On her knees in the corner where his workbench had stood, Nell sorted through scraps of prophecy hidden behind the baseboard, looking for her own name.  Instead she found the child's question, word for word, on the back of a bill for lamp oil.  On its other side was a sketch of a hand reaching up to uncap the brass tube that ran from the lock to the ceiling. 

She climbed the shelves, unscrewed the cap and heard, instead of the thin scream of an overwound spring, the companionable suck and slap of wavelets under a pier.  Of course he had set the works to local time, and at home that had never meant anything so simple as an hour.  Every seventh slap was missing; every seventh time her father lifted the peel, it fell back to his lap.  From her clockmaker's gear assortment she picked a seven-toothed star, the little wheel that let a mechanism rest between advances.  She eased the key around during the missing beat and in the instant before the door could kick she slipped the star onto the spindle. 

The door swung inward and she saw, repeated a thousand times in hauntingly bright silver, the thing that had jammed the works.  It was her own small thumb on the crank, thirteen years younger and still white with the effort of holding him there.  The hall kept everything that happened in a drowned town, and what was this building but so much timber from home?  All her training told her to knock the obstruction clear, but she had lately learned what came of trying to wrench something from a child's fist.  She put her hand around the little hand and turned with it, backward one last time, until her father took the curl away from his lips.  Then she waited, neither pushing nor pulling, through six little waves and the quiet place where the seventh belonged.  The thumb loosened, the star engaged, and her father ate his apple peel and wiped the knife on his trousers and went indoors. 

By the time the first borrowers climbed out of the clouds, the mirrors were full of gulls stamping the wet sand for worms.  The child with the question came last, a little shy about taking off her boots in front of strangers.  Nell took hers off first, and side by side on boards gone smooth as driftwood they waited for the water to come in.
