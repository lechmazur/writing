On the sky-bridge above the municipal orchard, Nell played violin for the changing of the pears.  At the first trill they turned from pewter to brass, at the second from brass to gold, and at the third the children were permitted to applaud.  Once a boy had gone on clapping after the third trill, and a marble gardener had held his hands apart with an exquisite gentleness until he stopped.  It was that gentleness, more than anything, that kept Nell at her post long after she had ceased to find the pears beautiful.  Somebody was still in there. 

The orchard had been put under a preservation spell the winter the blight took the valley, when even the sight of a leaf was worth saving.  The gardeners had stood among the trees of their own accord, never guessing how hard it would be, once saved, to do anything else.  Now their chisels clicked all night as they pared away new growth to keep the place exactly as it had been.  Nell's grandmother, who had planted the first pear, was the one with marble dimples in the backs of her knees. 

One morning Nell found a locust shell gripping the bridge rail, split neatly between the shoulders, still looking out through two windows of amber at the world its tenant had quit.  Below, her grandmother bent to uproot a seedling, and for the first time Nell saw a dark seam open across the shoulders of her dress.  She nearly called down, but what could you tell a woman who had spent sixty years bending over and must know exactly where her dress was tight?  Instead she tucked the shell into her pocket and knelt with her ear against the iron rail. 

Under the tick of the chisels she heard a resonantly soft double knock, like a plum dropping into a wooden bowl and rolling against its neighbor.  There was a pause whenever her grandmother bent; then the knocking quickened with the effort of straightening up.  Nell remembered being little and riding that woman's hip, her ear tucked under a damp and fragrant arm, and hearing the very same hurry on the stairs. 

At seventy she still possessed one reliable instrument besides the violin.  She put the scroll against the rail, set her wrist against the ribs, and let the hollow wood carry her pulse down through the iron trellis.  When she held her breath her heart slowed, and after a moment the heart below slowed too.  They played together like that until the pears were due to turn and the schoolchildren had assembled in their yellow municipal capes. 

Nell had meant to use the waltz to say something grand about escaping, but in the end all she did was invite the old woman to set the time.  She laid her bow on the strings and waited.  Up through her wrist came two quick beats, then a long one, the limping measure of the dance her grandmother used to do in stocking feet when her corns were bad.  Under Nell's bow one pear turned brass, another stayed pewter, and a third, as if abashed by the liberties allowed it, went a blotchy country brown.  Down went the marble woman on one knee, and in the gap between knowing what would split and understanding what would emerge, Nell played the little hitch that always came when those sore feet left the floor. 

Her grandmother stepped backward out of her dress.  She was old, not the fine straight-backed woman the spell had preserved, and the first thing she did was sit down hard on the gravel.  The other gardeners laid down their chisels; one by one they turned their backs to the rail, and Nell felt the iron fill with their various, imperfect times.  She was never going to manage all those parts alone.  She motioned to the children, who came and put their small wrists to the rail beside hers, and under the bridge the last of the gold went out of the trees. 

Years afterward people who had not been there liked to say that what took its place was a glory of white blossom.  Nell remembered a woman with gravel stuck to her cheek, biting into a brown pear with what teeth she had left and letting the juice run.
