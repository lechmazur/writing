At twilight the mist came in through the broken panes of the station atrium, and the old man on the dais began to look about eight years old.  He had his medal in his mouth and was sucking the blue ribbon.  Nobody liked to tell the Hero of the Flood to stop doing anything. 

June was a contact tracer for hearts, usually employed in the municipal wards, where a great many people died with nobody obvious to telephone.  Twilight was when the dead called, and the living heart they wanted rang so softly that the sound was often mistaken for a fly at the glass.  June found her people by tracking faint pulses in a decades-old hearing aid, a big flesh-colored lump that had belonged to her father.  He had lost his hearing coupling freight cars in this very station.  She still turned down the volume before passing between the iron pillars where he had warned her the echoes kicked. 

Tonight the mayor had engaged her to pick up the calls of the flood dead, who could hardly fail, he felt, to thank the boy who had rung the station bell.  Across the atrium hung an enlargement of the last photograph taken before the river carried off the houses on Mill Street.  There was the boy swinging on the rope, his heels clear of the ground; behind him three hundred schoolchildren were trooping up the hill to investigate the racket.  June had always wondered about the direction his face was turned.  All afternoon she had been asking the old man what stood beyond the left edge of the picture, until his nurse told her rather sharply that curiosity could tire a person out. 

Now the medal fell out of his mouth.  From the hearing aid came three small knocks, as if somebody were shaking the last matches out of a box.  She circled the dais twice before she located the answering pulse in the old man's fist, which was knocking against the underside of his chair.  The mayor reached down to plug his cable into her receiver.  June put her thumb in the socket.  She had taken the job in the wards to protect vulnerable voices, not because she particularly liked to hear what people said to each other in the dark. 

She unhooked the earpiece and settled it against the old man's good ear.  He listened with an expression of polite boredom until whoever was speaking called him Button; then his face seemed to fly apart. 

"I rang it, Mum," he said. 

June pulled the canvas loose at one corner and draped it over the two of them, as she might have drawn a hospital curtain.  The crowd made a shocked little noise and subsided into a hush that seemed to reel about the iron pillars looking for somewhere to fall.  Under the canvas it smelled of hot dust and peppermints. 

"Did the doctor come?" the old man asked. 

June could hear the woman's answer quite clearly because he had pulled the earpiece away in his dread of what she might say. 

"He came, Button.  I had his big warm coat on me the whole way up the hill." 

His fist came open on a peppermint worn quite round in its twist of paper.  "They keep saying I saw the water coming," he whispered.  "I never saw any water." 

"You saw me on the floor," said his mother, with something almost like impatience, the briskness of a woman trying to assure a child the cup he broke was only a cup.  "What else did you need to see?" 

By the time June drew the canvas back the river mist had beaded on the pictured children, each with one foot raised, forever going to see what Button wanted.  She let the mayor have his cable back.  Later he would ask for a transcript and she would send him a bill for one private call. 

The old man was looking past her at the loose corner where a square of bare brick showed through.  "We lived just there," he told her, and she waited for him to go on, and when he didn't she let it be.
