The first time her father walked home in his sleep, Mara found him trying to hang his cap on a nail that was now a barnacle.  She took his elbow and steered him back up the canyon without waking him, as he had once steered her past a dog.  By spring she was sewing bells into half the nightshirts on the upper wharf.  The bells had different voices, so that she could lie in bed and hear the old neighborhood assemble wherever the sea had briefly given it back.  It was only after the tide pools emptied through the fissure opened by the quake that the bells began. 

For centuries the lower town had inhabited a labyrinth carved into red canyon walls, and no two families agreed on the way to any given door.  The map in the school storybook showed a straight road, along which a prince rode scattering coins to grateful quarrymen.  Mara's father had printed it for forty years, though there had never been a prince and the quarrymen had been women as often as men.  Mara herself had composed the couplet under the picture in return for her first loom.  She could still hear children chanting gold and old as they skipped rope on the planks outside her shop. 

Now the council wanted the illustration plate retrieved before the lower tunnels were sealed, so that children yet unborn might have the book.  Not one request to salvage a personal possession had been granted, not even for the little blue urn holding the baker's boy.  A thing worth saving, it appeared, was a thing that could be reproduced.  Mara, who wove the wharf nets and knew what a weight the least of them could bear, offered to bring the plate up. 

Her father couldn't find his shop awake, and asleep he went no farther than the barnacle, where he stood rubbing his head in mild perplexity.  But the baker passed that spot on her way to the oven, and at the oven her path crossed that of the man who used to deliver paper.  For three nights Mara followed one sleeper at a time, paying out net cord and tying it off wherever two journeys touched.  On the fourth she went alone, with a tide lamp and the little sledge for hauling wet seines. 

The cord took her to a window through which the paper had been passed to keep it clear of the inky floor.  She went in feet first and felt the familiar bite of the sill against the backs of her knees.  There on its shelf lay the copper plate, the prince forever riding to the left so that on paper he might ride right.  Under it lay the aprons her father had spread to keep the damp from coming through the wood.  On the topmost one a quarrywoman had printed her hand in red dust, a hand with the tip of the middle finger gone.  Mara put her own hand down on her mother's for the first time since the funeral. 

Coming back past the oven with the copper wrapped in aprons, she saw a speck of blue in the lamp glow and stopped the sledge.  In the morning she set the urn on the council table with the plate beside it.  "Your first exception was for something that could make copies," she said, "and mine is for something that can't."  The baker took the urn and went out without asking, and whatever ruling might have followed went out with her.  No one proposed sending it back down. 

Before the tunnels were sealed, Mara took a last impression from the copper, on linen instead of paper.  She cut away the prince and his money and kept the little people on either side, their empty hands lifted as if to receive something.  Into that space she began to sew whatever the neighbors brought: a cap button, an oven mitt, a square of apron bearing a red hand.  They hung the linen where the road up from the ruined wharf met the new cut in the rock, which had no name yet.  In the cut they carved shallow cups for the pale cliff orchids that needed so little earth, and there the baker scattered what she had brought home. 

When the bells rang now, someone always got up to walk beside the sleepers and turn them gently at the flowers.  One night her father found the nail Mara had driven beside the linen, and hung his cap among the lifted hands.  Mara let him leave it there till morning.
