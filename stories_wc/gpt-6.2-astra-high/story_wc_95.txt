While waiting for the auctioneer to reach Lot Ninety, Vera Linn watched two duchesses conduct a silent, murderous bidding war over a plaster ham.  The sale was being held in the glass atrium beside a quiet brook where, for thirty summers, her company had staged its arguments.  As a dueling philosopher Vera had made a handsome living defending propositions with a foil in her good hand and a book in the articulated fingers of her mechanical arm.  Nobody ever asked who had marked the passages in the book. 

Lot Ninety was the company's name and repertory, which Vera meant to buy and bury before anybody could hire a prettier girl to play her.  The plaster ham went for more than she had brought. 

Across the brook, a flash of vermilion appeared on the face of the old scenery shed.  Someone had set their half-painted sundial in the window, propped at the angle for a question and turned so its wooden shadow pointed to the six.  Against unbleached canvas the bare half practically disappeared; you could read the tilt of that red semicircle at fifty yards. 

The company had toured in countries where few of the children hired as extras spoke its language.  To turn questions into destinations, Lise, the wardrobe mistress, had devised a system that spared the smallest spear carrier the indignity of having to ask aloud.  Where could you be sick, or get a torn stocking mended, or tell somebody something the director ought not to hear?  Tip the dial to ask; wait for the shadow to swing; go there. 

Doubtful of her memory after eleven years, Vera took from her waistcoat a Danish tin sewing needle card, the last thing Lise had given her before walking out.  On its back, among the stamped sizes of needles long since used or lost, Lise had scratched the key.  Six meant the fitting room, the one place nobody, not even a duchess, could follow without knocking.  To tip and turn the dial yourself was a hidden overture: would somebody meet you there? 

The duchesses stood aside for her, the victor allowing the vanquished the privilege of carrying the ham. 

In the fitting room Lise was letting out the cuff of a fencing jacket.  She had gone white at the temples and acquired spectacles considerably stronger than Vera's, but she still bit thread with the side tooth that had a notch in it.  Beside her lay two foils and an auction paddle. 

"I can just afford the name," Lise said, "provided you don't bid against me." 

"Who would you get to fence?" 

"That depends on what you think you're buying when you buy a name." 

Vera put on the jacket and took a foil; it was the way they had always begun to work, before there was money in it. 

"A promise of quality," she proposed, and Lise beat her blade aside as neatly as if the intervening years had been an interval for refreshments. 

"Then when you had influenza and we sent everybody home, what did we refund?" 

"The price of admission." 

"Because it wasn't quality they were promised; it was you." 

When they were young Vera would have stolen that line, beaten Lise with it the following evening and bowed to a house gone wild.  Instead she lowered her point. 

"Did you ever once want them to see you win?" 

Lise had always been diplomatically fierce, the one sent to collect unpaid wages without losing next year's booking.  Now she folded Vera's loose cuff back for her before she answered. 

"Every night, and you might have asked me rather sooner." 

The auctioneer's runner knocked to say the name was coming up.  Lise had not called her over to ask permission; she had called her over to see if they could still make something worth a ticket.  Vera fastened the jacket cuff around her mechanical wrist and offered Lise the purse. 

"On the posters," she said, "do you prefer alphabetical order or equal letters and alternating nights?" 

"Alphabetical," said Lise Aaberg, whose gift for the unanswerable Vera had grievously missed. 

They bought the name for less than the price of a ham; without Vera to bid it up, nobody particularly wanted it.  That left enough for posters, though not, as Lise pointed out on the walk to the printer's, for quite the size of letters she had in mind.
