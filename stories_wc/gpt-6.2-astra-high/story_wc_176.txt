After forgiving herself, Esme was able to look at the sole of her left boot and see something besides a sin.  Under the loose heel nail, just where she remembered putting it, was a street organ punched card scrap, folded twice to keep the nail from drawing blood.  She had been nine when the boats came for the children, and cardboard had been cardboard and her foot had hurt. 

Now she was a lost wax caster of forty, home to put up a tablet in the icebound sanctum where the boats had loaded.  The committee wanted the date and the number saved, both of which fitted on a tablet you could carry in your apron.  She had agreed until she unfolded the scrap and heard her mother calling someone in from the quay.  Not in words exactly; it was the deep dip and the little lift peculiar to a name called often and with pleasure.  The organ man had made his living punching those calls as tunes, then charging a penny to hear your sweetheart come round the corner in her absence.  His cards had lined the children's boots for the long walk down to the boats.  So much for the music, everyone said; yet the organ man had learned his trade somewhere, and Esme thought she knew where. 

Above the loading stair, under a roof of clear ice through which the aurora streamed like weed in running water, hung the harbor's oldest instrument.  It was a ceremonial rope, centuries old, braided flat and broad enough to lower a sailor on, which had been its other use.  Before a burial the family parted its braids to let ribbons of sky illuminate the dead face, and the dead sang back one thing the light found there.  Esme's mother had said it wasn't the voice exactly but a transcribed memory, the way a fiddle tune was still a fiddle tune when you whistled it.  No one had tried it on the living. 

Esme set her stool beneath the rope and showed the committee ladies the little stair of holes that meant the dip and lift.  Help me put the light on my face like that, she said, and maybe we can have one name on our tablet. 

Old Vi, who had counted heads at the gangplank, put down her knitting first.  She took the card for a diagram and began hauling the stiff braids apart as if she were dressing a child's hair for church.  One strand snapped, and they all stood quite still in the sound of its small dry report.  Esme was technically gentle, as anyone must be who frees a bronze lacewing from the clay that gives it shape.  She took each crossing between finger and thumb and warmed it until it yielded without a sound, while Vi held the new spaces open with knitting needles. 

At last green bars lay across Esme's upturned face, and her mouth opened on a rush of tenderness so hot it brought water to her eyes.  Aneli, she sang, drawing out the middle syllable, giving the last the upward flick of a dish towel against bare legs. 

Vi sat down hard on the stair.  That was me, she said.  Violet was the woman who took her in on the other side; at school they called her Little Vi, then Vi, and so it went.  I forgot there was an up at the end, Vi said, and laughed as if she'd been tickled. 

She slid under the bars beside Esme and tried it, and from her own mouth came a man's voice calling not Aneli but little Bo.  Esme understood then that the holes did not stand for a particular name; they stood for somebody wanting somebody home.  They went to fetch the other boat children, some of whom had to be carried up the loading stair.  By morning there were thirty names, and no agreement about how to spell even the shortest. 

She laid soft wax along the braids to take their likeness, then cast that open pattern in the committee's bronze, with a rim that would rest across two knees.  When spring came they carried it from house to house under a sky going pale as the underside of a leaf.  Children knelt in the last of the snow to catch the light through it, and old mouths flowered above them.  But the sound Esme loved best came afterward, when somebody at a warm door tried a name without the light and somebody on the road turned round.
