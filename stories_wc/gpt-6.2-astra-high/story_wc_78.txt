At the blue hour the pears began knocking together, although there was not enough wind to lift the hair off Sena's neck.  Their orchard floated on a circle of driftwood in the open seas, each tree on its own little raft, the roots knitting below into a single dark room.  Something in that room was trying to stand up. 

She fetched the xylophone mallet from the schoolhouse and laid her cheek against the hollow alder that served the children for a bass bar.  Through wood and bone came the thin ascending cry of a bellback calf.  Then came the slap of its flukes against the pear roots, and all along the eastern rim green pears jumped their stems. 

As leeward tide prophet, Sena was supposed to say where to cut.  The last time a calf had sounded beneath them, her father had opened a passage with an axe; eleven trees had gone sailing off together, still bearing the penciled heights of half the children in the orchard.  He was bringing her that axe now, handle first, as one offers a guest a knife at supper. 

She looked past him to the cloud streets, whose advancing ends were bending south while their bellies still hurried east.  She had learned to interpret their movements before she could read a clock: by the time the lowest clouds turned, the swell would come across their present drift and roll the calf in the net of roots.  Already its mother was calling from the north, where the roots hung thickest.  South, under the schoolhouse, there was a lane kept clear for launching boats.  If the calf would only turn its great blunt head. 

Sena took the axe and drew on its blade with the greasy chalk she used for teaching sketched resonance.  The children knew how to put a sound on slate: a stem for the first long note, a fork for the two shorter ones that grew out of it.  A bellback cow sang a fractal, generating each new phrase by splitting the notes of the phrase before, until what had begun as a groan ended in a delicious gravelly trill.  It was the trill that carried the length of the orchard; the low notes you felt only when the singer was close. 

She put the drawing in her father's hands.  "I want to dub another voice over hers, right here where we need the little one to come." 

He looked at the school instrument, nine bright scraps of stove enamel on a frame no longer than his arm.  "You couldn't call me to dinner on that." 

No, but the thing that barred the calf's way was itself a room full of wood.  All her life Sena had wanted to turn obstacles into instruments; as a child she had played the locked schoolhouse door until they let her in.  She went down into the launch lane with the mallet tucked into her belt.  The alder lying across its mouth was rotten at the heart, useless for keeping boats off the gateposts but deep as a drum when she struck it from the water.  Overhead her father distributed forks of the chalk tree among the children, giving the slow ones floats to stamp on and the quick ones pear poles to rattle against the floats. 

The first time through they ran away with the tune, as children will, and the calf kept on pushing north.  The second time Sena felt a tug at her belt.  The calf was there beside her, beating time with its jaw against the alder, waiting for this ridiculous mother to draw breath.  She ducked under the gate timber and came up outside, but the calf stayed where it was; overhead the children had begun again.  She wedged herself against the current and began to beat the long note on the timber, again and again, without going on to the fork. 

Her father heard the note that did not fork, the great chalk stem held out to him without instructions.  He quieted the children one by one until the only singing came from the cow, who had followed their counterfeit music round the rim.  The calf went out so close that its soft lip rasped Sena's ankle.  Then the cross swell lifted everybody, pears and prophets and the two slick backs traveling side by side.  From inside the circle came three impatient taps: her father at the alder with the flat of the axe, calling her in to dinner.
