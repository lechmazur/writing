Until I went down to liberate my mother's voice, I had only ever heard it tell people to stand clear of the doors.  The railway had other women for the weather, the time, the names of stops where nobody got off anymore.  The women themselves had gone home from the war with ruined throats and little brass cards which entitled them to ride for nothing.  Mother always walked. 

In the sunken laboratory under Central Station, a spider paid out a line from the playback horn to the wrist of the man who owned the voices.  He sat so still it took him for furniture, and Mother, who had made the appointment, touched my knee to keep me from brushing it away.  Through the thick glass ceiling we could see the familiar undersides of the ferries, green with weed like old pennies left in a fountain.  When the river took the lower platforms they had saved this one room by fitting a door like the lid of a preserving jar. 

A woman could take her voice away if she could pick her own spool from the racks; that right had been written into the women's contracts as a joke.  There were nine hundred spools, none marked, and the company allowed a claimant one guess.  Inside each hub was a sealed slip bearing the speaker's thumbprint. 

I made my living breaking ciphers for a music publisher, finding the forbidden hymns that country fiddlers had tucked inside respectable quadrilles.  Mother had brought me a journal full of such tunes, or so I thought until she emptied the hangman's dice into my palm.  They were bone, worn hollow at the sixes, the very pair the prison executioner used to settle which of two condemned men had to watch the other die.  The railway had borrowed them to assign each spool its secret address: one throw for rack and shelf, another for drawer and spindle.  Before each recording the overseer shook out the dice where the woman at the horn could see them. 

Prisoners were searched going home, but a woman was allowed to keep her doctor's journal, a record of the daily failing of her instrument.  Mother had brought the dice home under her tongue, where nobody on that last day had bothered to look.  We had thirty such journals in the shopping bag at our feet.  I had tried singing the entries all the previous night, and all I got for my pains was the tune Mother used to hum against my skull when I was small. 

Now she stood up and explained it the way she had explained a waltz to a child who kept expecting music to come out of her mouth.  She danced my part and then her own.  Six sliding steps to a phrase, four phrases to the little turn that brought her round to face me; and at each catch in the written breath she let her heel strike wood.  Not the notes, then, but the places where the notes gave out. 

I opened her journal to the day of the armistice, when they had all made their last recordings.  "Please stand clear of the doors" was written four times in a wavering hand, with the doctor's red mark of weakness at a different syllable each time.  All those women, their mouths inspected for sores, had saved the one thing nobody would dream of confiscating: the right to stumble where they chose.  They had put their strength there for one another to find. 

But when I reached for the first drawer Mother caught my wrist.  She pointed to the shopping bag, then to the ferry sliding overhead with its black fringe of shoes along the rail.  Upstairs, twenty-nine old women were waiting for me to tell them whether they could come down, and I had nearly spent our one good surprise on a private miracle.  I went up and brought them down. 

They came in their good coats, moving their lips over the entries as I danced them, until each woman had four numbers to say with her fingers.  The spider was tying off its last small room of air when the man behind the desk found himself confronted by thirty perfectly civil claimants with their hands already on the drawers.  He broke the seals himself, and it was his thumb that shook when he pressed theirs to the ink. 

Mother left her brass card on his desk.  At the station exit she put a penny in the public player and fitted her spool.  She stopped it before the first word, took the spool out again and slipped it into her pocket.  I had thought all the way down that freedom would sound like something.  We walked home listening to our shoes.
