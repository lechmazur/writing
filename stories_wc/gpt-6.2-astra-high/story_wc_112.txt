Nobody at the xenobiology research station liked to put a broken thing on a form, because the form wanted to know who had broken it.  Instead they hung it on the rail where Len, the obliging sky mechanic, tied up his little weather skiff.  He mended spectacles and tail fans and once the hinge of a brass walnut that a homesick woman kept her salt in.  On the morning of the evacuation he found a form hanging there instead. 

It certified that the station was empty, apart from the moths, and it wanted his signature.  Six months ago he had loaded his sister's skiff for a survey flight she had never made.  Ruth had gone into the moth house for her mittens and simply not come out.  The inquiry preferred a fall through lake ice, although the ice had borne the weight of the men sent to cut a hole in it. 

He took the form inside.  The moth house was mechanically warm, its floor pulsing under his socks like the throat of something swallowing a succession of small, hard pills.  Along the glass hung a thousand white wings, each with a fringe of ice where its tip touched the pane.  Ruth had studied their subdued awakenings, the hour when nothing stirred except the second image of each moth in the glass.  She had left him a notebook full of drawings of those images doing things their originals had yet to do, or never did at all.  In the margin he had written, rather crossly, that a reflection ought at least to have the decency to be simultaneous. 

What if it was, she had written back. 

He wanted to prove that time moves sideways, which was how Ruth had put it to him once over their morning salt eggs.  Not back to an egg already eaten, she said, but across to a breakfast where you hadn't cracked it yet. 

All that summer he had been polishing the ceiling.  The moths saw through holographic principles: each scale on a wing returned a little of the light from the whole room, and together the wings could build another room out of light.  He had given the moths a mirror big enough to see what was looking back.  Now he had to wait for the magnetic nexus at twilight, when the field that set their scales on edge stood upright in the lake.  The pilot called twice; the second time she said the runners were beginning to freeze down.  Len told her to lever them up and go, and it cost him something to say it without adding that it would only take a minute. 

Then, within the hush of beating wings at rest, he heard a pencil roll off a table.  Overhead, upside down, Ruth stooped to pick it up.  She had cut her hair with what looked like the scissors on his pocketknife, which she had always sworn she didn't have.  He put a hand up and hers came down, and both of them stopped a little short, having learnt something about glass as children. 

On the back of the form he wrote COME HOME.  She read it and turned her own paper round for him to see.  It was the same form, except that where his name should have been it said Ruth, and on the back she had written exactly what he had.  Behind her, through the frosted pane, he could see the amber riding light of a skiff tied to the rail.  Somebody there had mended the tail fan, sounded the lake ice, been good about the brass walnut.  He had imagined six months of her knocking in the dark, not six months of her looking for him. 

He crossed out HOME and wrote SUPPER, SIX TOMORROW?  Ruth thought about it and held up seven fingers.  Their hands went to their watches; the two minute hands ticked over together, and the ceiling was only a ceiling. 

Len took the form to the radio and dictated a change to the station inventory: two occupied quarters, one table in each.  The pilot asked for evidence, and Len looked down at the form, which had acquired, beneath his invitation, two faint words in Ruth's hand. 

BRING SALT. 

He turned it over and ran his thumb across the little ridges her pencil had raised in his world.
