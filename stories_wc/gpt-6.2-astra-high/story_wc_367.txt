Six weeks after the last print newspaper, the dead were still queuing at the emerald lighthouse under the stars, exact change in their cold hands.  They had never cared about the news, but they needed the harbor chart that the paper printed every morning beneath the tides.  Without it they could not steer the sun in, and without the sun the tide that came at night would never turn. 

For six weeks May had been selling them the same copy and buying it back for the same penny when the sun came up.  The dead liked this arrangement because they could go on admiring the same wedding photograph.  It was hers.  Her wife Kit was the storm herald, whose peculiar office it was to stand on the breakwater and laugh at approaching weather until it declared itself.  You could tell how bad things would get by how hard the weather had to work to drown her out. 

At their wedding Kit had said that the next time her mother visited Laundry Street she could row right up to the parlor window.  It had been a joke about the landlady's plumbing.  Tonight May could see a skiff knocking at that window, and from the breakwater she could hear nothing at all. 

She went down to the landing, where the dead were climbing into their pilot boat and unfolding the paper along its soft, familiar creases.  Beyond them the sun rode low in the black water, a banked coal the size of a house.  Between it and the harbor mouth lay the breakwater, fallen clean across the channel the chart showed clear.  The pilots would tow their cargo straight onto the stones. 

May took the paper back.  Not one of them looked up at what had happened to the harbor; what was right in front of them had never been their province.  But their shadows lay across the landing as they always did, each in the shape of the piece of shore its owner had once known best.  May had spent her childhood jumping across those little ditches of darkness, careful never to tread on a dead man's home. 

The oldest pilot cast the shadow of a river with a kink in it precisely like the bend in Laundry Street.  On its bank stood a willow, long since cut down, whose roots still lifted the boards of their parlor.  Kit had not known what she was saying, but the water knew where it used to go. 

May ran up the spiral stairs and lifted the glass lantern wick from its socket, trailing a green skein of filaments behind her all the way down.  Through total internal reflection the filaments carried the last of yesterday's light around every bend in the stairwell without spilling a drop.  She pointed the wick at the oldest pilot's heels and spread the newspaper in the path of his shadow.  Ink faded where the green light touched it; when she lifted the wick, only the river remained. 

The old man touched the crooked line as if someone had handed him a photograph of his mother.  "That's a bed of reeds," he said, pointing at the place where May slept beside her wife.  "They'll bend and let us through." 

Across the drowned town a woman in a yellow herald's coat was helping their landlady through the window into the skiff.  May laid her penny on the chart to hold it steady, and the pilots put their backs into the tow.  The sun came up Laundry Street, taking the front off their house as neatly as a carpenter opening a dollhouse with his thumb.  For a moment their kitchen shone entire, two cups hung by their handles and a twist of lemon peel still lying on the table.  Then the tide turned and took the table out to sea.  From the skiff came a low exhausted chuckle, the loveliest sound May had ever heard. 

By noon the river had settled between its ancient banks, and the women sat above it with their shoes off, making a list on the back of the new harbor chart.  Two cups, a table, a room high enough to be reached by stairs even when Kit's mother came.  They headed it WANTED, and while they sat there laughing in the shadow of the tower, somebody penciled in a spare room.
