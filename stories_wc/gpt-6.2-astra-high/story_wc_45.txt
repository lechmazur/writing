After the substitute body was found in the eel trap, my daughter had the distinction of being wanted for her own murder.  The alderman said there must be a proper corpse somewhere, since nobody went to the trouble of drowning a counterfeit without good reason.  I told him that was excellent reasoning apart from the reasoning, and lent him my coat, for he had gone in up to his neck to fetch her out. 

The thing on the slab was willow pulp built up around a changeling's baby tooth, the little blue peg I used to keep in my workbox.  I knew it by the cleft in its crown, where my needle always caught when I went fishing for the scissors.  At breakfast Nell had asked, in her shrewdly innocent way, whether you could steal what you had once given away.  I wished now that I had asked which of us she meant. 

I found her in the secret garden behind the hedge maze, where my old master had made me grow the willow for my first set of splints.  She was waist deep in the channel that fed the eel pond, feeling in the mud with her good hand.  "It got away before I could see if the wrist was right," she said. 

She meant wrong, of course: she had wanted the wrist exactly as I had left hers, with the little hitch that kept her from turning a key.  She had been six when she fell off the sundial, and I had been the only bone setter for thirty miles.  I could still feel how beautifully the ends had slipped together, and how sure I had been that I could trust that feeling.  Ever since, there had been a cleft in certainty for me, a place where a person might put the tip of a finger and feel the break. 

"I want your mistakes," Nell said, climbing out, and when I started on my usual apology she shook pondweed at me.  "Not for me to keep, you goose, for me to teach with." 

She led me to the potting shed where my master used to hang his diagrams of perfect limbs, pink as the hams at the butcher's.  On a bench lay a dozen imperfect willow hands, each grown around a shaving from the tooth, each faithful in some particular to the hand she could not turn.  The last had taken the rest of the tooth and enough pulp to make a whole girl, who had promptly rolled off the bench into the channel.  Now she wanted me to break a wrist.  I said I would rather break my own. 

She sat me down where I had sat as an apprentice, with the green light wavering on the walls and that old smell of wet pennies from the pump.  She put a hand in mine and folded the fingers over until I felt, through the pulp, the familiar give of a child's small bones.  For years I had let my pupils watch me work but never touch, as though they would learn bone setting by coming to yearn sufficiently for the knack.  Now I made the old mistake on purpose, and Nell slid a lamp under the hand.  Through the wet willow we could see the two ends riding one over the other, although my fingers had sworn to a faultless join.  On the glass above the lamp she marked their positions with pinpricks in black paper; when she lifted the hand away two crooked chains of stars shone on the ceiling. 

"When I lay awake in that splint," she said, "I used to turn the cracks in the plaster overhead into constellations and give them names."  "That's what I want to do with the mistakes." 

By evening the alderman had brought the body round in a barrow, rather sheepish about the murder, and I put him to work hanging a sheet for a better sky.  That winter we taught six apprentices in the potting shed, working by touch before we let them light the lamp.  We called those first crooked stars the Key, and by spring I could send a pupil out to a hurt child knowing she could see them even in broad daylight.
