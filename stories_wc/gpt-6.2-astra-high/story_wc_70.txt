At the toll window of the lightning collection tower, the first thing anybody asked after the city-wide blackout was whether the lift was safe.  "With those knees of yours, safer than the stairs," Ada told an old man, and gave him his penny back for making him laugh so hard he had to sit.  It had never been a virtue, her candor; a lie simply turned to the truth on her tongue, like a glove pulled inside out.  But she had learned something about asking questions, which did not have to hurt. 

On the counter she kept a lacquer box whose lid her late wife had painted with the two pear terraces that circled the tower like the brims of a splendid hat.  Inside were matchboxes with a riddle apiece for the children who rode up each evening with supper for the lightning crews.  They were apt to say there was nothing to see on the same old ride, a judgment Ada took personally. 

That evening a girl called Linn picked the matchbox marked with a wing and shook out a twist of yellow paper.  "I drink with the Bells and dine with the Reds; what do I carry between their beds?" 

Outside the lift cage, dusk lay thick as apricot syrup on the white hoods of the Bell pears and the pink throats of the Reds.  Between the terraces the little brass pollinators were pouring from the slots of the drone beehive, full of last night's lightning.  Linn let her father's soup go cold on her lap and rode the cage back down. 

"It's a trick," she said, laying one finger on each of the painted terraces.  "The upstairs ones stay upstairs and the downstairs ones stay down." 

Ada rode up beside her through the iron lace of the shaft and saw she was right.  From below, both flocks made a single golden gauze; only from the moving cage could you see the clear air between them.  Her wife had tended those trees for thirty years and had taught her what happened when Bell pollen went to Bell blossoms.  You got a beautiful soft rain of petals and not one pear. 

The tower superintendent came down at the closing bell and found Ada still selling tickets, for a penny that she handed straight back.  At her request every passenger had taken one last ride and traced a bee's journey with a fingertip on the lacquer.  No finger had crossed the little black groove her wife had cut to show the gap between the terraces. 

"Are you telling these people my hive has failed?" he asked. 

She wanted to say she was only playing her game, and felt the familiar small wrench in her mouth.  "I'm telling them the bees have been flying wrong since you got the lights back on, and I've spent all evening wishing somebody besides me would tell you." 

He had signed her wife's last pay envelope, and for an instant she saw how tired he was of being told what else had gone.  Then he put his thumb in the groove where all those fingers had stopped.  "We got them to remember where to feed," he said, "but not where to go next." 

By morning the breeze coming off the river would have stripped the oldest trees, already shedding petals on the people in the queue.  While he took the night crew to work on the hive, Ada unhooked the velvet rope that kept the lift passengers off the terraces.  She asked Linn to show the first arrivals how to take a blossom by the stem and dust its gold across the open faces of the other kind.  They went up with Bells and down with Reds, the lacquer lid propped in the cage to show which trees still needed visitors.  Each time a tree was finished, Linn stuck a petal to its likeness with a dab of cold soup. 

By the time the little brass bodies began to stitch the dark together across the gap, there were only two bare trees left on the lid.  Linn's father came down to fetch his supper and asked Ada whether the children had saved the harvest.  "Some of it," she said, and was glad for once that what came out was neither smaller nor bigger than what she knew. 

In the empty matchbox Linn placed two petals, white against pink, and slid it shut for whoever got the same old ride tomorrow.
