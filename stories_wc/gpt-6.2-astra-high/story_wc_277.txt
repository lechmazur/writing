After the journalist called, Ada Vale climbed the last flight to the observatory carrying the telephone, its long red cord uncoiling behind her like a tongue finally prepared to tell on somebody. 
Below the windows lay an unbroken floor of cloud; above her, the copper dome gave back each footfall twice, once for the newsprint heiress and once, she supposed, for the barefoot girl who had played here. 
"Still there?" she asked, and the dome asked too. 
The journalist said the auction would begin at noon unless somebody could establish that the town already owned the building. 

For its opening in 1936 her grandfather had offered the observatory to the town if any resident could settle a question printed on the wrappers of his newsprint rolls. 
Could a walker leave the mill, cross each of the valley's seven bridges exactly once, and finish back at the mill? 
"A route or a reason why not," the old man had promised, delighted with the economy of a puzzle that made even his wrapping paper worth reading. 
Nobody, according to the family history Ada herself had edited, had answered correctly before the winter deadline. 

"What makes you think somebody did?" she asked. 
"My mother never told a lie except about the dentist," the journalist said, "and she said she won this place for me." 

Ada set down the receiver beside the telescope and opened the shallow drawer where her grandfather had kept what he called his orbits of regret: all the letters he intended to answer when their writers had cooled off. 
Having been a devotedly observant child, she knew the drawer was shorter inside than out, and having been an obedient one, she had never discovered why. 
Now she wanted to know what truth had required those missing inches more urgently than she wanted to go on loving him without difficulty. 
She pulled the drawer clear and tipped the desk forward onto her knee. 

Out slid a wrapper, a letter, and the rosewood pen nib her grandfather had carved wide enough to lay a handsome black line on paper as thirsty as his. 
She remembered him using it to bar the losing routes, a little gate across the first bridge crossed twice; he had let her swing the gate shut herself on Saturdays. 
On this wrapper there was no gate, only three black dots on the island where the laundress used to spread her sheets. 

The letter was from that laundress, and its argument proceeded through proof by contradiction, although its author, addressing a man who sent his collars back if they scratched, had chosen the language of his trade. 
"Suppose I did your walk and came home, sir; then every time I carried a collar onto my bleaching island I must have carried it off again." 
"I should therefore use the island bridges in pairs, whereas you have given me three and forbidden me to use one twice." 
On the envelope her grandfather had written, in the broad black stroke she knew, "Received December ninth; ask if she will take cash instead." 

Ada could almost hear the reasonable voice in which he would have explained that the telescope was the one good instrument within a hundred miles and children put their fingers on glass. 
Indeed they did; there was still a whorl near the eyepiece that she privately believed was hers. 
She picked up the telephone and asked to be put on the loudspeaker in the auction room. 

To intervene meant interrupting her own son, who had spent a year arranging the sale, and she was grateful when he said, quite distinctly, "Go ahead, Mother," before she had to ask. 
She read the letter, the postmark, and the note on the envelope, then asked the journalist what his mother would have wanted done with the keys. 
"Nothing fancy," he said after a moment. 
"She wanted to take me up there on a clear night and show me what it was that pulled the water out from under her bridges." 

That evening Ada steadied the ladder while a man of seventy put one eye to the telescope and both hands exactly where they ought not to go. 
The moon stood half in shadow, its battered face so close that when he whispered "Look, Ma," the dome, with perfect courtesy, invited her again.
