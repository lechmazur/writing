There were two boxes on the harbor survey, HOME and CONCESSION, and my mother sold crullers through the window over my bed.  I had designed the form. 

That summer before privatization, the carnival on our raft city ran all night as usual, but people had begun sleeping in their good clothes.  At dawn a photographer came out from the bank to take pictures for the auction catalog, and nobody wanted to look poor enough to move. 

My municipal badge said LIAISON; the payroll clerk, who also rode the ghost train as a professional screamer, called me our culture shock absorber.  I explained to visitors that a towel across a gangway meant somebody was bathing, and to my neighbors that a smiling man with a tape measure was not necessarily measuring them for curtains.  I liked the clean columns in the harbor office, where the price of replacing one rotten float could be set against the probable cost of a drowning.  My brother had been one of the probable costs. 

Without the sale of the concessions there would be no money for new floats.  With it, according to my neat columns, there would be eleven homes left out of ninety. 

On the morning the catalog proof arrived, my mother was using our foundation stone chip to hold down a stack of greaseproof paper.  It was all her father had salvaged of the old town hall when the river took the square, a wedge of red sandstone still bearing half the carved word PUBLIC.  She wrapped a hot cruller for the photographer and handed it across, and I saw that the stone had left a rusty kiss on the wrapper.  The man folded the stained paper into his wallet as carefully as if he'd bought a little print.  The red dust gave me an idea, and an ache at what it would cost. 

We had long conducted the business of the carnival through messages encoded in graffiti art.  A fiddler painted with one eye shut sent the next relief band to the east landing; two shut eyes meant west.  Children learned the pictures before they could read the tide clock. 

I asked my mother if I could grind up what was left of the town hall.  She lifted the paper and shook the crumbs into my palm first, in case they might be enough.  They weren't. 

Town hall red meant mourning; when my brother died, every painted fiddler had worn it for the single minute the whole city went quiet.  This time I mixed the powder with cooking oil and painted two red hands pointing straight up on the clock faces our crews watched for shift changes.  Below each clock I stenciled the little hanging quilt that told relief crews which rooms were ready for the tired to come home to. 

At noon I brought the photographer and the harbor commissioners down the avenue of mirrors to see what, precisely, they meant to sell.  The mirrors folded flat.  Behind them a barber took the board off his bath; a woman lifted the ducks out of the shooting gallery and lowered a cradle on its pulley.  My mother swung the fryer out over the water and let down my quilt, still scorched along one edge.  Only the ghost train kept running, its cars full of people paying a penny to see us at home.  It took more nerve than a strike, to let strangers see the shape your body had left in a bed.  The photographer didn't lift his camera until my mother nodded. 

In my pocket was the stamp we used for winter berths, with spaces for starting and finishing times.  I proposed we reorganize the auction around trading hours instead of square footage, the way we let two boats use one berth.  Buyers could bid for a shift at a counter; they couldn't buy the deck out from under a sleeping child. 

The chairman asked whether hours would fetch enough for ninety floats.  I said I could promise him nothing about ninety, but I could show him which six were likeliest to give way by winter.  It was those six the commissioners agreed to put out for repair against the first sale of hours. 

His two pictures, taken from the same plank, went on facing pages in the revised catalog.  That fall my mother lost the supper shift to a man who put powdered pearls on doughnuts, but at eight he had to knock to get his hat back.  She let him in, of course; he was standing on a perfectly sound float, in the rain, outside our door.
