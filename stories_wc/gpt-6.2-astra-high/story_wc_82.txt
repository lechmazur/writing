After the shadow detached from her mother's feet and settled under the tea bushes, Nell took the shears from the slack hand and finished clipping the row.  It was a mean little row, blighted at one end, and it was the last thing in the world her mother had wanted done.  Or the next to last, if the glass basilica was to be believed.  Already its bell was calling the pickers out of the floating island's tea garden to witness the last dream of Ada Vale. 

The island rose as the evening air cooled, and the basilica, weighted like the wick in one of Nell's storm lanterns, slid down through the island on brass runners.  Thus it descended while ascending, its glass floor passing the roots just when they shone with the newly dead.  A sapphire set into the floor caught that shine and held it; this was how a dream was recorded before the roots drank it dry.  Turn the sapphire toward the vault and the whole garden could watch. 

Nell had watched her father drown there twice, once because the neighbors loved him and once because an aunt had come late.  Afterward her mother had taught her to fold origami cranes out of the paper sleeves the good tea came in.  There was a moment when you had to push the neck in with your thumb and the paper seemed bound to tear, but it made a head instead.  Nell wanted that for her grief now, something other than the shape it took when light was simply let through. 

She went into the sinking church with her lantern bag, ahead of the verger, whose bad hip obliged him to wait until the steps were level.  The sapphire was still warm when she eased it from its socket with the shears.  In its depths she saw a woman with a basket going slowly down a row of heads that floated at the height of tea blossoms.  At each head the woman stopped and held her palm beneath its chin to see if it was breathing.  Nell knew whose head would come last, and that the woman would have to go on checking it as long as anybody cared to look. 

She got out her chamois and her tin of diamond dust.  She earned her living polishing gems for lanterns; give her a stone that flung a flame half a mile and she could make it lay that flame like a warm penny in your hand.  The trick was not to polish the face that met the light but the one it left by. 

"I've a right to see her," said the verger behind her, and she remembered that before he was a man with keys he had been her mother's little brother. 

"Have you got a tea sleeve?" she asked. 

He emptied one into his hat, and she slit it into squares and held one to the facet she had polished nearly flat.  Light left the stone reluctantly, as milk leaves a breast sore with too much milk.  There on the paper lay a single instant, Ada's open palm beneath a sleeping child's mouth, and in the sapphire the woman had moved one head farther down the row.  The verger put out his fingers for the square. 

"That's me with the chipped tooth," he said. 

They worked together as the stars came level with the windows, using up the last of the sleeve and then the tissue skins of Nell's unsold lanterns.  When her father's face came through, his wet mustache lifted a little in the draft of a breath he had never drawn.  Nell had been wrong about where the row ended.  There was one head more, her own, gray at the temples as her mother would never see her, creased at the mouth from some long pleasure yet to come. 

She took that slip outside, where her uncle was showing the pickers how to transform their squares without tearing through the little patch of shine.  Down every row of bushes there were hands at work, some making cranes and some just cupped around the light.  She pushed her own bright neck in with her thumb, and when the head came right she set the bird down in the gap at the blighted end.  The hand glimmered along one wing, palm up, with nothing left to test.
