The kingfisher feather quill nib was so small that a bead of ink could hide it entirely.  From across the council table it looked as if old Hen held a single drop of the frozen lake suspended over the blank page.  Below them the millwheel beat inside its house at the foot of the obsidian spire, fed by deep water that came out black and never froze.  There was money for one improvement before the masons sealed the millhouse wall for another generation.  Hen had dipped the pen to order a second pair of grinding stones. 

"Before you write our future," said Nell, "let me show you what the second pair buys." 

Nell was the lake district's ignorance cartographer, a post created after a surveyor mistook an unmeasured depth for a shallow one and drowned three horses.  She marked what nobody knew and what it might cost to find out.  On her map of mill traffic there had long been a white interval between a farmer's setting out with grain and his coming home with flour.  Everybody remembered the queues and nobody remembered the hours when there was no queue to remember.  She had spent a winter in the tollhouse recording the passage of each sled, including the ones that turned back when the lake began to speak under their runners. 

Now she laid a long strip of linen across the table, each day divided into hours by stitches she could feel in bad light.  Barley grains stood for sacks, and two ivory buttons stood for pairs of stones.  She gave Hen the toll book from a winter she had not used in making her model.  He called out the loads as they had crossed, and she advanced the barley by the measured appetite of one pair of stones.  Before he turned each leaf she told him how many sacks would go home unground.  Once she missed by six sacks; the rest of the time she came within two. 

Then they played the same winter with both buttons.  On the worst day every sack went home as flour by noon, a sight that made Hen look at her with something like reproach.  His brother had lost two toes waiting on that shore.  Nell let him enjoy the little clean sweep before she unrolled the rest of the linen.  Beyond that day lay nineteen days when a single button had eaten everything by noon, and eleven when no sled had dared the crossing. 

"I used to draw wheels in those spaces," she said.  She turned the linen over, rather shyly, and there they were: a rag beater for making paper, a lathe for crutches, a set of cams to lift and drop a fuller's hammers.  Each drawing ended at the same square hole.  All she wanted built into the new wall was the end of the millshaft, with a clutch inside under the miller's hand.  Outside, anybody with an idea and a matching socket could borrow the river. 

"And my brother can stand there and freeze again," Hen said. 

"Yes," said Nell, and made herself leave the barley where it stood on the worst day. 

Hen set his pen across the inkwell and asked for the buttons.  He tried a winter with twice the grain, then one with half the safe crossing days; Nell watched her beautiful empty intervals shrink without quite disappearing.  At last he took the little knife he used for trimming nibs and cut a square hole in the bottom of an empty barley box. 

"My brother has a gristmill you turn by hand," he said, fitting the box over the square handle of the knife.  "Not much of a mill, but suppose he brought it down on the bad days." 

She nearly told him the gearing he would need, then caught herself; his brother had been mending things before she was born.  That was how the socket came to prevail over the stones, while the page was still blank and not a penny had been spent.  Hen picked up the quill, and Nell rolled her linen to the first open hour, where there was just room to stitch his brother's name.
