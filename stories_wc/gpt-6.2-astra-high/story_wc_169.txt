On the last day of school, June took the desks down to the tide-polished grotto, one for herself and one for Kit.  The council wanted the schoolhouse for a clinic, and nobody, including Kit, thought a doctor ought to examine people behind a map of France.  What he objected to was the plan for his education, which consisted of packets coming over on the Tuesday boat.  Who, he had asked the council, would get the other end of his skipping rope? 

June was a logical dreamer: she liked to puzzle out new equations for hope, but she insisted on being able to show her work.  She had brought the desks to the one place on the island where one plus one did not reliably make two.  At low tide, through a seam in the basalt, you could look across parallel timelines into other editions of the grotto.  Nothing substantial had ever come through except a pear, already rotten on the side where somebody's thumb had pushed it.  But the fishermen used to send trial balloons into the seam before a launch, to see how many other crews had judged the sea worth risking.  The little bladders came back smelling of other men's tobacco. 

Kit blew up three of the new red kind and tied on slips asking whether anybody over there still had a school.  All three bumped the rock and hung there, turning slowly on their strings.  June consulted her father's balloonist's altitude gauge, an instrument that had always entertained him by reporting changes in the height of his kitchen.  We call it height, he used to say, but what it feels is the weight of air.  Sure enough, just before the seam admitted the first red balloon, the needle stirred as if the grotto had begun a gentle ascent.  Kit marked the place on the dial with jam. 

By noon they had eleven answers, all variations on a theme: one scholar and a shut school, two scholars and a teacher going blind, a fine brick classroom full of onions.  June sat at her desk composing a careful proposal to trade lessons by post, each teacher taking the other's scholar in turn.  Meanwhile Kit unpacked the paper decorations the council had given him after the little farewell party at which nobody had known quite what to celebrate.  He began creating patterns with decorations on a balloon string: two gold stars, a paper fish, two gold stars, a space.  At the jam mark he let it go. 

It came back with a fish in the space and, farther down the string, a row of blue moons grouped in threes, then fives, then sevens.  June put down her pencil.  Kit was lucidly watchful now, quite unlike the boy who dutifully guessed at the answer he thought she had in mind.  He threaded nine moons, considered, and took them off again. 

"Could be the odd numbers or it could be the primes," he said, and sent back a perfectly bare string with a question mark dangling from it.  From the other side came a sound neither of them had ever heard in the grotto: several children arguing. 

For all her plans to revitalize the island school, June had supposed a school meant somebody at one end of a room who already knew.  She tore up her proposal for exchanging lessons and wrote an invitation instead, specifying not an hour but a height above sea level, as measured at the sea. 

On Tuesday the boat brought the first packet, which made excellent stiff paper for cutting out blue moons.  By then the doctor had moved in under France, the teacher with failing eyes had taught the children a knot they could count by touch, and June had learned to keep her mouth shut while a question was still traveling.  She had also learned what to do when the needle fell and the rock became just rock again.  She took the other end of the skipping rope, and they went outside.
