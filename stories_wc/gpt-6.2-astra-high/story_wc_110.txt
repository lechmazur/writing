By the time Ada lifted her hands for the encore, she had been with the orchestra long enough for words to have become unnecessary.  Even the substitute horn knew from the little stirring of her left thumb how softly she wanted him to come in.  I listened from my booth, the quiet night registrar who had signed her in for her conducting trial thirty years before and happened also to be her sister.  Nobody watching that thumb could have doubted she had deserved the job.  That was what made the next thing so hard. 

Instead of taking her bow, Ada came down the aisle and laid three old concert tickets on my blotter.  On the back of each, in my hand, was a little constellation of blots surrounding what might have been a broken comb.  She had kept them all these years in the pocket of the score she had conducted that night, the one they wanted for the permanent exhibition.  Under the glass it would lie open to the page where she had brought the horns in so beautifully, without a rehearsal or a moment of hesitation. 

"I can't let them put it in there like that," she said. 

I looked past her at the people waiting to kiss her cheek and asked if she had to do this tonight.  For answer she held out her hand, and for the first time since we were girls I let my little sister lead me somewhere. 

Outside, the crystal forecourt was whispering after rain.  Water stood in the sockets of its glass paving blocks, making the low notes of the departing traffic sing against the soles of our shoes.  As children waiting for our mother to finish cleaning the hall, we had discovered something about that pavement which none of the grownups seemed to know.  After a shower you could stand on certain blocks and eavesdrop on what went on in the rooms beneath, where the committee played through the music it meant to spring on candidates upstairs.  The sound was ambiguously clear: you could no more make out a word than hear a fish talk, but a phrase of music rose intact through the wet glass. 

The blots were the blocks, and the broken comb was the flight of steps beside which you had to start counting.  I had slipped Ada the tickets on three successive evenings because our mother was dead, because we owed rent, because I had a hundred reasons and needed only one.  Now Ada stood where I had put her and listened to somebody downstairs picking out the encore with one finger. 

"We should have brought him out here with us," she said. 

No need to ask whom she meant: Emil, who had lost the trial to her, had come back tonight to present the score to the city.  He had once asked me point blank if the librarian had let Ada see the music, and I had said no, with all the force of somebody telling the exact truth.  Ever since, he had sent his requests for scores in writing instead of going downstairs to fetch them from his old friend. 

Back in the hall I took the lens from the projector we used for showing scores during lectures and laid it on the blotter, curved side up, over the clearest of the tickets.  When Emil arrived with the archivist, he bent to the glass, and I showed him which end of the comb was chipped and told him where to put his feet.  Ada told him the rest, though twice I had to stop her taking credit for things that had been my idea.  He did not tell us it was all right, and we did not ask him to.  He asked for the librarian's number, and a chair where he might sit awhile before he dialed it. 

The score went under glass with the tickets face down beside it and our signed account beneath, in type large enough to read without bending.  I still work nights, but when my sister comes by after a concert we no longer have to talk about the rain or the trains or the price of eggs.  Sometimes we sit there without saying anything, which turns out to be quite different from having something you must not say.
