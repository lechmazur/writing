By the time the ice went out of the harbor, every pilot had to pass an oral examination in the language of the capital.  This was awkward in a town where even the gulls expected to be cursed in two dialects, neither of them official.  Night school was free, but the first man who went there came home to find a slate tied round the neck of his oldest goat.  The joke had been Lena's father's, and now he could not bring himself to go either. 

Lena was a glassblower, given to long considerations of what a thing might hold besides what you put in it.  The abandoned control room above the dry dock held a desk, six chairs, and the possibility of asking a foolish question without acquiring a goat.  All it lacked was privacy; anybody on the quay could see through the tall panes that had once let the dockmaster watch a keel settle onto its blocks.  She fitted the lower panes with ribbed glass from her shop, leaving the upper ones clear for the sake of the light.  From outside, six pilots would look like six wavering pillars, their caps indistinguishable from their heads. 

She told her father she needed help carrying up a stove.  He saw through that, but a stove was a stove, and he could not in conscience let his daughter carry one upstairs alone.  At the desk she laid out the handbook with his cracked leather bookmark in it, a strip cut long ago from the tongue of a boot.  Her mother had used it to keep his place in the adventure stories she read aloud to him after supper.  Since her death he had left it in the middle of a mutiny, the captain tied to the mast for seven years. 

"You might have let the poor bastard loose," he said. 

Lena opened the handbook to the instructions for lighting a cabin stove and laid the strip under the first line, as her mother used to do.  Then she raised the lid of their newly installed stove with all the care due the opening of a royal tomb.  "Behold," she read in the capital accent, "the removal of the accumulated ash." 

Her father bowed and took the bucket.  By the time the kindling consented to receive the match, three more pillars had come up the stairs, bringing damp wool smells and a most gratifying willingness to play courtiers.  Nobody had to read a sentence until somebody else had performed it, so nobody was guessing in the dark. 

Lena was pleased enough with this arrangement to miss the moment when it stopped working.  A young deckhand had reached the business of banking the fire and was doing a fine imitation of a man depositing his last coin.  Everybody laughed except her father, who got up and took his coat. 

"Which is it, though," he asked at the door, "put it out or keep it in?" 

Lena nearly answered him in the voice of the queen, but his hand on the latch was white.  He had made a joke of another man's trouble and had every reason to expect the favor returned.  She had meant to build a room where anyone could ask anything; she had better be willing to abandon a perfectly good performance for the sake of one question. 

"Keep it in," she said in the voice she ordered bread with. 

Her father hung up his coat.  Then he took the leather strip and moved it back two lines, to a word that had troubled him while everybody was busy being delighted.  Outside, a sheet of harbor ice tipped, shed its little cargo of stones, and rose lighter by that exact weight. 

Before the examination there were rooms like that over the bakery and behind the net loft, with bits of ribbed glass in the doors and a rule that a raised finger stopped the show.  Her father took charge of the one behind the loft, where the man with the goat agreed to come after her father apologized in two dialects and some quite serviceable fragments of a third. 

On the first night of open water, Lena found his new license propped like a little sail in the old adventure book.  "I can do the voices," he said, putting the leather strip in her hand, "but you'll have to tell me when the captain speaks." 

She drew her chair alongside his, and together they got the ropes undone.
