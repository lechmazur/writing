Once luck became measurable, nobody would insure a blind woman to live alone in a maze.  At my mother's assessment I lied and said I slept at her cottage, which bought us seven months, until the assessor came by at breakfast and found the spare bed full of onions.  She made him an omelet while he explained that without cover she would have to give up the cottage, which belonged to the town, as did the garden maze surrounding it.  The little dial on his wrist gave her about the luck of a match in a wet pocket. 

"Luck is what you need in somebody else's garden," she said, sliding the omelet whole onto his plate.  "This one knows me." 

As a child I had believed her, because in those days the garden knew me too.  It had a way of commemorating small victories so elaborately that I put off learning to whistle until the fuss over my first shoelace knot had subsided.  That knot got a triumphal arch of two young Muscat vines, with a bow of white willow at the crown and a blue marble hung in a net of silver thread.  Only when I was twelve and found her collection of cracked marbles in the potting shed did I understand who had been up all those nights.  She got them for nothing because they wouldn't roll true, and would polish each one until the fracture shone like a twig of lightning. 

The assessor finished eating and offered to time her from the cottage to the street, as he might in any building with a doubtful fire exit.  "No prompting," he told me, and to her he said, louder, "Three minutes."  She tucked her napkin into his ticking hand and went out among the grapes. 

I had seen tourists shake their heads over the withered vine dresser, all bent knuckles and a neck as corded as the wood she tended.  From behind, trying to keep up, what I noticed was the fit of that bent hand to the long low shoulder of the vine on her left.  Each winter she had cut at the height she could reach without hurting, a little lower every year, and the green wood had come down with her like a courteous partner.  At the first fork her thumb found a fat seam of grafted wood: the trumpet she had grown to celebrate my whistle.  She bore right under its flared bell, trailing a faint scent of grapes going soft in the October sun. 

But where the shoelace arch had stood, a blight had left two stumps, and she went three paces into the wrong alley before she stopped.  My mouth opened on the word back.  My mother, who had never mistaken pride for a useful implement, took her folding cane out of her apron.  She swept it back along the border until it struck the iron collar she had put around one stump to keep the mowers off its single surviving shoot.  From that clear note she took her bearings and came out into the street with eleven seconds to spare. 

"You could do that today," the assessor said, "but things die in there." 

"They do," she said, "and that is why you'll want somebody to walk it with me after a hard frost."  She did not look my way, though I would have given quite a lot just then to have her do it. 

I took his form and crossed out the nights I had never spent; underneath I promised a walk every Sunday, and after storms and frosts.  He initialed the correction against the gatepost and put her copy into the hand she had held out to receive it. 

That Sunday we took the last sound willow rods and bent them over the surviving shoot to give it somewhere to go in spring.  For the crown she chose a marble the color of onion skin, shot through with gold, and asked me what we ought to call this one. 

"First honest Sunday," I said, and she made me tie the bow myself.
