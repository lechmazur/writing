My mother got a man out of prison by telling the parole board that his wedding ring was heavy with remorse.  She never said whose remorse it was, a distinction that meant little to the man who went home to his wife.  It began to matter the following winter, when the board refused to release a girl on account of the anger in her thimble. 

Alma was seventeen and had served two years for taking a pair of shears to the foreman who took a pair of shears to her braid.  The sewing room at the prison had asked to keep her another six months, on the ground that she was coming along beautifully.  I was the clerk who typed that up, and I remember how nicely it fitted on one line of the form. 

Mother read the carbon over my shoulder while I was peeling potatoes and said she wanted the board assembled at the quartz arbor in the morning.  Once a bandstand, it was open on four sides so that no skeptic could accuse her of having somebody crouched behind a curtain.  She also wanted my car, which had spent the night outside, and the glass prism my father had ground for her when his hands were still steady.  Unlike most mystics of my acquaintance, Mother was firmly grounded in the physics of her trade.  She knew to the minute how long we had before the sun burned off the mist that kept the arbor cold. 

By eight the quartz pillars stood knee deep in milk, and the three board members had arrived with Alma and the matron who ran the sewing room.  Mother set the prism on the dashboard where it caught the beam from a headlamp and laid a bar of color across the frosted windshield.  It was through the patterns in frost on a car window that she could commune most readily with anything small enough to rest against the glass.  Nobody had ever asked how she discovered this, but I remembered the winter after my father died, and all those mornings we walked to school instead. 

At her request I had brought three more thimbles from the prison, all steel, all stamped with the same little crown.  With her back turned I drew them one by one from my pocket and fitted them over the fingers of her left hand.  She pressed four silver freckles into the strip of rainbow, and we watched the cold do its work.  Under three fingertips the frost lay still, but under the fourth it grew a forest of needles that stabbed clear through the violet band.  That was the shifted hush, as my mother called it, the instant when a silence sealed up in a thing passes into the people standing round it.  I felt the intolerable necessity of putting my fist through something, and from the set of his jaw I knew the chairman felt it too. 

"You have proved your point," he told her, reaching for the folder on which I was supposed to record our findings.  Mother wasn't watching him; she was watching the matron, who had slipped her right hand into her coat pocket. 

"It isn't mine," Alma said, and then, because nobody thought this worth answering, she said it again. 

The matron had recognized the little split in the side that let the steel ease out over a swollen joint.  At Mother's request she took her hand from her pocket and showed us the knuckle, red and big as a cherry. 

"I have used that one thirty years," she said, and the chairman, to his credit, did not ask her what a woman with a salary had to be angry about.  Alma had borrowed it the morning of her hearing because hers had rolled under a cupboard. 

He wanted to know which of the other three was Alma's, and Mother said she neither knew nor cared. 

"You told us objects absorb emotions," he said, bewildered at being deserted on what he had understood to be common ground. 

"I came here to prove they do," she said, easing the steel caps off her fingers like a woman undressing for bed.  "It's you who say they take depositions." 

On the way home she asked what I had put in the space on the form where it said Evidence of Fitness.  Alma, in the back seat in her own coat for the first time in two years, leaned forward to hear my answer.  I told her I had drawn a line through the space, which was true as far as it went.  I had used a ruler and pressed hard enough to score the next six forms, which were for people neither of us knew.
