I could set type in my father's temper so exactly that my sister heard his cough at the ends of my lines.  That was how I got away with the letter, and also, in the end, how I was caught.  He had been too weak even to scratch an X, but the MIND YOUR SHOP I sent her from my topaz workshop on the ridge had his own unmistakable rasp.  Nell stayed in the valley and sold boots, and I buried him in a pair she had made on lasts that still bore his name. 

Six months later, on the night of the double occultation, she climbed the ridge with the letter in her pocket.  She had found a mistake so characteristic of my work that no other printer in the county would have made it.  Where our father would have used a period, a little door slammed shut, I had left the line open. 

"You always like to leave yourself somewhere to go," she said. 

Above the roofless workshop the moon was edging toward the Twins, two blue points no wider apart than two stitches in a boot sole.  Their light came up through the polished topaz floor as well as down from the sky, so that we stood to our ankles in stars.  I put on the magistrate's grieved patience, which had got me through many interviews with angry customers, and asked what restitution she proposed. 

"Take that man off," Nell said, and when I tried our mother's weary smile she slapped the stone beside my hand.  "Him too, and her too, and whoever you're looking around for now." 

Out of people, I lifted the lid of the press and showed her the halo fragment bedded in its iron cheek.  It was a sliver from the ring above the head of a wooden saint whose paint had long since flaked away, leaving only an astonished carpenter.  Under a single Twin its gold could carry a feeling across the width of the press and bring another back.  For years I had tried to work out the mathematics of that exchange, weighing the type, timing the contact, asking each customer how much lighter or heavier he came away.  My ledger had columns for everything except the fact that people seldom knew which feeling had been theirs to begin with. 

"Is that what you came for?" I asked in a voice so thin and uninflected I supposed it must be mine. 

Nell nodded as the first star vanished, and I inked the words I had set ready in the chase: You spared me.  The trick was to disorient us both, to leave it deliberately unclear who had done the sparing and whether the speaker was grateful or accusing.  Once we lost our footing in the sentence the gold could make the transposition, and Nell, who knew her way around a press, took the handle herself. 

There came up through my soles a delicious slackening, like taking off a boot at the end of a market day.  For one blue instant I was delighted beyond measure that I need not go up the ridge to watch a man die.  Across the press Nell doubled over and hugged her elbows in a rapture I recognized with shame, the pleasure of having our father entirely to myself.  My own mean pleasure was still in me too; I had entered a minus in each of my columns where there should have been a plus. 

"He asked for you," I said, before the other star could go and leave me free to pretend I had said it. 

"I know," she said, "because when he wanted me to mind the shop he always came down and minded it with me." 

The second star went out, and the grief that settled back upon us was quite ordinary and did not shine.  I asked if she wanted me to burn the letter.  She smoothed it on the stone and asked me instead to sign it, not in type but with the pen I used for my accounts.  It took me two tries to get my hand moving without the familiar hitch of his, and she waited, holding the page down at the corners as if she meant to keep it.
