In the hush of morning fog, I stood in Bell's water clock repair shop and watched a woman put her thumb over a little brass mouth.  "It used to stick," she said, and the painted hand above the tank stood still. 

I had brought the clock down from the Home for Unmarried Mothers, where we were making a museum out of six white rooms and a staircase worn hollow on the right.  I had been its last aromatherapist, in the easier years when girls who came in pregnant generally went home with their babies.  After a fever took my sense of smell, I stayed on to collect the stories that began to arrive as soon as we announced the closing.  I wanted the small miracles, the sort that would otherwise go into the skip with the chipped cups. 

The woman with her thumb on the outlet had given me only her first name, Dot, and asked to meet before the streets filled up.  She had refused to come to the Home.  I knew that much of the map by now: some women could bear the stairs but not the landing, some could stand in the delivery room but not look out at the pear tree.  In my old trade a woman chose her own distance from an open bottle, and I had learned not to bring anything closer uninvited. 

Bell eased a screw out of the outlet and laid it in her free hand.  "That's from a midwife's birthing clamp," Dot said.  "See the head like a little butterfly, so she could turn it with wet fingers?" 

The screw had held a square of leather against the brass; with a quarter turn the outlet could be shut without so much as a drip.  In the surrender ledger each mother was allowed a quarter hour alone with her child before the couple waiting downstairs could come up.  Whatever the hour of arrival, the times entered on the lines marked In and Out were always fifteen minutes apart.  Looking at that bit of leather, I thought I could read at last what lay between them. 

"Which midwife did it for you?" 

Dot lifted her thumb, and into the copper basin at our feet fell a bright unbroken thread.  "I was the one who scrubbed the stairs," she said.  "It was what they offered me instead of a bill when I came downstairs without mine.  Midwife let me take the screw when the jaws on her clamp went crooked." 

She turned the clock sideways on Bell's bench until its hand was hidden from us behind a rank of cloudy measuring jars.  Through their thick bottoms I could just make out her fingers on the screw. 

"The clock stood on the landing, dial to the stairs, works to the frosted glass in the door," she said.  "From the bed you could see me as well as you see me now." 

She spread her fingers, a pink blur opening like a fan behind the jars.  "I told them when they first came in: if you want me to stop it, do that where I can see." 

"And when they were ready you let it run again?" 

Dot put the screw down carefully because her fingers had begun to shake.  "One girl held up her fist the minute they put the baby in her arms.  I went straight in and took him downstairs.  Will you put that on your little card as well?" 

I had come prepared to praise somebody for stealing time, never thinking that a minute might be something a woman needed taken off her hands.  I took the card from my pocket and turned it over to the blank side. 

"Tell me how to say it," I said. 

Bell set the little leather square back under the butterfly screw, and for a while there was only the brushing sound his sleeve made against the bench.  Beyond the shop window the fog still hung so thick you could look straight at a lighted house and not see in. 

"Say the girl upstairs had her hand on the clock," Dot said at last. 

I wrote it down exactly, and when she laid her palm across the card to steady it, I waited until she lifted it to read the words back.
