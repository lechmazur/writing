Above the clouds, where no passing cart could jar a balance, the magnetic arbor held an iron bed suspended between four living boughs.  People came up to die in it for the sake of a question that had outlived six kings: when a spirit leaves a body, what takes its weight?  Enna's mother had built the balance and, with the economy that characterized all her arrangements, had also been its first subject.  Three days after the spirit left, the pointer still stood at zero. 

Enna had not come up in time.  She had been down in the city translating cuneiform for a widow whose claim to a barley field depended on the difference between rent and tribute.  Her mother would have approved of her finishing the job, which did not make the empty bed any easier to approach. 

In the little drawer beneath it lay the knowledge fruit seed her mother had worn against her wrist for forty years.  A seed kept whatever the hand had learned, as faithfully as a cutting kept the taste of the tree.  At the university, where they wanted the design of the balance, a chair had already been endowed for the person who could make this one sprout.  Enna wanted very badly to sit in that chair.  Lately, in the dream in which she gave her inaugural lecture, she could see her mother trying to raise a hand to ask a question, and went on talking.  She also wanted to know whether her mother had reached for anybody. 

She washed the dead woman's cup, rolled her stockings one inside the other, and asked the boy who swept the instrument room if he had eaten.  He had been present at the death; everybody had asked him about the pointer and nobody had asked him anything else.  Over bread and salt he confessed that at the end he had taken her hand, although a red line on the floor marked the nearest anybody was supposed to come. 

"I thought I could just touch her without counting," he said. 

The director had found the boy there and closed the arbor to visitors until somebody could reestablish the conditions of the trial.  Downstairs in the guesthouse a man with a wasting sickness was waiting to go up, and his wife was waiting to be sent home. 

Enna turned the seed between her finger and thumb.  To sprout it she must give it something to do; the first task set the grain of the wood forever.  She could give it wire and it would grow into the intricate curls and stays of another balance.  Instead she laid it in the boy's open palm.  A green shoot crooked itself about his smallest finger, loose enough that he could pull away, firm enough that he knew he need not. 

"Yes," he said in surprise, "that's how we were." 

There were barley accounts in which the scribe had weighed the carter with his laden cart, then weighed him again driving the empty cart home.  It did not matter whether he sat quite still or stooped to kiss his mule, provided he stayed on the scales.  All her life she had thought of the bed as a pan to be kept clear of everything that was not dying.  Now she saw how wide a pan might be. 

She asked the boy for the cutting, and together they trained its tender wood into a bracket that would hold a second bed fast against the first.  Joined like two fingers, the beds swung free of the floor; whatever passed between their occupants would be an affair internal to the balance.  Whatever passed out of the pair of them would register against the steady pull of the boughs. 

At dusk the sick man and his wife came up through the clouds with their little bag of night things.  Enna showed them where to lie and how to call if they wanted water or the screen drawn.  She did not tell them that the polished clasp between their pillows had once been meant to buy her a chair. 

Long after midnight the pointer dipped one division, held there for the space of a breath, and came quietly back.  Enna wrote down the time, the temperature, the deflection, and the fact that the two hands had not come apart.  Then she put her pencil down and stepped across the line.
