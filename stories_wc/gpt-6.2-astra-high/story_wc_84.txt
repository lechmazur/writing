After the pilgrim arrived, Kit unlocked the ancient observatory and let the last of the dusk in with him.  He had come barefoot, as promised, but carried his shoes full of glass.  Through the tall west window the splinters threw little blue lozenges on the stone, so that he seemed to be emptying his shoes of light. 

Kit had advertised for the missing pieces of her mother's sky for eleven years; this was the first time anybody had brought one back.  She was a sharp-eyed drone pilot who could pick out a drowned sheep in acres of whitecaps, but she had stopped looking at the road. 

"All from one house?" she asked. 

"Six houses, three countries," he said, spreading the glass on the light table where her mother used to cut negatives.  "They want them back by winter." 

That was news to Kit, who had spent half her savings on a frame big enough to hold the sky.  Her mother had exposed the enormous plate on the last night before the valley was flooded, then cut it up for the departing families.  Kit had got the observatory, high and dry on its knob of basalt, and the conviction that staying counted for rather more than leaving.  Every autumn she kept the lamp burning for the people who climbed back up; every autumn she caught herself counting them, trying to decode the frequency of belonging.  How seldom could you come home and still call it home? 

The pilgrim had the perpetually surprised look of somebody whose spectacles magnify his eyes, an impression undone by his hands, which knew exactly what they wanted.  From a seam in his shoe he picked a type foundry matrix chip, a winking bit of brass with a pointing finger sunk into it. 

"My wife cut this," he said.  "Your mother traded her a square of sky for enough little lead hands to point the way here at every fork from the coast." 

Kit remembered those hands on the pilgrimage bills, the tiny nail on each forefinger black as if bruised.  The old man had brought the matrix because the last road in had washed away; would Kit print new bills if he cast fresh type?  Otherwise next year he did not think he could make it, let alone bring anybody with him. 

She switched on the table.  By following star trails across the cuts, turning each shard until the fine silver scratches continued their sweep about the pole, she fitted five pieces together.  The sixth belonged down near the horizon, an island of emulsion scarcely bigger than her thumbnail.  Under her flying loupe it showed the opposite ridge and a light in the upstairs window of a house. 

"My wife's," the pilgrim said.  "She was up packing the foundry that night." 

Kit had imagined her mother cutting that plate with shut eyes, out of fairness or grief; now she saw how neatly the cut spared the house.  She thought of the old man asking at six doors for the loan of something that could go to powder under one careless heel.  Not one of those people had asked him to bring them the whole sky. 

She took the backing board out of her expensive frame and laid sensitized paper against the assembled glass, weighting it flat with the pilgrim's empty shoes.  One brief exposure was enough.  In the developing dish the sky came up in patches, dark where there had been no glass, deeper dark where the ridge shouldered its way among the stars.  The lit window emerged last. 

"We'll put this on the front," Kit said.  "On the back we'll print the road." 

She could fly the coast route tomorrow and plot the firm ground, the places to drink and the places where an old man might sit down.  Tonight there was just enough light left to wrap each piece in its own square of the velvet she had bought to line the frame.  She gave him those first, before she hung the wet print up, before she could begin to wish for anything back.
