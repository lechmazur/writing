At the top of the year's highest tide, when gravity inverts for an hour, the people of Wick go indoors and lie down on their ceilings.  That year the ceiling of the infants' school came off in one piece, and the children went up into the fog with their little beds still bolted to it.  The catch net across the ravine held, but nobody had opened its winding house since the year Nera was born.  From her kitchen she could hear springs squeaking as somebody swung a leg, and a teacher saying in her ordinary voice that now was not the time. 

Nera got her mother out of the kitchen by promising her she would not ask for any numbers.  Since the stroke, numbers distressed the old woman; she could tell you who had worn a hole in a step but not how many steps there were.  A prism-minded archivist, Nera had spent the winter trying to find where forgotten combinations hide, breaking every account of the old net crew's drills into color, sound, and movement.  Her only discovery had been a manuscript margin doodle, a hen stepping over a sill with one foot raised absurdly high.  Her mother had drawn that hen on a thousand lunch bags, always in the same attitude of offended dignity. 

They went by the covered way, walking on the undersides of its slates, until they reached the serpentine grotto in mist so dense that Nera had to feel for the bell rope.  She rang to let the teacher know somebody had come; three knocks traveled back along the taut mesh.  On the stone overhead, where workmen used to walk in ordinary weather, a trail of clandestinely luminous specks led to the winding house.  Nera knew the green grit in the paint; it drank daylight all winter for the one bad hour when a lantern dropped from your hand would smash against the sky.  Her mother called such small provisions the seeds of vigilance, and had once painted all the lunch bag hens with shining toes. 

Inside the house the two brass brake wheels stood overhead, just beyond her mother's reach.  Nera made a stirrup of her hands, but the old woman kicked free and went outside again.  From the net came the creak of separate boards beginning to pull apart.  Then her mother stretched up, set both palms against the worn stone overhead, and tried to walk on her hands. 

Nera understood the hen at last.  At home her mother sometimes recovered a lost intention through threshold effects: she would reenter the pantry the way she had first entered, and reach without hesitation for the flour.  Everything here was as it had been at practice except the soles of her feet were on the wrong stone.  Nera gathered her up like a bride, then tipped her slowly backward until her shoes were planted on the shining prints.  With a grunt her mother raised one knee to clear the sill, exactly like that ridiculous bird.  She stepped across on Nera's strength, and her left hand went out as naturally as if there were a banister to catch it.  High, low, high went the brass notes of the first wheel; the second answered so quickly that the sound was almost a chord.  Something in the rock began to tick, gathering weight and speed, and out in the fog a little boy laughed himself sick as his bed started rolling toward shore. 

They landed the roof with six minutes left, enough to get the children into the covered way and pass the mattresses along to cushion everybody's second fall.  Later the council asked Nera for the numbers, to be copied into the register in a good clear hand.  She had them by then, from the settings on the wheels, and she entered them in ink that shone.  But the first thing anybody saw on opening the book was the hen her mother drew there, one imperious foot lifted, with an invitation in Nera's hand underneath.  It said to bring your lunch on Saturdays, when the keeper would be glad to show you how.
