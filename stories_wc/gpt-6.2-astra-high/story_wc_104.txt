When the wall came down, Mara was waiting in her leg irons to be photographed walking through it. The governor wanted the woman who had opened the prison gates, then served eleven years for doing it, to be the first citizen across. Mara had asked only that the photograph be taken from the east, so her mother could see her face in the morning paper. This put forty newspapermen squarely in the path of the machines waiting to flatten the old fairground.

Through the first breach came an arrow with strange fletching: three folded paper birds, their wings stiff with paste. It landed at her feet, to the alarm of everybody except Mara, who remembered how poorly those things flew. Once, for a penny, you could shoot one across the carnival pond and win whatever the birds had been folded from. Mostly you got laundry coupons. Mara bent as far as her chain allowed and unfolded a bird to find a music lesson in her mother's handwriting. Five lines, a treble clef, and not a note on it.

She turned away from the breach and made for the abandoned carnival prize redemption center. The photographers went backward before her, taking pictures of her extraordinary mistake, and the foreman killed his engine rather than pulp a journalist. She had calculated on being able to impede the work for perhaps ten minutes; it was a mercy she had learned in prison to do most things slowly without wasting time.

The piano still stood behind the prize counter, where her mother had played during the long negotiations of children with insufficient tickets. Mara raised the bench lid. On its bottom someone had ruled a musical staff, just like the one the bird had carried, and into the wood had pressed the teeth of twenty brass keys. Their round heads were the missing notes. Mara hummed them under her breath and heard the tune her mother had composed to teach her pupils the names of the valley villages, going upriver. One key for each village, each in its place in the song.

Under the counter were twenty steel lockers in which the expensive prizes had been kept. She took the key for Bell Ford, where nothing green had grown since the authorities diverted the river, and opened the first locker it would turn. A little cold breath touched her wrist. Inside stood brown glass jars, each sealed with wax and labeled with the name of a grower and the year of the last good crop. All those years her mother had complained about the electric bill, and Mara had advised her by letter to unplug the piano lamp.

By now the governor had reached the counter. Behind him, through the breach, Mara could see her mother sitting in a folding chair beside a boy with a bow. The old woman lifted one hand, palm down, the sign she used to give a pupil rushing a difficult passage.

Mara had meant to carry the jars outside and stand for her picture with the evidence of what had been saved. Instead she put the Bell Ford key on the counter and called the name of the village. A man in a road crew vest came forward, looked at the jars, and took off his hard hat to carry them in. For each village she called there was somebody, and for the one village now entirely under sand there was a girl whose grandmother had lived there. No one asked the governor to declare the vault open, and presently he went outside.

At the last locker Mara's knee gave way. The girl from the buried village caught her under the arm, and together they went out in a kind of lopsided dance, the chain allowing one full step and one short one. Her mother was rubbing a pinch of earth between finger and thumb, showing the boy how deep the dry crust went and where, beneath it, a bean might put down a root. Mara sat beside them in the gap where the wall had been. By the time a locksmith came to take the irons off, she had both hands in the dirt.
