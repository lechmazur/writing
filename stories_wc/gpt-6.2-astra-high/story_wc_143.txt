When the space age reached our mountain in 1964, it took the form of a little box that could report a tremor by radio and did not have to be fed.  My mother made the mistake of admiring it in front of the man who had come to dismiss her.  She even asked him to open the back.  That was how the women of the nightwatch had trained me to be curious: always have a particular question, and never let your opinion of the man with the screwdriver keep you from asking it. 

I was the crater inspector by then, the first person from our village to hold the post, and my signature was required at the bottom of the transfer sheet.  Before I signed I wanted a photograph of the last of the human watches, for the long shelf in the village school where there were already six photographs of me.  Not one child who passed that shelf would have guessed who taught me to read a mountain. 

I carried my plate camera up to the moonlit summit on the last night of March.  The four women were at the stone table, three with their chins in their collars and my mother with her bare cheek laid against the stone.  Down in the throat of the crater a lake of hot mud was turning over in its sleep.  Mother raised a finger and the others reached for the hourglass.  Every half minute that the table hummed, somebody turned the glass and somebody made a mark.  They could do it in the dark by listening for the little hiss to stop. 

Inside the nightwatch's hourglass one red grain of sand always clung to the upper bulb a moment after the rest had fallen.  As a girl I had waited for that grain as other children wait for a familiar face at a train window.  I set up facing east, so the first light would show through the glass, and asked the women to stand on either side of it.  Nobody moved. 

"Are we supposed to stop work," my mother asked, "so you can show them what we do?" 

I'd brought a flashbulb, but I put it away; it would leave them staring at a blue hole where the lake should be.  In my bag were strips of the silver paper we used to time exposures in the field, each under a scrap of negative bearing two words in different densities.  Daylight worked a slow chemical change in the silver salt; first WAIT and then CLOSE appeared, messages timed by the very light that was making the picture.  An hourglass could tell me how much time had fallen through it, but not how much light. 

I pinned a strip beside the lens and pointed the camera down at the table instead.  I would have to outlast the moon, then catch the few minutes before sunrise when there was light enough for faces and not enough wind to roughen the lake.  Mother shifted along the bench and made room for me where I had sat as a girl. 

"You know we asked for the box," she said. 

I hadn't known. 

"Your aunt has slept sitting up for thirty years, and now she would like to lie down." 

Around us the dawning hush gathered, that interval when the night wind stopped coming down the mountain and the day wind had not yet begun to climb.  In the quiet I heard a small, dry noise and realized it was my aunt rubbing the painful hinge of her knee through her skirt.  I uncovered the lens and the strip together.  WAIT came up pale as a scar.  Under the table our boots touched, and above it four pairs of hands passed the glass, the pencil, the book, with the unthinking grace of people eating supper together.  When CLOSE appeared I capped the lens and signed the sheet on my mother's back. 

In the photograph their faces are blurred, and you cannot pick out the red grain, though children at the school still try when I tell them it is there.  What came out sharp was the edge of the stone table, worn into four shallow cups by the patient pressure of their elbows.  My aunt slept through the ceremony at the school, on her side, with both hands open on the quilt.
