The living came up to the grotto for their weddings, and the dead came up to have their hair done.  My brass plate on the landing said BEAUTICIAN MORTICIAN, and there was just the one chair.  It hung on gimbals, so that when our shelf of pumice tilted in the high wind nobody slid out of my hands.  Through the open side of the cave the aurora let down its long green fringes, close enough sometimes to infuse a basin of rinse water with light.  It was that light people paid for when they brought me their dead. 

Dot came with her mother and an emerald chip so small it had worked its way into the crease of her palm.  At school I had sat behind Dot and watched the two black plaits lying like bell ropes down her back.  Now her hair was clipped as close as mine, and her mother had died with her fingers crooked from the work that had bought Dot her passage down to earth.  Those fingers had put the cane seats in every chair in our school, including the one in which Dot sat to take her scholarship exam. 

I thought she wanted what everybody wanted, the smile that a face will give up at the threshold of stillness if you know how to ask.  Set the stone in the lit water, wet your thumbs, and touch the corners of the mouth.  The last little current goes through your hands into the stone, and whoever holds it afterward feels that smile steal over their own face. 

Dot took one look at my thumbs and shook her head.  "She had to smile every time somebody asked if she couldn't do a seat for less," she said.  "I didn't haul her all the way up here for that." 

Under the thin lids her mother's eyes made the aimless little jumps that meant we hadn't long.  I put the chip in the basin anyway.  I wanted to capture one last spark before oblivion, and if a smile was all I knew how to take, at least nobody could say I had sent Dot home empty.  She did not try to stop me but stood lucidly watchful, as she had in school when a teacher did a sum wrong on the board.  I felt the old heat come into my cheeks. 

"Show me where to put my hands, then," I said. 

Dot knelt by the chair and laid her cheek against her mother's open palm.  "There used to be a little pull here," she said, touching the skin just above her ear.  "It used to drive me mad." 

I got my comb and parted Dot's stubble with the tail, and for the first time since she stepped off the landing she shut her eyes.  A ticking came from somewhere close to my knees, a little dry shower, as if somebody were shaking out a handful of pins.  What they had taught me about finding patterns in static was to listen for a beat, but no beat came.  Then I saw the crooked fingers coming together in threes, and understood that what I took for noise was each nail striking the wooden chair arm at a slightly different pitch.  Not a beat but a tune, the outside strands passing over the middle one and changing places, over and over again. 

I took the chip between my wet thumb and the pad of the old woman's forefinger, and Dot bent her shorn head to meet them.  The green in the water gathered to a point so fine that for an instant I could see the whorl of a fingerprint written in light on Dot's temple.  Then the water was only water, and the weight in the chair was all mine. 

I wrapped the chip in a square of chamois and started to tell Dot there was no charge if it hadn't taken.  She had already slipped it out of the wrapping.  Her head tipped sideways with the little cross jerk of a girl pulled away from her book, and she put up one hand to fend off a hand that wasn't there.  Then she sat quite still and let her mother finish.
