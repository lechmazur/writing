On the four millionth evening of his employment, Ivo closed the lost property ledger on his thumb just to see if it still hurt.  It did, and this too was getting rather dull.  The prismatic escondite, a refuge cut into rock salt, admitted fugitives through any door on which the sun fell at the proper slant.  They stayed until it was safe to go home, or until the children they had brought in grew old enough to want a different danger.  Ivo saw them off with a bag of salt for luck and the word that shut the door.  He had taken the job to discover what happens after goodbye, an astonishing error of judgment for an immortal to have made. 

Then came the solar storm, and the knocking from inside the lost property cupboard.  Ivo opened it expecting moths and saw, through a fringe of little winter coats, a kitchen he had last seen nine centuries ago.  He knew the blue tiles because he had laid them for a woman called Paz, who liked the sea but had never seen it.  When she went out, the kitchen had been folded into the walls to make room for another family.  Now its tap ran in the deliberate rhythm of a finger spelling on a palm.  Where did she go? 

The rooms were dimensionally aware; it was how they fitted so many lives into an abandoned mine without ever putting strangers in the same bed.  He had not known they remembered anybody.  While he stood listening, every needle in the sewing room swung north, and the kitchen tilted a little farther out of storage.  The rooms spoke to one another through magnetic fields; tonight the sun had given them voices strong enough to reach across the seams.  Somewhere underfoot came a sound like thunder heard through a quilt.  One by one the empty rooms were refusing to stay put. 

At his throat Ivo felt the cursed amulet fragment turn cold, warning him to put down this spatial mutiny before an outer wall gave way.  Its missing half had once hung round the neck of a second keeper; between them they could conduct a household safely in either direction.  Alone, his half knew only how to close things, and every door it closed stayed closed to him.  He could still seal the cupboard and all these importunate rooms with it.  Instead he did what he had loved doing as a boy, before the centuries wore him smooth: he began a little intrigue. 

From the sewing table he took two needles and asked the kitchen to teach him the alphabet of their shivering.  Then he went among the occupied rooms, quietly winning them over to a plan the stone foundations must not suspect.  At the first crack in the stone the fragment would make his mouth say the word whether he willed it or not.  Would the nursery mind becoming, for an instant, the thickness of a nursery door?  Would the chapel consent to lie flat, with all its candles burning sideways?  By dawn he had persuaded the whole inhabited refuge to step aside without shifting a single sleeper on her pillow.  Between its two halves lay a bright seam just wide enough for the uninhabited rooms to pass through in single file.  Beyond it he smelled low tide. 

The kitchen hung back, tapping out the same question, and he had to tell it that Paz would have been dead for nine hundred years.  Still the needles trembled in his hand.  Not when, they spelled, but where.  The room had kept the slant of Paz's last light intact, as faithfully as it had kept his tiles.  He understood then and walked with it as far as his curse allowed, to the lip of a shore where the sun was coming up.  There were blue tiles set into the harbor wall, not his but cut to the same crooked pattern, and more set into the steps above the beach.  A potter was opening her shutters on a shop hung with little squares the color the sea had been in somebody's imagination.  The kitchen settled against the shop with the soft decisive sound of a cup returning to its saucer. 

Ivo put the needles away, took hold of the cold fragment, and said goodbye.  This time he kept his eyes open until the seam was shut, and saw the potter put a hand on the warm tap in wonder.  Afterward he walked the waking corridors, asking people what they meant to take home and what they hoped to leave behind.  One boy wanted to take the echo under the stairs, so they went to ask it.
