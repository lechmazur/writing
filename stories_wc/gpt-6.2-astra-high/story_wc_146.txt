The week before the collapse, the people of Lint carried their town across the ridge, everything except the streets.  They took doors off hinges, pears off trees, and the mill bell out of the tower that was already leaning like a man listening at a wall.  On the safe side of the ridge stood an ancient archway, the last stone left of the first Lint, which had stood there before anybody thought to put a mill beside the river.  It was through this arch that my grandmother proposed to bring the streets. 

She was a bell tuner, infinitely patient with metal and rather less so with people who wanted things explained.  "You can carry a door," she said, "but what are you going to open it onto?" 

All around us lay the wild hush of a place where foxes had been having things their own way for six hundred years.  One sat quite openly where we had hung the bell from a pine.  Beyond its red ears I could see the river town, a ghost town now, with the white scars of missing doors showing clear across the valley. 

I had been the girl paid to ring everybody in to work, so my grandmother gave me the ebony tuning fork to hold against the bell while she walked beneath the arch.  Ebony gives a little knock of sound that dies almost at once in the air but goes on traveling through the bones of your hand.  I felt, through the fork, the rhythm of footsteps on cobblestones, though her boots were coming down on grass.  Where she had passed there lay three yards of shiny black setts, including the one with a chisel nick that used to catch my stocking on my way to work. 

"It remembers us," I said, and without waiting to be asked I rang the morning shift. 

The fox vanished and two hundred people did what they had been trained from childhood to do at that sound.  By supper we had a handsome street from the arch to the door of a mill that no longer existed, and not so much as a turning off it.  We set our doors along its edges, but they opened on the backs of other doors. 

"You have given us a lovely place to be late," said my grandmother. 

It was the mill owner's voice I heard coming out of my mouth when I told her how little would get done if everybody went where they pleased.  For three years I had watched my father running up that street with his bad knee, and never once had I given him an extra minute.  I took the rope down.  Then I asked my father where he would go if nobody rang. 

He took a while to think, and in that while we heard a vixen barking for her cubs. 

"Your aunt would have her kettle on about now," he said. 

His uneven tread went tapping through the arch, and a narrow lane uncurled from the mill street, with a little step in it where my aunt always sat to shell peas in the cool of the evening.  She brought her blue door over and it fitted.  After that we worked in pairs, somebody going somewhere and somebody making it worth going there. 

By moonrise only the alley behind the dye works remained to rebuild, where my mother used to meet me with a hot egg to roll between my hands on winter mornings.  I stood at the entrance that wasn't there until my father warmed a pebble in the fire and went round to meet me at the other end.  The cobbles came up crooked, laid to the stride of a girl going out and a lame man coming back. 

Before dawn the cliff let go, and we felt the fall of the other Lint through the soles of our feet.  Not one door jumped its hinges.  My grandmother spent the rest of the morning tuning the harshness out of the bell, stopping now and then to ask somebody across the street how it sounded.  We hung it within reach of everybody, and now it calls us to a birth, a fire, a supper made for six in a house where only two sit down.
