Flora Pike had put snapdragons on a miser's grave and taught half the town's children how to make them ask for money.  It was generally agreed that the cemetery suited her better than she suited the cemetery.  Only Nell, her sister, knew what it cost Flora to keep up the spirits of people who went home afterward to houses with mothers in them. 

On the eve of the foundlings' supper Nell came up the path swinging their single inheritance, an antique sovereign case on a much mended chain.  "I've been polishing it for forty years," she said, "and that's forty years too long to be polishing a question." 

Downhill stood the cobalt silo under stars, its blue enamel all that survived of the railway company's splendid idea of sending grain out and bringing prosperity back.  After the trains stopped, the parish had cut a cradle hatch in its side, with a bell a woman could ring before she walked away.  The girls had come through together with the case between them, sufficient identification if anybody ever came to claim them, not enough to let them claim anybody. 

Inside, voices adhered to the curved walls as stubbornly as the smell of rye.  The parish had once employed a listener to recover messages for children old enough to ask, but lately that office too had fallen to the gardener.  Flora had kept the place as tenderly organized as a drawer of baby clothes.  On the listening table lay the parish tapestry, pieced from scraps of cradle linen; beside it stood the chess clock that gave each petitioner five minutes to speak and the walls five minutes to answer. 

"What I want," said Nell, "is to discover the shape of intentions, not to hear a poor woman say she was sorry." 

"If she said it at all," Flora answered, and pressed the brass button. 

Nell set the case against the hatch and snapped its little sprung lid shut, the only syllable of their mother tongue that either sister knew.  She let the rest of her five minutes pass in silence, then pushed the button that started the other hand.  From the velveteen gloom above them came a click, the fretful bleat of one infant waking another, and the long bronze shudder of the bell.  Not a word followed, though Flora watched her sister's mouth move as if supplying some deficiency in the acoustics. 

The chess clock ran down.  Flora should have entered a dash in the register, but in the sudden quiet she heard something traveling round the silo at the height of her knees.  In all her years of listening she had never stayed after time to hear where the uncharted echoes went.  She took up the tapestry, meaning to untangle their mother's footsteps from the patter of feet that had carried other babies to the hatch.  The listener before her had sewn a plan into its hem, and by decoding frayed threads in the tapestry's border, counting a rib for each upright and a knot for each bolt, she found where to put her ear. 

Nell crouched beside her at the old inspection grille directly opposite the cradle hatch.  There was their mother's breathing, quick from running the half circle that separated the place for giving a child up from the place for watching what became of it.  They heard the matron lift one baby, then the other, and carry them away down the gravel walk.  Still the breathing went on.  It broke at last into the unmistakable whistle of somebody sleeping with a stopped nose, a sound so like Nell on a winter morning that Flora put her fist in her mouth.  Presently a cock tried out a cracked note and half a dozen others put him right. 

"She stayed the night," Nell said. 

It settled nothing about the forty years to follow, but they had both imagined that walk away from the hatch so often that it made a difference to be able to stop walking.  Flora took the clock off the listening table and tucked it under her arm for the children to play with at supper.  With the empty sovereign case lying open between them, the sisters sat hip to hip against the grille, two women getting on in years keeping a young one company until morning.
