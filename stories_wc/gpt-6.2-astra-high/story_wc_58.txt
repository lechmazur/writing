After the last ship sailed for the winter, I went up through the shadowed garden to ask the woman who had impersonated my father how she wanted to be remembered.  I had spent eleven years traveling the coast writing obituaries for the living, who could generally be trusted to improve on anything their children might say about them.  I liked the corrections best: not the trawler but the little blue pilot boat, not on his knees when he proposed but sitting down because his shoe pinched.  It was an occupation that allowed me to go almost anywhere except home.  Now the hospital was coming down, and before the wreckers reached the old palace theater somebody had to settle what had happened there. 

Along the path quinces lay split like yellow fists opening to show a little brown money.  From a bench under the lindens Ida watched the steamer smoke come unstitched above the sea.  Beside her stood the lamp the nurses used to wheel behind the silk curtain at the end of the fever ward.  A remnant of the curtain hung from a line between two trees, and an overturned flowerpot held its corner against the wind. 

In the old days families had gathered here at dusk to see their people cast, enormously and briefly, upon the silk.  We recognized a sweetheart by the angle at which he wore his cap, a grandmother by her way of stabbing a coil of hair into submission.  There were explained absences, too, a nurse coming out to say the occupant of this or that dark square was having a bath.  My father appeared three evenings running after the date eventually chiseled on his stone.  Ida had written to tell me whose hand it was that I had seen, and I had let her letter travel in my coat until the folds grew furry. 

"I can show you," she said, opening a matchbox on her knee.  Inside lay a length of crimson wool, a palace carpet weft thread she had teased loose from the underside of the stage runner.  She lit the lamp.  She put a loop around her little finger and drew it tight with her thumb; on the silk the lamp threw the crooked little finger my father had always straightened against his leg for photographs. 

"He showed me how to do it when he knew he was going," she said.  "He wanted your mother to get aboard the hospital tender for her operation, and she wouldn't leave while he was sinking." 

I had always thought the cruelty lay in those three extra nights of hope, but what came back to me was the fourth evening, when I had stood by myself below a square of dirty silk.  My mother was across the strait by then, my father was in the ground, and nobody had thought to explain my absence from school or his from the window. 

"What did he do when he'd finished teaching you?" I asked. 

"Tried to get the knot undone with his teeth, because I was turning purple." 

She held out her hand and I raised my smallest finger level with hers.  Mine bent that way of its own accord, a thing I had noticed in shaving mirrors all over the coast without once letting myself consider what it meant.  On the curtain two hands hung side by side, one contrived and one inherited.  I loosened the knot on Ida's finger, then rubbed the deep white groove until the blood came back; it was the nearest I had come to touching him in thirty years. 

I had traveled, I suppose, to understand the geography of healing, expecting to discover some distance at which an island shrank to a bearable size.  Yet three steps to the left of the place where the child had stood I could see both the hand and what it cost to hold it crooked. 

"Don't put down that I did it for him," Ida said as I got out my pencil.  "Put down that he asked me, and I did it, and I never came outside to see who was still waiting." 

Under the lindens I took down her words exactly, leaving room at the bottom of the page for whatever she might yet do.  When the first cold drops fell she carried the lamp inside and came back out for me.
