Before the rainforest burned, Ada made her living as an echo-harbor violinist, playing duets with the iron sides of stranded ships.  People paid her for knowing just where to stop, so that a note came back over the water looking for its mate.  She liked a tune best in that condition.  Then the trees went up, and the people who got out came down to the harbor, and among them was her father without her little sister. 

They lived that winter in the sunken orchard greenhouse ringed by shipwrecks, where pears still ripened six feet below the level of the tide.  The chronic hush that settled there was not quite silence.  Ada had always been acoustically perceptive; she could hear a woman four rows away ease a cup into its saucer rather than risk the small report of china.  Everybody knew what her father had last seen of Nell, because everybody but Ada had had the decency to ask him only once.  A red skirt going over a fence, and which way she went after that he could not say.  By spring he had begun to say he thought she turned inland.  Ada knew this was a gift intended for her, and she wanted it badly enough to let him keep on giving it. 

When the packet came to take the survivors to their various ports, they had one last night among the pears.  Her father asked if she would play something for Nell, something with an end to it, so that people would know when to stand up.  He had borrowed a black ribbon and wound it round the neck of her fiddle.  She put her thumb under the ribbon to loosen it, then saw how carefully he had made the little bow and left it alone. 

Instead she went looking for the mendicant's bowl.  Her grandfather had begged his way along this coast with it, and when he had a house at last, he still went begging once a year.  At the family bonfire he would hold out the bowl for a scrap of story, then carry it to somebody else to beg for the next bit.  A mouth too shy to speak could send him on his way with a tap.  They had always done it on the longest night, but nobody objected to a fire in April. 

They built it outside on the shingle with wood from a wreck so old the nails had worn away to threads.  Ada held out the bowl to her father and asked him for something Nell had done before the day of the fence.  He looked down into it as though the habit of a whole winter might be written there and need reading out.  Then he said she had once spent a morning trying to put a pear back on a tree.  Ada remembered only the scolding for picking it green. 

The woman who had lent the ribbon leaned across the fire to ask for the bowl.  She had supplied the child with needle and thread, she said, on the understanding that a button had come off somewhere.  A boy remembered the green pear swinging all summer by its stalk of blue cotton, getting smaller and blacker but never coming down.  From hand to hand went that ridiculous pear until her father began to laugh, and the laugh was so like Nell's that Ada turned her head. 

At last the bowl came back to him.  She saw him getting ready to pay what he thought he owed her, the direction and perhaps by now the name of a road.  Before he could speak she tapped the rim for him with the tip of her bow.  Across the water a hull gave back the little ringing sound.  Her father listened until it died and handed her the bowl.  She let him unbind the ribbon while she held the fiddle steady, as she had once held still for him to undo a knot in her hair.  Nobody got up; there was still good wood in the fire.
