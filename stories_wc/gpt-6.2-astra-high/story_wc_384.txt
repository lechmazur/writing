When the stalactites began to drip, the families of the drowned came up to the marble sanctuary to hear the voices the ice had kept for them.  Under the aurora their faces had the look of spoons in a drawer, each holding a little of the same green light.  Mara set out thirty chairs, although the governor had authorized six.  The other twenty-four were for the families of deserters, who were supposed to wait outside. 

She had been the governor's calligraphy forger long enough to know exactly how much disobedience he would overlook in a heated room.  His own phrase for her profession was historical repair.  He sent her proclamations that had been given too late, and she made fair copies in the hands of dead commanders, with dates that suited the outcome.  She spent her fees on the rent of a greenhouse where her mother's one surviving rose grew against the boiler.  It was not a pretty rose, but it had come north with the first miners, and its thorns had a use that the double pinks from the capital did not. 

From her pocket she took a heritage rose thorn, cured brown and cut obliquely, and fitted it to the reproducer.  A steel point would play the soft wax once; a thorn might let a child hear his father twice.  Last of all she took out what her mother had left her, a key with no apparent lock, square at the business end and polished by a thumb.  It went over the spindle of the mine recorder, and the spring began to wind. 

All winter divers had been bringing up cylinders from the watch office where her mother had stayed, calling the roll as the galleries filled.  Of thirty people lost, only the six found at their stations had been allowed death pensions.  The rest had been following the children of the picking floor up a stair which, by one landing, fell short of daylight.  The children had got out through a ventilator, lifted from below. 

Mara had the last cylinder on the felt when the governor came in and counted the chairs.  He did not ask her to put them away.  Instead he laid on her desk a penciled transcript in which her mother ordered everyone to remain at work.  He wanted the woman of the old recruiting posters, wisely enduring everything from widowhood to a cave-in, still good for a joke at the whistle.  All Mara had to do was supply the handwriting. 

She lowered the thorn.  Out of the brass throat came the knock of rising water lifting the wooden floor, a sound that made several people lift their feet.  Then her mother said, in the exasperated voice Mara knew from having once nearly drowned herself, "For pity's sake, pass them up."  Somebody asked if that was an order.  Her mother got as far as the first broad vowel of her answer before the spring in the watch office ran down. 

Mara used to dream about turning that key from miles away, giving her mother another minute in which to say something meant for her alone.  Even now her fingers closed on it.  Across the aisle a boy was rubbing the tops of his arms where somebody had gripped him hard enough to leave white crescents.  It struck her that there had been something better for her mother to do with that minute.  To forgive the unfinished was not to finish it for her. 

Mara took the brass clip off the two sheets on her desk, to unlink the public transcript from the certificate on which the treasury actually paid.  She erased his pencil and transcribed what they had all heard, down to the open vowel.  On the second, in the governor's narrow hand, she altered one figure: thirty persons lost on duty instead of six.  It was calibrated treason, just what he had taught her, the smallest movement of ink that would oblige the world to move with it.  She passed him the certificate and offered him the pen to strike it out, before the people in the thirty chairs.  He looked at the wet nib, then tucked the sheet inside his coat to carry down to the treasury. 

Outside it was pearl twilight, and water was running freely off the sanctuary steps.  The boy asked if the voice would keep.  Mara showed him how to change the thorn, and let him turn the key.
