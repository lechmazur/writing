Long after Ada closed the nursery for the night, somebody rang the little brass bell she had taken off the counter and buried with her husband.  She saw the sound before she quite heard it: three blue hoops rolling down the gravel, the last one wobbly because Kit had always let his finger rest too long on the plunger.  She followed them into the yew maze, past the turn where customers invariably got cross and the turn where they generally forgave themselves, to a gap no customer had ever found. 

Within lay the pear garden, its trees trained into tiers of boxes around a stage made from the stump of the orchard's oldest tree.  The dead comedian on the stump was bowing to the moths, who had never yet obliged him with an encore.  At the sight of Ada he straightened and fished something out of his mouth, his one really disgusting trick and consequently Kit's favorite.  It was their hand-painted tarot coin, the Tower on one face and the Star on the other, chipped on the rim where a small boy had once tested its purchasing power in the gum machine.  In the old act whoever drew the Tower had to invent a disaster, and whoever drew the Star had to get them out of it.  She and Kit had been quite good until presented with an actual river coming in through the windows of their car. 

The comedian rolled the coin across his knuckles and dropped it, not flat but edgewise, into the crack that had run through the stump since the night Kit drowned.  Two gardens shivered into focus like pictures in a badly printed book; in one the pears were still in blossom, in the other Kit sat under branches bowed with fruit.  He was turning a brass bell in his hands, and his hair was gray in places she had never seen it gray.  Beside him stood a young pear espaliered into the shape of her initial, with her wedding ring tied loosely to a twig by a length of nursery tape.  So that was who had come up for air on the other side of that long black minute when each had shoved the other toward the broken window. 

She called his name until her throat hurt, but only when the branches tossed it back in a green tangle did he lift his head.  The comedian cupped a hand to one transparent ear and waited, the oldest invitation in the business.  If she could get their voices to intertwine the way their fingers once had, perhaps more than sound could pass.  She climbed onto the stump and sounded the Tower's familiar alarm, slapping her thighs to produce the exact noise of a duchess falling downstairs.  From somewhere impossibly far away came the plump answering thud of the footman who had broken Her Grace's fall.  By producing echoes off the pear boughs, watching their colors and leaving just enough room between them for Kit's reply, she got a whole household down those stairs.  She could smell again the hot dust of the boards they had played as newlyweds, see the white crescents his cheap paste mustache left on his lip when he laughed it loose. 

The last to come down was always the baby, and neither of them had ever settled what would catch it.  This time the comedian held out his hat, and into it fell a small fat pear from Kit's autumn.  Kit reached for Ada and she for him, but where their hands should have met there was only the worn rim of a hat and the sweet weight it would just bear.  For years the only thing she had wanted was to go back into that water and change places; it had never occurred to her what a poor present that would make him. 

She took a bite of the pear and held it up to show him, as she used to do when he worked the box office and they ate supper on opposite sides of the window.  Kit bent his head over the hat and took the next bite, and so they went on until there was nothing between them but a core full of pips.  The gardens slipped apart to the sound of two people calling for an encore, and the old ham bowed so low his forehead touched the stump. 

In the morning Ada planted her half of the pips along the path from the turn where people got cross to the gap they had never been able to find.  Then she lettered a board in the high, narrow capitals Kit had taught her, with the usual backward S for luck: FREE SHOW AT DUSK.
