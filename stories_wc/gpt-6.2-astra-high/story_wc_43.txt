When the blood work came back, Ruth asked whether another transfusion would buy her enough breath to sing both parts.  The doctor said it hadn't bought her enough breath to get to the sink last time.  Ruth considered this, then asked for June, the hospice music therapist, and the good blouse with the pearl snaps. 

Below the windows of the old summit hotel a cloud bank lay so level that the light coming off it made the undersides of people's chins shine.  June sat in that borrowed radiance long enough for Ruth to do up three snaps herself.  She did the fourth when Ruth tipped her chin. 

"I want my girls in here together," Ruth said, "and I don't want anything to go wrong."  She had written out a program on the back of a menu, down to the minute when the girls would kiss.  June recognized the handwriting from the cue cards Ruth used to hold up in the window of the town radio station.  As a child June had stood outside that window on Saturdays to watch Ruth and her daughters sing the supermarket specials.  Even now she could not see a tin of peaches without hearing the little lift on halves. 

It was Ellen who'd stayed on after their mother retired; she'd sung the specials alone for thirty years, in the same bright soprano.  Her sister Kit had walked out one morning at sixteen, leaving a piece of a broken vinyl record on the turntable.  Ruth produced it now from the blouse pocket, a black crescent fine as an onion slice at one end.  The station had pressed a record of the girls for its tenth anniversary, and the crescent was all that remained of Kit's copy. 

"She wanted me to understand something," Ruth said, "but I went and swept the rest up instead." 

Downstairs Ellen was warming the coffee cake the sponsors used to send over while Kit sat in her rental car with the engine running.  Neither had refused to come; each had asked to be told when the other left.  June took the program down in the lift and laid it beside the cake. 

"She could have taken me with her," Ellen said, though June hadn't asked.  "I was twelve, but I could have kept my mouth shut till we got wherever we were going." 

June went out to the car and relayed exactly those words, without the softening she might once have thought it kind to add.  Kit switched off the engine. 

"I went to the swings," she said.  "I sat there till dark waiting for her to find me.  What on earth made me think twelve was big enough to come and find me?" 

June left the car door open and went in to get Ellen. 

When they came up in the lift the cake was still downstairs, but the smell of warm cinnamon had gotten into their clothes.  June knew it from standing outside the station, hoping for a heel of cake after the broadcast; it was funny to be forty and still have hope smell exactly like that. 

Ruth tapped the crescent against the rail, once, twice, and Ellen straightened at the old count while Kit reached back for the door.  June held out her hand for the record, and after the third tap Ruth let it fall. 

"We came to see you," Ellen said.  "Not to be good at anything." 

Under the blanket Ruth's foot went on beating time for a while.  "Even at home I counted you in," she said.  "I can't think what that must have been like." 

Neither girl said it was all right, and after a while Ruth asked for some cake.  Kit went to fetch it and came back singing under her breath the way you do when both hands are full and you need somebody to get the door.  "Mind the plate, mind the plate," she sang, and Ellen took the low part, which turned out to be "I've got it." 

Nothing in the program about this: a fork working a hard brown edge loose, two women hymning the ordinary business of getting a little sweetness into somebody's mouth.  Ruth ate the crumbs that would fit on her tongue.  She did not ask for the record back, and June did not try to stretch the song past the small task it had been made to last.  The sisters pulled their chairs together so the plate could rest between their knees.  When June went out they were still sitting like that, breaking off the good edges for each other without looking down.
