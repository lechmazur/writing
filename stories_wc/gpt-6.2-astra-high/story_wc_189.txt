"Did my mother ring late," Fen asked, "or are you going to put her name back on the bell?" 

In the ancient grotto under the stars, the place on the crossbeam where the name had been cut away showed pale as a bandage.  I was the tidal-pool chronicler; my word had taken it off, and mine could put it back.  Fen had brought her mother's little carving iron, a tool with a handle of folded leather because her mother got blisters even husking an ear of corn.  I had always liked that about our bell keeper, a woman who could make herself heard three islands away and needed mittens to do it. 

Between us lay my open ledger and the jade cicada I used to weight its pages against the draft that smelled, even on the hottest nights, of cold pennies.  It had belonged to the first keeper of the pools, who had cut on the underside of its folded wings two characters meaning "not yet."  In my youth I had understood that inscription as an injunction to patience; you sat until the tide declared itself, however badly you needed your supper.  Only lately had I begun to wonder whether it might be the name of something. 

From the mouth of the grotto a stair went down to the mussel beds, past a pool in which a mason had long ago chiseled the shape of a thumb.  When water touched the nail, the keeper rang everybody off the beds before the gut that crossed their homeward path filled.  Two months ago six pickers had spent a night on a tooth of rock with their baskets gone and one boy holding his grandfather upright by the belt.  My ledger gave the hour the water touched the nail and the hour the keeper rang, a quarter of an hour later.  At the inquiry nobody had asked how a person decided when one wet thing had touched another. 

Fen was not looking at me; she was watching that stone thumb, which from where we knelt appeared quite dry.  Without lifting her eyes she nudged the cicada out of its nest of paper and set it beside the carving, at the very lip of the pool.  It lay there green and unremarkable until she took my chin in her cold fingers and brought my cheek almost against the step.  From there I could see a second cicada lying belly up beneath the first, in a skin of water so thin that the stone nail showed through it down to the last nick.  Then the little green reflection went to pieces and the water drew back, leaving the nail black, as I had seen it from above on the night the pickers were stranded. 

The keeper had told the inquiry that for a quarter of an hour the sea had been trying the step the way a nervous customer tries a mattress.  People had laughed, and so had I, glad of the excuse to look down into my book.  I had wanted all my life to discover the texture of time, and when somebody told me exactly how a particular quarter hour felt, I had made two dots of ink with nothing in between. 

If I gave Fen a simple no, I could foresee the ceremony, the fresh shavings on the steps, another keeper standing at the nail while somebody down on the beds heaved a grandfather onto his back.  Fen's mother had waited for the water to stay, as every keeper before her had waited, while I had entered the first wash against the stone as if the sea had come up the stairs and shut a door behind it. 

I turned the cicada over in the wet and pressed its belly to the page between my two entries; for an instant the words "not yet" stood there in the old keeper's narrow hand.  Fen put the carving iron into my hand, leather first, a small courtesy that nearly undid me. 

"Your mother wasn't late," I said at last, "and when I've cut her name again, you and I are taking this bell down to the gut, where that boy can show us where to hang it."
