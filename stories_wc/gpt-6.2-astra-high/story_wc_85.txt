On the cobalt terrace at dusk my sister laid a brass watch hand beside my supper and asked how much our father had weighed.  I knew the hand by the little crescent that my file had bitten out of its tip.  In a hundred people's memories it had pointed to noon on the day of a wedding that never happened.  Only in Ruth's had it pointed to a number on a scale. 

She had found it while clearing out my workroom for the move uphill.  Left alone with all the implements of a black market genealogist, she had recognized the needle on the dock scale that had weighed us aboard the last boat.  Dad had stepped on and the needle had jumped into the red.  He had stepped off again and we had sailed.  She could remember that quite clearly, right down to the crescent of missing brass.  That was the trouble with a good piece of work. 

She took the bread off my plate and weighed it in her palm.  "I used to do this with everything I ate," she said. 

I had known what it cost to put a lie into my sister but not what it weighed once it was in there.  I wanted to find out and was already afraid I had.  Down below the terrace a rib of the old breakwater showed black against the shoal.  I told her we had until the tide turned. 

We had used the lee of that breakwater for a tip, back when a whole trade worked the outer docks.  All the little driftwood carvings that failed inspection ended there, along with the ones nobody needed after their papers came through.  Even a bad likeness held something of the life that had passed through the carver's fingers.  The trick was to shave away what you didn't want, then handle the wood until whatever you wanted instead came as naturally as the feel of your own teeth.  What fell through the fine screen on my bench we called sieved hush: all the things a family had agreed not to know, in a dust so light it clung to your eyelashes. 

It was Dad who'd taught me, though he had never acquired the knack of making anything up.  His pieces had all been confiscated for the same fault: he put himself into them, and he had a prisoner's number across the backs of his fingers.  At the gate the inspector never saw your face; he held the family's piece while you waited on the other side of a steel door. 

That night Ruth and I went sifting through driftwood carvings along the forgotten shore, feeling in the dark for the one thing Dad had known how to cut well.  The gulls had blunt beaks, the dogs had blunt noses, but his pears were pears to the life, down to the dimple where the flower had been.  Ruth found one and tucked it into her armpit to warm it as he must have done when we were small.  I knew which one she had by the broken stem; I had carried it to the gate and thrown it away myself.  Her face altered under the weight of unspoken words, and then she put the pear against my cheek. 

There was the gate again, and Dad offering me his knife with the pear cupped in his numbered hand.  He had put his last hour into that pear so I could pare the number off and give him a clean hour to show the man.  I had a clean piece for Ruth and me already warm in my other hand.  But the line was moving and Ruth was six and I was fourteen and the man had already started counting backwards from ten.  It had taken me eight of those seconds just to get her through the gate. 

Afterward she would wake up yelling that we had to go back because Dad was still counting on us.  To quell that cry I had given her a scale, a needle, a reason that had nothing to do with anybody choosing anything. 

I asked if she wanted me to take the scale away. 

"No," she said, closing my hand around the pear.  "I want to be able to tell the difference." 

By the time we climbed back to the terrace the bread on my plate had taken up the damp.  Ruth cut herself a slice, but she weighed it first, and I kept my hands on the table and let her.
