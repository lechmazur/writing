The summer I turned fourteen, the Mermaid's mirror cracked, and the whole quay found out that the Mermaid had knuckles like a bricklayer's and was my mother.  Until then you put a penny in a slot, sat facing your reflection, and presently a painted seashell slid down a brass chute into your lap.  On its white lining you saw yourself aboard one of the red fishing smacks that had gone for scrap before I was born.  Mam could still give every boat its proper rigging, though she generally gave the customer a little more hair.  The silvered glass let her see through from her dark side, and the tide caves under the quay let her come and go unseen. 

At home we lived on much the same terms.  She knew by the angle of my left elbow when my sums were going badly, but if I laid my head against her shoulder she found some business with the kettle.  I thought she must be saving up her tenderness for somebody.  After the mirror cracked I followed her to find out who. 

She went ahead with the broken halves under her arms, moving fast enough on the wet ledges that I soon lost the little yellow square of her apron.  At a fork I took a wrong turn toward what sounded like her whistling; it was the wind sucking at a hole in the roof.  Instead of the back of the booth I came out among ferns, with the sky a blue coin overhead and a freshwater spring bubbling out of a bank of maidenhair.  Somebody had stood a kitchen chair in the pool, and on the chair lay a school slate with a rag tied to it.  I knew the slate by the corner I had chewed during long division. 

On it, in blue chalk, my mother had drawn me chewing.  Not a pretty child: one eye screwed up, jaw thrust out like a drawer left open.  She had even got the way my ear folded over when I leaned on my hand.  Looking at that ear, I felt the peculiar shock of being kissed where you thought nobody had ever seen you.  Then I heard her coming and did what still seems to me the bravest thing I ever did for love: I went away without letting her know I had been there. 

That night the proprietor came round to say people still wanted their boats, Mermaid or no Mermaid, and with the trick exposed she might as well work out front.  He could fit another dozen sittings into the time it took her to get through the caves.  Mam sat with the two halves of the mirror on the supper table, picking old putty off the edges with her terrible thumb.  She said a dozen pennies would buy a rabbit. 

Privately I promised myself that whatever else we ate she would have her hour in the ferns.  I had painted in the rigging for years on rush orders; all I needed now was to learn the people.  I started with the proprietor, on the smaller half of the mirror, using lampblack so I could wipe him off if he came to collect.  Mam corrected the mouth with her thumb and said if I could get him to look that pleased with himself on a shell, I could take the dinner trade. 

For the next thirty summers we had rabbit every Sunday and something between us that we never named.  When she died I went back to the pool expecting to find a gallery, though of course I knew what a slate and a wet rag were for.  There was only the chair, the slate scrubbed clean, and a scallop balanced on the seat.  Inside it she had painted a woman at a stall with a brush between her teeth, scanning the dinner crowd for her next face.  On the back, in the space where we used to write the names of boats, she had put the hours: Twelve till one. 

I still paint the old quay as if the red sails might come in on the evening tide, and I still close for dinner.  People suppose I go down to the caves to draw, but mostly I sit and watch the wedge of sunlight work its way across my knees.  That was what I could give her for a dozen pennies: all the time in the world, every day at noon.
