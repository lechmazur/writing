The first passenger to complain about the bird was a boy who said it had tried to take him swimming.  He delivered it to the repair shop in his cap, although its wings were perfectly sound and it could have flown.  Outside, the mist-coated rail depot was full of people waiting for the harbor ferry to lift anchor on her maiden voyage.  The boy had come to watch his mother take her first command, and all he had seen so far was somebody's coat. 

Ruth put down the shoe she was stitching and held out a finger.  The artificial hummingbird drone stepped onto it with the absurd delicacy she had insisted on when she made its feet.  Anybody could stamp two claws out of tin, but these things had to land on people.  At her touch it repeated its last instruction, a rapid double knock meaning turn to the right.  The signals were printed on the back of every paper ticket, with little arrows for people who couldn't read.  Every direction the depot birds gave had first been worked out against the skin of her wrist.  Since going blind she had overseen their maintenance from the same bench where she still mended anything that could be brought in the door. 

She asked the boy to show her exactly where he had lost faith.  Ivo, her apprentice, took her elbow, and she gently put his hand back on the bench.  "Bring my work apron instead," she said. 

They went by the tick of her cane through the crowd to the long stone pools under the departure clock.  For the occasion somebody had turned the fountains on for the first time in years.  Ruth could smell the wet pennies people used to throw in for a smooth crossing.  The boy set the bird on his shoulder and started toward the viewing terrace, then stopped so abruptly that Ivo bumped into him.  "Right again," he said, and on his right the water lipped against the stone. 

The birds followed a green stripe let into the paving, laid down when the depot still belonged to the railway.  Ivo swore that not an inch of it ran anywhere near the pools. 

"Get down where she flies," Ruth said. 

He knelt; the boy, who was already the right height, saw it first.  Examining the way light bent through the water spilling in sheets from one basin to the next, they could see the old green tile on the bottom jump sideways.  From shoulder height it made a bright, crooked continuation of the stripe, pointing straight into the pool.  Ivo wanted to patch the bird's eye with amber film to cut the green glare. 

"And what color does that leave her to steer by?" the boy asked. 

Something vast began to turn beyond the wall, iron taking up iron link by link.  The boy stood listening to his mother's anchor come up, his cap crushed between his hands.  Ruth had spent so long teaching these creatures to turn what they saw into directions that it cost her something to admit the bird was right.  It was the depot that was telling a lie. 

She drew a square of black rubber from the pocket of her apron, a patch she had cut for the sole of the shoe.  By touch she found the offending tile, square under her fingers where the boy had described a long, wavering tongue.  She pressed the patch over it and weighted the corners with four wishes for a smooth crossing. 

"Try now," she said, her sleeve dripping cold against her knee. 

The little feet picked their way up the boy's cuff, settled on his shoulder and walked him neatly round the pool.  Ruth followed the tap of his shoes until the crowd thinned and the salt came clean through the smell of pennies.  Out on the terrace the boy said nothing at all, so she stood beside him and said nothing too.  Then across the quiet water came a whistle fit to call a boy indoors for his supper.  Something brushed her cheek; he had taken off his cap and was waving it with both hands. 

When the sound had gone he put the bird back in his cap to carry it home to the shop. 

"She knows the way now," Ruth said. 

"I know," he said, "but she's been working."
