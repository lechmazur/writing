When my mother retired from training courier pigeons, she asked me to help her sell Mars.  Mars was the attic of our vintage bookshop, where children once paid a dime to send a pigeon to another planet and get a song in reply.  The money, she said, would pay for recording whatever the children, now mostly grandparents, could still sing.  She had a list of twelve who were alive and one whose phone just rang. 

I made my living cataloging collections, a trade I owed to having grown up in a house where nothing my mother said about herself stayed said.  In one newspaper clipping she had learned her trade carrying dispatches in a siege; in another she had trained the white birds for a queen's wedding.  At seventeen I had used the siege in a scholarship essay and had to give the money back.  Now I told her that if she wanted my signature on an inventory we would start with the stone. 

Under its cheese dome sat a Mars meteorite chip no bigger than the seed you spit out of a grape.  Children had touched it before tying their questions to a bird's leg; the journey, Mother used to explain, was downhill all the way for a pigeon that knew the smell of Mars.  Against expectation the chip was genuine, with a university accession number like a tiny row of ants along one edge.  I followed the paper trail to a loan form signed by my grandfather, renewable annually for as long as the exhibit admitted children.  It was the only item in the attic worth selling, and it wasn't ours.  Mother put the dome back so gently that the glass didn't ring. 

That week she let me take the stone back; the next she gave me the keys.  Without the dome to draw the eye, you noticed how the painted apple trees on Mars had faded, pink blossoms gone brown as the glue that held them.  Mother could still balance on the narrow sill where she had received the returning birds, but when I asked her to sing an answer she couldn't get beyond the first three notes.  I had always believed it was her voice we heard pouring out of those paper trees. 

"I only did the flying," she said, and for once she let a small life stand. 

The birds had only circled the roof to their loft, long since pulled down, and Mother couldn't remember where the pipe for calling out the questions had been.  By mapping the swirl of dust motes under a single skylight, we found the mouth of a speaking tube behind a blossom that trembled even with the windows shut.  I took the stairs down while Mother sang her three notes over and over, and found myself kneeling in the shop with my ear against the returns cupboard.  Inside, penciled on the door where only a singer shut in the cupboard could have read them, were names, dates and little rows of dimes.  All those tidy paper trails upstairs led to a speck of stone; the accounts for an entire planet were kept on wood. 

"Girls from the bindery," Mother said when I brought her down, and ran a thumbnail along a row as if she could lift the coins.  "We split the gate." 

I knew one name from her list, a girl I had supposed was just another child who had stood on tiptoe for her turn at the glass.  She lived six blocks away.  Mother asked if I would be the one to knock. 

By September we had five women sitting in the shop on folding chairs, waiting for somebody else to begin.  Mother set the empty dome on the counter and asked into it, in the piping voice of a customer, whether there was anything on Mars that didn't die.  The woman from six blocks away sang that there was a kind of apple there that ripened backwards into blossom, and another supplied the climbing tune on which the blossom tightened to a bud.  I had the recorder in my pocket but waited until they asked for it.  On the label Mother wrote their names in the order they had sung, leaving room beneath for the ones we hadn't found.  Then she handed me the pencil, and I put her down for the child who asked.
