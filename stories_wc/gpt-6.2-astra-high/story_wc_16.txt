In the extinct language archive, two porters were arguing over a widow's laugh.  One had a blue cart for the coast and one had a red cart for the hills, and the laugh came halfway through a duet.  By noon the carts would reach the fork, where forty years of collecting would go irrevocably left or right.  The porters wanted a decision from Nell, the arcane linguist whose neat little labels had got them into this fix.  On the wax cylinder in dispute she had written HILL MUSIC, although the widow had never set foot above the tide line. 

"You can tell them now," said the widow, who was Nell's older sister Ada. 

It was Ada who had got the archive closed, by asking at a public hearing to see the paper on which she had supposedly given away her laugh.  Nell had sat through that hearing with her hands folded over a key in her lap.  Opening the drawer would have cost their father his pension; keeping it shut had cost Nell every Sunday supper since. 

Now she unlocked it and took out their father's glass ink well, with the fine split down its belly that had always made it sing a little flat.  He had been the first director, back when a man with a recording horn could expect a kitchen chair wherever he went. 

"He never kept the papers," Nell said, and saw Ada hear, as plain as another sentence, how long she had known.  "He washed the ink back into the well so he could call everything a gift." 

That was the queer virtue of the old language: what you undertook in it stayed undertaken, even when the water went clear.  An undertaking couldn't be rubbed out, but without the others on which it depended it made no more sense than a single fiddle part.  The hill people had called such arrangements symphonies of truth, not because nobody lied but because you could hear exactly where a lie left somebody else hanging.  Nobody alive could ask for a cup of tea in that language, but children in both towns still sang the promises over their skipping ropes. 

To unsnarl forty years of washed ink, Nell would have to begin with an undertaking of her own.  She did what Ada used to call a carefully reckless thing: she tested the nib on her thumb before signing away her livelihood.  She wrote her undertaking in the old script, giving the verb for standing witness the hooked tail that meant even against one's own kin.  The well gave out a low, enduring note. 

Ada put her ear to the split, then tightened her sister's fingers around the nib as she had done when they were small.  She caught the note and bent it upward, the way she used to bend a nail sticking through a crate so it wouldn't tear anybody's hand. 

"I can't catch the words," Nell admitted, because her ear had never been as good as her eye. 

"I'll see you heard on the coast, which is all I can answer for." 

Something dark as a tadpole swam up the side of the well.  One of the porters whistled under his breath and the thing sank again; tunefulness wasn't what was wanted.  Then the hill porter took the pen and asked Nell to show him how to undertake the carrying of a witness up a mountain at his own expense.  That made three notes, and under them the old ink began to play its hundreds. 

Now they could hear how the archive had been built, not out of gifts but via interconnected promises: a singer lent a song to a neighbor who would sing for her son when he went down to fish.  Against Ada's laugh there rose the reedy tenor of her hill husband promising her mother that any children of theirs would know what their mother sounded like at seventeen.  All these years Ada had supposed he had been singing to her.  The blue porter had to look away while she listened. 

There was no time to settle every claim before the paths diverged, but there was time to peel off every label and write LOAN on every blank back.  The red cart took the duet because Ada's boy lived in the hills; the hill porter signed for its return when the boy came down for winter fishing.  Nell wrapped the well in her coat for the hearing, and when the carts started up she found her sister walking beside her. 

"Don't get excited," Ada said, but she had taken the high part of the squeaking wheels, and after a safe interval Nell ventured the low.
