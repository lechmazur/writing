After the final bus pulled out, the vacant station gave back the sound of a single grape dropping onto the pavilion roof.  In October the roof was amber, not because the glass was colored but because the leaves lay flat against it, each with its little skeleton of lead.  From her stool there Ada could see the whole bottom vineyard, where the boars would come if they came at all.  She had been its lone sentinel for thirty autumns and knew a foot on gravel from a cloven hoof.  She set out the second stool before Ruth reached the door. 

The new history of the valley was due at the printer on Monday, and Ruth, who had collected every testimony but Ada's, had come up on the bus with the proofs.  She laid them on the stool, open to a photograph of two girls in this pavilion on the last night before the river was dammed.  Underneath was the caption: The lucky ones, whose houses were above the waterline.  Nobody seeing it would know that one girl had gone away next morning or that the other's hand, apparently resting on the bench, was holding hers. 

Ruth held out a narrow flake of paper, black down one side.  It was the burned edge of the love letter Ada had pushed into the stove that night when the photographer, Ruth's father, lifted his flash.  All that could still be read was the word come, with its tail of blue ink where Ruth's nib had caught. 

"I thought if you wouldn't tell me why you burned it, you might at least tell the people who'll read this book," Ruth said. 

"What makes you think I'd rather tell the whole damn county than tell you?" 

Ruth considered this, then sat down on the proofs.  Ada slid the flake between two sheets of glass she kept for pressing leaves, so they could look without breathing it away. 

"Do you remember how your father took that picture?" 

When she helped the old pickers rediscover their drowned streets on the survey map, she began gently like this, with something their hands might remember.  Ruth raised one arm high and crooked the other against her ribs to cradle an imaginary camera. 

"You remember the survey pictures he made, how you could put a glass to them and see the bucket sitting on our well?  I thought he'd be able to put a glass to us and read what you wrote." 

Ruth's arm stayed up a moment longer, and Ada watched her discover what it felt like to be on the wrong end of that burst of white. 

"Now do me," Ruth said.  She took Ada's hand and held it out toward the little stove, palm up, the way you offer a ticket to a conductor.  "I had the two fares in this hand.  Then I didn't need to read your answer because I saw what you did with the question." 

Outside, something heavy shouldered the wire, and Ada reached without looking for the spoon she used to beat the stovepipe.  Ruth put it into her hand, as if in all those years neither of them had moved an inch.  Three blows, and they heard the sullen retreat through the leaves. 

Once Ada had drawn comfort from supposing that there were destined mismatches, girls born to get on buses and girls born to watch over a few stony acres.  It hurt considerably more to know that for the price of turning over a scrap of paper they might have had a life. 

"Would you have come?" Ruth asked. 

"Not that morning," Ada said, thinking of the neighbors asleep in rows between the barrels in her father's cellar, their muddy good shoes outside the door.  "But I'd have known where to write." 

Ruth slid the proofs out from under her and for a while they worked on the caption, which was too small a space for everything and quite large enough for the truth.  They put down their names, and that they were saying goodbye, although at the time only one of them knew it.  At Ada's suggestion they added one instruction: Look at the hands.  Then Ruth tore off the old caption and on its clean underside wrote down the address Ada had never had.
