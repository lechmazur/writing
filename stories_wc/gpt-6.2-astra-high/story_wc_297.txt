At dusk the ferry came in thirty years early, with my son at the tiller and my son on the bank.  The one on the bank dropped my supper, and the one at the tiller said, with feeling, "Oh, not the good bowl."  That settled for me the question of whether the future, in arriving early, had brought along the actual people.  Nobody but Kit knew how much I loved that ridiculous bowl, which had little blue feet. 

From the edge of the cobalt glade I could see passengers lining up behind him, holding white tickets between their teeth to keep their hands free for luggage.  Not one of them could step ashore.  Where the landing ought to have been there was only the thin, unshadowed deck of my dream, which would bear no weight but mine.  And I had not moved in nineteen years. 

That was the bargain a visionary made when she let the town plant her: a hundred miles of sight in exchange for the length of a stride.  We worked out the rest of the bargain under a total eclipse, when even the mayor could look up and see how thoroughly one bright thing can hide another.  Nothing I foresaw should be allowed to take root unless somebody else, dreaming independently, saw it too.  In time people forgot the covenant and took to asking me the price of wool next winter, which I generally gave them.  I forgot it too, especially on nights when Kit talked about leaving the valley and I let myself see him coming home.  Now there were thirty or forty people out on the water whose safety depended on the substance of my wish.  Already the names on the life belts had begun to run like ink. 

The older Kit bent and put one finger through the insubstantial planking, much as the boy used to test the skin on cooling milk.  "Never pictured this part," he said, and it occurred to me to heed the exact words instead of the terror underneath.  It wasn't the landing I had to make him picture; it was the intersection of our dreams I had to find. 

I asked the boy to bring me a clipping from the maze topiary, a length with a fork still in it.  We used those forks to teach apprentice visionaries to follow two lives at once without letting either life know the way the other turned.  He was purposefully curious, my Kit; before he cut so much as a leaf he wanted to know whether I meant to tie him to that tiller.  Somewhere behind the older Kit a suitcase fell over, and we heard the rolling, diminishing music of a jar of buttons emptying across the deck.  I wanted so badly to say anything that would hurry him. 

"I tied one of you there already," I said instead, and waited while he found his knife. 

Linking through scars is ticklish work even when you have all afternoon and nobody you love is watching.  Kit fitted the heel of the clipping into the white notch where my planting rope had bitten, then laid his thumb against one cut tip.  His other self leaned over the rail to touch the other tip with the same small crescent of a scar, got opening oysters the summer he was eight.  There was such a rush of pictures that the roots swelled in my shoes, splitting leather I had kept sound through nineteen winters.  Not once in all those brilliant miles did either Kit see himself bringing a ferry home.  But both of them, whenever they thought of me, saw the same silly thing: my feet cooling in the shallows while we sat together on the lowest step.  That was the part I had dreamed too, without even noticing it under the dazzle of the ship. 

I let the ship go.  Under the water a single stone step took weight, then another above it, and the passengers started down into the glade with rolled blankets and birdcages and a great many loose buttons in their pockets.  Behind the last of them the ferry went to mist; my grown son stood on the bank with his hands still curled around an absent wheel.  He opened them slowly, with the wild grin of somebody taking both hands off his bicycle for the first time. 

The boy picked up the good bowl, chipped now, and sat down with the man to help me work my swollen feet out of my shoes.  I felt the little fish come nosing round my roots and took good care not to look any further ahead than that.
