For twenty years I have had a mother only when it rains.  After each shower the old viaduct glows from the feet of its piers to the tips of its iron birds, and she appears underneath it, walking upside down.  People used to buy postcards of our city's famous gravity defier, but the boy selling them has grown up and sells insurance now.  I'm the one who still comes running. 

Yesterday I brought the thing my father died getting down for the museum: an ancient clay tablet with runes so deeply stamped you could read them with a mitten on.  It took me six years at the museum to get a key to the case and another six to understand what I meant to steal.  The thing was a set of instructions for making a way across a river when neither bank would stay in the same century.  The builders had solved the problem through procedural generation: for every step a traveler took, the bridge laid down the next span according to what that traveler expected to find.  On a sound bridge that got you home.  On ours, with its fractured timelines shining through the mortar, it got my mother to the place where my father was falling, again and again. 

The bridge was periodically eternal, a property the builders had marked with the sign of a wheel with one long spoke.  Between the drop and the ripple, when the last bead of rain fell from a certain iron beak but had not yet struck the river, you could spend as long as you liked.  Getting in was simply a matter of putting the tablet back, which I did standing on the rail, with the water making a cold fist of my skirt.  Getting out was what I had come to teach her. 

She was young enough to be my sister, with both gloves worn clear through at the palms.  Behind her stretched a terrible brightness, miles of illuminated arches, and under every arch hung my father in his one good blue suit.  She had caught one by the sleeve, one by the hair, one with her lovely bare feet clamped around his boot.  None of them were breathing. 

"I used to think it was a labyrinth of stone," she said, not surprised to see me.  "Now I know it's a labyrinth of me, and somewhere there's a woman fast enough." 

She was setting out to conquer that last inch of herself when I called her by her name, which was Ada.  Mother would have meant the little girl in the red coat she had left sitting on the bank; I wanted her to look at the woman who had got in here on her own.  She looked, and the light went out of the nearest arch.  Where his body had shone there was a single wet thumbprint, the print of the hand that had laid the original brick. 

"You don't have to find the way," I said.  "We only have to retrace it." 

I took a backward step and promptly fell toward the river, because what I expected from empty air was perfectly ordinary.  Ada caught me by the belt.  For the first time in my life I felt what it cost her to hold somebody up: not strength but the incessant small corrections of a seamstress feeding slippery cloth under a needle.  We went back like that, the arches winking out overhead, until we came to the first and brightest, where his hand was still going down and hers had not quite missed.  I felt her making room in her grip for him. 

I could have told her that he would have wanted her to choose me, but all I knew of him was a blue suit and a signature on a museum card.  "I want you," I said instead. 

Her fingers closed around the worn leather of my belt, exactly where they had made a place for somebody else.  The last light out was the gold of his wedding ring.  Then the river took the drop and the rail took our weight, and neither of us was anything but cold. 

At the insurance office the lights were on; they let us in and gave us a towel that said something cheerful about the future.  She dried my hair as if I were six, then turned around and let me do hers.
