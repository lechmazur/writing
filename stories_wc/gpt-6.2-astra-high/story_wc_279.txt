In the first week of the unlearning, Nell drew little feet on all the commas in the council minutes, so they could run away before anybody mistook them for stops.  The clerk threatened to bill her for the slate, but later she saw him rub out a stop and leave the feet.  That was how you knew a joke had landed in this town; nobody laughed, but something moved. 

For a hundred years a motion in the whispering grotto had carried if it found a second voice across the water.  The builders of the tiled gallery had understood the difference between a second voice and a voice coming back, but their little brass speaking marks had long since gone green.  Now the town was trying to unlearn assent by echo, without quite knowing what else assent might sound like. 

On the morning they were to decide whether to board up the lower entrance before the autumn tides, mist packed the grotto from floor to lip.  A mauve wash from the heather outside stained people's collars, giving even the children the queasy dignity of mourners.  Nell had been hired to chalk a circle for each household on the gallery floor, so the clerk could at least tell who had come.  She had brought colored sand as well, to make a mandala in the empty middle where motions went to be heard.  She wanted to discover meaning in the mess of scuffs that people left at meetings, the only minutes that had never told her a lie.  Inside each chalk circle she set a little tail of sand, drawing all the tails inward to meet in a many-colored wheel. 

By noon the wheel had grown ragged everywhere except along a wedge of blue belonging to the women who gutted fish on the lower quay.  They were there in their good shoes, standing as still as if somebody were taking an expensive photograph.  "Board it," said the foreman, and from behind the women came a soft but unmistakable "Board it."  Every blue shoe turned toward it, breaking the tails clean across, but not one woman looked at another.  Nell, who made her living drawing strangers, knew the difference between turning to meet a face and turning to look for one.  Before the clerk could enter the second she stepped into the wheel to interject, a thing she had never dared to do except in chalk. 

"Would the seconder be kind enough to put a toe in the blue?" 

No toe came forward, but out of the wall came the last two words, in Nell's own piping register.  Feeling along the grout, she found a flake of whispering gallery tile smaller than a thumbnail, rocking loose against the hollow that had held it.  When she steadied it with her finger the foreman could say blue as often as he liked without getting blue back. 

Then the foreman laughed in relief and proposed the boards again, louder, as if a broken tile had been the only trouble.  Now there was no second, and Nell could see him getting ready to take the absence of an objection for exactly the same thing.  Nell lifted the flake free and slipped it into the nearest fishwife's hand.  The woman looked down at the wheel, then used the flake to cut a channel through the blue and bank the displaced sand across its mouth. 

"That's the boards," she said, and trickled a little spit into the channel on the grotto side of the bank.  It pooled where the tails met, where in winter the women worked by lamplight mending pots.  The foreman knelt down beside her, his good trousers soaking up the purple, and asked where in God's name the water was coming from now. 

"Up," she said, tapping the floor, and at last he understood what the lower entrance was for at ebb.  Nell saw him take a breath to ask how much came up, and let it go when one of the women bent to draw a line on his boot.  Those close enough to see stopped explaining to their neighbors, who stopped in turn to hear what it was they were missing.  The hush got all the way around the gallery without finding anybody in charge of it.  From somewhere well under their feet, distinct as a cat lapping milk, came the sound of fresh water working its way up through the stone. 

The clerk put down his slate and came to kneel in the sand with the others, to see what sort of door would let that little tongue through.  On the seat of his trousers, when he stood, he had a blue comma with feet.
