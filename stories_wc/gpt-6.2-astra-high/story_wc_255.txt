Each spring, after the phoenix rises, the glassblower sweeps its nest into a heap in his courtyard and sets out the pots of kitchen herbs.  By noon the dead have their fingers out of the ash, and the bargaining begins.  To awaken someone you must burn a piece of that person's work, and the work must be your own to give.  Nobody would have called these bartered awakenings fair, but nobody had ever found the phoenix eager to discuss terms. 

Most of the dead were particular about what went on the fire for their sake.  They arranged the herbs at the lip of the heap, spelling out by stalk and leaf the same old household messages that gardeners left for one another on market days.  A ring of thyme meant enough, a gap in the ring meant more, and anything tipped on its side was a flat refusal. 

The colorblind painter Nell had come with the last thing her father had made, a concertina reed tongue no longer than a baby's fingernail.  She had already burned the bellows, the buttons, even the little wooden feet, a prodigal history of afternoons spent trying to get his portrait right.  Each time, anxious to please her, he sat up straight and did something handsome with his mouth that he had never once done alive.  Now her own daughter was asking what her grandfather had looked like, and Nell had six creditable paintings of a stranger to show her.  She wanted the face he used to make when he played the one note in the concertina that had always sounded like a goose being stepped on. 

She had brought a child's concertina with a socket that would take his tongue, but of course she couldn't both burn the thing and play it.  She set the instrument beside the thyme and showed him the tongue on her palm.  His fingers walked two pots of mint apart and laid a stalk of rosemary across the gap.  It was the arrangement for a bridge, which in their village meant to carry a debt until market day.  Then he tipped the thyme over.  No fire, but something to be carried on credit.  Even dead, he was innocently cunning, like a boy who has discovered that a locked gate can be lifted clear off its hinges without the least affront to the lock. 

She fitted the tongue and put the concertina within reach, wondering what good a pair of hands could possibly get out of an instrument that needed knees and shoulders too.  He tried, and the push sent the thing scooting across the stones to the child's shoe.  Her daughter sat down without being asked, wedged one end against her chest and let the ashy fingers draw the other end away.  At first there was only the small domestic sigh of air escaping through a leak.  Then came the goose note, indignant enough to turn a dozen heads, and her daughter screwed one eye shut and bared three lower teeth in a sort of sideways grin. 

There he was.  Nell had spent six springs trying to see him all at once, as if the man had been a view from a particular window.  What she remembered was a thing a face did, and here it was doing it again on quite another set of bones. 

She took out her charcoal, the one stick she never had to read a label to recognize, and followed the tune across a sheet of paper.  At the left was the child listening, in the middle the outrageous note, and at the right, where the child's cheek creased, the beginnings of her father's face.  She left the line of the jaw unfinished because the bellows were coming together now and the face was already going away. 

When the fingers finally loosened, her daughter took the instrument on her own knees and practiced until she could make the goose speak at will.  By then the herbs were only herbs and the glassblower was out with his broom.  On the way home the child asked if she might keep the concertina, bad note and all.  Nell gave it to her outright, so there would be no question, when her turn came, whose it was to give.
