My sister brought her old ear back in a tobacco tin, packed in wool as tenderly as if she meant to forgive me.  She set it on the potting bench and took off her mitten to show me where the last two fingers ended now.  I knew where they ended, but it was evidently important that I look. 

All winter I had been the only hearing aid designer on the ice coast with an empty order book.  That was not why I had asked Ruth to come.  I wanted to find the sound of trust breaking, and I had one afternoon to do it, while the cells of the new ear divided. 

The sunken orchard greenhouse still had twelve feet of air under its salt-scored dome, enough for the upper branches to put out leaves and for a woman in a skiff to tend them.  A ring of shipwrecks kept the worst seas from the glass; the ships had been towed there to die so the trees could live.  On each new twig I grew a little listening drum in a wax mold, using the same transparent skin that trembled on the blossoms when a bee came near.  Whatever pattern I chose to imprint as that skin doubled would last the wearer until the drum wore out. 

I made my patterns by collecting absences.  I would have a customer listen to a piece of harbor music, a capstan turning or gulls quarreling over guts, and tap whenever a familiar note went missing.  From the taps I taught the drum to supply what the damaged ear could not.  Ruth had been my first customer and the harbor smith our father, so naturally for her test piece we had chosen his hammer. 

From our bench we could see his ashen forge under the aurora, on the one wreck that still stood high enough to serve as a landing.  Green light made a thin singing in my teeth, as colors sometimes did for me, so faintly that for years I had thought everybody heard them.  That evening a particularly bright fold came down over the landing and I caught myself listening for iron, though our father had been buried since the thaw. 

Ruth took the drum from the tin and fitted it into my ear.  With her good hand she struck the bench three times with the handle of my knife, the little skipping stroke our father had used to call us across the ice for supper.  She laid the knife down.  Against my cheekbone the soft fourth blow went home. 

I had imagined her going through the ice because she could not hear it crack, our father going in after her, and all winter I had bred thickness into skins to make that crack as loud as a gunshot.  But he had stopped calling when he saw the black lane opening between them, and my clever drum had called her on. 

"How many more did you hear?" I asked. 

She looked at the knife.  "Enough to think he still wanted me to come." 

Under the mold the new skin was going cloudy, each cell drawing its bright belt tight about its middle.  I could teach it now to repeat nothing, only to lift the sound that reached it, but I could not make that promise about my own head.  I still heard the hammer in the wool of my pillow some nights, and I had never worn one of my ears. 

Ruth picked up the knife again, and this time instead of beating out a pattern she slipped the blade under the wax.  "Will it grow plain if we leave it alone?" 

I wanted to tell her what a poor thing a plain drum was, how it would lift the racket of the wrecks along with the human voice.  Instead I held the twig steady while she peeled away the mold, taking care not to bruise the wet rim with the stumps of her fingers.  By dark it was clear again, and I cut it free and let her fit it herself. 

On the way home I rowed with my back to the landing and let Ruth watch the ice.  Halfway through the wrecks she raised her mitten and I shipped the oars, though I had heard nothing.  We sat there until a sheet of green ice slid from a listing yardarm and went into the water where our bow would have been.  Then she tapped me once between the shoulders, and once was enough.
