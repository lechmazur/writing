After the reservoir broke, Nell Pike found dry beds for six thousand people and made it impossible for any of them to send her a postcard.  I had a better excuse than gratitude for looking her up: I had a piece of the moon in my handbag, and it belonged to her.  She had lent it to my boy the night we spent on the roof, to give him something to hold besides my throat.  By the time the boat came he was asleep with one fist shut.  Now he was at school again, and I was discovering how many hours there are between taking a child to school and fetching him in a town where nobody knows you. 

The card in the little case identified the fragment as part of a lunar meteorite and Nell as the third grade winner of a statewide essay contest.  I asked the school secretary whether anybody remembered the girl who won the moon.  Her brother read meters with a man whose greyhound had come from a woman who swore by the little railway station up the valley for getting impossible parcels delivered. 

Ivy went in through the ticket window and came out through the clock, which had long since lost both hands.  Under it Nell sat dispatching parcels on the principle that whatever the county regulations might say, nobody drove home with an empty trunk.  She thanked a building inspector very nicely for his condemnation notice and gave him two accordions to take to the Polish social club.  He took them. 

When I set the moon on the counter she said, "Good, that should do something for the tides," and when I didn't laugh she said, "Small tides, obviously."  She kept on smiling as if I had a camera that wouldn't click.  Behind her a hairbrush and a single cup hung from the old brass hooks marked INQUIRIES and COMPLAINTS.  I ought to have gone then, but all I had waiting for me was an afternoon of listening to somebody else's pipes knock. 

"Have you got anything for me to take home?" 

She looked at the empty shelves; she looked at the moon.  "When the story changes," she said, "that's when my orders generally come through."  She showed me the county paper, which for the first time since the dam went had nothing about us on the front page.  The county had canceled her posting that morning; even the cup had a property number. 

I could have offered her our couch and watched her load it on a truck for somebody worse off.  Instead I told her what I had really come to find, having mistaken her for somebody who knew the cure for loneliness. 

"I'm all right till lunch," I said, "but I can't eat another lunch standing up at the sink." 

She put the moon in her pocket, got her coat off the camp bed, and came along without making any promises.  We had scrambled eggs, and when she started on a joke about the chicken I asked her quite politely to let me eat my eggs in peace.  She rested her chin in her hand and finished chewing, and I got my first look at her face off duty. 

She made lunch the next day; the day after that the meter reader came with his own fork, having heard that the price of admission to the lunar society was an onion.  By Friday there were too many for my kitchen, so we reconvened at the station, in strict observance of the notice, which condemned the building but said nothing about the platform.  The inspector himself helped take down the doors to make a table, on the understanding that we would not thank him in the paper. 

At the end of lunch my boy borrowed the moon again, on the understanding that whoever took it home would have to bring it back for lunch tomorrow.  I have seen Nell on a bad morning, in her coat with her bag packed, turning that bit of gravel over in her fingers.  "They'll want it back," she says, as if we give a damn about the moon.
