After my fever broke, I came downstairs to find the mountain weather station full of children in their good coats.  They were waiting for the oracle to say whether the school bus could take the high road down for the winter.  The little ones had been told it was a picnic and kept asking when they could eat.  Through the glass I saw the northern lights folding green linen over the place where the road disappeared.  The sun would not clear the ridge again until February. 

Our fault-tolerant oracle could lose an anemometer, a whole mast even, and still put out a forecast good enough for a driver to stake thirty lives on.  In weather like this the hairpin mast iced up, so for that blind mile she relied on sixty winters of observations taken at three elevations along the road.  At least, that was what the brass plate said.  She spoke in my father's voice because he had been the last man here willing to read ten thousand words into a microphone. 

"Good to go, Nell," she said, and for a moment I was ten and he was steadying my bicycle. 

The driver stood up, but a boy by the window went on rubbing a patch of clear glass with his sleeve.  "How come the snow goes both ways?" he asked. 

He had the peripherally aware gaze of a child who has sat through a great deal of school looking elsewhere.  Across the yard the windsock pointed east; below it, in the notch, a loose white braid was blowing west.  My father had called those braids the white ropes, something never to be caught underneath. 

"Give me the likelihood of a reversal at the hairpin," I said. 

"One winter in sixty," said the oracle. 

I asked the driver for five minutes and went to the cabinet where we kept the original returns, long drawers that smelled of mittens drying.  In the winter my mother lay dying, the ministry had threatened to close any station that could not maintain its outposts.  My father would have lost his house along with his job, and she wanted to die looking out of her own window.  Nobody had gone to the outposts that winter, or for the next eleven, while the money for the observers paid off her nursing bills.  At the kitchen table I had learned three hands, including the slow, square capitals of a man who did not exist. 

All these years I had wanted to find the weight of a swallowed truth, and here it was in my hands, twelve winters of paper.  But there was something else in the drawer: a botanical illustration of mountain avens, the last plate my mother ever drew.  I remembered sharpening her pencils and begging her to draw something that moved.  She had obliged me by occupying margins, the only part of the expensive Bristol board her publisher would let her waste.  Beside the avens ran a column of dates and tiny studies of the white ropes as seen from her pillow, each with its answering arrow for the wind up here.  A little graphite had come off on my thumb; I took my hand away before I could smudge the dates.  On nine mornings that single winter she had drawn the winds at odds. 

"These are real," I told the oracle, holding the plate to her eye, and then I fed her the returns and told her which were not.  I wanted her to tell me, in his voice, that anybody would have done the same.  On the dial her needle rocked between green and red, the old swaying certainties of a man at a kitchen table deciding what he could bear to lose.  At last the needle dropped below both colors into the unpainted space that meant she did not know. 

"We'll stay for breakfast," said the driver.  I expected questions, but he took off his coat and began laying it over the knees of two girls who had fallen asleep sitting up.  Outside, the green linen went on folding and unfolding, and nothing happened on the road that we could see. 

I called the ministry while I still had the nerve, and when they asked who had made the false returns I gave my name before my father's.  The boy at the window had got his sandwich out at last.  He ate with his eyes on the notch, and when the snow began to run one way again he didn't have to ask if I had seen.
