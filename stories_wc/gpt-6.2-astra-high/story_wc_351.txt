In the moonlit canyon the people of Mercy stood in their nightshirts, listening to somebody else's canary sing inside their heads.  At dawn they would scatter among six resettlement trains, and whatever still belonged to the town rather than to any one sleeper would go quiet.  Already the dam had turned the cemetery road into a silver tongue of water.  Nobody had thought to miss the bird until the sleepers gathered for their last untangling and heard its small throat working against the dark. 

Mara had come home to save precisely such things.  She was a helix-minded engineer, the kind who put a finger to a stripped screw and felt for the ghost of the thread.  She meant to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams, beginning with the town that had paid her tuition in eggs and small change.  In her case were wax cylinders enough for all the futures Mercy had rehearsed in its sleep and never built.  She wanted especially the aviary, her father's glass palace for birds, which children who had never seen so much as a parakeet could still navigate blindfolded. 

Mercy had learned to dream together when a shift below ground lasted longer than a child could bear to be without a parent.  "Through synaptic plasticity," the nurse who wired the first two pillows had written, as though naming the process explained what happened when a miner fell asleep imagining wings and his daughter woke with sore shoulders.  Now the nurse was dead and the entangled memories were beginning to pull painfully at people who would have to wake up in different towns.  Each cylinder could take a sleeper's part and give it back on distant nights, provided Mara could find where that part began. 

She put her father's pillow to her ear and heard, beneath the song, the chaotically precise ticking of a bird trying every bar of its cage in turn.  Across the circle old Inez, who had cleaned the school, was making the same noise with her tongue. 

"It wasn't a palace," Inez said when Mara knelt beside her.  From her nightshirt pocket she drew a broken periscope mirror, one edge still bedded in putty that bore the whorls of a little thumb.  "We used to poke it up the air shaft to see if it was day." 

Mara had forgotten that before the town employed a nurse it had employed children small enough to crawl under a stalled ore cart.  Inez tipped the glass until a piece of moon floated in it, and fifty heads tipped back for a better look. 

"Your daddy made us a bird for up there," she said.  "We couldn't see a thing but the light, so he said to listen." 

She saw how, in the long nights of retelling, the shaft had widened into a dome and the sliver of daylight had acquired panes and mullions.  Mara felt the canyon tremble, though the water below lay smooth; it was the shiver of fifty narrow backs remembering the weight of rock.  She had been about to give her father a cylinder for the bird and Inez a cylinder for the cage.  Neither would have made a sound worth keeping. 

She sat down in the dust to modify the recorder, winding the return wire around the cutting arm so the sleepers could hear what went into the wax.  Instead of asking whose bird it was, she asked Inez to show them how to let it out.  Inez turned the mirror slowly, carrying the moon from face to face, and each sleeper whistled the note that seemed to come next.  Some contributed only breath; even that was audible in the stillness before the wheels of the first train began to turn.  When the tune came round to Mara she had no memory of a mine to offer, only the ache between her shoulders.  She stretched her arms, and in the dream every narrow back straightened and the glass roof lifted clean away.  One by one their tongues went still, not with the awful wrench of waking alone but because a song, unlike a cage, could come to an end. 

Afterward she cut six copies, marking none with a name.  On her own train a little boy played the cylinder twice and then whistled an ending she had never heard before.  She made room on the label for the date and place of this discovery, steadying her pencil against the shake of the rails.
