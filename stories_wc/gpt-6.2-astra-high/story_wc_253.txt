The film of my father lasts as long as it takes him to whittle the bowl of a spoon.  He was slow at it, and for this I am grateful, since there is no other film.  It was made at our farmhouse a week after my mother died, when the happiness mandate still required a demonstration of recovery.  Behind him, through the kitchen window, three giant wind turbines turn in a white sky.  One blade keeps laying a long gray finger across the table where he works.  The spoon is the kind with a bent handle that a child can get into her mouth without tipping all the milk out. 

The child it was made for is six now and would like to see her grandfather, who died in April.  On the soundtrack a man announces that from the jaws of the timber my father wrests a little breakfast.  At this there is applause in the kitchen, although I was there and remember how we sat.  We used the recovery money to settle my mother's account at the pharmacy.  My signature is on the release. 

After the mandate ended, the blank-faced performer who supplied the voice went on the radio to sell mattresses.  Even at the height of his career, dramatically narrating mundane tasks for the recovery crews, he had looked like somebody waiting for a bus.  I found him through the mattress people and asked if he had kept the sound that went under his voice.  He came out to the farm with a small black case and had me play the film against the kitchen wall.  The blades on the wall and the blades going by outside gradually fell out of step. 

There was no other track, he said; in our district the crew had owned a camera without a microphone.  He opened his case and showed me a dried flower bouquet tied with a black shoelace.  By rolling the stiff heads between his palms, with the microphone close, he got the sound of several people clapping in a room where someone was asleep.  The noise I had taken for my own small, disloyal contribution came from a bunch of statice.  I asked him to do it again, and watched his hands until I believed it. 

He offered to leave me the film with the sound stripped off, a service for which he usually charged half his original fee.  We tried it that way, but without a sound to occupy the room my father's mouth looked sealed, and the spoon might have been soap.  I wanted my girl to have the small ease of sitting with him, something I had not known was an ease until it stopped.  I got a stick of pear wood from the shed and sat where he had sat. 

The performer set his microphone on a folded towel and told me to watch the shoulder rather than the knife.  Each time the shoulder on the wall went down I took a shaving, and between strokes the machine gathered in the low turning noise that made our water glasses walk.  Once my father stopped and reached sideways out of the picture.  I had forgotten about that, and so had the man who had filled the interval with an account of the spoon's prospects in the world.  Then I saw a little cloud of dust go up in the wedge of sun beside his head.  My mother's flowers had hung there from a nail, in the way of anybody reaching for the dustpan.  The performer lifted his bouquet and let it graze the plaster just once.  I paid him for the afternoon and asked him to leave our two names on this version, too. 

When my daughter came home I showed her the film with its remade hush, telling her which hands she could see and which she could hear.  She ate cereal out of the spoon while she watched him make it.  Halfway through she held it up against the light from the projector and fitted its shadow into his fist.  His hand moved and she had to chase it down the wall, spilling a little milk on the floor.  I left the sound running while we wiped it up.
