After the first snowmelt, when the little boats could nose out from under the white ribs of their winter sheds, I took my mother's lamp down to the bronze grotto.  All winter I had been collecting the weights of different burdens, as a fractal weaver must before the village can divide the coming year's work.  A net mender had given me her needle, a midwife her stool, the boy who washed the dead a cake of soap with a black hair curled in it.  Only my mother's work remained to weigh, and she had hidden the kerosene lamp burner deep in her pocket. 

"I'll have that back when you've finished," she said, keeping pace beside me in her slippers. 

The grotto had been cast around the skeleton of the last whale to come into our bay, and through the long slot where its spine had lain we could see the stars.  At high water the bronze ribs sang, each a little later than its neighbor; at low water the silences overlapped in the same fashion, making what we called the shingled hush.  In its shelter a thing set down went on bearing for a moment the weight it had borne in a life.  My trade consisted in catching that moment in a web of loops, each loop composed of smaller ones clear down to the width of an eyelash.  The more loops pulled tight, the more hands the village owed the worker.  By morning the lime in the meltwater would calcify my web and I could take it up whole, a delicate white reckoning. 

I set the glass reservoir on the last empty loop, and it lay as lightly as a bubble on a sleeping lip. 

"There," said my mother, and held out her hand. 

She had kept the harbor light forty years, the last twelve alone, since my father's boat had slipped past it going out and never passed it coming in.  Nobody forbade her the dances or the long walks out to pick the sour red berries that grew on the headland.  She was trapped without a boundary I could put my finger on, not even a door she couldn't open.  Once I had taken her dancing and seen her thumb turning an imaginary wick wheel against the palm of her partner's hand. 

"Let me weigh the part you touch," I said. 

She closed her fist, but not before I saw the little brass wheel bedded in the groove it had worn across her thumb.  "If they give it to somebody else, what am I to do when I wake in the night?" 

I had been thinking of the wheel as something she turned to send a light out over the water, and of my father as the one man it couldn't reach anymore.  Now I remembered coming home late myself and seeing, well before I could make out the harbor mouth, the flame lift a little and subside, a question to which I dipped my own light in reply. 

"Wake me," I said, and she let the burner roll into my hand. 

I screwed it back where it belonged and set the lamp down, but only a single strand came taut.  My mother knelt in the wet and turned the wheel toward herself, away from the sea, the way it stood on the sill when she was waiting for an answer.  A thousand minute loops drew tight under the brass, and the sound they made was like a whale taking a breath a long way off.  In all my weighing I had never thought to ask which way a burden faced. 

When we showed the white web at the wharf next morning the fishermen counted out six hands to trim one wick, and began to laugh until my mother showed them how to read it.  Five to meet the boats at the five little landing stages and bring word of anybody still at sea; one to sit by the lamp and receive the word.  Nobody was to do either thing two nights running, not even her. 

That night she woke me as I had asked, and we lay listening to the slippers of the man on duty scuffing up our steps.  "All in," he called softly through the wood, and in the dark her thumb went still against my wrist.
