After noise became impossible, the winterglass conservatory kept its orchestra on the payroll for one more winter to see what it could do with light.  Mara came up from the ferry office every evening with the tuning fork case tucked under her arm as if there were still a pitch somebody might need.  The fork itself was at the bottom of the strait, along with the ferry and seventeen members of the old string section.  Of the musicians in the capsize only Mara and a silently expressive boy named Lev, the page turner, had come up under the hull together.  She had ridden his narrow back to daylight with the empty leather case still strapped across her palm.  She remembered letting go of the fork and keeping the case, one of those decisions the hand makes without consulting anybody. 

The remaining players wanted to translate silence into music, not into a pantomime of seventeen people drowning, but the town would pay for a memorial or nothing.  Mara thought they might satisfy both parties by heat-mapping emotions onto the glass, which was already fitted with thermal eyes to detect a killing frost among the citrus trees.  With the eyes turned inward a blush went up the wall in bands of rose, and a hand gone cold could turn a whole bay violet.  She arranged the chairs in a scale around the room so that a person could follow a phrase by turning slowly in place. 

For the first rehearsal she asked each player to bring to mind, on her downbeat, somebody who had been aboard.  Nothing climbed the walls until Lev began to glow such a deep, sustained red that Mara gave him the entire last movement.  The title she chalked on his music stand was Gratitude, which was what she felt whenever she looked at his back. 

He read it and took off his jacket and shirt, quite deliberately, in front of all the other players.  Across one shoulder ran a ridge the precise length of the brass hinge on her case; its angry margins were what the glass had magnified.  Then he rubbed out her word with his shirt and put the shirt back on. 

She wanted to rectify the mistake by writing Pain, until she saw his hand waiting beside the chalk to wipe that away too.  Instead she set the case open on his stand and showed him how its lid, swung between his shoulder and the eye, could cut the red short.  He shut it off before she finished demonstrating, let it back in for one beat, then cut it off again.  It was the little impatient figure the lost first violin used to play whenever Mara spent too long tuning the winds.  Three desks away a cellist caught it and answered with the back of her score, and somebody out among the oranges actually doubled over laughing. 

At the memorial the bereaved stood among the players, because there were not enough chairs and because by then almost everybody had something to play.  One old woman could keep her thin gold line going for exactly as long as it used to take her husband to draw a bow.  When she let it go there was a proper rest, of the kind that leaves a space with a shape to it, and nobody rushed in. 

Mara had been saving a name for the piece, Unshaken Marvels, meaning those few precious things a wreck could leave intact.  Now, watching Lev mark time with the very thing that had cut him, she understood how little she would have had to work with if that name were true.  She kept the name, but when the council asked which marvels had come through unshaken she pointed not to the survivors but to the strings.  In all that music not one of them had had to move. 

On the first paid morning of spring rehearsal Lev laid the tuning fork case on her stand, and she pushed it back along the desk to his.  He would have to give the pitch now, she signed, and took her place among the winds to wait for his red on the glass.
