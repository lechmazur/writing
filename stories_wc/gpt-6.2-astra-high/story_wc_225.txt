The groom had been waiting sixteen years for an answer when the exiled chess tutor came back over the hill with a pair of spectacles.  From up there Bess could see the whole marriage party, down to the little boy whose handful of barley hung between his fingers and the earth.  She had carried away a picture of that boy as scattering seed, but she saw now that he had been trying to catch it.  Even in the matter of a handful of grain she had remembered what suited her. 

At the village gate the weather tapestry still hung between its posts, its woolen hills fitted so exactly to the hills behind them that Bess had to feel for the border.  In the old days, during the lull of distant thunder, a shepherd could throw a woven hush over the village and hold a lamb in the instant before it died.  Outside the gate its mother could be milked, a midwife fetched, a clean knife boiled.  It was never meant to hold a bride. 

On the morning of her gate wedding, thirteen-year-old Ida had come to lessons with a question about the service.  If the priest asked whether she came freely, was she allowed to say no?  Bess, who could winnow thirty plausible moves down to two in the time it took to peel an apple, had not even considered saying yes.  She had been so afraid of losing her place at court that she had told the child to play for time.  She had actually shown her how to lift the platinum labyrinth, that little maze of wire through which the border threads ran, and turn it like a key.  She had promised to stand outside the gate and turn it back when she had spoken to the king.  But the king himself had cut the labyrinth free and sent her into exile, and until the royal effects went up for auction last winter Bess had never seen it again. 

Now under the first long roll of thunder she fitted the maze to the ragged ends that had once run through it.  Her hands had always been better company than her thoughts; even waiting for a kettle to sing she would be twisting loose straw into spills for lighting the fire.  All the way here her restless fingers had been teasing apart the tangled fringe of her shawl, and now they did as much for the wet wool.  Through her spectacles she could see what no one had thought to tell a shepherd, because every shepherd knew.  A broken thread kept the shape of the passage it had traveled.  Here was a flat elbow, here a corkscrew curl; by decoding the frayed threads in the tapestry's border she began to see precisely how the child had turned the key.  Not round once, as she had been taught, but round and back a quarter turn.  Bess followed the small fierce bends until the last strand lay in its groove and the wire sat warm in her palm.  She could feel Ida holding it on the other side of the cloth. 

The thunder stopped.  She had imagined bringing news of the king's death, as though that could make the next minute easy, but the groom was alive and so was the priest and so was the habit of obedience.  She wanted to forgive herself; she had come all this way privately hoping the child would do it for her.  Instead of preparing a speech she put her shoulder against the gatepost, where her pupil could see her, and turned the key. 

The barley struck the ground with the sound of a little fist knocking.  Ida let go of the wire. 

"Well," said the priest, not unkindly, and the groom bent his handsome adult head to hear the answer.  Ida looked at Bess, whose old face must have seemed to her one more astonishing provision of the marriage service. 

"You can say no," Bess said. 

"She can speak for herself," said the groom. 

"That's what I mean to wait for," said Bess. 

She stood with all her weight on both feet while the boy picked up his barley grain by grain.  When thunder came again nobody moved to take shelter, and in the quiet that followed it Ida opened her mouth. 

"No, thank you," she said, as if declining a second helping, and stepped out of her shoes and through the gate in her bare feet.  Bess picked up the shoes and carried them, leaving the girl nothing she would have to come back for.
