After blowing the whistle to call my mother to her last watch, I waited until its echo had gone before I opened the door.  At the horizon measurement station we tried not to carry any more noise indoors than was strictly necessary.  Across the fjord a black notch in the hills admitted the January sun for a few minutes each noon.  What interested the astronomers was how much longer the sun lingered there than their tables promised, lifted by the bending of light in cold air. 

My mother and the chief observer each watched through a separate glass and pulled a cord when the last light went.  For eleven days running she had seen it go a second later than he had.  She was to have the pension, but on the printed sheets her observations would end with the previous winter. 

I was the hydraulic clock shepherd, which meant that I kept a flock of three glass vessels fed from a tank of constant depth.  Each cord opened a valve; when I lowered a bar, all three valves shut together.  Through counting backwards from that common stop, allowing one second for each marked division of water, I could tell when each observer had pulled.  The third vessel was a spare, and it was chiefly on account of that spare that I had asked my mother not to sign her papers yet.  Since childhood I had wanted to understand what clocks really measure, and here for once was a chance to ask one a question to which no person knew the answer. 

On the workbench stood our kitchen timepiece with its face removed, an eminently peculiar sight to my mother, who had dusted that face every Saturday of her married life.  I held out the mantle clock key, and from habit she gave the spring its usual six turns before she thought to ask why.  In place of the minute hand I had fitted a black vane that crept across a narrow slit lit from behind by a lamp.  The slit and the vane had razor edges; seen through a glass, the last of the light lay between them like a hair on clear water.  At the instant those edges coincided, a contact on the vane opened the spare valve. 

I had the chief sit down beside my mother and set up a screen to hide everything but the light.  Neither of them minded the first trial, but when I went to wind the vane back for a second the chief asked what was wrong with the first.  Nothing, I said, except that now we all knew how long it took. 

I varied the starting place and let them sit through seven more extinctions while the fjord lay still enough to hold a gull and its white image foot to foot.  Each time the light shrank to a grain I saw the chief squint, as if he could squeeze that grain out of existence.  Each time his vessel held a little more water than the spare, and my mother's a little less.  He was pulling before the light went out; she was pulling after, but by scarcely the width of a pencil stroke on the glass. 

The chief took off his spectacles and breathed on them and did not put them on again for some time.  Finally he said that a lamp was not the sun. 

No, my mother said, and neither of them was the sun either, so perhaps they had better keep all the figures.  She went to her glass for the noon watch without waiting to hear what he would say. 

Over the notch hung a thin flake of gold, its lower edge dissolved in its own reflection on a shelf of ice.  I could not have said when the gold ceased to be gold and became merely a place where I had been looking.  The two cords fell, and I brought the bar down.  On the sheet the chief entered both readings, and after each he put a name, something nobody had thought necessary in sixty winters. 

That evening my mother put the face back on our clock and hung the key on its nail.  When it struck six we were still at the table, picking the last soft bones from our fish, and neither of us hurried to get up.
