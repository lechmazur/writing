After the hitchhiker vanishes, his thumb stays up a moment longer, a little gold hook with nothing left to catch.  Nobody else at the mountain scenic pullout sees him go; they are watching my daughter die for the nine hundredth time.  I am supposed to play through this part. 

Before the fire I was the bridge-song fiddler, paid out of the tolls to send each wagon across with a tune in the measure of its wheels.  Now the museum pays me to do it for light.  On the belvedere wall the bridge assembles itself from a photograph my husband took two days before it burned.  Once the lamps warm up, the people in the picture begin to move, always forward through those two days, always faster as they approach the end.  No one knows why old silver remembers the future, but there are coaches from three countries parked outside. 

My little Bea comes running in her pinafore, and the fiddle under my chin goes as dumb as a piece of furniture.  She stops at the scaffold where the painters have hung their white wraps, looks straight at me, and is gone in a rush of sparks.  The visitors file out past the tin box where they can drop a coin to have me recount what she was like.  I used to say dynamically observant, a phrase her teacher put on a report card because she hated to say fidgety.  Bea could tell you which horse had a pebble in its shoe before you could tell her there was a horse coming. 

Today I close the lid.  The hitchhiker has never vanished before; he usually goes over with a thumb still hopefully extended toward a wagon already full.  I wind the lamps back to morning and watch the cloth instead of the child.  One wrap lifts at its hem, then another, then a third well below the level of the deck, as if somebody were ducking under laundry.  There are stairs there, a secret mapped by scaffold wrap flutters, and at the bottom one square of absolute dark. 

I know that square.  A wet-plate collodion glass flake sits in the locket at my throat, the one piece of my husband's negative that the restorers couldn't prise away from me.  It holds no face, only the end of a plank, but he cut his finger there and under a lens you can see the whorl.  I open the locket in the beam.  The plank appears, and on it, suddenly arrested in the act of climbing back for somebody, stands the hitchhiker.  He has gone gray since breakfast.  I take the flake away and his foot comes down somewhere it has never come down before.  From inside the wall comes a sound no visitor has ever paid for, an old man laughing himself breathless with relief. 

All these years I have watched Bea's hands fly up and thought she was trying to ward off the fire.  For nine hundred shows I have tried to sculpt possibility from the ashes of "never," playing a shade faster each time in hopes the wagons might get across.  I play the three lifting hems as three lifting notes, and when her hands fly up this time I give her the fourth she is asking for.  She catches it with both hands like a rope.  Out of the hole steps a woman about my age with a scorched pinafore folded over her arm.  She puts it down and takes my fiddle, tucks it under the wrong side of her chin as she always did, and plays for the people still on the stairs. 

Later the museum will call what we did pixelated amnesty, and fit each picture in its keeping with a switch that lets one square stay dark.  Nobody in a photograph owes the living an appearance, not even if the living have bought tickets. 

The possibility I had in mind was a small girl running into my arms.  Instead my daughter has a wedding ring, a gap where a back tooth ought to be, a whole repertoire of tunes I never taught her.  When the last painter steps out into the astonished mountain light, I ask the question I used to ask when she came home from school. 

"Anything happen today?" 

She considers this with her father's gravity, then draws up a second chair.
