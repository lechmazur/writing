After the anesthesia wore off, I could tell where my left foot was for the first time in nineteen years.  Without looking under the sheet I drew a circle with my big toe, and felt it close.  The surgeon called me proprioceptively enhanced; I called my daughter and told her I would be at the sentencing.  She said my old boots were still by the door, full of seed packets. 

Our ultraviolet forest grew under glass on a moon where nothing native had ever needed shade.  In daylight the trees looked dusted with flour, but under the night lamps a sick branch shone orange among the violet, and somebody had to walk the rows and chalk the trunks for pruning.  We had brought the trees as cuttings from home; in those days everybody wanted a turn at tending them.  Only after the arrests did the night walk become a punishment, barefoot and with nothing over the eyes. 

The governor who instituted ultraviolet penance was the first man our new court condemned to it.  They gave him one row for every survivor of what his office called enhanced interrogation, and one for each person his questioners had buried.  Eighty rows of pear trees stood between the governor and his bed.  I had asked to speak because I wanted to discover the mathematics of mercy, which was a grand way of saying I wanted to know how much of that walk was mine to give away. 

My daughter brought my boots to the forest and carried the seed packets loose in her apron.  At the first tree the clerk offered me smoked goggles, a courtesy extended to witnesses.  The pewter mask strap made a little sucking noise coming unstuck from the damp lens.  I had to brace both hands against the trunk.  Before I knew what I remembered, my good foot slid sideways and my new foot followed until there was room for another pair between them. 

In the cellar that sound had meant the questioner was putting his mask on, and the woman cuffed to me was about to faint.  I used to catch her sitting down so that neither of us took the whole hurt in the wrists.  Her name was Linn; her husband was standing across the aisle with chalk dust on his funeral coat.  He had come straight from his shift, like half the people there. 

I had meant to ask the court to strike my row from the sentence.  One from eighty, an operation even the governor could have performed in his head.  But there I stood with my feet apart for Linn, who would never be asked whether she wanted her row walked.  I took the goggles to the governor and waited until he put out his hand. 

"All eighty," I told the clerk, "but let him see what he's doing." 

Someone behind me spat into the dead leaves.  The clerk said that with shoes and goggles it would just be a shift, and Linn's husband said it would be nearer six.  He knew because he had been working doubles since she died, with nobody to bring in her wage.  That was where my arithmetic had failed; I had counted what a man could bear and left out what another man was carrying. 

"Six shifts off with pay," the clerk said slowly, writing it down, and looked up at him for consent. 

Linn's husband unhooked the chalk bag from his belt.  He watched while the governor knelt to put his shoes back on, and didn't let go of the bag until both laces were tied tight.  I stayed for the first row to make sure he marked the undersides where the blight began. 

Walking home, I felt a little grit in my left boot, and at our door I tipped a single pear pip into my palm.  My daughter made a hole for it beside the step and I pressed the dirt down with my heel, exactly hard enough.
