The baker had never seen the man who killed my father, but he knew the man's nose well enough to deny his daughter bread.  I had put that nose on every tower of bone in our canyon of endless echoes, painting by lamplight whenever the watch scraped my work away.  For seven years the face of Oren Vale had served the valley as a shorthand for the night he came drunk to the ferry and put a pike pole through my father's chest.  Now his sentence was up, and at sunrise he would come across the water to ask my pardon.  By custom I could give it or refuse it, but either way he would be coming home. 

The evening before the ritual, his girl came to my shop wearing a little false nose of dough.  She had pinched it off a loaf while the baker's back was turned and shaped it into an excellent likeness of his own.  Would I paint it pink so she could go back and buy a bun on the strength of the family resemblance?  She was eight and did not know quite how good a joke she had made. 

I took her to the baker and bought us both supper, which we ate on the steps beneath the enormous nostrils of her father.  Across the water a pale spire in mist marked the landing where mine had died.  The old ferry lamp at its tip had a chipped lens that could refract one flame into a trembling fan of colors.  I had painted the pole and the blood and Oren's face by the light of one fact, namely that my father had gone to work one morning and never come home.  Yet here was a child going hungry in its glare. 

While we ate, the girl made up a rhyme about a baker who baked a new nose every time he sneezed.  A ferryman waiting out the mist contributed a verse in which the baker sneezed himself a wife.  Soon we had a dozen people squatting on the steps, taking turns the way we used to do on my father's boat when fog kept us tied up for hours.  I gave them the line I had painted under every portrait: Here is the man who took my father.  The ferryman found a line to go before it: Here is the boy your father taught to row.  The baker, who had come out wiping flour from his arms, supplied another: Here is the child I sent away with nothing. 

We moved from tower to tower, holding our impromptu poetry sessions wherever there were people sitting up with a lamp.  I wanted what that broken lens had, the power to refract a single truth into many without putting out the flame.  By dawn I had a pocketful of lines, some on paper and some on the backs of my hands. 

The mist lifted and the ferry brought Oren over, a small man whose face I had forgotten was smaller than my own.  When he asked my pardon the hush was like a thing we had hammered out among us in the night, fitted so closely around us that even the canyon found nothing to repeat.  I could have said yes and gone home to sleep with my father's killer still nailed up over every door.  Instead I gave Oren my paint pot and asked him to steady it while I climbed. 

I left the face on the first tower, but underneath I painted a boy rowing badly and a man resting one big hand on his shoulder.  On the next I painted the baker's words, and on the third I had to stop and ask the girl what she would like written there.  She wanted a clean wall for drawing noses on, so we scrubbed the whalebone white. 

At noon Oren asked whether this meant I forgave him, and I said yes, although my father was still dead and I still wanted my seven years of painting to have been right.  One of the old boatmen was already at work on my father's hand, shortening two fingers to the stubs the mooring rope had made of them.  I very nearly shouted at him to leave it alone.  Then there it was, the hand I remembered, that I had never once got right.
