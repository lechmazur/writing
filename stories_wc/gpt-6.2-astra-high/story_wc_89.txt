After the phoenix rose from the stump of the lightning tree, the first apple flower opened, and something with very small teeth began eating its way out of the moon. 

Nell had seen this happen for other people.  As the village beast whisperer, she had stood under those silver branches and told a widower that the badger coming down the ladder of light wanted to smell his boots before it wanted to be held.  It was different when the boots were yours. 

She kept her eyes on the hole in the moon and her hand in her pocket, where the fortune from a cookie lay folded about three grains of cloud cardamom and a single thread of comet saffron.  She had sold their bed for those spices.  The bed was too wide anyway. 

Down the light came a hare, black except for one white forepaw, which it shook as if trying to get a cuff over a wrist.  Nell knew that movement; for thirty years she had done up the last button for him. 

"Hello, Kit," she said. 

The hare sat on her shadow.  All around them the apple trees were blooming under the permanent moonlight of the orchard, and beyond its wall the sun was coming up on a world where she meant to button his cuff again. 

She took the little packet out.  In life Kit had made a habit of adding the words "if you insist" to whatever prophecy came out of a cookie.  This one, from the cheap supper they had eaten on their wedding night, read YOU WILL HAVE YOUR HEART'S DESIRE.  His penciled amendment was almost too faint to see. 

Nell opened the paper and crushed the spices together with her thumb.  With their scent it was possible to conjure the body in which a visitor from the moon had once been loved, and if the visitor ate an apple in that body it would hold.  An apple began to swell on the branch above her. 

The hare stood up.  Through its thin hide she saw the two long bones of a man's forearm turning slowly into place, like the hands on a clock that had finally been mended.  His mouth came back before his voice did.  She reached for the apple, ripe now and heavy, and held it to his teeth. 

He did not bite. 

Under the racing pulse in his bare throat she could feel a second beat, small and paradoxically steady, that she had felt in the chest of every rabbit ever brought to her in a snare.  It was the beat that came when a creature had finished struggling and was saving what was left of itself. 

She set the apple down.  "Is it the dying again?" she asked. 

He shook his head and looked past her, up the ladder.  At the ragged hole two leverets were waiting, one washing the other's ear with little businesslike strokes.  An entire winter had passed on earth since Kit had died, and it had never occurred to her that time might be passing for him too. 

She folded the paper shut.  Already his fingers were drawing together, but he caught her wrist with his good hand and brought her thumb, all yellow with saffron, to his nose.  Then he rubbed that thumb along the edge of the paper and nudged the packet back into her pocket.  She knew what a creature meant when it marked a thing with scent, but she had never heard of scent surviving the journey between the worlds. 

"You could find me by that," she said. 

The long ears tipped forward. 

At home she put the fortune on the sill, weighted with the one button she had left in her sewing basket that matched his wedding coat.  She knew better than to expect another visit; in all her years of going to the orchard she had never seen the same creature come down twice.  But that night, across a distance it took light itself a little while to cross, three noses made three clear patches in the frost upon her window.  She breathed on the glass beside them until there were four.
