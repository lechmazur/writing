At dawn the hum of streetlamps went out of the wires, and Nell could hear the woman at Canal breathing again.  By dusk, when the lamps came on, there would be nobody left in the subway utility tunnel to listen.  The city was keeping the tiled booths on the platforms, on account of their beauty, and taking out the phones.  As the last underground phone booth operator, Nell had been issued a screwdriver and twelve little cards that said HISTORIC RECEIVER LOCATION. 

"Before you do me," the woman said, "will you get me the man who boils an egg?" 

Nell knew the man, though not the egg, which was presumably something the two of them had discussed in private.  He collected the station clocks for repair on Tuesdays; he would be standing at Pearl now with a clock the size of a washbasin between his shoes.  Once he had tried to call his wife from there and got the woman at Canal instead.  Nell could have fixed the crossed pair, but in those first months after the shipyard closed, half the numbers people asked for belonged to empty rooms.  Through wrong number friendships the booths had acquired regulars again, people who would pass one another on the stairs without so much as looking up.  Nell tended this cultivated estrangement as carefully as her father had once tended the connections, never letting a name slip, never asking what the egg was for. 

She plugged Canal into Pearl and put her finger to the lip of the glass harmonica disc mounted on her father's little hand crank.  A single wet turn sent a clear singing note down both lines, his way of announcing that the operator was leaving two people alone.  Even after she took off her headset the tunnel remained melodically haunted; her screwdriver striking a pipe seemed to finish the phrase the glass had begun. 

By noon she had unscrewed eight receivers and eaten her sandwich sitting on their canvas sack, which had the hard shifting feel of a bag of knees.  When she got back to the board the two lamps were lit again.  She cut in and found herself the subject of a small, civil argument. 

"You ask," the clock man said, and the woman said she had already done the asking once today. 

They wanted to know how to make the note.  At seven that evening, without exchanging names or addresses or pledging anything whatever about tomorrow, they proposed to sit down in their respective kitchens to a soft egg.  The note would be their grace, though neither of them used that word. 

Nell was about to offer one of them the disc when it occurred to her what a useless thing a single heirloom was to people determined to stay apart.  Instead she took two thin tea glasses from the shelf, a shelf that had held six when her father employed a full shift of night operators.  At Canal she taught the woman to loop a damp finger round the rim, adding water from the drinking fountain until the pitch came true against the disc.  At Pearl the man caught on quicker but made her scratch a line at the water level with his diamond file. 

"In case I'm thirsty," he said. 

When he lifted his clock she saw the dark ring it had left in the dust between his shoes, and the sight of that ridiculous little zero nearly undid her.  All afternoon she had been trying to teach endings how to begin, as if it were a matter of handing out the right equipment.  Yet she had made no provision for the person who at seven would ordinarily be hanging up a headset and putting the kettle on. 

She was tightening the last brass screw at dusk when the hum of streetlamps returned, this time through the iron under her hand, since the wires were quite dead.  On her way home she bought an egg.  She ate it standing up because she had forgotten to buy bread and the yolk was running, and only afterward remembered to sound the glass.  Somewhere two people might already be washing up, but for the first time in her life she could take her finger off the turning disc and stay on the line.
