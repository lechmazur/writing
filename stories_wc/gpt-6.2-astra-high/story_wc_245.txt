The first thing Nel wanted when her mother died was to sleep until noon, and the second was for nobody ever to learn the first.  At the funeral her brother Ivo reached for her hand, as he had done as a child, and got a fistful of her coat instead.  Nel absorbed languages through her skin, and that morning she had already taken in six different ways of saying that a light had gone out.  She could have consoled anybody in the room except the boy who had emptied the basin while she changed the sheets. 

That evening he set their mother's platinum compass ring in her palm, with a strip of bark threaded through it like a finger.  Being blind had never kept Nel from making maps; she laid them out in raised lines of wax, and she knew their mother's hand in this one by the little blobs at the turns.  Only this time the wax rose and fell like the teeth of a key. 

"She said it was the way to the stillpoint," Ivo said.  "She made me promise to take you if you started talking like the visitors." 

Nel almost said what a comfort it was to have him, which settled the matter. 

Above the ultrasonic forest the constellations shifted every night, slowly dragging the crowns of the trees after them like iron filings after a magnet.  Between the setting of one pattern and the rising of the next came an hour when even the smallest twig held its pitch.  Whatever ground they meant to cover they must cover across that pause, or the directions under Nel's thumb would be so much cold fat. 

At the wood's edge she put on the ring and began to dowse, sweeping her fist low over the beds of fallen needles.  The platinum had outlasted three steel bands eaten away by the sour resin; inside it a loose tongue beat the trees' high singing down to notes her bones could hear.  She matched the first bump of wax to a deep C that ran along a root and crossed the stream without getting her ankles wet.  Ivo followed with one finger hooked in her belt, an old arrangement that spared them both the indignity of being led. 

From C the trail went up a third, then down a fifth, until Nel began to hear, behind the separate notes, the melody their mother used to whistle through her teeth while cutting their hair.  Always she had stopped in the same place to blow the ticklish clippings off their necks.  Nel's thumb came to bare bark where the breath should be.  She kept hunting for the next note until Ivo let go of her belt and blew gently on her nape. 

Then she understood and turned her fist, not toward the singing but into the narrow interval where the ring hung utterly still.  One step took them into a pocket of warm air hardly bigger than the space under a kitchen table.  She touched the bark at her shoulder and not a syllable entered her skin.  Everywhere else the forest had something to say about enduring or flourishing or letting dead things fall; here for once she would have to find the words herself. 

"I wanted to sleep," she said, and heard how little of the truth that was.  "Sometimes when she rang for us I lay there and let you get up." 

Ivo sat down on her foot, the way he used to when there was only one dry stone to sit on.  "I know," he said. 

She waited for absolution, and in its absence felt the first small live coal of something better: he was going to tell her what it had cost. 

"I could hear you taking care not to breathe too loud," he said.  "That was worse than the basin." 

"I should have come and sat on the other side of the bed," she said. 

"There was room," he said. 

Nel pulled off the ring and took his hand; there was no country in his palm she didn't know the language of, and still she had to listen.  Outside, the stars got under way again, and the tune that had brought them in went to pieces in the branches.  By the time he finished, it was a wood she had never mapped.  She asked him if he wanted the ring to find their way home, and felt his fingers close around it before he said yes.
