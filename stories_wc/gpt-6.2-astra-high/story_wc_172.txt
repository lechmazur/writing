On the salt flat my father bought, there were seven villages and one word for thirst.  My father gave each village a school and took, in exchange, its share of the river.  The children learned to write their names in dust on the blackboards, which saved him the price of chalk.  He sold the water to the copper works on the other side of the ridge.  That summer even the rain that fell in the hills went through his pipes. 

When distant thunder began its long afternoon muttering, he took me out on the flat with a recording machine and a parcel wrapped in one of my mother's slips.  She had been dead eleven years; I recognized the little scorch from an iron over the heart.  Inside was the egg of a ladder teal, greenish where the silk had kept off the light.  Somebody had blown it through two holes, one round and one shaped like a thumbnail.  Not somebody, of course.  My mother could blow across those holes and bring a flock down so fast the water stood up in white stitches where their feet went in.  She had given up hunting when there were six left.  Six proved to be as good as none. 

Father wanted the call for the schoolchildren, who knew the teal only as the little blue ladder printed on their exercise books.  At home he could get a serviceable squeal from the egg, but what he wanted to preserve was the echo, the tumbling reply that used to come back from under the salt.  He had offered a year's wages for the place where Mother used to stand.  Nobody would show him; there were kinds of work, it seemed, that he had made too expensive even for himself. 

I found it by the map the cracks had drawn in the dried riverbeds.  Up on his truck roof I could see what was invisible on foot: little scales of mud down the middle of each channel, great plates along the margins.  Where seven ribbons of fine cracking converged I sent him digging, and his shovel rang on the brick lip of the old water divider.  Under our feet seven vaulted culverts ran away into the glare, one to each village.  Here the women had called the ducks before there were guns, before a girl with a good ear had married a man with good credit. 

We waited through a roll of thunder that seemed to have got snagged on the ridge, and in the lull he lifted the egg.  What came out of it was small and exact and went away down the brick throats and did not come back.  He tried until a spot of blood appeared on the shell. 

From the nearest culvert a woman said, quite distinctly, "You can do it till you burst, Emil." 

It was my aunt at the far end, half a mile away.  Every afternoon she took a tin chair to the village gate to sit in the cool draft from the bricks. 

"Did you think it was the bricks answering her?" she said.  "It was us telling the next lot on that the ducks were coming." 

Father put the egg away, then took it out again and cleaned the blood off with his tongue.  "I can pay," he said into the dark. 

"We know," said my aunt. 

Behind us on the ridge the copper works shone like a second, more dependable sun.  He walked to the truck and on the mine radio ordered the release of the afternoon's water into the old course.  One shift, he specified, to be charged against the schools' music allowance.  He was still himself. 

It took an hour for the first brown tongue to reach us, pushing a raft of beetle shells ahead of it.  By then the mouths of the culverts were full of children fetching buckets, and the thunder had gone quiet again.  Father blew; my aunt answered; a boy behind her broke on the high note, and a girl somewhere took it up without a break.  In my headphones I could hear them learning how to be a flock. 

He made me play it back twice on the drive home.  Both times, just before the children came in, he leaned close to the little grille as if somebody in there were drawing breath.
