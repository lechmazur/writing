The afternoon both moons lined up with the sun, the sea backed out of the glass district and let the old bell over the greenhouse door ring once.  Mara had heard that bell in other people's mouths for thirty years.  She was a chef who could taste memories, and whenever one of the surviving glassblowers made a pot of soup for the kitchen crew, there it was in the first spoonful: a little cracked note that meant dinner. 

Today she had brought the last glassblower with her, and he stood outside the rusted greenhouse arguing with a boy from the Office of Recovery.  "Anything we lift goes into the collection," the boy said, for perhaps the fourth time.  "Then you'd better not lift me," said Lev, who had fallen twice on the walk down.  Mara put a heel of bread in each man's hand and got them through the door chewing instead of talking.  The alignment would not come again in any of their lifetimes; they had until the bread was gone, and a little after that. 

Nothing remained of the knowledge fruit orchard except a row of black stumps in copper tubs.  Once, an apprentice would eat a slice of the pale flesh before taking up the blowing iron; the fruit worked through synaptic plasticity, making an afternoon's practice take hold like a season's.  Without it, nobody had been willing to pay for the years it took a pair of hands to stop spoiling glass.  Lev had spent the last thirty years cutting windowpanes blown by nobody, flat as water in a tray. 

"My wife saved one knowledge fruit seed," he said.  He had been saying this for years, but some days his wife was still alive and some days he was late for school.  Mara had come because on quite ordinary Tuesdays his cabbage broth tasted of a woman slipping a brown pip into a green glass tube and pinching the hot end shut with tongs. 

"She'd just been out here to prune," he said, running his fingers along the knuckles of the nearest stump.  The other trees had been cut low and even, but this one had a fork left standing, exactly the height for hanging a coat or an apron.  Down in the tub, where the apron would have rotted, the boy found three bone buttons and the tube.  He wrapped all four in cotton before Mara could say a word.  Water was coming in under the door again, ringing the bell with every swell. 

On their way back up the hill Lev asked the boy what they would put on the label.  "Well, what did your wife call the piece?" 

"Insurance," Lev said, and the boy carefully wrote that down. 

Mara had meant to steal the tube, but she couldn't take it from a boy who had buttoned an old man's coat for him with fingers still cold from digging.  She took them into her kitchen instead and cleared a space on the sill between the rosemary and the little furnace where she kept sugar soft for pulling.  "How big is this collection of yours?" she asked. 

"Two cabinets and the room they haven't finished painting." 

"Better start a branch." 

The boy considered the sill, which looked out on the bus stop, where half the town would have to see whatever went on it every morning.  He pasted his numbered label on a chipped blue mixing bowl, and Lev cracked the tube against its rim. 

It took four summers to get a pear no bigger than a baby's fist, and by then Lev needed both hands to lift a cup.  But he could still tell them when to turn the iron, and Mara could shave a pear so thin you could see daylight through thirty slices.  She had kept the broken green tube for this day, wanting something beautiful to come of it besides the tidy line of writing on the bowl.  Mixed with clear cullet in the new furnace, it gave the glass just the color you see looking up through shallow water at the sun.  The first bell they blew from it had a little catch in its voice; the second rang true.  They sent the second to the collection.  When the first rang over Mara's kitchen door, Lev got up without being told and washed his hands for dinner.
