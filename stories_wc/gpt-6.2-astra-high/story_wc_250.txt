On the cobalt dock after rain, with the blue paint shining like a fresh bruise, Nell set down her instrument case.  "I need a room where my husband can't find us," she told the silent canal guardian, "because last night he put his hands on our girl the way he puts them on..." 

Darkness came up through the wet boards and caught the word before it could leave her mouth.  This was an old courtesy of darkness, which knew what happened to women whose secrets traveled faster than their feet.  It drew the three of them under the dock into the little wedge of time that opens within the edge of a half-spoken secret.  Above their heads a drop hung from a nail, fat enough to fall and unable to do it.  In the hollow beneath the boards there was room enough for Nell to crouch beside her daughter, but not enough to stand or to go anywhere at all. 

"You see my difficulty," darkness said to the guardian, who had met it here before.  "I can keep them safe as long as she doesn't finish, but her little one is going to get cold." 

The guardian unwound her scarf and passed it to the child without fussing with the collar of her coat, which somebody had already buttoned so hard the top button was through two holes.  Then she held out her hand to Nell, not palm up for a payment but curled as if she too had hold of an instrument.  Nell had filled the guardian's molars for twenty years; she recognized the precise crook of that finger and thumb.  She opened the case and took out her dental mirror.  It occurred to her that not once in twenty years had she given this woman a way to say stop except by taking hold of her wrist. 

The guardian laid the short steel handle in the child's hand and showed her how to lift it through a knothole.  In the round glass, no bigger than the penny her father gave her after each bad night, the girl saw the upper windows of the canal. 

"Oh, don't let anybody look down here," Nell whispered. 

The guardian shook her head and opened a box set into the underside of the dock.  Inside lay the ends of the old flood wires, by which she could once wake a whole street of sleepers by rattling their shutters.  Years before, when she had first set out to teach darkness the value of stars, she had asked the people along the canal to leave those wires in place.  Now she began to weave, taking a strand over one finger and under the next, creating patterns with shutters as far as the bend.  In the little glass the lit slits came and went, spiralingly calm, each circle of answering windows smaller than the last as houses without a spare bed dropped out.  At length just three squares of lamplight hung in the glass like stars, and in one of them a woman lifted a quilt to warm before a stove. 

"But now they know," darkness said, and Nell felt the wet boards press together overhead. 

The girl, who knew a thing or two about keeping other people's secrets, understood before her mother did.  "They know to warm a bed," she said, "but they don't know who it's for." 

Darkness had supposed that every light was a searchlight, because those were the lights men brought when they came to drag the canal.  Now it looked again at the quilt, which had yellow hens on it, and saw that nobody was even standing at the window anymore.  It withdrew from the boards just far enough to let the drop fall on the lid of Nell's case. 

The three of them stood on the dock again, and Nell closed her mouth over the word that was still hers to give or keep.  She wanted the house with the quilt, but before she took so much as a step she held out her wrist to her daughter.  "Give it a squeeze if you ever want me to stop," she said, and waited until she felt the small experimental pressure before she started to walk.  Darkness went with them, keeping the blue boards dark behind their heels and leaving the three good stars alone.
