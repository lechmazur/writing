On the drifting prism terrace, where the glass threw six pale suns across the floor, two women were waiting to claim me.  Each had brought a kitchen stool and was sitting on it with her hands folded, as if this were the audition for my mother. 

I was the corn maze architect's lost child, famous because everybody enjoyed the idea of a woman who designed bewilderment and then misplaced her daughter.  In fact I had got lost at the dentist's, looking for the toilet, and flood wardens put me aboard the plane without her.  Nobody put that on the postcards. 

After the butterfly effect there were two equally solid versions of our valley and exactly one of the dry country beyond it, where the plane had put me down.  For seven years the terrace had been drifting down the seam, admitting relatives from either bank at towns that still had the same name.  Now it was our town's turn. 

The rule was that nobody could be assigned to a household, but you could step across its sill of your own accord.  At seventeen I had crossed enough official thresholds to want to discover why some thresholds feel sacred and others merely make you take your hat off.  Here were two strips of brass set into glass, and I couldn't lift a foot. 

"Take your time, chick," one woman said.  The other's mouth shut on the words. 

Both stools were ash wood from our farm, where analyzing the grain in wooden furniture had been our rainy day game.  We used to intuit what a tree had lived through by following the lines back to the hurt around which they swerved.  Under each woman's knee the same black river divided around the same little drowned face of a nail.  They had brought the stools because each thought a stranger could learn what to call me, but no stranger could grow that tree again. 

"There must be another girl," said the one who hadn't called me chick. 

Out came the evidence, in identical oilskin wrappers.  Each had borrowed the pilot's logbook from the town archive to prove that the plane had returned empty to her particular valley after delivering me.  Each return entry ended with FG, the pilot's weather code for fog. 

"That's when he said it must have happened," they said, almost together. 

All these years they had thought there were two planes landing in two fogs, two girls in the dry country to be found if only people would keep looking.  I put the books side by side as we used to do with boards from a single trunk.  In each, the G trailed a little forest of blue where a wet cuff must have brushed the ink.  Even the last fork of the last twig was there twice. 

"He was already back," I said.  "There was only ever one trip." 

Somewhere below us a spoon fell and went spinning a long time on its rim.  The first woman picked a feather of ash from the split seat of her stool and set it upright between her lips, a habit I had forgotten so completely that seeing it was like being kissed. 

"Then which of us gets you?" she asked. 

A maze has necessary distances, she used to tell the men who wanted to save seed by planting its paths narrower.  Two people must be able to pass without one turning sideways to let the other live.  I could hear her saying it in both kitchens. 

I stepped back far enough to make room on my side of the brass.  "I've got a place out there," I said.  "It's a single room, and if you come we'll need both stools." 

They rose with that featherly determined motion of hens getting up onto an impossibly high roost, first one, then the other.  But neither crossed until I put out my hands. 

So that was what made the difference: not the brass, nor even what you stood to lose by stepping over it.  It was that little width of floor across which nobody was going to reach and pull.
