When the storm came close enough to silver the undersides of the oak leaves, the path took Nell's brother by the feet.  He was walking uphill with the other children when the turf rolled under his boots like a tongue and deposited him on the track that led down.  He did not cry until his friends disappeared over the ridge.  That was how it had been with their mother, except that nobody had gone after her. 

Nell caught up with him where a curtain of vines hung from a concrete lip in the hillside.  The forest had taken back the county so completely that a person could stand on the roof of the flood bunker and think the whole world wild.  Kit had a hand on the latch, tapping the little tune their mother had used for getting a sticky lock to turn.  Three brisk knocks and one absurdly dainty one, precisely playful even with the skin peeling off his knuckles.  Something inside tapped back. 

The old women called Nell a seer, but her foresight was hindsight only; every disaster she had ever predicted had already happened to somebody.  She got these histories by sleeping against things that had outlasted their owners, and generally woke with a stiff neck and no idea whose grief she had acquired.  Now she needed to know why the high path had accepted twenty children and spat out one.  She told Kit to keep knocking and laid her cheek against the door. 

She dreamed of a woman at a kitchen table teaching a little boy to sign his name in time rather than ink.  She dreamed of a hill that opened for that boy grown tall, and of his wet coat steaming on the back of a chair.  Then came a long succession of coats, each smaller than the last, until the sleeves of one hung barely halfway down the chair back. 

She woke with grit between her teeth and Kit beside her on the threshold of an open door.  Inside, a ledger lay chained to a desk with a child's stool pushed under it.  Each page bore a name, a pattern of dots, and a print in mud left by a bare heel.  On the last page was the long narrow heel that had rubbed a hole in every stocking their mother owned. 

Nell looked at Kit's boots; even standing still he shifted his weight in threes and gave a little skip on four.  It was not blood the paths recognized, or bravery, or anything a child could help.  The keepers had once taught their apprentices a walking song so the forest could find a trained pair of hands in a fleeing crowd.  The song had lasted longer than the teaching. 

Above the desk, under glass, the directive that governed the tracks was decaying from the edges inward, its green script threaded with the same white rootlets that lifted the turf.  At the center Nell could still read BRING THE KEEPER; of the qualifications on either side nothing remained but pale nibbled lace.  A red wafer seal at the bottom bore four dents, the last so light it scarcely showed.  Beyond the desk a sluice wheel shook in its socket, waiting for a child to do a dead woman's work.  Kit put his weight on it and spun with it like a beetle caught on a spoke. 

In the dream the coat had been steaming, Nell remembered, and the boy had come in wet to a fire already lit.  She pressed her temple to the glass over the seal and slept again, looking this time past the boy the road had chosen.  He was beating on a drum whose hide was red and dented, and up the hill a hundred shovels fell ringing from their hooks. 

She lifted the glass and hit the seal three times hard and once as if flicking a crumb off Kit's nose.  The sound went out through the roots, and on the ridge every boot began to knock.  Not one turned downhill until Nell called into the trembling wafer that they needed six strong backs and a length of pipe to fit over a spoke of the wheel.  Eight people came, one shouldering the iron barrel of a dismantled pump.  When the flood struck, the wheel was turning, and all Kit had to do was mind the coats. 

Afterward they tore the order loose and fed it piecemeal to the roots, saving only the red seal.  A call for hands could transcend the reach of any track; Nell heard it answered that winter with bread, with blankets, once with a fiddle for a wake.  Nobody had to go, and somebody always went.
