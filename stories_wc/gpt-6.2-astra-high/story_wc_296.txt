At the inquiry the mirror showed Lieutenant Vale opening his right hand to let a black seed fall from it.  That was where the magic trick failed, although of the six hundred soldiers watching, not one stirred.  It was Vale's mother who stood up and quietly asked the mirrorwright to unbend those fingers again.  She had splinted them herself after a cart went over them, and they had set around the curve of an egg.  The mirrorwright put out his lamp. 

On the opposite shore the bereaved companies lowered their white inquiry flags to half mast, the agreed signal for an hour's recess.  By noon either the town would accept responsibility for the orchard or the guns would speak. 

Vale's mother, Siv, was the town druid, and a woman so guarded that even her son had called her Druid in public.  She found lost things for a living, rings mostly, and once the little brass weight a grocer shaved to rob the people who trusted him.  She had never offered to find her son. 

Now she took the second flesh-eating plant seed from its niche in the armory and walked down to the ice.  She was not going to clear his name, although she let the cheering townspeople think so.  She wanted to discover what mirrors don't show, what a hand feels like from the inside as it lets something go. 

She searched as she always did, through expanding circles, beginning at the nail where the old pontoon bridge had been made fast.  Each time she crossed her own outbound track she paid out another fathom of cord from the nail.  Under the clear ice the drowned orchard revolved with the slow precision of troops wheeling on a parade ground.  At the heart of the cursed lake the trees went round so fast their white roots made a single rope.  The guns on either shore tracked her without haste. 

On the seventh circuit the seed bit down on the web of her thumb.  Its husk always split in the direction of the nearest wound; the druids had bred it to clean dead flesh out of living men.  They had never meant one to fall into water where a whole company was bleeding. 

In the ice below her boots a red line ran straight into the swirling orchard vortex.  She followed it on her belly, pushing the seed before her like a compass, until she lay above the first green shoot.  There, caught in the fork of a root, was her son's brass speaking horn.  In the old campaigns a runner would carry one of those horns full of a captain's breath and let the order out a mile away. 

Her little knife could not cut through a foot of ice, but the ice itself was laced with veins where the orchard fed.  She pressed the split seed to the red line and watched a round black mouth open all the way down to the horn.  She hooked it out with her cord before the lake could grow another tree. 

Back on shore she peeled the sated seed off the ice and locked it in its box.  The two banks waited while she warmed the horn inside her coat, against the breast where his head used to fit.  Then she put her mouth to the narrow end and blew his frozen confession out across the water. 

"I put it in the drummer's leg.  He was only a little fellow and I thought I could lift him off the bridge before the roots went through.  His coat was blue, if his people come asking.  I don't know his name." 

For a while the only sound was the horn ticking as its metal cooled.  Across the lake a man laid down his rifle and tapped a roll on his belt buckle with two sticks.  It was taken up on her bank by boys whose wrists were still too thin for their cuffs. 

Siv signed the paper accepting the town's debt, and beneath her name she wrote the uses to which she herself had trained the boy.  Then she went back onto the ice with a longer cord.  There were mothers on both banks now, holding nails for her to start from, and not one of them asked her to find a ring.
