At the ember nexus at twilight, the seven furnace canals gave back just enough light for me to take one portrait before the locks went dark.  The subject was supposed to be the crowd on the chain ferry, gathered for the scattering of my father's ashes.  My mother had made it as far as the landing before her bad hip locked, and now she sat in the ticket shed with a hot brick on her lap.  I had promised to bring the deck back to her. 

This is the sort of promise you make without thinking when you are the family's pocket-sun alchemist.  Half my living came from putting absent grandmothers at wedding suppers, complete down to the butter melting on the potatoes.  Yet between the time my friend Em brought her lips to my ear and the time she took them away, I had to learn my trade again. 

"You know that stone up on the ridge," she whispered, "the one for the woman who went into the water with her little boy?" 

I knew it because my father used to lay two ferry tokens there every winter when the canal froze over.  The little boy had been just my age.  My thumb was already rolling the sun out of its leather purse; the deck came up in miniature on my palm, a hundred faces the size of lentils. 

"Well, that's my stone, honey, and the tall lad with your father's cap on is my boy, and his father still comes down to put flowers there." 

She had hold of my elbow rather than my wrist, the way you catch someone carrying a brimming cup without making them spill it.  Somebody at the bow lifted the lid of the ash tin.  I could feel Em taking the small careful breath that meant there was worse to come.  Portrait suns were little gluttons for detail, and my mother, proud of a turnout like this, would set hers burning in the ticket window for anybody to see. 

My father had called me scientifically romantic, meaning I could measure to half a grain the weight it took to make a lover's footstep distinguishable on a stair.  What I wanted, even before Em whispered, was to capture the density of presence, the actual heft of all these people who had got out of bed and come.  Under my boots the old deck dipped as the crowd made room at the rail for the tall lad.  There it was, the thing my mother had missed when her hip gave out: not the sight of a face but the feel of everybody leaning. 

"And we aren't the only ones he took across," Em breathed against my ear, as I tipped the little globe into the hollow brass chain guide. 

Three twists of mineral wick trailed from its base, my father's jokingly named palette of resolve: red for a grip, ocher for a shift of weight, blue for a release.  When he was teaching green hands to bring the ferry in, we used those colors to show them what their bodies already knew about a moving deck.  They had never seemed worth much beside a sun that could give you back your wedding day.  Now I had a second in which to revalue them, and an instrument ready to hand.  The chain guide had been shrieking all summer until Em's boy fitted it with a nautilus shell columella curl, a spiral of pearl worn slicker than any grease.  Light sent down the guide along that curl could get out only by returning differently, its direction reversed and its image rubbed away to a sheen. 

I jammed the wicks against the brass as the boy upended the tin.  A whole deckful of hands tightened on the rail, the weight swung forward, and then, as the ash touched water, everybody let go.  My palm caught the sun on its way out.  Nothing in it now that anyone could put a name to, but lay your hand underneath and you stood on those planks and felt a sorrow big enough to tip a boat. 

Em was whispering the last of her secret, so low it might have been the tick of the chain settling back into its groove.  "Your mother," she finished, "always put a hot brick under the blanket in the bottom of the boat." 

I had meant to show my mother how many people loved my father; instead I would give her a place among the people who loved her too.
