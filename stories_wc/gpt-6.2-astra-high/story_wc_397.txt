On the clinic form my mother gave her occupation as euthanasia tourist, and when I crossed it out she made me write it back.  She had been a court stenographer for thirty years and knew the difference between what a person said and what somebody thought would look better on paper.  After the operation she woke up changed: she could still take down a hundred words a minute but might spend half an hour getting out four of her own.  She had brought the silver tip of an heirloom lace parasol to knock against the table when she was finished speaking.  Her grandmother had used the whole parasol that way, to summon a waiter to collect the empty oyster shells.  The rest of it had rotted, but the little acorn still made a noise that could get you service. 

The sea came into the drift-ice marina the night before her first appointment, lifting twelve floating houses clean off their pilings and setting them down like cups in a sink.  The clinic became a dormitory; we got beds by the pharmacy hatch.  By morning Mother had talked herself into a job at the hatch taking requests for the boat crews, who would trawl for whatever the residents most wanted back.  I thought she had volunteered out of pride, to earn our keep, but on the margin of her first list she had typed a private note.  Trying to learn the sound of approaching endings.  She meant the interviews that lay ahead, when they would ask if she understood what she was asking for, and I would have to sit on my hands and let her answer.  She meant me to read it. 

Three mornings running a man in borrowed slippers came to the hatch and said, very distinctly, "Yellow kitchen."  The crew brought him a colander, a drawer full of swollen wooden spoons, and finally a yellow enamel pot with two sweet potatoes still buttoned up inside.  He accepted everything and sat beside the hatch until it closed, his slippers making dark prints wherever he put his feet.  On the fourth morning Mother pushed the silver acorn across to him.  She showed him how to rap it when he was finished and then folded her hands.  The wind shouldered the canvas over the broken hatch window into the room and let it out again.  I came to think of that pocket of moving air as a fold in patience, a place where you could put the unbearable business of doing nothing without having to set it down for good. 

"My wife," he said at last, "grips reality loosely."  Those were his words, and Mother took them down without improving them.  In the dormitory his wife woke up every hour and got dressed to go home, but for twenty years, even in the worst of her illness, she had known the yellow of their kitchen.  He wanted a piece big enough to hang beside her bed.  He struck the acorn against the counter and the crew chief, who had been waiting behind him with his wool cap full of salvaged teaspoons, turned around and went back out.  They couldn't trawl for a color, but they could walk the rim of grounded ice where the houses had come apart.  By supper there was a cupboard door the color of egg yolk hanging on two lengths of gauze from the pharmacy rail.  That night his wife got up twice instead of seven times, and once it was only to pee. 

On Friday the doctor cleared a room for interviews and I sat with Mother at another borrowed table.  He asked whether the flood had changed anything for her.  She said yes and he started making a note; the acorn lay silent on the wood.  I put my hand over his moving pen.  She wanted the man in the slippers to keep the yellow door when the clinic went back to being a clinic, she said; somebody ought to make sure it went with him.  As for herself, she had come a long way and would like to finish what she had come to say.  The doctor turned his paper over to give her a clean page.  Outside, in the thawing marina, the wind went from door to door trying the latches, and this time I heard the small silver knock above it without once having to listen for it.
