When the tile factory shut down, the first rain in forty years fell on the town without smelling of hot pennies.  Nell Vey took three jars of it, one from the schoolyard, one from the station steps, one from the fresh scar where a kiln had been lifted off its bed.  She collected petrichor by laying squares of clean linen on dry ground just before a shower and sealing them up the moment they darkened.  The town considered her sensibly eccentric: she could tell by the smell of a cellar wall whether to send for a roofer or a man with a spade. 

Only the kiln jar baffled her.  Under the clay was a smell she knew from the cuffs of her father's trousers, though he had been dead thirty years.  He had been the only one to walk out of the expedition that went under the limestone cliffs to look for water for the kilns.  All his life afterward he had kept trying to tell Nell about a particular blue, using things that weren't it.  Not the milk jug, not the jay.  She had become a collector partly to find the place where explanations fail, where he had run out of things to point at and had simply held her hand. 

She went back with a trowel to delve in the wet clay, and struck brass before she had gone a finger deep.  She knew the lost expedition compass from the little crescent filed in its lid to accommodate the thumb her father had broken as a boy.  If it had come home with him, why had he buried it? 

Nell could do a psychometry reading on most things that had been much handled, though what came through was generally no more interesting than the sweat on a hatband.  Hinged things were different.  Open a locket and the person who had last shut it woke to you as you woke to them; her father, who had taught her, called these meetings hinged awakenings.  He had warned her that you got only one, since the next hand to close the hinge would be your own.  She put the compass unopened in her pocket and waited for rain.  If he pointed at something this time, she meant to be there to see. 

When the shower came, she followed the smell from the kiln bed to a drain newly exposed by the demolition crew.  A trickle ran out of it, against the direction on the factory plans, carrying pale shreds of a weed that had dried to lace on her father's cuffs.  She took the broad service stairs alongside the drain, down through courses of sweating brick until the brick gave way to limestone and the stairs to a beach of white grit.  The lagoon went on under the cliffs as far as her lamp could see.  Not a drop ticked from the roof; all the rain coming down through that enormous thickness of stone came quietly, as if through folded linen. 

She opened the compass and her father's hand came up under hers, cold enough to hurt.  Beside him two men lay on the grit with their eyes closed, and a third, very young, lay with his eyes open.  Her father had been holding the compass to the boy's lips to see if the glass would cloud.  The boy said, quite distinctly, that he could smell school letting out.  She had brought her three jars to check her bearings, and now she unscrewed the schoolyard lid and put the linen into his hand. 

"Rain," he said, and after a while his fingers went slack. 

Across the black lagoon spread the high blue windows of the school, each pane ajar for summer, each propping open its own square of sky.  Beneath them children were pouring out into the wet yard and one of them was the boy, running so fast his satchel banged his knees.  Her father made no move to follow him; he sat perfectly still until the last blue pane swung shut.  Then he looked at Nell, and by the little tug he gave her hand she knew he knew her.  She told him where to leave the compass, under the bed of the kiln they hadn't built yet, and let the lid fall. 

Back on the service stairs she screwed the cap onto the empty jar, a lifetime's habit, then stopped and took it off again.
