After the string broke, our sky gondola went drifting east with the mayor still holding the ceremonial scissors.  It was only packing string, meant to give him something to cut once the ground crew slipped the real mooring, but he stood looking at the blades as though they had failed him personally.  Below us the school band struck up early, and above us the last stars had the dull shine of studs on a dusty boot. 

We were there to memorialize Bell Street, which had been bought house by house and scraped flat for the new runway.  The plan was to photograph the mayor unfurling a banner over the site, then have breakfast at the airport hotel.  My job as the airport's aviation meteorologist was to call the launch according to the wind; my private business was to prove that empty spaces remember. 

I had brought my mother's brass telescope and the strip camera she used to take to weddings, where nobody ever stood still long enough for her liking.  The camera had a slit instead of a shutter and took one continuous picture on a roll of film as you turned a little crank and panned.  Spooled wholeness, she called the result, even when a child who raced from one end of the group to the other came out twice.  I had two laughing brothers in her picture of my wedding and only one in life.  My brother had sold first, needing the money, and had since quit coming to anything where people might ask about the sale.  I had signed last and made quite a point of telling people so. 

The mayor got the banner loose just as a skin of fog formed over the dirt below, and the official photographer asked if I could get us underneath it.  My mother had taught me weather by categorizing clouds by emotion, and for a moment I heard the question in her terms.  Need was fog that filled every hollow; reluctance was the kind that came up to something and hung back.  Through the brass tube I saw reluctance all along the pale bank, little square bites out of its advancing lip.  The paving had been hauled away but not the footings, and the ground above the buried brick still held some of yesterday's heat. 

I could see the gap between our house and the grocer's, where we had turned sideways to get our sleds through.  Then I could see the grocer's back addition, an illegal kitchen whose hot rolls had on certain mornings constituted practically the whole attendance at early Mass.  I asked the balloon pilot to hold our height and the mayor to sit on his banner.  By then the band was too far away to hear and we were traveling the length of the street at the pace of somebody looking for a number.  I laid the camera on the rail, slit crosswise to our drift, and cranked as steadily as I could while the town went slipping onto the spool. 

Halfway along, the holes gave out, although I knew there had been six more houses between the last black oblong and the creek.  I stopped turning, and the mayor put one hand on mine. 

"Keep going," he said.  "Those were the company houses; they stood on skids." 

"We got to keep the skids," he added with quietly explosive courtesy; it was the first time I had heard him say anything personal about the sale.  I put the blank stretch in, and the creek came up shining like the worn place on a sleeve.  By the time the mayor stood up again the sun was on the dirt and there wasn't even a street. 

At the hotel the official photographer had a perfectly good picture of the mayor sitting on the name of the airport, which he discreetly undertook to destroy.  Instead of breakfast we cleared a table and the mayor counted off the missing houses on the paper cloth with a spoon. 

Later, when we hung the long print in the departures hall, we left a pencil on a string beside it.  My brother came while I was still tying the knot and wrote in the company families, for whom he had delivered rolls every morning before school.  He stood there rubbing the dent the pencil had made in his finger, and I asked if he had eaten.  Before we went for breakfast he marked our mother's name under her old window, and I reached around him and wrote it again, because that was how she liked her pictures.
