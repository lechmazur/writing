Three days after the memory of her past life surfaced, Ruth recognized the back of her daughter's head in a queue for onions.  It was the way she cocked it to let a stranger know she was listening, although the stranger was speaking to somebody else.  The head was white now and Ruth herself was only thirty.  She bought two onions she had no use for and followed the woman home.  The brass plate on the door said ADA LIN, MIDWIFE, and the sight of the name did not give Ruth the smallest right to knock. 

In her workshop she put the onions in a saucer and tried once more to draw what she remembered.  Usually her work as a camera obscura artist spared her the uncertainty of deciding what belonged in a picture.  Light came through the lens of her little black tent and laid the world upside down on her table; she had only to follow it with her pencil.  But all she could draw of the fever hospital was a ceiling, a brown blanket and, at the foot of the bed, a square of astonishing brightness.  From beyond it came her daughter's voice, calling her by a name nobody else had ever used. 

What use was it to be spiritually curious, to have attended the lectures of swamis and the dissections of anatomists, if at the first real mystery you went and bought onions?  She went back and knocked. 

"I want to find where unused love is stored," she told Ada, having meant to ask whether the fever hospital was still standing. 

Ada kept one hand on the latch.  "Whose?" 

"Mine for you, I think." 

It was the description of the ceiling that got her admitted, not the declaration of love.  Nobody who had not lain on that ward could have known about the fish a bored plasterer had drawn with one finger above each bed.  Ada listened without offering her tea, then took a small silver pear out of her sewing basket. 

"Kwan Yin's tear," Ruth said, and knew before she turned it over that one face was polished flat. 

At twilight they climbed the old railway viaduct together, pushing Ruth's folded tent on its cart between the rails.  The viaduct was in bloom from end to end; the pear trees espaliered along its parapet to feed the patients had outlived both the patients and the gardener.  Fog had taken everything below the level of the rails, so that the two women seemed to be walking on the white backs of sheep.  Opposite the brick shell of the hospital Ada stopped. 

"I used to come along here catching the evening sun in a shaving mirror," said Ada, holding the pear by its stem.  "They sent this back with your blanket.  No visitors on the ward, but nobody could stop a little light going in." 

"My pilgrim of sparks," Ruth said, remembering the name she had given the child, and then what the child had called her. 

"Bedfish," they said together, and Ada had to sit down on the rail. 

"I saw every flash," said Ruth, "but I couldn't lift a finger to answer." 

"Is that what you came to heal?"  Ada sounded almost cross.  She made Ruth set up the tent with its lens facing the hospital windows, which still held a last clear band of sky.  Inside, Ada lay on the ground with the silver pear on her breast and Ruth's white drawing board propped against her knees.  Ada moved the board until a little reflected fish of light lay still upon it.  Then she began to breathe the way Ruth suddenly remembered breathing: two quick draws of air, a pause, a long release.  The fish leaped twice and slid down the board. 

"I could see it on the ceiling from out here," Ada said.  "You never did those two little hops for the nurses." 

Ruth sat down beside her daughter, careful not to touch her until Ada took her hand and set it on her narrow ribs.  "Feel that," Ada said, and this time her breath did a little skipping dance that made the fish turn somersaults.  She had taught it to frightened women in labor, she said; it helped to have a silly thing to do together between pains. 

Ruth let the drawing board go dark.  Below them a lamp came on in the fog, and then another, in houses where Ada had been called out of bed to put her hands to use.  On the walk home Ada asked what was for supper, and Ruth, who had been hoping for a harder question, had to admit it was onions.
