At seventy, my mother applied to become a stray philosopher, and the board sent her to me because it expected me to say no.  A stray went to sleep with a question and woke wherever in the mechanical rainforest an answer might be had.  Only after the sixth sense awakened could you feel the tug of such a place, and even then you had to consent to be taken.  My mother had spent forty years going where other people needed her to be.  On the line marked Purpose she had written, in the block capitals she used on jars, TO FIND OUT WHAT ELSE I CAN THINK. 

Her test question concerned the bare strip down the middle of our ridge, where no rain had fallen in eleven years.  Below it grew a forest of brass willows and cup beeches that caught fog in their crowns and passed it uphill through hollow roots to the sprinkling palms.  The board blamed a faulty pump; three licensed strays had slept on the problem and all three had woken in the pump house.  The pump was fine. 

Mother tucked a small gear inside her sleeve, the first one she had ever cut, from her days in the shop that made the willows turn their leaves against the wind.  For luck, I supposed, and lay down beside her with our wrists tied together as the examination rules required. 

I woke with my cheek against bark warm as a loaf and her bare heels hanging on either side of my head.  We were high in a cup beech, looking down into a willow so wet it seemed impossible that anything above it could be thirsty.  She asked me to shut my eyes and listen to the wind working its way through the different trees.  The beeches made a pleasant knocking, like neighbors who know they can come in, but the willows, which ought to have hissed like skillets, were silent. 

"When I cut that gear," she said, "these beeches only came up to my waist." 

I began to unravel the loose end of our wrist cord, paying out a single strand until it hung among the willow leaves.  Up here it streamed sideways; down there it hung plumb.  Enough water but not enough wind to drive it uphill, and all because eleven years ago the slow beeches had finally overtopped the willows.  Already I could see the order going out to fell every beech on the slope, and the bare bright stumps like so many newly minted coins. 

Mother was rubbing the little gear between finger and thumb, a habit she had when waiting for the right words to catch up with her.  "All my life," she said, "they paid me to make these for willows." 

I had to lean down and tie the free end to a willow leaf, while she slipped her gear onto the bare spindle at the joint of a beech twig.  She looped the thread around its hub.  Each knock advanced the gear one tooth and wound the thread a little higher, until a wet willow leaf rose into the sun between us.  That was when I felt what the manuals call the hum of insight, though they say nothing about how like embarrassment it feels when an idea goes ringing through a head that had considered itself furnished. 

"We could let the beeches drive the pump," I said. 

"On windy days," she agreed, and we sat there awhile letting it be an ordinary proposal with costs and difficulties, instead of the miracle I needed it to be if I was going to sign her papers. 

Finally I asked what question had got her here when all the rest of us could dream of nothing but the pump. 

"I asked where the noise I used to come home to had gone," she said. 

I signed on her bent knee, in the green light, with the pencil tied to my nightgown. 

By autumn there were gears in forty beeches and every afternoon our ridge got ten minutes of rain.  I went to tell her about it and found her bed empty and the little jar of questions on her windowsill lighter by one folded slip.
