On the morning the stern vanished, Nell did what she always did when a piece of the commuter ferry went missing: she found everybody somewhere else to sit.  "Best view on the river," she told the man whose usual bench was now a strip of cold green water.  He took her hand to step across the gap, which was why she said things like that, and not because she thought they were true. 

Her father had been master on the night the river broke its banks and people crowded both landings, begging to be taken off.  He had turned to port, as half the town remembered, or to starboard, as the other half remembered whenever the clocks ran sideways.  At such times the hands traveled bodily across their dials, keeping the same hour but moving toward another town with the same name.  First a chair had gone across, then the brass tiller cap, then a schoolboy who came back a day later carrying somebody else's lunch.  By rights the service should have stopped, but the flats were on one bank and the jobs on the other, and nobody could afford to move. 

Now the wheelhouse went, taking the master with it, and Nell heard rock scraping along the keel.  Where the two rivers overlapped there was a grotto under starlight, all the drowned rooms of both towns hollowed into its walls, their curtains billowing through solid stone.  A deckhand all her life, Nell had glimpsed it through missing planks but never stood there with forty passengers awaiting her instructions. 

She took the empty hourglass from its bracket beside the gate.  In the flood her father had dropped it and the tip had broken off; he had kept it to remind him what three minutes on one bank had cost the other.  She had kept it because even a broken thing could tell you something if you put your ear to it.  Through the hole came the thin domestic clatter she sometimes heard on quiet crossings, a spoon going round and round inside a cup.  This time she could pick out a little double knock at the end.  At her elbow the man from the stern was shaking the drops off his spoon in precisely that rhythm.  Not an echo: his knocks came first on one turn and second on the next. 

"Will you radio the other deckhand for me?" she asked the boy with the lunch, who had been given a pocket set after his adventure.  He knew the channel as he knew the kitchen where his sandwiches had been made, a place he could find again but not point out on any map.  Instead of her own voice Nell heard a woman's voice she had missed for nineteen years. 

"Nellie, I've got a stern and a wheelhouse and nothing in between." 

Her father had never told her who had been waiting on the starboard landing, and through her childhood she had been careful not to ask.  She wanted so badly to step through that the boy had to catch her belt. 

"What if we made one crossing out of both?" she said into his radio.  She held the hourglass against the microphone and had the man stir his tea until the knocks fell together.  His bench slid back aboard, dripping. 

"Don't everybody start tapping," her mother said, with the old edge that made Nell straighten up.  "Listen for somebody doing what you do." 

The passengers knew one another's habits better than they knew one another's names, and Nell went among them fitting one small sound into the intervals of the next.  The spoon gave the schoolboy his cue to snap his lunchbox shut; its latch set off the conductor's punch; the punch brought in the slow impatient heels by the gate.  On the radio each sound found its fellow until what came through the broken tip was neither ahead nor behind but simply louder.  Around that ordinary racket the two passages knit, plank against plank, and the stars in the grotto went out like lights in rooms no longer occupied. 

At the landing her mother put a hand against Nell's cheek, then went to let down the ramp because there were people with trains to catch.  They found they could not go ashore together; the moment both touched the gangway it began to thin.  But there was a length of deck between the gates where they could walk abreast, and two towns now set their watches by the sound of its approach.  Nell took to bringing two cups and leaving them unwashed between crossings, the way you might in a house you meant to stay in.
