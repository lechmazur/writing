The morning after the hail, the marble cavern under the schoolhouse gave back the sound of somebody teaching a goose to whistle.  It was July, and the harvest of echoes was not due until the low light of November reached the shelves.  By the time Ada Vale came down with her bag of keys, the goose had learned two notes and was plainly refusing a third.  The children on the cellar steps laughed; their teacher, standing with a preserving bottle held too late against the singing stone, began to cry.  That was how Ada learned whose goose it had been.  Last winter the teacher's little boy had died of an infection that had started in one thumb. 

Every family in town had a shelf down there, a thin lip of marble into which you could speak something you wished to keep.  In November you came with your bottle and the school clock tipped a mirror and a square of sun moved from shelf to shelf.  Once stoppered, a sound would last for years, but once loose in the air it had no more privileges than any other sound.  Hail had punched daylight through the roof and sent the mirror crashing down among the wheels that turned it.  Ada closed the hole with her coat while the teacher found a board.  On the way upstairs neither of them said that there might be another lesson on the shelf, nor that there might not. 

Ada was a locksmith, not a clockmaker, but in either trade it helped to be peripherally focused: look straight at the thing that wouldn't move and you missed what moved instead.  She took the broken timer mechanism home in her apron, down to the basement where she kept her notebooks and every lock she had ever failed to pick.  Her husband used to call this room the cellar of reason, because everything in it, however baffling, had to have an explanation.  He worked nights at the bakery; she liked to store wonder in the cellar of reason, for later, when there would be two of them to take it apart. 

Across the lull of a wounded season, while the orchardists waited to see which stripped branches would put out leaves again, Ada tried to reverse-engineer the clockwork.  She could make the mirror travel at a steady pace, but near the end of its arc three shelves would go by in the time it took to pull one cork.  Whose bottle would you hold against the stone then? 

Her husband suggested studying reflection angles at different times, as if she were casing the sun for a burglary.  Ada put a white board over the shelves and spent long afternoons below it with a protractor, a stub of pencil and the mirror balanced on a borrowed music stand.  She was watching the square crawl over her ruled lines when something flickered on the steps behind her.  At the edge of the mirror, a splinter still attached by its silver backing was throwing a second, smaller patch of light.  It climbed the steps exactly as fast as the big square crossed the shelves.  What looked like a fresh break had a little bevel worn smooth as a thumbnail. 

At each chalk line the little light touched one of the iron strips nailed across the treads to keep boots from slipping.  She knelt to test one with her thumb and felt, through the sole of her boot, the next strip rise.  Nobody remembered working the timer because nobody had ever worked it on purpose.  The waiting families themselves, shifting their weight on the stairs as each bottle was filled, had checked and released the escapement.  All that summer she had been trying to fit together, inside a brass case, a machine half of which was people.  Once she knew what the long empty slot in the case was for, it took an hour to file a new tongue to fit it. 

In November Ada let the teacher go down first, with the same green bottle, and stood on the bright step behind her.  The stone gave back a long silence.  The teacher put her hand over the bottle, ready to take it away, and Ada, who could feel the clock straining gently against her heel, stayed where she was.  Then came the sound of a small boy drawing breath, and a solemn adult voice saying that perhaps it would be easier to teach the goose to hum.  She had come for his voice and found, quite as strange to her now, the woman he had known as his mother.  She pushed the cork in with both thumbs.  Only when she looked up did Ada lift her heel.
