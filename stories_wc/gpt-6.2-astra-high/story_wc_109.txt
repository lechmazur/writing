In the flaking gold leaf booklet sold at the ridge fort, the saint goes through the mountain alone.  On the last page she stands in daylight, looking rather pleased with herself for someone who has just left six hundred people underground. 

I was mildly obsessed with those people even before I became one of them.  I had a little brother who liked the pictures, and every time he brought me the booklet to retell the story, I counted the heads in the procession going in.  They dwindled into dots at the back, but the artist had given every dot a hat.  Our father, the pebble-shore oracle, said a man who took such trouble over hats would not have misplaced their owners. 

He earned his bread by summarizing silence: you brought him the ambered hush from a sickroom or a marriage bed, and he told you what had happened while nobody spoke.  It was a property of our mountain that quiet, if it lasted long enough, set like resin on the walls.  Father had learned his trade among the shore stones, where the quiet between waves yielded scarcely enough to wet a finger.  He could get a great deal out of a little. 

When the sea rose to the gunports of the storm-tossed ridge fort, the commandant sent for him.  Behind the chapel altar was the cell where the saint had dictated her account of the crossing, pausing often to draw breath.  The walls were thick with what the monks had been too reverent to scrape away. 

"We have her words," the commandant said, putting our booklet on the altar.  "What we need is to draw a map of an endless night." 

The mountain road had a hundred forks, and the sea had spoiled our powder and all but a flask of lamp oil.  Father set his palms against the marbled hush, where bands of chapel music ran like veins of milk through the clear brown.  Beyond the shutters only one star remained in a dimming sky; the rest had gone under the high black shoulder of the wave. 

"Somebody is rubbing bread on stone," Father said. 

My brother, who helped in the chapel school, took a crust from his pocket and rubbed it across the slate on which the monks had taught him his letters.  It made a small, gritty sigh, and the letters came away with the crumbs. 

"They kept making her do it over," he said. 

Father listened again, this time laying his cheek against the wall as if it were the cheek of someone he loved.  "Not her," he said.  "There is a sleeve with a button that clicks, and a sleeve that drags, and once there is no rubbing because the next hand follows the line exactly." 

I saw then what the artist had tried to get into his little book: not a line of people going in, but a queue of people waiting their turn.  I took the iron candlestick from the altar and drove its foot into the amber.  The commandant caught my wrist, but my father put his hand over mine and together we levered a whole slab loose.  Beneath it the wall was black with charcoal thumbprints, and through them ran a groove worn white into the stone.  Six hundred hands going over a road will cut it deeper than any one hand can rub it out. 

My brother opened the booklet flat against the groove and with his dirty cuff burnished the gold across both pages until the road stood up under his finger.  We went in as the first green water shouldered the chapel door off its hinges.  At each fork my brother felt for the next turn in the gold and whispered it back along the file; nobody spoke between turnings.  Once, far back, I heard the minute click of a button traveling along the rock, and understood why my father had laid his cheek there. 

I would like to tell you what the daylight looked like at the other end, but I was at the rear, counting, and for a long time all I could see of it was the light on people's hats.
