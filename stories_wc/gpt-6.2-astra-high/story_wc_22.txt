After the reef died, the two islands became one island at low tide, and the first thing their people did with the extra land was fight on it.  Thirty years of storms had heaped the battlefield above the tide, a mile of white shingle stitched from end to end by the wheels of the gloom-hill coachman's black carriage.  Emil had driven that mile so often with the blinds drawn that his mare still stopped halfway to let the other island's bearers take the handles.  Tonight she stopped and nobody came. 

He had come from the blooming jetty at twilight with a collection of dried seed pods on the seat beside him and six sacks of sweepings from the municipal dovecote inside.  The jetty women had given him one pod from every garden they kept in the old fish boxes along the rails.  None of them had been willing to come with him to the middle and be the first to put a spade through somebody's grandfather.  Even his daughter, who had washed and dressed the dead with him since she was twelve, had refused.  Something alive there would suit him better than the granite obelisk the councils meant to set up in the morning, but wanting it did not tell him where to dig. 

The only survey of the burials had been cut into the slab of the peace treaty when it still stood clear of the water.  Now the submerged concord showed its face for a few minutes on the lowest nights of the year, somewhere out in an acre of identical black humps.  Emil could still speak the dead languages of both island courts, having been apprenticed to an undertaker when mourners still wanted the formal addresses to their ancestors.  He could read the crabbed surveyors' marks if he could only find them. 

He stood on the box and listened.  His father had netted plovers, and from him Emil had learned the difference between the thin cry a bird gave passing over and the little quarrel two birds had over a place to put their feet.  That quarrel broke out now, not from one pair but from a dozen, all along what sounded in the dark like the edge of a table.  Only one of the humps was broad and level enough for that.  He drove his spade into the bank, hung a lantern from its handle and waded out on the line of the calls. 

Up to his knees beside the plovers he found a stone lip slick as soap and, laid across it, a pair of hands.  His daughter had come round from the other end with a stick of white tailor's chalk tucked behind her ear.  "I thought if I found them you wouldn't have to look," she said.  She had rubbed the chalk into the grooves, but what had come up was no map, just two columns of little hooked verbs with numbers beside them. 

Emil sounded them out, his lips finding the old shapes as readily as his fingers found the buckles of the mare's blinders.  What looked like figures for the depth of a grave proved to be distances measured from the place where the bearers exchanged their loads.  "Take eleven paces in the wheel rut and lay our brother down one stretcher's length to your left," he translated.  Both columns said left, which meant that each crew coming from its own village had buried its men on a different side of the track.  Both had kept the track itself clear for him. 

He saw his daughter look back at the two ruts with the spade standing between them, lit like a needle by its little lamp.  "Then we'll have to carry them," she said. 

It had not occurred to him until then that he was asking her to give up the business he had promised would be hers.  He might drive back now and she could go on carrying the islanders across to lie beside their kin as they had done since the peace.  While he was trying to find the words to offer her that choice she took his elbow and guided him ashore.  She backed the mare out of the shafts herself and left him to fertilize the strip between the ruts while she broke open the pods. 

When the councils came at dawn they found her still at work, pressing each seed down with the white thumb that had brought the figures up from the stone.  Asked how the dead were meant to travel now, she held out the empty sack by its corners, and two of the men, without thinking, took the other end.
