At dawn in the pea arbor, with the harvest moon still caught in its western arch, Nell put a paper ticket into the mouth of her brass hen.  The hen laid an egg stamped with the number forty, to the delight of the children and nobody else.  Behind it stood the wooden hopper, forty sacks of flour and the miller, who had not brought a chair because he did not expect the business to take long. 

Each egg was good for one sack, once its holder had given the clerk a name to enter against the number.  It had been Nell's remedy for the year of the short measure, when the first ten families out of the arbor had flour on their boots and the last ten had none.  Through his last winter her father had lived on turnip peelings rather than have his name go down in a book that anybody in the parish could read.  She had discovered this only after his death, when she found a little nest of numbered eggs in the drawer where he kept her school reports. 

Before the hen, before even the short measure, there had been a service in the arbor on this one morning of the year.  The village faced the setting moon and sang while whoever needed flour helped themselves behind the singers' backs.  The singing covered the shuffle of feet, and the moon gave everybody something quite particular not to take their eyes off.  Nell remembered that much, but of the songs themselves she could recall only the shape her father's lips had made around the first long note.  She had come to ask the village to try again, and now that she had them all assembled she could think of nothing to do but polish the beak of the hen. 

The miller caught her wrist.  "You promised me a way that nobody goes short," he said, so quietly that the children leaned in to hear. 

Usually Nell could spin golden stories out of a bit of brass and a bad temper; it was how she had sold him the hen.  This time she handed him the egg and asked him to rub his thumb over the number.  It came off yellow on his skin.  She had filled the stamping pad with pollen instead of ink. 

"Give an egg to every house as usual," she said, "and let them drop it into your empty hopper if they want their sack, and keep it if they don't.  The hopper will take the eggs but it won't ask them anything." 

The miller counted heads, and as he passed among the families Nell heard the small discreet chink of brass against wedding rings.  Still nobody turned to face the moon.  As Nell lifted the hen to put it away, its ridiculous comb snagged a vine tendril overhead and the whole green canopy trembled.  A little girl reached up to ease the tendril loose, turning her finger round and round against the way the pea had wound it. 

"Which way home?" the miller sang.  It came out of him like a sneeze, surprising him more than anybody.  His sister's finger was already turning in the air as she sang the answer: "Against the sun, against the sun."  They had learned the winding song here as children, following the curls with their fingers before they knew the difference between praying and playing. 

One by one the old songs came back, the winding song drawing the water song after it, the water song the one about the stone that would be bread.  By the time the sun looked straight through the eastern arch into the white face framed in the west, every back was to the flour.  Under the singing Nell could just distinguish the thump of the first egg against the wooden hopper, and then the basses came in and she could distinguish nothing.  She kept her eyes on the moon until it went down. 

In the hush that gathered afterward, nobody asked how many sacks were left.  The girl who had unwound the tendril wanted to know if the pea really did go home against the sun.  Nell might once have made a lovely answer out of nothing.  Instead she lifted the girl to a green curl still holding fast, and together they traced it back the way it came.
