My father had a price for the admiral's lost compass rose, down to the penny, and no price at all for the three fingers he had lost looking for it.  He had reached into the millrace at what his tables said was slack water.  By the time I took over his bench he could still mend clocks, but I had to start the screws for him.  I became the town's broken-clock tinkerer; he became the man who sat in my doorway telling strangers what they could have for three fingers. 

The rose had been the size of a dinner plate, eight points cut from silver, screwed to the observatory floor above the mill.  When the observatory burned, everything beneath the ashes went at auction, including the right to fish the silver out of the race.  Father still had his receipt. 

The admiral's granddaughter came to see us the week before the race was due to be filled with concrete.  She didn't want the rose; she wanted the names scratched on its underside by the schoolgirls her grandfather had taught navigation, one of whom she thought might be her mother.  My father named his price and she laid a sheet of paper and a stick of pencil lead on his knee.  "For a rubbing," she said, and left him to work out what it ought to cost. 

What Father had actually seen, it turned out, was a single silver point below the inspection grille, and that had been twenty years ago.  We walked down to the cavern where the race came out of the hillside in a subterranean waterfall as thin and wide as a bedsheet.  Father held his good hand behind his back to keep from consulting his watch.  A yellow leaf came over the lip, stood on edge halfway down, and shot out sideways like a coin flicked off a thumb.  Presently another leaf did it, then two together, and Father made the small interested noise he makes when coincidence becomes pattern.  Something was sticking out through that sheet of water. 

We took the only way left to the inspection grille, through the swirling leaves in a neglected greenhouse where the pupils had grown lemons against an imaginary scurvy.  Father knelt on the brick floor and laid his damaged hand flat on the iron. 

"If that's the rose down there," he said, "I don't know what I put my hand in here for." 

"How sure are you it was the rose you saw?" 

"Uncertainly certain," he said, the first discount he had ever allowed on that particular article. 

We sent for the granddaughter.  Instead of measuring the intervals between rushes of water, as he had done before, we tore up yellow leaves and let the pieces fall between the bars.  The granddaughter watched from below, and when we joined her she showed us seven scraps pasted to the cavern wall at seven quite different heights. 

"It wags," she said, and drew in the dirt the little arc the scraps had described as they flew. 

We had been picturing a thing wedged tight in stone; she had seen something hanging by one corner.  Father sent me for his long-handled gaff, then called me back before I was out of earshot.  "Bring the whole mess," he said, meaning the box of clock parts under the bench, and this, from a man who used to lay out tomorrow's socks, was practically an invitation to improvise. 

The gaff would reach the place but would not hold anything flat, so we lashed two brass chime rods across its throat to make a fork.  The granddaughter fed leaves to the race while I stood on dry rock and Father sighted along the handle.  Each time a leaf flew he nudged my elbow, until we felt a little kick travel up the wood.  Then I lifted, and a dinner plate came down into an inch of water with the intimate, domestic sound of somebody doing the dishes.  A single screw still dangled from its hole on a long snarl of wire. 

Of the sixteen names on the back the granddaughter knew eleven, but her mother's wasn't there.  Father turned her paper sideways so there would be room for them all.  No money changed hands that day, though eventually the school bought the rose for rather less than three fingers. 

On Sundays now we take the rubbing and go looking for the other five women, who might remember a quiet girl too shy to cut her name in silver.  We put two addresses in his pocket before we leave, one for where we mean to go and one he lets me pick without telling him.
