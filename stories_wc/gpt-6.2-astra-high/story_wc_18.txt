The first time the glass mountain appeared above the orchard, Nell found her husband halfway up a dead pear tree, holding out a mitten.  She got Abel down with a ladder and did not ask him whom he had seen.  There had been only one pair of red mittens in their house, and the other mitten was buried with Kit. 

For three hundred years thick fog had kept the ruined orchard district lost to the world beyond its ridge.  Children born there knew mountains only by the noise stones made coming down them in the dark.  Kit had invented a peak of blue glass that rang under his boots; when he woke he could still sing the notes.  Nell, a mirror maker with a workshop full of silvered scraps, had given him plenty of material for imagining it. 

Upon the shimmer of twilight frost it came again, clear from its needle summit down to a small boy dragging something by a string.  Abel went out in his stockings.  This time Nell caught him at the lip of the old packing pit, where the mountain laid a shining blue foot across twenty feet of empty air.  She had meant to let the runaway dream wear itself out, the way such things generally did for want of a warm head to inhabit.  Now she would have to capture it before it took somebody home. 

Her father had been the last astronomer in the district, an occupation that required a sense of humor and another source of income.  From his years repairing clocks she had inherited a firmly grounded workbench; from his years expecting stars, an astronomer's broken sextant.  Its scale was cracked across the numbers, but its little pivoting mirror could still bring two distant things together in the eye.  At the bench she fitted a disk of freshly silvered glass where the split scale had been and covered it with the black cloth she used for polishing. 

She knew how to fix a dream on silver: you had to recreate the instant just before the sleeper opened his eyes.  This was not, as people thought, a question of pillows or familiar wallpaper; it was a question of persuading the dream that what lay just outside it was home.  For the last six weeks of his life, home for Kit had been two faces leaning over a narrow bed, close enough for him to feel their breath. 

They carried the bed out together and set it on the sound brick floor of what had been a sorting shed.  All afternoon Abel worked without asking a question, but when Nell angled the little mirror to catch the empty pillow, he put his palm over it. 

"If you get him on that glass," he said, "will he ever get where he's going?" 

She looked again at the speck hauling its burden up a slope so sheer the eye slid off it like a drop of rain.  For the first time she made out what was on the string: her best tea tray, dented at one corner from a winter hard enough to freeze the cellar steps. 

"I think he means to come down," she said. 

Abel bent over one side of the bed and Nell over the other, the sextant firmly planted on a fruit box between them.  To bring the boy to waking they had first to meet him on the far side of sleep, as they used to when his bad lungs kept him frightened and awake.  Through synchronized breathing they had done it then, the three of them erasing boundaries in the dark until nobody could tell whose ribs were working so hard.  Now she watched the small shoulders lift and fall against that terrible brilliance, and Abel watched her lips, and gradually the mountain stopped ringing. 

At the summit Kit turned his tray around.  Nell tilted the pivot until the long blue slope ended on the pillow, and Abel lifted the cloth.  Down came the tray with a sound they both knew, the astonishingly dirty laugh of a boy who had learned it from the men who carted coal.  For a moment the silver disk was warm enough to leave a red circle on Nell's hand. 

Afterward there was only the fog and the actual ridge and a bed they would have to carry home.  On the glass, if you breathed on it, a fine curling scratch showed where the corner of the tray had caught.  Abel wanted it hung over the stove, low enough to catch the steam whenever they put the kettle on.  Some mornings they looked and some mornings they just made tea.
