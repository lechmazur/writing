On paper I lived for eleven minutes, which was how long my brother lived in the room behind the rat catcher's cupboard.  The registrar had put the cross beside the wrong twin, and by the time I needed a pension there was nobody left in the office who knew how to take it off.  Somebody suggested the woman who delivered us might have kept a book.  I took the tram down to the water with my mother's sewing tin on my knees.  Inside was the label from a poison bottle, a little square of blue linen with a skull printed on it in white.  My mother used to let me feel the edges before she put it away.  She said the rat catcher had given it to her instead of a photograph of the two of us together.  I had never quite forgiven this economy. 

At the free port the shipping containers stood eight high, with alleys so narrow a man walking ahead of me disappeared when he turned sideways.  Once there had been families in the bottom tier, although the company only allowed its men to sleep there between shifts.  The city museum had a picture of that tier with the washing touched out, so that a line of little shadows lay under nothing.  My mother used to look for the shadow of a nightgown she had made from a flour sack. 

At low water the older hands sat on bollards waiting for the grain barges to float off the mud.  If I asked about a birth book they shook their heads, so I bought a paper cone of shrimp and sat with my back to them.  I had spent forty years mending sails; even in talk it was the places where one piece joined another that caught my attention.  One man remembered his father running for the rat catcher with a kettle, another the blue skull pinned to a door to keep the foreman out.  A woman shelling shrimp for the next tide said the skull was never pinned up until the kettle came back.  By collecting these scraps I got as far as the cupboard, or the spot where it had stood before the stacks were shifted inland. 

At high water the barges came in and the bollards emptied.  I went home and held the linen against the kitchen light.  There were four pinholes at the corners, but down the middle ran two rows of holes so fine I had taken them for the weave.  All night I wondered what had been unpicked.  I remembered the little drifts of white fluff on my mother's black skirt when she sat with the tin at dusk. 

On the next ebb I laid the label on the shrimp woman's knee, and she turned it skull side down without even looking.  Her finger went along the holes and stopped where the shorter row stopped.  She pulled her blouse loose and showed me a blue square sewn inside the tail, with one long ridge of white stitches.  There was a stitch for coming into the world, she said, and another for each night you stayed.  At fourteen stitches the room went to somebody else, and the mother took the scrap with her as the only birth paper she would get.  She picked a thread from her hem and offered to show me. 

I had come for something I could layer with affidavits until even a clerk would feel the weight of me.  What I wanted now was to know what my mother had felt under her thumb when both rows were still there.  We sat through the turn of the tide, the square stretched across my knee, while I worked a strand of white through the longer row.  For the single stitch beside it the woman gave me the needle but made me find the holes myself. 

Eventually the pension people accepted a stack of payroll slips and a dentist's bill dated half a century after my death.  They asked whether I wished to have the eleven minutes struck from the record.  I had them put on a sheet of their own, without a name, since he had not had one.  I layer the linen between his paper and mine, and both sheets have begun to take the impression of the stitches, the long low ripple and the one small tooth.
