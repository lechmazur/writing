After the missing boys came home changed, their mothers took turns sleeping in the herbalist's underground garden, where there was a ceiling low enough to kiss a son goodnight.  The four had spent six nights in a snowed-in mine and come out light as milkweed, with their hair hanging down over their eyes.  On visiting day people brought grapes and asked the boys how it felt to be angels, until the herbalist put a lock on the hatch. 

I got in as a buyer of medicinal roots, which entitled me to carry a scale and to ask peculiar questions about weight.  In fact I was a cryptid researcher looking for the animal the miners called the counterweight, known principally by what failed to fall in its vicinity.  My grandfather had seen it once, the winter his partner died, and had lost his job for putting it in the accident report.  All my professional life I had wanted to slap a photograph of that beast down on the mine superintendent's beautiful level desk. 

There was gentian in blue flower under the lamps, and I made the mistake of offering to buy it by the stem.  The youngest boy, Kit, took one look at my scale and said that if I wanted to weigh something interesting I should weigh a secret.  He handed me an old cordless phone with potting soil impacted in the numbers.  The boys used its intercom to talk to whichever mother was up in the kitchen; in the presence of their actual faces, Kit explained, mothers inclined to be brave. 

I thumbed the button and told the static who I really was.  Across the root cellar a flint arrowhead tied by its notched waist to a bean string swung slowly out of plumb.  Kit had made this instrument himself with a point he had knapped during the interminable afternoons, and could now read it the way a sailor reads a compass.  "She's coming to drink," he said, and pointed at the saucer under a little crab apple tree. 

The mother upstairs confessed through the crackle that some days she wished her boy were still missing, because in those days at least she had known what to hope for.  The water in the saucer rose to meet a muzzle that wasn't there yet.  One by one the boys took the phone and fed that hungry interference the things they couldn't say in clear air.  Kit, who stammered, had liked it in the mine after the lamps died because for once nobody had tried to finish a sentence for him. 

I watched four separate shadows on the frost-whitened ceiling metamorphose into a single lean doe, whose hooves made small wet stars wherever they stepped.  With each step a boy came drifting down until all four stood stocking-footed in the dirt.  I lifted my camera, and the flint point turned on its string and aimed straight at my heart. 

Granddad used to say that what embittered him wasn't losing the job but losing the joke: such a gravitationally playful animal, and nobody left to laugh with about it.  What if instead of making the superintendent eat his words I could give my grandfather back his punch line?  Kit was watching a last wizened apple that hung directly above his head.  I set the camera on the scale and motioned for the phone. 

"Newton got a whole law out of this arrangement," I said into the handset, "and nobody ever asked what the apple might discover if a man fell on it."  The mother upstairs began to laugh, a thin December sound that came down through the static like sleet on glass.  The doe inclined her head; the apple came loose and hung in the air, and I fell up six inches and struck it smartly with my forehead.  The law it discovered, according to Kit, was that a scientist will do anything for a little grant money. 

There was a harmony untamed in that cellar then, the mothers at the hatch laughing in different keys, the boys whooping and the doe stamping as if to call for another verse.  Kit's mother climbed down to ask if he wanted to come home, and nobody, above or below, said a word while he worked his way through to yes.  I took my grandfather the bruised apple, which required some explaining, but then we had all winter.
