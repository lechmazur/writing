My job at the House of Waiting was to notice the things that happened too slowly to set off an alarm.  Salt came through the plaster in the shape of a white fern; the south door shaved a little more wood off its sill each spring.  I had been hired because, at twelve, I had noticed that the Virgin in our church was gradually turning her back on us.  The shelf was coming loose. 

That April the parish wanted an inventory of the furnishings before the bishop came down to our quiet bay to sanctify the house.  It had belonged to my grandmother, a perfumer, and its kitchen had been the place people went whenever a boat failed to come in.  After the loss of the Agnes, which took her husband and both my brothers, she gave it to the town.  The council kept the kitchen exactly as it had been on the morning the search was called off.  I took the job partly to understand why some houses feel alive, since this one, with everything in it that I loved, did not. 

I made my rounds while the streetlights daydream, as my grandmother called that stretch of spring evening when the lamps burn pink in full daylight.  In the glass case on the kitchen table lay a shard of orris root from her workbench, the size and yellowish color of a child's first tooth.  The card beside it read A SHARD OF HUSH, which was what a reporter had called it in his account of the vigil.  He had seen my grandmother pass it to a woman who was grinding her teeth hard enough for everyone to hear.  She had rubbed the root between her palms and the warmth had brought out its smell of violets.  No one had needed to tell her to be quiet. 

That evening I found a blue cup on the wrong side of the glass partition that divided the kitchen from the little porch where visitors stood.  It had been moving for weeks, first from dresser to draining board, then to the sill beside the ticket slot.  I would have put it back without thinking if I hadn't seen a second cup setting out by the same route. 

On the porch a girl in a shipyard fleece was pouring tea from a flask for an older woman whose bare ankles were purple with cold.  The cushion off my grandmother's stool was tucked behind her back, and the rag rug had made its way out under her feet.  Neither woman looked at the exhibits; they were watching a green pilot boat edge out beyond the mole.  I tapped on the glass and asked if somebody was missing. 

"Not yet," the girl said, and then blushed at how it sounded.  Her father was taking the boat out for the first time since his stroke, and her mother wanted to see him bring it in.  They had come every night he went down to the dock to practice climbing aboard.  The cleaner had left the porch unlatched and carried things out as the evenings turned cold. 

"It isn't bad news you need a kitchen for," the woman said, holding her cup out for a little more tea.  She wasn't trying to settle the question I had been walking around with for eleven years. 

I opened the case and took out the root; the ticket slot was just wide enough to let it fall into the girl's lifted palm.  The boat had gone behind the mole now and the girl's jaw was working steadily under her ear.  I showed her how to rub it, and we got the faintest breath of violet, more heat than smell, like the ghost of a struck match.  Then I got the partition door propped open with the bishop's kneeler and went to see if the gas was still connected. 

When the pilot boat came in, the three of us were at the stove frying bread in the cleaner's butter.  By then the lamps had come awake; across the black water our kitchen window lay in one long bar of fire.  The girl set the root on the sill for whoever might want it next.  On my inventory I put down where everything had been, in pencil, and left room beside each entry for where it might go.
