Every fallow year the county folded up its roads as a woman folds a tablecloth when her guests have gone.  The surveyors laid them out again to suit the next crop, and if an old friend woke a day's walk farther off, what court would hear a case against a mile? 

An explorer of the folds, Nell had made her living going after things that slipped between, mostly barrows and the occasional bewildered cow.  She had once been caught inside a crease for an afternoon, with the clay so close to her face that she had left the print of her eyelashes in it.  Ever since, she went in feet first and kept one hand on a line she had knotted at intervals of one easy breath. 

This year the road appointed to disappear was the one her sister walked each morning with a lame boy on her back, to set him down among the other children at school.  The county had agreed to pay for a slate so he could do sums at home.  He had asked Nell what use a slate was at marbles. 

On the oldest survey there was a mark at the junction of all the roads, a little ear with a stem like a cherry's.  Nell's grandmother had called it the listening key; before the surveyors locked the church organ to their score, there had been one key that let the roads themselves sing back.  Nobody now alive had seen it, but Nell knew how readily a working part could vanish under a new edition of a map. 

She had made a transparency of each edition, fine enough that a blueprint drafting compass point could transfix the whole stack without tearing a sheet.  With the point through the steeple for a pivot, she swung the sheets until their north lines coincided, and saw the successive junctions describe a spiral tightening toward the church.  Tonight, when the organist began the folding, she would know where to dig.  She borrowed the boy's best agate marble and promised to bring it back with a story. 

At midnight she stood in that shadowed nexus under starlight, the church so near now that she could smell the beeswax burning in the loft.  Through the reverberation of a lonely church organ she felt the roads coming loose, each with its own small tug at the soles of her boots.  She set the agate down, and where it spun without rolling she cut a flap in the turf.  Underneath lay the narrowest fold she had ever entered, smelling of the wet pennies her grandmother used to give her for sweets. 

She tied her line to the church rail and let herself down, taking care not to look up and see how little sky remained.  At the third knot her heel touched something that gave like the treadle of a sewing machine.  To steady her breath she hummed the nonsense song she sang with her nephew on the road to school.  Overhead the organ took up the tune, badly at first, like somebody picking out a song remembered from childhood, and against her other boot she felt the school road unfolding. 

The listening key was not for the organist's hands at all.  They had buried it where a whole parish once could put its foot on it.  She could keep her heel down until the piece was finished, take her marble home, and leave some other woman to wonder where the road to her door had gone.  Instead she hauled herself out, found the sexton's spade, and started stripping the turf along the curve she had drawn. 

By dawn she had uncovered a treadle as broad as the church steps, and her sister came looking for her with the boy riding pickaback.  He sang first.  By noon people were dancing on it, calling back lanes so old that apple trees grew in the middle of them.  Up in the loft the organist turned the county score over and began writing on the clean side.  The surveyors wired the capital to report a spatial mutiny and asked for instructions on how to arrest a crossroads. 

Nell sat on the steps with her nephew and showed him the little scuff the turning earth had left on his agate.  Down a lane she had almost forgotten, somebody was frying onions, and for one foolish instant she expected to see her grandmother coming up it with a penny in her hand.  She let the boy roll first.
