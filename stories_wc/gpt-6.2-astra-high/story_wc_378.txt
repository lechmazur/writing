By the time magnetic north had wandered off the edge of the nursery map, Nell was the last machine on the desert rose farm that still took her bearings from it.  She could have been refitted, but the compass was behind her face, and she liked having the face the children remembered.  They came back sometimes with children of their own and touched the dent in her chin before they said hello.  Nobody else remembered that the dent had been made by a flying shoe. 

A disagreement had developed between her compass and the survey grid in her newer feet; standing perfectly still, she heard the strangely organic sound of somebody chewing celery.  To resolve the static without losing her face, she had begun teaching herself the children's way of getting around. 

Under her cot was a box of childhood fears, the work of thirty winters when the sand came up against the glass dome and there was nowhere to play but inside.  She used to miniaturize whatever frightened a child, working in the pink wax she peeled from the grafts.  A pump with a hungry belt; the cistern that gave your voice back wrong; a faceless little Nell, contributed by the boy with the good throwing arm.  Each child set the offending thing in its proper place on the plan inside the lid.  By containing chaos in a space two hands could cover, they acquired a little authority over it.  Nell, practicing with her eyes shut, had got as far as the cistern when she smelled smoke. 

It was burn day in the west bay, where the old cane screens were being taken down with their annual cargo of scale.  The controlled burn was supposed to stay in the brick yard outside; she saw black flakes lifting past the glass like a film of snowfall run backward, then drifting in through the vents.  Then the cane still hanging over the roses took light, and the glass went brown. 

Her feet set off for the north door and put her up against a potting bench.  From somewhere below bench level a man's voice said, quite conversationally, that he had done something stupid to his leg.  It was the boy with the shoe, the west bay foreman now, still trying hard to make bad news sound dull.  She felt for his boots; one was on backward, except of course it wasn't. 

"Up you come," she said in the voice that went with her old face, and he put his arms around her neck.  Her compass said left and her feet said right and the celery noise was loud enough to drown out the fire bell.  She wanted to turn them both off, but the switch for that was also behind her face.  Instead she pressed the little box into his hands and lifted the lid for him. 

"What did you hate most," she asked him, "apart from me?" 

His thumb sorted through the wax pieces until it came to a blunt little wedge with tooth marks in it.  "Under the cistern," he said.  "The little door they pushed the dead plants through." 

She remembered making it for him after his father died, remembered that for a whole winter he had believed it was where dead things went, fathers as well as flowers.  Now it gave them a way out that neither of her norths knew anything about.  With his finger on the wedge and hers on the plan, they went by the smell of wet brick, then by the queer doubled sound of their own breathing.  At the little door she had to put him down and push him through ahead of her, and he lost a boot in the process. 

Outside on the compost heap they lay in the fine rain of water meant for the burning screens.  The static had stopped, not because the two norths agreed but because, for the length of that crawl, she had asked neither of them for anything.  When the medics lifted him he was still holding the wax door.  "Keep it," she said, and he shook his head and fitted it back into its hollow, a grown man's thick thumb doing the delicate thing she had taught it. 

The next morning she went to the shop and asked how much of a face you really had to take off to get at a compass.  Not much, it turned out; you went in through the chin.  She asked them to mind the dent.
