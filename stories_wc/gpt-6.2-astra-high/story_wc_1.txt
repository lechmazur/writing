Six months after surviving the attempt to end her life, Nell could spend an hour engraving stars in luminous glass and then would have to sit down on the packing crate until she wanted something again.  Usually it was tea, which her apprentice had learned to leave within reach without asking what else she might want.  She had begun to wonder what these pauses were for, besides the obvious business of getting from one stretch of work to another. 

That winter her aunt died and left her, on a strip cut from a charcoal smuggler's ledger, the care of three rooms that no surveyor had ever mapped.  On the face of the strip Aunt Eda had entered weights and prices in the tidy hand that had so often cheated the excise men.  On the back, just clear of a brown scallop where a coal had fallen, she had written, "For whoever has to stop, and Nell to put the windows in."  Three squares followed, each enclosing a different crudely drawn fern and marked with the measurements of a standard cellar pane. 

Nell might have put the scorched testament away with the dead woman's spectacles if it had not been for the mail boy, who took his boots off in her shop to warm his feet.  His toes had the grey waxy look of tallow left to set.  He was sixteen and the canyon was forty miles and it was considered a point of pride to do it without a halt.  I let him go out like that, she thought, watching his footprints fill with blown snow. 

Next trip she went with him, carrying three small panes in the panniers of a mule.  At sundown she unwrapped them to drink the last light, and by the time the quiet canyon lay under stars they were giving off the dim persistent green of a cat's eyes at the far end of an alley.  The boy had agreed to retrace Eda's road, a longer one than his, because as a little fellow he had once been allowed to ride on her sacks.  He remembered the song she sang to the mule but not where she stopped singing. 

At first light they found the first fern, not a thing growing but a pattern in the morning frost above a crack in the cliff.  Damp air coming out of the crack had feathered the stone white in the shape of a fan, down to the notch in its rim that Eda had drawn.  They shifted the slab that made the crack and went in sideways to a dry floor raised on planks, a folded quilt, a tin of biscuits so old they came apart like shale.  Over the door a little square of sky admitted precisely as much daylight as one of Nell's panes could hold. 

She bedded it in putty and meant to be off, so they could reach the next room before the sun got to the frost.  But the boy was still on the quilt, one foot bare, trying with both hands to uncurl a toe.  She took his boot away and sat beside him and presently he put his other foot in her lap. 

They missed the rest of that day's frost.  They ate what they had brought, and in the afternoon the boy slept, and at dusk the square above the door turned green.  From inside, Nell could see what she had made; from outside, anyone obliged to travel late would see where somebody had thought to leave a quilt. 

The following morning they found a white fishtail on the shaded side of a boulder, and on the morning after that a crooked plume.  Nell set glass in both.  Each room had its own way of breathing and each held some small improvement on the last, a peg for wet stockings, a shelf too high for mice.  Eda had kept no house in town, and here was her housekeeping, steadily adaptive, attentive even to the education of a mouse. 

They got the mail through two days late, for which the boy was docked until Nell presented the postmaster with her bill for three green windows and offered to forget it if he would.  On her next bad afternoon she sat on the crate while her apprentice worked, and for the first time noticed that the girl held her breath as she cut.  She moved the teapot between them and turned up a second crate, and after a minute the girl came over.  The girl's spoon rang once against the pot, and from the shelves of finished windows came the faintest imaginable answer.
