When the mountain gave back the voice of Ruth Vale, it gave her back separately to the people who wanted the pit reopened and the people who wanted it sealed.  At the university radio station, her daughter had the disagreeable job of making both recordings available to the public.  Inez was a celestial signal interpreter, which until that winter had meant teasing the songs of distant ice apart from the whine of the campus laundry.  She knew her mother's voice down to the little intake of breath before a hard consonant, as if Ruth had to get a purchase on it.  Both recordings had it. 

As the community divided, the pit company paid to put one recording on the air every hour, and the widows' committee set the other playing from a speaker on the soup van.  These were power plays, but nobody who had eaten off a pit wage or washed a body for burial could mistake them for games.  Inez's brother drove the van on his days off and wouldn't come in for coffee anymore.  He knew she kept a third recording, made alone in the booth, in which Ruth forgave her for selling the house. 

With the council vote due at noon, Inez spent the last dark hour on the studio floor among her astronomical charts, holding a saucer down on corners that wanted to curl.  The mountain only seemed to be speaking; its iron face caught a transmission from the east and threw it downhill.  Each time Ruth spoke, a different one of three faint stars was climbing over the eastern rim.  The charts gave an old surveyor's name for the three: the Borrowers.  Underneath, in pencil, some predecessor had written, They sing the note you lean on.  For years she had thought it a remark about faulty equipment. 

She listened again, not to Ruth but to the silence before Ruth answered.  On the dial for sounds below human hearing, every living voice made its own slow wobble, like a thumbprint pressed into wet clay.  In all three recordings the wobble came back from the mountain just before the words, like the tug on a line before a shining fish breaks water.  Was that how the Borrowers knew what people needed to hear?  She was cautiously hopeful, an uncomfortable feeling rather like getting blood back in a foot. 

To outwit the tricksters she would have to give them a longing so private that no one else could possibly take the answer for their own.  She called her brother on the van phone and asked him to bring the committee chair; the company man was downstairs guarding his investment in airtime.  By sunrise the three of them were in the booth with her, their wet boots printing the carpet with black half moons.  She left the red ON AIR light burning.  Inez showed them the switches that would let her interfere through subsonic frequencies while leaving every audible word untouched.  Her brother studied the label on the third recording and put his hand over hers. 

"You don't owe them this," he said. 

"I know," she said, and waited until he let go. 

The company man went first, asking as he had before if it was right to put the men back to work.  This time Inez sent the mountain the low wobble from her own recording instead of his. 

"Sweetheart," said Ruth to the man who had signed her last pay slip, "a house is just so much brick." 

The committee chair leaned into the microphone to ask about her husband's bones, and the insubstantial woman offered her precisely the same comfort about the bricks.  Inez hadn't bargained for how cheap the words would sound the third time, or how badly she would still want them. 

Nobody applauded; the company man asked for his money back for the spots that hadn't run, and the chair took a long time buttoning her coat.  At noon the council voted to postpone the decision until somebody besides the dead could testify about the strength of the rock. 

That evening her brother brought two paper cups of coffee up to the station, where the stars were rising through the coal grit on the glass. 

"She'd have hated that you sold it," he said, pulling his chair up beside hers. 

"She'd have hated the price I got," Inez said. 

They sat there while the coffee cooled, working out between them what the old woman might have said next, and sometimes they got it so nearly right that they laughed.
