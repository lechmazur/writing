A century after civilization collapsed, the beacon-tower custodian at Sill still kept a box for complaints about the noise.  The box had stood empty for nineteen years when a child put in a tooth wrapped in paper.  On the paper, in the square script they taught in the root cellar, someone had written, Please tell whoever keeps stopping to go on.  The tooth was because the box said complaints without a deposit would not be entertained. 

Ada found the gap in the smile of a girl named Linn, who slept against the cellar wall where the buried glass came through.  Once that glass had carried a single steady note from tower to tower clear across the flats; now it only hummed, everybody said, when the wind blew through the glass humming pavilion over Ada's head. 

"The wind stops," Ada said, giving back the tooth. 

"Not in the same place in a song," Linn said.  She sang it, a long nothing and two short nothings, filling her cheeks to show where they went. 

Ada knew that tune; her father used to knock it on the supper table whenever she seemed likely to fall asleep in her soup.  He had died believing there was somebody left across the flats, and Ada had loved him enough not to ask for evidence.  Now she wanted to prove that coincidences had meaning, even if all this one meant was that two children had once been roused by fathers who knew the same tune. 

By tracking star paths through the pavilion's clear ribs, she and Linn established that the gaps came four minutes earlier each night, just as the Swan dipped its beak into a notch in the western sill. 

"It's the change of watch," Ada said, remembering her father's cold beard against her forehead when he came to bed.  Somewhere a custodian was asking who was awake. 

Above them, cold in its cradle, lay the broken crystal prism that had once sent Sill's answer.  Light passing through a whole prism laid a finger on the singing glass and stilled it; the old keepers had called that finger a shard of hush.  Ada was retrocognitively blessed, which meant that what her hands touched they remembered, though she herself had never been there.  She generally used the gift to find where somebody had put the spare wick or the good scissors. 

Now she took the prism between her palms and let them go back before the break, seeking the angle at which to set the lamp.  Instead her fingers curled around the remembered weight of a hammer, and it was her father's swollen knuckle that ached when the blow went home.  But he had not struck in anger; she felt the tender, fussy way he wound one half in wool and pressed it into a mittened hand no bigger than Linn's.  She remembered now the families who had gone west the year the blight blackened their potatoes, and how her father had given them everything, as her mother bitterly said, but the roof.  For nineteen years Ada had dusted half a thing, supposing the other half swept up and thrown away. 

"If theirs works," she said, "so does ours." 

It took the two of them to rekindle the beacon: Ada to thumb the flint and nurse the wick, Linn to turn the half prism until its narrow light fell square on the glass.  At the change of watch Ada lifted and lowered the shutter to send the three gaps back; to her dismay the far keeper simply sent them again. 

Linn shouldered in beside her and took the shutter cord.  "When your dad knocked on the table," she said, "did you knock back or did you wake up?" 

She played the beginning of the skipping song that every child at Sill knew, the one about the thistle seed that couldn't find a place to land.  There was a long wait, long enough to have been wind or grief or somebody running to fetch the one who would know.  Then from under their feet came the second verse, with a hitch in it where the rope was supposed to catch your ankle.  Down in the cellar the children who should have been sleeping jumped, all at once, and shook dust out of the pavilion floor. 

Ada left the deposit box open that night in case anybody wanted to put a letter in.
