My father drew breath to dictate the story of his scars, but the breath went no farther than his throat.  Across the table my two girls waited with their chins just clear of the rim, solemn in the borrowed collars that kept scratching their ears.  Here on the floating rock, high above the harbor where he had lost three fingers, not even a gull cried out to cover his embarrassment.  Rain fattened on the silk fringe of the canopy without falling, and the copper kettle wore a skin of tiny drops.  It was a breath between storms, the only weather in which the rock rode level enough for the ceremony of the last cup.  Tomorrow he would give up his pilot's coat, and whatever we wrote in the cup today would go into its pocket for the next pilot to find. 

I was a budding rune scholar, still young enough in my trade to believe that the point of learning a difficult thing was to make it look easy.  I had brought a crow quill whose trimmed tip fitted precisely between the ruled lines inside the porcelain.  The tea of that coast crystallizes when stressed; press a word into its surface and a skin no thicker than an onion's will keep the word.  Once the cup was drained the skin would shed from the porcelain in a little scroll, amber and light enough to carry for a working life. 

All through my schooling I had wanted to turn scars into stories, and for my first commission I had been given the scars I knew best.  Or thought I knew: I had already cut the rune for sacrifice so lightly into the tea that it lay there like a hair on a pillow.  Father could have accepted it with a nod, without having to tell the girls about the towline or the awful lightness of the severed end.  Instead his thumb curled under his one remaining finger in that old furtive motion I had never seen except by lamplight.  Under the table my smaller girl was doing the same thing with her plump whole hand, trying to tuck the middle fingers out of sight. 

She was making the harbor hare.  I had taught her the trick without telling her whose it was, just as I had passed on my childhood rules about never counting boats out loud and never leaving a hare shut up in a fist overnight.  In the great flood there had been eleven of us children in the wheelhouse, packed so tight we could feel each other swallow.  All the way in, with his hand bound in a bloody grain sack, he had kept a hare with one good ear hopping along the sill.  For years afterward I made him let it out at bedtime so it could find the ten other children, wherever the county had sent them.  Some nights he had been so tired I had worked the little animal myself, using both my hands to lift the weight of his. 

I turned the quill around and lifted my clever word out on the feather, whole as a drowned fly.  Then I slid the cup into the long shadow of his hand and folded my fingers the way I had when I believed it mattered.  On the bright tea inside the cup my hare put up its two long ears, and beside it his old crooked fellow lifted one.  The girls tipped their heads together to see.  I followed the edges of his shadow with the split point, taking care to get the notch in the ear where the towline had bitten.  He tipped his hand forward, and I drew the hare again, this time in midair with its little belly stretched to clear a wave.  Underneath, in the space that was left, I wrote what I thought another pilot in a crowded wheelhouse might actually need to know. 

Keep the other hand on the wheel. 

My father read it, the rain still clinging to the fringe, his chest still full of the air he had drawn to tell us how a man gets hurt.  He let the air go through his nose in the quietest laugh I ever heard, and on the table four small hands set their rabbits running.
