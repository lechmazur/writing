The last wish granted at Paper-Lantern Cove, with the last spoonful of salt, was for everybody to wake up where they would rather be.  In the morning there was one cup missing from the kitchen shelf, the blue one that Linn's daughter had apparently counted as part of anywhere worth going.  The deserted alchemist went down to the quay and blew out the paper lanterns one by one, since there was nobody coming home to need the channel marked.  She did not tell herself that at least the girl had taken something to remember her by.  It was a good cup for drinking. 

Six years later a boat came nosing between the black piles in broad daylight, sounding the depth with a broom.  Her daughter had come back for the roof of the school. 

"We have sixty children up the inlet," Ada said, "and they get wet at their sums." 

Linn had rehearsed so many things to say that all she could get out was a question about the cup. 

"It broke," said Ada, and they went to look at the roof. 

It was a fine copper roof, made in the years when a whole civilization had lived on the yield of one wish a winter.  Linn had meant to remake the world spoonful by spoonful, though in practice people had mostly wanted roofs.  The coast had sent delegates with specifications, but whatever they settled on had to pass through the working memory of the alchemist in the moment it took her salt to catch fire.  Ada had been seven when her mother let her try it with a pinch of scrapings.  On the schoolroom sill still stood the result, an amber bottle with no opening, because between holding the lovely color in mind and imagining the round belly she had lost track of the mouth.  Everybody had laughed except Linn, who knew how many such omissions the sea had covered up.  She had made fishing boats by the dozen the winter her husband died; she had remembered everything except to give the men a way back aboard if they fell. 

Ada put the bottle in her pocket and climbed the rafters.  "From up here," she called, "I can see two more boats." 

One brought men with hooks to strip the copper for a hospital gutter; the other brought a woman who needed it to patch a ferry bottom.  Ada's letter asking if anybody minded had traveled the coast on the back of the tide tables.  All three crews had brought copies of the same charter, which said the school belonged to the people.  Nobody in those days had been able to conceive of the people living in more than one place. 

By dusk they were asking Linn to judge between a dry bed for the sick and dry slates for children and feet that didn't go through the bottom of a boat.  She felt the familiar terrible pleasure of being the one in whose head they could all go home and leave their troubles. 

Instead she asked Ada to lend her the bottle.  She set it on the floor and asked the ferry pilot to explain what Ada wanted.  Whenever Ada heard something missing she was to tap the glass with her nail.  It rang at "children," for it was not the children who minded the rain so much; it rang again at "slates," because what the inlet school could afford was paper.  When Ada in her turn had understood the hospital men, she took them outside to look at the gutters already hanging from the eaves.  The pilot took six sheets from over the vestibule, and under the sound part of the roof they laid out sixty places to write. 

The ferry came through the channel on Monday with thirty children and came back at noon with thirty more.  By winter there were enough people living at the cove to take turns with the lanterns.  Nobody spoke of rebuilding the old capital, but all along the coast when a village called a meeting somebody would set a bottle in the middle of the floor.  Sometimes Linn sat in on these meetings and sometimes she stayed home, an astonishing liberty for the woman who had once been obliged to hold everything in mind. 

Ada lived up the inlet and taught at the cove, and on the nights when the tide was wrong she had supper with her mother.  She had a key now, not because Linn thought she would stay but because sometimes the old woman went to bed before the ferry came in.
