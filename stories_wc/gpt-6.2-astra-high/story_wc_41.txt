My father knew which boat the tide would lift off its blocks because an hour before it happened he could no longer draw that boat.  Down at the breakers' yard they called him the junkyard prophet and left a square of black wall free for his chalk.  When the breakers' yard became a fog sculpture garden, the council kept him on as a watchman, cheaper than moving him and his kettle.  He had worked there since he was thirteen, and most of what the council called sculpture was stuff he had never got around to cutting up. 

Every November on his birthday I made a videotape of him striking a suspended plate from the hull of the Agnes May, the trawler his father had died aboard.  Shrinking a little with each year's rust, it gave out a note that he said was slowly going home to the gull that taught it to sing.  I wanted to capture the sound of growing older, which at nineteen I imagined would be a nice thing to have on a shelf.  At forty I still brought the camera, though neither of us had ever put a tape back in it to listen. 

This birthday he stood by the black wall amid the hush of old videotapes left unwatched, making three straight lines with a piece of plain chalk.  He was trying to draw the Agnes plate for me and could not remember which corner had been bitten off.  The fog nozzles were off for the winter, but the sea was doing their work, shouldering its way between the iron shapes as the tide came up.  We had maybe forty minutes before water covered the old slipway and whatever had come loose there disappeared into the sucking silt. 

From the shelves I took the tape marked NINETEEN, pushed it into the camera, and for the first time looked at the place as it had been.  On that tiny screen the iron was blue instead of brown, and my father's fist closed all the way around the hammer.  After the blow came a confusion of ringing, and I kept the camera on the plate while somewhere out of sight my father asked for a hand down.  There was no sound of an answer from the girl behind the camera, who was busy clinging to echoes. 

He took the camera and held the little image an inch from his eye until he could locate the plate in relation to the ribs of the launching cradle.  We had to go by those ribs, he said, because the planks of the shortcut had rotted through two winters ago.  I slipped in a fresh tape and followed him out into the white.  He kept stooping and arranging driftwood as directional markers, forks pointing back toward the hut wherever two paths met.  I thought he was afraid he would forget the way; then he asked me, almost shyly, to lead us home afterward because he couldn't see so well anymore. 

I could see the plate before he could, water already lapping at the lower edge and drawing little greasy rainbows out of the rust.  He took up the hammer that hung beside it, but when I raised the camera he motioned me over to his side.  Together we could have swung it, and for a moment I think we both wanted the high clean cry that neither of us had actually heard for years.  Instead he put the hammer down and held out his hand to get off the platform, and this time I took it.  Behind us a last link gave way and the plate settled into the water as gently as a tongue going back inside a mouth.  My father said the name of the trawler, suddenly his again, and in his voice I heard both the boy who had waited on a quay and the man who knew better than to wait. 

On the walk back I left the camera running, pointed at nothing in particular, while we stopped at each fork and he got his breath.  When I played it back in the hut, he put his hand over mine at the sound of our feet slowing to the same pace.
