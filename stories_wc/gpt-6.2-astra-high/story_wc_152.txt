In the last autumn before the coup, the cobalt observatory rang with the sound a spoon makes against an empty cup.  Every evening Iona, the galaxy-chart embroiderer, struck hers once for each valley that had sent no testimony to the assembly.  By leaf fall she could play a little tune.  The aldermen liked her tune and sometimes put a copper in the cup; nobody asked what she was counting.  Being taken for a fool had its conveniences, chief among them the privilege of staying in a room after the clever people began to speak.  That was how she heard the chancellor say that a quiet spool was as good as a signature. 

By custom each valley kept a square of silk cut from the Andromeda chart that lined the dome.  A woman speaking into the square set its loose ends trembling; here the corresponding threads unwound from a spool and spoke against the hollow cobalt wall.  Here, too, the law required that whatever the chart said must be entered in the minutes, even coughing.  Iona had taken the post for the coughing, for what came after it when a shy person found she had not been interrupted.  Her own mother's single contribution to the minutes had begun with a cough and ended with a bridge across the Rill. 

On the eve of the vote to make the chancellor regent, Iona carried her cup downstairs to the women who wound the spools.  They sat shelling chestnuts in a silence so complete she could hear the skins settling on the floor.  On every spool somebody had tied the little mourning knot that meant a village declined to speak. 

"We agreed," said her sister Nell, the eldest winder, "that he should have the silence he gave us when the bridge went down." 

"And he has agreed with the clerks to record no objection," said Iona. 

Nell bit into a chestnut and spat it out raw; for an instant her face was the face of the small girl pulled from the Rill.  "But that is not what our silence sounds like," she said. 

Iona could have cut the knots, but that would have made her just another clerk who knew what other people meant.  Instead she took a flame from the eternal candle in the vestibule, where every new council lit its lamps from the last council's fire.  By its light a stitch cast several shadows, for that flame had never gone out, not even in the worlds on either side of this one.  An embroiderer learned to travel through adjacent possibilities one stitch at a time: here a needle had gone over instead of under, here a hand had hesitated.  Beyond those small divergences lay whole skies she could not read. 

She asked Nell to hold the light while she worked a second way home for each loose end, following the nearest shadow in which its knot had not been tied.  From the wall came the thin, persuasive voices of people almost but not quite their own.  Some wanted the regency; somebody wanted the bridge painted yellow; a child kept calling for a dog that in this world had died last winter.  To bring the threads home she had to synchronize those voices with something that existed only here, something that would come back to her if she called. 

She struck her cup, and across the valleys every square of silk gave out the little note the aldermen had paid for all autumn.  "If you want your silence entered as silence," she said, "touch your square when you hear the next one." 

The spoon struck; the wall went quiet under her hand as one by one the threads drew taut and the borrowed voices ceased.  Then came a noise like chestnut skins falling, the dry brush of a hundred fingers on silk, each valley answering in its own good time.  Nell led the clerks upstairs and put their hands against the wall until the last faint pressure lifted.  Nell made no speech, even then; she borrowed the clerk's pencil and drew the little mourning knot beside the name of every valley that had answered. 

In winter the chancellor took the chair by force, but he never quite succeeded in having those knots explained away as ornamental bows.  There was always somebody's grandmother willing to find a bit of thread and show a child the difference between a bow that comes undone for the pulling and a knot that holds.
