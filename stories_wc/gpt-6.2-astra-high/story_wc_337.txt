On nights when the stars were sharp enough, the prisms in the sanctuary roof laid a little white road across the floor.  The dead walked it all night and got nowhere.  Mara used to watch their feet while her mother stitched the split linen faces they wore on feast days, when the living liked to think the dead came home for love.  Now Mara was the restorer of broken masks, and her mother walked with the rest, one hand perpetually lifted as if to keep a door from closing. 

Tonight a boy had come to claim one of the faces.  He had brought a drawing of his father with a notch in one ear and little blue dashes on his chin where the razor always skipped.  She knew the face; she had been mending it every winter for eleven years, and every winter the mouth split open again.  She put it in his hands. 

"Was there time for him to say anything?" he asked. 

Under the flagstones something pulled so hard that mortar dust sprang up between their shoes.  Every house in the port had a scrap of the same centuries-old fisherman's net under its threshold; the great square from the middle lay here, under the sanctuary.  It caught what went unsaid in all those houses and drew it here, an interlaced hush so tight by now that it could bear the weight of stone.  Under it, where there had once been an open well, lay the bodies of the last crew that had gone below to sweeten the town water.  The town taught its children that those men had died at once from the bad air, without fear or knowledge of what was happening. 

Mara got down on her knees and lifted the loose stone her mother had fitted over the lip.  She slipped her blunt tapestry needle under the first knot; a sharp needle would pierce the hemp and pin one strand more firmly to the other. 

"There was time," she said, and as the boy took in the words the knot went slack.  The stone beside the loose one tilted up like a lid. 

She had been his age, small enough to ride the bucket, which was why the men had sent her up first when the candles went out.  At the top her mother had pulled her clear and started down, but the winchmen had taken their hands off the handles. 

"He told them not to send anybody else," she said.  "He said it more than once because my mother kept pretending she couldn't hear him." 

Another knot opened, then three together, and across the floor the long procession faltered as if the road underfoot had given a lurch.  She could see the white caps of skulls below the net and the green copper buttons of the coat her mother had lowered against the cold. 

"Why didn't she tell us?" the boy asked. 

"She tried to," Mara said.  "They asked her for every word, and she was afraid that if she gave them the first ones, she'd have to give them the last." 

The needle had stuck in a hard little nub of salt.  "At the end he changed his mind," she said, and then she heard the thin clear voice that visited her each time she put a stitch through his lips.  "He wanted us to go down and get him, and we didn't go." 

She had expected the boy to ask if they could have saved him, the question she could neither answer nor stop answering.  Instead he whispered, with a sort of incredulous relief, "He wanted to come back." 

The last knot yielded, and the net turned itself inside out the way a net does when a catch heavy enough to drown a fisherman comes aboard.  Out of the well it lifted eleven bodies in their work clothes, each cradled in a separate bight; it had taken the span of a confidence to exhume them all.  The linen face in the boy's hands tugged him toward a little heap of bones that Mara could not have told from any other.  They carried the bones to the strip of earth beyond the prism light, where a dead man with a name could lie down and have his weight again.  Behind them the others stood waiting their turns, quite still now, except for Mara's mother, who had let her hand fall and was working the stiffness out of her fingers.
