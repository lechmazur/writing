After the breach of contract over unpaid wages, the pear company took its ladders away but left seven tons of pears hanging over the nave.  The town got its orchard back on a Thursday, with about as much ceremony as getting a boot back from a dog.  By Friday one of the great limbs had begun to split, and pears were dropping into the font. 

Ruth, the quartz-eye mender, heard them plop as she unlocked the vestry where she had once kept her tools.  The abandoned cathedral smelled gloriously of pears and disgracefully of wine.  A lush orchard spanned the rafters, its roots sunk in the hollow walls, its branches trained along oak older than the town charter.  Half the people who knew how to tend it now walked with sticks; the other half were underground.  During the company's thirty years nobody local had been allowed up there except to pick. 

"I can get up," said Ruth's granddaughter May, who had never understood why being told she resembled a squirrel was supposed to hurt. 

"And once you're up?" Ruth asked. 

May considered this excellent question sufficiently answered by taking off her shoes.  The old pruners gathered under the splitting limb, each shouting advice which the vault returned in magnificent and useless harmony.  May reached the fork, looked down through forty feet of leaves, and spread her empty hands.  Ruth remembered making that gesture at her own mother, back when the saints could see. 

Each stone saint in the gallery had one quartz eye looking up into the orchard and one looking down at a little white table.  Between the eyes lay mirrors; a pruner too stiff to climb could sit at the table and see the work as if it lay in her lap.  Ruth had last serviced those eyes the summer the company came, the summer her daughter Nell was to have learned to prune.  She had voted for the lease because it promised Nell a wage instead of a summer at somebody's elbow.  It had never occurred to her then that a person might want both. 

Saint Jude's table was still there, under a drift of pigeon feathers, but somebody had taken the lower eye for a keepsake.  Ruth's tool chest was empty except for a small bag with a rabbit button, left there by Nell and forgotten by them both.  Inside were a barley sweet gone white, a reading pebble filched from Ruth's bench, and her daughter's hair ribbon, still tied in the difficult bow she had practiced on her knee.  The pebble was quartz, ground flat on one side; Ruth fitted it into the socket with the ribbon wound round its rim for packing.  Nothing appeared on the table.  She turned the pebble over and got a green blur with five pink toes at the margin. 

"Those need washing," said Nell behind her, and Ruth, who would ordinarily persist until she could count the hairs on a gnat, let her daughter try.  Nell spent her days grading fruit by touch; two fingers to the rim, and she brought the cracked bark into astonishing relief.  Old Abel took the barley sweet, discovered it was inedible, and idly angled its silver wrapper toward the socket to see what would happen.  High up in the leaves May yelped; a little star had traveled back through Jude and landed on her big toe. 

"A body could teach with that," Abel said sheepishly.  He moved the star to the sound fork, then to the sagging one, and Nell sent a coil of clothesline up to May on the old picking pulley.  May wound the line where the light lingered, and when the pruners hauled together the split closed like a mouth deciding against complaint.  Nobody proposed saving seven tons of fruit with clothesline, but the limb would hold while they picked it bare. 

That evening the town revived the deciduous vows, the old agreements that fell away with the leaves and had to be freely made again each spring.  Abel pledged to teach May until leaf fall, provided somebody put a cushion on Jude's stool.  Nell offered to learn the eyes, on condition that the town pay its pupils as well as its masters.  Ruth started to say that was not the custom, then remembered what the custom had cost them.  She voted yes. 

Next morning she found May at the white table and Abel high above her in a chair they had rigged to the pulley.  "He says I ought to know what it is I'm asking when I point," May explained.  Above them the star trembled on a pear, and an old hand reached out to pick it.
