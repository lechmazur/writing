Midnight was a thrush about the size of a teacup, but when she opened her beak in the resonance chamber under our town, she could suck the light off a needle upstairs.  Each night she took the whole town's light to bed in a little iron breast that our smiths made new for her every evening.  At dawn a drop of dew fell on a seam fine as a hair, rust opened it, and out came the day smelling of pennies.  We called it the covenant of rust: nothing with the power to take the light could be made to keep it. 

My father was a music box repairman, my mother a smith, and I could tell which evenings she had made the breast without even going downstairs.  Her hammer left three little flats in the curve, and when the dew came we got a ting, a tang, a lovely long tong.  The last thing she made before the fever took her was lying on our draining board beside a peeled apple gone brown.  Father ate the apple, core and all, standing up.  Then he took the breast into his shop and shut the door.  When he brought it out for Midnight, there wasn't a thumbprint on it. 

Next morning the dew fell, and we heard the ting and tang but no tong, and the roosters went back to bed looking foolish by candlelight.  We were in the balance, that instant between night and day which usually lasts just long enough for a child to turn her pillow over.  Now it lasted long enough for Father and me to get dressed and go down to reclaim the light stolen by Midnight. 

The dew stood on her breast in a bright bead like the dot at the end of a song.  "Bad iron," Father said, and the thrush wilted until the tips of her wings touched her toes.  Midnight wilts near dishonesty; the town used to swear its mayors in beside her, until the mayors moved the ceremony.  I held my hand under her chin and waited. 

"I didn't want it to be the last time," he told her, and she raised her head enough to let him see what he'd done.  Under the dew the seam shone yellow. 

Father kept a chip from the philosopher's stone for treating the teeth of music boxes whose owners wanted them never to wear down.  He called it mending what isn't torn, and he charged twice his usual fee because he knew he'd never see those customers again.  I pictured him behind the shop door gilding that fine seam to keep the three notes safe, forgetting what made the last one sound.  He took out his file, but his hand shook, and inside the bird the bottled light began to knock like somebody frantic to get home. 

I had Mother's apron on, with the leather ties she used to tether her hammer to her wrist when she worked up on the weathercocks.  I looped a tie through the file handle and around my wrist and then climbed into the high brass cup where Midnight sat.  Three strokes and I was through the gold, but when the dew bit iron the cup jumped and the file flew out of my hand.  The tie caught it; the file swung back against the cup just as the breast split, and the whole town woke to a tong with a little unexpected tinkle at the end.  It was the sound of a spoon dropped into a saucer by somebody bringing you breakfast who had tried very hard not to wake you. 

Light ran up the stair ahead of us and put a square of sun on the draining board where the apple had been.  That evening Father came out to the forge to hear me beat a new breast out of the thin gray stock.  I hit it wrong twice and started to flatten the dents, but he laid two fingers on my hammer. 

"Couldn't we hear it first?" he said.
