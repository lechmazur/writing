By the time the butterflies came back, the eleven strangers who had inherited one dead woman's orchard had become a family.  To my considerable surprise, this meant I could be loved and still be expected to scrub the privy.  None of us was actually related to Aunt Linn; aunt was what you called anybody on Pippin who left her land to the people who picked it.  She had left the water to us, too, which was how the trouble started. 

From orbit our planet looked like a bitten green apple, the bite being the salt pan where no trees grew.  Every spring the blue pollinators crossed that white waste by resting in the shadows of the floating rock arches that riddled our sky.  For a week you could look up into the cool hollows and see a blue dust turning slowly among the stars.  The nebula of respite, Linn had called it in the ledger, in the same plain hand she used for sums.  This spring the arches drifted six miles south, and the butterflies came down where their shadows used to be.  By noon there were wings stuck like little wet stamps to the salt. 

Linn's ghost spoke to us through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn.  Usually it was something like YOUR CHIMNEY IS ON FIRE, which once it very slightly was.  That Tuesday the message outside our kitchen said REEXAMINE MY ACCOUNTS.  Under it, in smaller letters: I DID NOT BUY THAT MUCH SOAP.  There was something strangely trustworthy about an accountant inviting scrutiny from beyond the grave.  I fetched the ledger. 

Soap, six deliveries at six mileposts, every spring for forty years.  My sister Kit, who had grown up on the pan before the last of its little mining towns emptied, ran a nail down the column.  "Those are bathhouses," she said.  Sure enough, the distances marched across the page from our well to the first standing trees on the far shore.  Under all that salt the old bathhouse main was still ours; Linn had been paying its upkeep out of her housekeeping allowance.  "She used to let us wash for nothing," Kit said, and shut the book very gently on my thumb. 

At the first bathhouse I turned the tap and nearly wept at the honest stink of iron.  Kit knelt to make a drinking pool, but the first blue to land there went in over its head.  She fished it out with a thorn.  I went home for Linn's butterfly garden watering bell, a clay pot with a thumbhole above and a hundred pinholes underneath.  Dipped, stopped with my thumb, and lifted, it held just enough to freckle a square of sacking without soaking it.  Three blues settled on the wet cloth and unrolled tongues fine as eyelashes.  All afternoon I worked that one square in the shade of the bathhouse porch, and all afternoon the white ground beyond it fluttered. 

Eleven people could save a porchful, not a skyful.  We needed the neighbors, whose own trees drank from wells that our newly opened main would draw down too.  That night I sat on the empty sidewalk and told Linn I hadn't the face to ask them.  At dawn, outside every house, she had chalked exactly what a day's water would cost its trees in lost fruit.  No pleas, no promises, just the figures and a drawing of the bell.  At our door she added: TELL THEM ABOUT THE SACKING. 

By breakfast Kit was showing the potters how to pierce the clay, and I was cutting up our picking sacks with people whose names I still didn't know.  They brought thorn prunings for shade where the porches had fallen in; nobody had to be told to take a shift at a tap.  We kept the stations wet for nine days.  On the tenth morning a blue settled in our first pear blossom, its wings ragged where the thorns had caught it. 

That autumn the pears were small enough to close a fist around, and some fists closed.  Not everybody thought the trade a good one.  On the bathhouse porch Kit hung our bell on a nail where any passing hand could take it down.  Next spring the arches might swing north again, or farther south than any of us had yet imagined.  Under the nail I chalked how to use the thing, and where to find our house.
