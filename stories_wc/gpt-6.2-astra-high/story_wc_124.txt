People came to Ivo for the light they remembered, and he had done good business finding it for them.  He could match a wick to the yellow of a kitchen thirty fathoms underwater, though he would have given his entire stock to sit in the kitchen instead.  He was the last lantern broker on the ridge who had actually lived in the drowned valley.  That qualification got him the job of lighting the greenhouse for extinct plants. 

There were no seeds to set under its magnificent roof, only glass trees whose shadows laid splinters of charcoal across the gravel.  His mother, once the valley perfumer, had contributed the smell of the little winter pear, reconstructed from what she remembered of its skin.  She had died in April, leaving him a crystal perfume vial and the alarming discovery that one drop of its contents could make him weep.  He could sniff her coat all afternoon and feel nothing but the slight embarrassment of a grown man standing in a closet.  Why the pear, which he didn't even remember eating? 

By opening night he had become aromatically cautious, as his mother used to say of customers who tested everything on somebody else's wrist.  He carried the vial stoppered in wax and kept to the windward side of the little fan that would send the pear scent through the house. 

The trustees wanted the glass orchard to change at dusk from a winter wood into the wood in fruit.  Clear pears hung on clear stems; the glassblower had mixed a pinch of mineral into each pear so it would glow green under ultraviolet light.  Ivo had arranged two banks of lanterns and a lever to lower one light as it raised the other.  During the rehearsal a green thumbprint appeared on the trunk of the nearest tree.  He took it for a smudge until he saw four fingers below it and, further down, a child's whole hand.  Across the aisle the glassblower was watching him rub at it with his cuff. 

"Those are the pickers," she said, and showed him how, if he stood where the path turned, the hands reached out of the dark for the shining fruit.  "Your mother had to sit up in bed to give me hers." 

The thumb was bent at the tip where a ladder had once closed on it.  He had forgotten that his mother had been anything before she was a perfumer, although he'd seen the photographs of the picking crew and the slung canvas bags.  The glassblower held her hands around an invisible pear, twisting gently, and his own hands came up to receive it. 

All at once he knew what came after the smell.  His mother would take a handkerchief out of her picking bag and unwrap a bruised pear, the only kind the pickers could take home.  He would sit on the back step, swinging his legs, while she pared away the soft brown flesh with her thumbnail.  Even after the valley went under, the cupboard in their rented room had smelled of clean cotton and pear; she must have been working on the scent almost from the day they arrived.  He picked the wax off the stopper and sniffed again, and there it was, the faint scorch of an iron in the folds of clean cotton.  All these years he'd thought it was the fruit he'd been smelling in her version, but she had put the handkerchief in too.  His nose had been waiting for her to unfold it. 

When the doors opened, Ivo tipped the contents of the vial onto the felt behind the fan.  He let the warm lanterns gutter slowly, until the last amber branch went out and the pears hung in their hundreds in the waiting hands.  As the transformation completed itself, a woman beside him reached up on tiptoe and turned her wrist, a quick and practiced twist.  Her companion put out both hands without looking, and the two old women laughed so hard they had to lean against a tree. 

Ivo had been afraid that if he began to grieve in public somebody would take his elbow and lead him to a chair.  But nobody minded his wet face in that company, and one of the women even asked if he would take their picture.  He waited until she reached up again and her friend cupped her palms, and took it just before the pear would have dropped.
