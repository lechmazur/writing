After the last dictionary was printed, the men came to take the press apart, and the children came as usual to the shadowed library at dawn.  For forty years the bindery had paid a woman to mind them there between the start of the early shift and the opening of the village schools.  Now parents would catch the early ferry to the mainland mills, and the villages were to arrange their own care. 

From his desk under the gallery, Emil watched Nell hang up six little coats and one enormous coat whose owner had yet to grow into either sleeve.  He had worn that coat himself when his mother worked the folding machine.  The library was sonically reflective to an almost spiteful degree; a dropped spoon came back from the tiled vault with company.  The bindery children had always talked with their hands, at first to spare the readers, and then because some of the children could hear and some could not and all had things to say. 

As a scholar Emil had spent thirty years tracing the descent of words for that last edition, yet here was a language whose grandparents he had actually known.  He wanted to decode the pattern of growth before the seven went their separate ways.  He had counted on doing this privately, as he did most things, but Nell had asked him to testify at the hearing on her petition to keep the room.  They had given her ten minutes that morning before the library keys changed hands. 

What he had of the old signing came through fractured sequences of film his mother had taken with a camera borrowed on Sundays from the chemist.  Again and again a child lifted a pinched finger and thumb over an upturned palm; again and again the reel ran out.  He had written 'rain' on the tin, though the younger children now made the same sign indoors on cloudless days.  When he showed them the film that morning, the boy who had worn his coat stood up and tapped the screen. 

Nell went to the mantel and took down a long crystal from the chandelier that had fallen the winter Emil turned six.  She pinched its narrow end and caught the first sun on her palm, then tipped her wrist until the light spilled into his.  He remembered the suspended hush of waiting for his turn, the delicious weightlessness of the thing that was his for as long as he held still.  By the time the little ones were born, nobody needed the crystal to offer somebody a turn.  But as he watched, the boy used the sign twice while explaining something to Nell, tipping his fingers first toward an empty chair, then back toward himself. 

"He's telling me what his sister said and what he said back," Nell explained. 

In Emil's day you could pass a piece of sunshine, but you couldn't give a turn to somebody who wasn't there.  These children could bring a whole quarrel home without ever losing track of who had called whom a pig. 

When the trustees arrived with their ring of keys, Emil put away the film; he wasn't going to spend any of Nell's ten minutes showing them his own childhood.  He stood where the echo would do the least harm and told them what he had mistaken for rain.  His voice shook quite badly until he got to the pig, at which point the boy corrected him.  It had been a pig to begin with, Nell translated, but by the time his sister got through with him it was a pig in trousers.  Then Emil asked the trustees which village they proposed to put that language in. 

They could not give Nell her wages, but they could give her a key, and by the end of the week the parents had worked out a rota.  On his first Thursday he brought the tin with the wrong word on it and asked the children what he ought to write instead.  They tipped their fingers toward the smallest girl and waited while she thought.
