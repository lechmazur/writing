In the last photograph of my wife, there is flour on her left ear and a thumbprint in the flour.  I knew whose thumb it was, but our daughter wanted to feel the print, the way she read the raised dots in her schoolbooks.  All she felt was paper; the ear was going pale, as the faces in all the other photographs had gone pale before it. 

That was what sent me to the living bridge garden to discover the texture of time, though I told the trustees I was there to trim their yew.  I was a hedge maze designer; nobody questioned the shears or the ball of red surveyor's wool. 

The bridge was a saddle of willow roots, humped at midstream but curled up along its edges to keep the little plots of earth from spilling.  In its middle stood the baker saint, whose golden halo had been broken into pieces small enough for mourners to borrow against a deposit.  A halo fragment laid on a photograph would warm until the owner lifted it, and in that interval the owner could put a finger into the pictured hour.  The woman who took my deposit said not to put in anything I couldn't bear to have held. 

I pressed the little arc down over the flour and set my thumb in its old whorl.  My wife was laughing so hard that her ear shook, and the tremor ran down into the willows until the whole bridge was lightly dusted with the flour of her laugh.  Then my wife was laughing so hard that her ear shook.  I tried lifting the gold, but that was not a movement I had made in the kitchen, and my hand could only make the movement it remembered. 

Retrocognitively trapped was the trustees' term for those mourners you saw standing among the yews with their elbows working gently like the elbows of fiddlers.  I had thought them the luckiest people alive.  At each pass my thumb came away a little whiter, and the photograph beneath the gold a little paler; I was rubbing her out. 

I couldn't change the gesture, but under my feet the bridge kept shivering, as if uncertain whether it really had to be a kitchen floor.  I let the red wool slip from my pocket, keeping its end under my heel, and watched the ball roll downhill between two hedges.  Each time her ear shook the wool jumped into a different alley, although it always set out almost exactly the same way.  I had laid those alleys out myself, gaining room for many paths on one narrow crossing through hyperbolic geometry.  On a saddle two nearly parallel tracks could spread like the ribs of a fan without either one turning aside. 

All I needed to do was miss her by a hair and keep on missing in that direction until the hour opened wide enough to walk out.  I could feel a nap under the flour now, like the nap of velvet when you stroke it the wrong way and the cloth seems to stroke back.  Bracing my heel against the wool, I let the next tremor take my thumb across that nap instead of along it.  I felt the soft lobe, then the hollow beneath the jaw, then air cool from the river; between my thumb and her ear lay the width of the bridge. 

The paper began to disintegrate, not into flour as I half expected but into small gray curls that stuck to the sweat of my palm.  I picked them off the way my wife used to pick scraps of dough off the board, unwilling to waste enough for even one biscuit.  The woman gave me back my deposit and helped me fold the curls into the slip. 

At home my daughter listened to what I'd done, then tipped the little heap into her hand and asked for flour and water.  We made a paste that set quite hard around the impression of my thumb.  She keeps it beside her bed with the side I didn't notice uppermost: the print of her own palm where she held it while it dried.
