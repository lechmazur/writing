When the moon came close enough to take a ship, the abandoned radio tower city suspended over Sickle Gorge lit up one window at a time.  From the bank it looked serenely electric, like a tray of blue pilot lights on a stove nobody had remembered to turn off.  Mara crossed on the service rope with her mother's piece of Hollywood in her teeth, since the knots required both hands.  Below her, the mailboat scraped its belly on the lip of the gorge for the second time.  At the third pass the moon would have it. 

Mara was a celestial signal interpreter, which meant she could hear the difference between the moon pulling at something and the thing pulling back.  The trick during lunar perigee was to tune a boat's keel to the latter sound, the note of its own stubborn weight.  Then it would drop out of the tide and go wherever its rudder pointed.  It was work a pilot could generally do by ear, except here, where every loose screw in the empty city was broadcasting her mother. 

"Come in on my voice," said the screws, in the easy contralto that had once sold coffee to half the earth.  During the great flood her mother had stood at this microphone for six days talking boats into harbor, until the towers took up the words themselves.  Boats kept coming in on her voice long after the harbor went over the edge.  The county had evacuated the place and put red ink on the charts, but the mailboat had gone in after a boy whose kite had lifted him.  Through the grating Mara could see the kite wound around the mast and two small figures bent over the wheel. 

She spat the Hollywood star fragment into her palm.  It was pink terrazzo with half a brass microphone and the last three letters of her mother's name.  After the funeral, when the people on the boulevard stood quiet for a minute, that minute had gone into the stone; the mason who cut it up had warned Mara what she had.  A shard of hush could be spent, not lent.  She had saved it for twenty years against the day she could no longer remember what it felt like to stand among people who missed the same person.  She had gone into salvage to find the beginning in every ending; until now she had privately exempted this particular ending. 

She set the stone on the live contact and heard ten thousand people stop breathing.  The screws shut up.  So did the service rope, the wind in the grating, and the little bell on the mailboat that ought to have been ringing as the boat rolled into its final turn.  She set her headset to iron, the usual keel metal, and got nothing.  Thirty seconds, said the dial, which had never in its working life been asked to measure a funeral. 

Her mother had taught her to listen by discarding assumptions, starting with the one she most wanted to keep.  Mara wanted this to be the same mailboat that had brought her birthday parcels when she was small.  She looked down again, not at the wheel this time but at the long pale scar where the belly had scraped.  No rust, no gleam.  Of course they had built another boat; of course they had painted the old name on it.  She slid the selector past the metals to timber and heard the answering groan of a green oak twisting in a gale. 

She could have sung the note into the microphone but the hush still had nine seconds to run, and once it ended her mother would be all the pilot heard.  On the console was the big pitch wheel her mother used to raise her voice when she had a cold and the coffee people wanted her to sound well.  Mara took hold and bent the last syllable of that endless sentence to the pitch of living oak.  When the hush gave out, the pilot got his note.  The keel sang back, the boat fell a clean six feet, and its rudder bit into the ordinary evening air.  From the deck the boy waved one ragged red wing, all that remained of his kite. 

Later the county asked Mara if she would stay on and tune the towers for other boats in trouble.  She said yes, provided they put a switch on the microphone.  And when she crossed back for her things she left the spent stone on the console, propping open the logbook at its first blank page.
