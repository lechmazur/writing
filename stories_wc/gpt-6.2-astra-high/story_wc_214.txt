When the last king of heaven died, a hundred years went by that the royal historians later bridged by gluing two pages together.  My mother and I lived in the glue. 

She kept the shooting star tracking station on the rim of the salt basin, and I was the cosmic janitor, which meant that I went down with a mule to collect whatever heaven dropped.  Most mornings it was iron, which bought us flour, but sometimes it was glass, always the deep yellow of a wolf's eye.  The iron we sold by weight; a piece of yellow lantern glass without a crack in it would pay for a winter's wood.  Mother would hold each golden cup to her ear before she let it go.  She wanted to know whether the soul made any noise leaving the body, and being a practical woman she began with the bodies that came to hand.  What I wanted to know was whether anything that had lived in heaven could bear to be parted from it. 

All through the hush of that overlooked era there had been no births in the sky, although the deaths continued at a steady pace.  By the time my mother's hands began to shake, you could have covered the remaining stars with a spread hand held at arm's length.  Still she would not teach me to take a bearing, for fear I should send her down to sit by the stove.  That was exactly what I meant to do. 

Then came a night when seven went down together and she spilled the ink across the chart and sat there with her black hands open.  "I could see where they were going," she said, and that was the first I knew of the particular hell of being able to see and not to set it down.  I put my finger in the ink and waited until she took me by the wrist.  Her grip was light but sure; it was the fine work at the ends of her fingers that had gone.  By tracing the path of falling stars with my flesh under hers, we found something neither of us had seen in all our solitary years of looking.  Just above the salt the seven tracks had bent, as swallows bend to enter the low door of a barn.  Whatever was coming down out there was landing on purpose. 

In the morning I put her on the mule and took her for the first time to the bottom of the basin.  I had never waited for a star to cool before lifting it; my tongs had a good reach, and there were other people who needed wood.  Now we sat beside seven hollows and watched the salt go from rose to white and our shadows draw in under us like tongues.  Mother had me put her ear against the thinnest of the golden domes.  After a while she beckoned me to put mine there too.  What I heard was the little rasping sound I used to hear through the wall when my mother grated a nutmeg for my birthday cake.  Something inside the glass was filing its way out. 

Toward evening a dealer came down with his cart and offered a year's wages for the seven if I could lift them whole.  All I could think to do was stand over the hollows with my tongs like a fool defending seven eggs from a fox.  Mother, who had lately learned a better way of getting another body to do what yours could not, asked the man to lend her an ear.  He knelt where she pointed, the salt biting white stars into his fat red knees.  When the others came with their sacks and hammers it was he who put a finger to his lips.  In that flourishing stillness we could hear each shell give, a thin click like the breaking of a tooth on a plum stone.  Out of each narrow wound came a thousand white sparks, so light that the mere rising of the heat from the salt bore them up. 

That winter we burned the station charts for warmth, saving only the one with the black print of our two hands.  When the new king came to count his inheritance he found the heavens thick with stars and hired a man to name them after his ancestors.  In his book of names we are nowhere, unless you count the place where the paper lifts a little under your thumb.
