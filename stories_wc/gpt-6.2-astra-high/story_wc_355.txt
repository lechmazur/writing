In the final stillness before the fireworks, my son came into my workshop carrying the thing I had given him instead of a blessing.  The roof was rolled back so that the stars could serve as my stage lights, an economy of which I was inordinately proud.  Under them my instruments stood in shadow, except for the white mouths of the listening horns.  Into the largest of these Emil put his little red diary, still locked. 

Every year the town gave me the minute before the first rocket, to fill as I pleased from my catalog of silences.  I had played them the hush in a hive when the queen dies and the quite different hush in a theater when a dropped plate refuses to break.  To catalog the varieties of silence was my way of arguing that it mattered what came after the guns stopped.  I was that tiresome sort of pacifist who would bring a tuning fork, a beehive, and a troupe of jugglers to a debate, but would not let his opponent finish a sentence. 

Tonight I had promised them the silence of a street down which the first soldiers in twelve years were walking home.  It was there for the taking, just beyond my door, if only I could get my son to move his book. 

"You always said a locked diary clasp keeps the silence of the hands that hold it," Emil said.  "Can you recover this one without opening the book?" 

I remembered buying it for him on the morning he left, because he was seventeen and might still be susceptible to the eloquence of blank paper.  On the flyleaf I had written that no man obliged to keep an honest diary would ever consent to kill.  I had been rather pleased with the inscription and had often quoted it at meetings. 

By tuning frequencies on the brass wheel I could make the clasp vibrate in sympathy with the original pressure of a thumb.  The trick was to stop where the metal ceased to sing and the pressure passed into the small bones of the listener's ear.  I shut off the horn that fed the square; whatever the boy had come to tell me had not come addressed to them.  He stood with his forehead against mine so that our ears could share the narrow bell. 

First came the silence of my own thumb, brisk and conclusive as the period at the end of that inscription.  Then came his, and with it the astonishing sensation of trying to hold a door open against someone who had mistaken you for the wind.  Nothing in all my drawers of specimens had prepared me for the length of time it took that door to close. 

"There was something you wanted to say," I ventured, and he nodded without taking his head away. 

"I thought if I stood there long enough you would have to put your arms around me, if only to get me out of the doorway." 

The wheel was still turning under my fingers, hunting the frequency at which his grip on the book had at last relaxed.  I stopped it there myself; I had no business following him the rest of the way down that street.  Outside, the people who had politely held their tongues for my performance were beginning to shift their weight from foot to foot.  How many boys had walked the length of that terrible attentive street while their parents stood waiting for a man with a horn to explain what they felt? 

I reached for the speaking tube, meaning to give the finest apology ever delivered under those impartial stars.  Emil, who knew my gestures from the time he was small enough to sleep backstage in a fiddle case, took the tube first. 

"He's done now," my son told the town, and set it down so gently that there was no thump. 

From the square came the sound of somebody calling somebody else by a name plainly never intended to be used in public.  Then everybody was calling, and in the last dark instant before the sky caught fire I got both arms around my boy.
