As the moon clears the skyline, Kit feels the familiar loosening behind her teeth and knows she has perhaps ten minutes left to be herself.  She is spending them in a rusted library under stars, watching a man with one good thumb try to thread a needle.  Once she would have done it for him, but he has asked her not to spend any more of his life deciding what he cannot do.  Sol gets the thread through and grins around the end of it. 

Their friendship began sorting books at the prison library, back when this room had a roof and each had a number instead of a key.  Sol sorted by title; Kit, then a fastidious woman with a taste for jam, sorted by the stains that told her which books had been taken to supper.  It was how they found the missing last page of a love story, mustard fitting mustard like two banks of a river.  Kit molts personalities monthly, keeping her skills but losing the inclination to use them and the faces of those for whom she might.  Every month in prison Sol had handed her the mended book and let her find the seam.  Sometimes it was days before she would sit beside him again. 

Since the uprising Kit has been a vagrant penny poet, sleeping in doorways and hawking verses to people waiting in the bread lines.  Her best customers are the ones who have lost somebody and have exactly one thing left to say about them.  For a penny she makes the words come out in a shape that fits inside a folded hand.  Even now an account of a prison death can cost its teller a week's bread.  Under the coins in her coffee tin, below a secret false bottom that all the ration inspectors have missed, she keeps the trimmings.  They are scraps of blotting paper, each bearing a pale backward verse that comes right if you hold it to a light. 

The council means to rebuild the prison as a Hall of Peace, with a wall for the names of those who died here and no space for how they died.  Tomorrow the masons come to measure.  Kit has promised Sol a book of the dead that nobody can chisel down to a list.  The families have come back tonight to see it finished, and she can hear their shoes shifting on the gravel outside.  She wanted to make something beautiful out of the anarchy of those queues, and she had thought that meant putting everything in order.  Yet the strips lie in a heap on the floor, and every time she puts one beside another she has to decide whose grief comes first. 

Sol has sewn just one gathering, six thin leaves fastened at the foot instead of down the side.  He stands it in a bolt hole where a shelf once stood and the leaves fall open like a crocus.  "You don't have to make it one book," he says.  Light pours along the gutters between the leaves and the voice behind Kit's teeth goes out. 

The woman who comes after has a needle in her lap and no patience at all with sitting on cold iron.  She reads the words on the little standing flower: My sister whistled through the gap the warden left until the smoke got in.  Outside the gate people are waiting with empty hands, as if somebody promised them a show.  "Are these yours?" she asks the man beside her, who shakes his head and points beyond the gate. 

She carries the tin and the flower outside, where a woman recognizes the whistle and asks to have it back.  Kit pulls the thread free; it seems indecent to give the woman five strangers along with her sister.  Very carefully the woman winds the strip around her finger, pinches up a pair of petals, and asks for a pin.  By the time the moon has lifted clear of the broken rafters there are flowers in buttonholes all along the queue.  Not everybody wants one; a boy eats his father's words, quite deliberately, and Kit lets him.  She sees how it will be when the masons come to stretch their tape and find the wall they mean to build already standing, dressed for work and needing a wash.  Beside her the man holds out the needle, not to her but to the next pair of hands, and she decides she likes him.
