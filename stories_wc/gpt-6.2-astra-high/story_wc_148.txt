When my father was a boy he cried wolf for the pleasure of seeing grown men run, and when I was a boy they used to pay me to do it at weddings.  The bride would hover at the edge of the dance, pretending fright, until the groom had chased me out among the coats.  No one ever thought to ask what else my father had taught me. 

He was the carpenter at the lighthouse prison off Gull's Tooth, a jagged reef that showed a single yellow point even at high water.  I went back there when the commission sent word that divers were coming to look for the six men lost in the flood of my eleventh winter.  My father was past going anywhere, but he had something he wanted taken back.  From under his pillow he drew the walnut lattice from the confessional, still dark on one side where the prisoners had leaned their foreheads.  "Before they find the bodies," he said, "let those men finish what they were saying." 

Five of the divers were strangers, but the woman who took my line at the landing had her father's two front teeth, one riding a little over the other.  The lamp had stopped revolving years ago, and in the steady copper light of evening the gulls on its gallery looked cast rather than hatched.  I asked the woman to come down with me and to leave her tank behind. 

Below the chapel there is a labyrinth made not of wrong turnings but of all the right turnings that people have declined to take.  My father called finding your way through it chasing hush, and had shown me how when we went to oil the hinges.  To thread that labyrinth you need your own story, including the parts you usually get a good run at and jump over.  Other people's stories take you only as far as the place where you learned to repeat them. 

We entered through the scuffs on a narrow door left ajar for decades, not through the opening, which led to a cupboard full of brushes.  I held the lattice against the wood until each worn spot shone through its own little square, and the woman put her hand in mine.  Walnut sustains impossibilities better than oak; oak would have split under the weight of all that seawater standing above our heads. 

"They asked if I could swim," I said, and we were standing in the chapel on the morning the sea had taken the stairs.  I could smell the wet wool of the six men shut up there to wait for the launch, and hear a bare knuckle tapping at the grille.  Beyond it, in the priest's little box, I was a boy again, still dry above the waist.  One of the men asked me to fetch the key to the gallery where there was room for them all above the water. 

"I went for help," I said, and found myself back at the cupboard with a whitewash brush against my cheek.  Beside me the woman waited with the exquisite patience of somebody who has already waited most of her life.  I set the lattice to the wood again. 

"The key was already in my pocket," I said, and there it was in my small fist, with my thumb gone bloodless from pushing it the wrong way in the lock.  "One of them made a sound I thought was laughter, and I took the key out and ran." 

This time we stayed long enough to hear the rest of the sound; it was a man coughing the sea out of his mouth.  He put his lips to the wood and said very distinctly into the empty box what he wanted his little girl to know.  The other five did likewise, as men will follow the first man into cold water. 

With her forehead to the dark side of the lattice the woman listened and I listened on the pale.  She heard her father say where the money for her teeth was buried, under the rabbit hutch, in a treacle tin.  At that she sat down in the dust and laughed, and for once I knew what the sound was and whom it was for.  Afterward we took the five other messages upstairs, and I sat in the copper light on the landing while she wrote down mine.
