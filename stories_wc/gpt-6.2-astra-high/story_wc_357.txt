On the morning the last salt pear died, a green one knocked against the glass of Nell's ferry.  It was going up, as everything not tied down would go for the next hour.  Nell opened the mail slot and caught it by the stem.  Her father had grown salt pears; she knew the sandpaper feel of the skin before she bit into it and tasted home. 

Somewhere on Pomona a tree had lived through the rust that had taken every pear in the public rows.  She wanted to know what had kept it alive more than she wanted to keep her pilot's license, which was just as well.  When the tower ordered all orchard craft to moor for the inversion, she let out her sail. 

Pomona was a planet of orchards, but from below you could see how little of it was solid ground.  Most of what looked like hills were floating rock arches so thickly planted that the tips of neighboring branches touched.  Now water was pouring up through the arches, and the trick was to discover which stream had delivered the pear.  She trimmed the sail until she hung motionless beside a climbing sheet of water and could put her face into it.  No salt, no grit, only the faint copper taste of the wash that accelerates plant growth.  That ruled out the kitchen drains and left three nursery streams, two of them running yellow with rust.  She took the clear one. 

Navigating by water flow meant going where the charts showed nothing, down the twisting silver ribbon as it threaded arch after arch.  Petals turned about her in a whirling silence; she had never been so far from the noise of pumps.  Then she heard her own cough returned to her from below, first sharp, then soft, then so much like her father's little bedtime cough that she answered it.  Her answer came back a gathering of echoes, and she understood that all the openings ahead of her gave onto one hollow. 

Inside it, by the greenish light coming through those mouths, she saw an orchard growing upside down.  The trees had rooted in the roof when the world was right side up; now the pears were falling toward their roots and escaping through cracks with the water.  She hooked a branch and drew her boat in close enough to see that the base of each tree wore a little collar of blue thread. 

She knew those collars.  When somebody died on Pomona you cut a shoot from their favorite tree, bound a stone head to it, and sent the arrow down through the mist.  The weight was supposed to carry your grief where it could never come back.  At one funeral there had been no time to chip a proper head; a girl had tied on a broken arrow tip with a notch like a bite out of one edge.  She found it by touch, running her thumb around the swollen foot of a pear tree until the notch caught her nail. 

On the next branch a procession of rust mites had come to the end of a pear leaf and was turning stupidly back from the bitter plum that touched it.  In the public rows a mite could walk a mile and never leave a pear tree.  Here nobody had laid out rows; here a woman who loved plums had died the same week as a man who loved pears.  Nell sat a moment with her thumb in the notch, listening to water and the sound of her own breath multiplied.  She had come to find the source of resilience and had expected to carry it home in a bottle. 

By the time the tower caught her sail in its searchlight, she had a lapful of cuttings and wet feet and four minutes left.  She stripped a length of blue thread from her cuff and tied the cuttings together so she could have her hands free for the landing.  When the superintendent met her at the dock and asked which one they ought to plant, she put the whole dripping bundle in his arms.
