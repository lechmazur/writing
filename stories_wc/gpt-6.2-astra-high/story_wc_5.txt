When the roof of the winter road fell in, the children were walking home under the orchard, and nobody knew which branch of the passage they had taken.  Above them stood three hundred withered pear trees, deep in a subarctic crater where the sun had not cleared the rim in living memory.  The trees had outlasted the mirrors that once fed them sunlight; their roots still held the earth up, except where they didn't. 

Els heard the roof go from her workshop, a sigh so mild she thought her old dog had settled by the stove.  Then the school bell rang from the teacher's end of the road, and somebody began ringing the dinner bell from the village end, as if the children might come to either sound. 

As a flash-paper alchemist, Els made the little white squares that midwives slipped between a stillborn baby's heel and a thumb before they let anybody say the word.  Even the faintest heartbeat would spend a square in a brief blue flame that gave off no heat.  Once, under her own thumb, the flame had come so late that she had already drawn breath to say it.  That boy was six now, old enough to walk the winter road without her. 

She went first to the schoolhouse, not to the blocked mouth where the fathers were already digging.  Above the press on which the teacher printed reading books hung a storybook illustration plate, copper worn thin from printing the same picture for three generations.  It showed a hare being chased along the winter road by a fox too fat to follow him up the narrow air shafts.  The engraver had given the hare boots and spectacles but had been perfectly faithful about the shafts; the story was how children learned to find them. 

She took down the plate; in her apron she had the last bottle of the safekept starlight that primed her paper.  At each shaft the diggers uncovered, she lowered a strip on a thread until it touched the floor below.  She called down the same instructions every time: if you can hear me, hold this against your wrist.  Not a thread stirred. 

Her husband wanted to open the shaft nearest the village, because surely their boy would have run toward home.  She laid the copper hare at his feet and asked him to fetch the sweepings from her workshop.  She had spent the boy's infancy trying to translate heartbeats into maps, to show a midwife not just that blood moved but where it could not go.  The trick had been to stop watching one square and watch a pattern instead. 

Around each shaft she laid a little star of things the broom had gathered: paper twists from screws, torn labels, long curled shavings from her husband's plane.  Anything that had once been wood would take the starlight; a root, so long as it lived, could carry the pulse of a hand.  She pared a patch of bark from each exposed root and tucked the ends of the star against the wet grain.  Then she poured out the stars her mother had gathered on clear nights, one bright drop to each scrap, until the bottle held only her magnified fingerprint. 

Below, she hoped, some child had remembered what the hare had done when even his spectacles could not show him the way: put out a paw and take hold of a root.  Els knelt with her knife still under a flap of bark, holding it open so the shaving would not slip.  Snow slowly filled the hollow between her knuckles, and she let it fill.  There is a silence while the world holds its breath in which you can hear your own heart, loud and stupid, insisting that you live.  She waited through that, too. 

Three shavings flashed at three different shafts, each on the side toward the old pump tree.  She pressed the plate facedown in the snow and lifted it; the hare stood in his proper country now.  On that print she traced the three lines inward with a blackened finger; they met where the fat fox had got his belly stuck.  Her husband set his spade there. 

They came down on a tangle of polished wood and small bare hands, the children strung along the root in order of height as if waiting to have their school picture taken.  Her boy came up last, his mouth full of dirt because he had tried to answer her.  After the others had gone he helped her seal the cuts in the bark with wax from the stoppers of her bottles.  At the last shaft he kept his dirty thumb over hers long after the wax had set, and she did not take her hand away.
