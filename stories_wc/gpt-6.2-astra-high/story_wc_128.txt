After the symphony ended, Nell Rusk climbed to the roof of the customs house to put a red velvet seat into the public library.  She had only the ticket, of course, but she carried it flat on both palms, as if a small soprano might still be sitting on it.  Up there the stars supplied most of the light, and a breeze off the water kept trying to read ahead of everyone.  The librarian had a dish of oyster shells for holding pages down.  Nell selected a particularly frilly one for the ticket, in recognition of the occasion. 

She brokered cargo on the fish quay and could reckon the cost of an empty cubic foot all the way from Cork to Lisbon.  The velvet seat had cost her nothing, which was not to say that nobody had paid for it.  A shipowner had given her his season ticket for getting six grand pianos out of the rain before so much as a hammer swelled.  She meant to initiate a new kind of loan with it, starting with Dot Bell, who washed the white shirts in which the orchestra took its bows. 

Their friendship had begun with a brown envelope addressed in error to Number Eight, Fish Quay, instead of Number Eight, Fish Lane.  Inside was a locket photo of a girl in a man's evening shirt, along with two shillings and a request for an enlargement.  Nell took it round to the photographer in person; she knew what became of loose silver in the dead letter office.  When Dot came to thank her, Nell asked about the cuffs, which in the picture were rolled up nearly to the girl's armpits.  Dot said her father had lent her his best shirt to audition for the conservatory; it had been easier to get a scholarship than to leave six younger children.  Neither of them made the mistake of saying it had all been for the best.  They had taken their tea together on ironing days ever since. 

Now Dot came up between the shelves, smelling of starch, and looked at the little square of red pasteboard.  "Saturday nights I do the hotel linen," she said.  "They won't eat Sunday breakfast off bare wood just because I'm listening to Brahms." 

Around them the readers kept their eyes down, with the awful concentration of people trying to let somebody refuse a kindness in private.  Nell felt the heat in her cheeks; she had allowed for every expense except the price of sitting still.  She was about to offer to do the linen herself when Dot took her hands and inspected them, not unkindly.  "You would put a lovely crease in a bill of lading," she said. 

Nell looked down through the iron railing at the people still queuing at the box office for next Saturday.  "How much to hire somebody decent for the evening?" 

Dot named a sum, and the librarian, who had apparently given up reading the same page, whistled.  It was exactly what it ought to have been, thought Nell, and about a quarter of what a businessman would pay for velvet under his trousers.  She turned over the shell and on its chalky underside worked out a tariff in pencil.  Sell the seat one week in four, at the going rate; lend it the other three, with enough in the kitty for a pair of competent hands whenever the borrower needed them. 

"Don't put my picture in the paper," said Dot, which Nell understood to mean yes. 

The librarian opened the ledger, and a subdued, delicious uproar went through the stacks.  Someone shook a newspaper in lieu of applause; the boy who trimmed the reading lamps wanted to know whether a fellow could borrow the seat for his mother.  Dot put his name down to follow hers and made him promise to ask his mother first.  By the time the lamps went out Nell had sold the fourth Saturday to the shipowner, who was tickled to be buying a little of his own present back. 

The following Saturday, Dot sat in red velvet with her cuffs rolled up exactly once.  At the interval she wrote a note for the boy's mother, to be slipped under the oyster shell.  "Wear what you please, dear.  They turn the lights out, and then there is nothing but the sound."
