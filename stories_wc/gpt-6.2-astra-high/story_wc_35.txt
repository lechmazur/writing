Rain had passed over the desert fortress of Kesh, leaving every sleeper caught at the close of the same unspoken dream.  Behind their lids a woman stood at an iron gate, trying to get her apron off over her head.  Whatever came next would not come, and nobody woke except Mara, the cook, who had bitten her tongue.  In the salt of her blood she tasted a button of horn, two front teeth pulling at a knot, and the particular caraway soap her mother used to boil.  Other cooks tasted iron in blood; Mara tasted what the blood remembered. 

It had been her mother's business before hers to sandpaper rough memories, as the garrison contract delicately put it.  A man brought back from patrol with his hands locked on an imaginary rifle could eat a bowl of their thin soup and open his fingers enough to hold his child.  The grit went into the stock salt, and the salt went down a chute to the cold store, since remorse was emotionally contagious through touch even after the owner could no longer feel it.  Mara had never known her mother to treat herself. 

She knelt beside a sleeping scullion and shook his bare ankle; instantly her own hands went up to wrestle with the strings behind her neck.  On the stones under his cot a white crust was climbing the legs. 

The store had been cut off when the dunes swallowed the lower town; even the chute had a bend no lamp could see around.  But the sea that had made this desert still ran underneath it, and after rain its buried streets took water like the channels of a reef.  Her mother had taught her to find lost cellars by analyzing tidal pool formations, a useful trick when all that stood between supper and starvation was a buried jar of beans.  The round pools held still; the long ones emptied southward, one after another, until only a square of wet sand trembled like the lid of a pot.  She dug there with a skillet until she struck the rungs. 

Below the last rung stood the coral pavilion, icebound beneath the dunes, its delicate pink vault just the shape of the jelly molds that came down in the family.  Its iron gate stood open.  In the doorway lay the apron, twisted into a rope, with one end fast to the gate and the other trapped under a slab of fallen ice.  Mara put her tongue to the salt furred along its hem, and the dream went on. 

Her mother was trying to haul a boy up through a hole in the ice, gathering apron string between blistered fingers.  She had sent him down the chute with a mallet because there was a jam and his hips were narrow.  Now his shoes were tapping the ice from underneath, very politely at first, like a guest who could wait.  Then the apron came up light.  Before the taste faded Mara got his name, the last thing her mother called down the hole, long after there was any use in calling. 

Above her, through the listening ice, she heard a hundred pairs of heels begin to tap.  The stored remorse of one woman was about to subsume the little private worlds of everyone who had slept on those wet stones.  The contract provided for a leak: dump flour down the chute until the paste set and the salt froze hard again.  Somebody had done that once already; the ice that held the apron was thick with dough. 

Instead Mara took her skillet and broke the slender coral props, one after another, working backward toward the ladder as the pink roof settled and cracked.  Salt and ice went down together into water too vast to take their taste, and her trade went with them.  But the little bones in their canvas coat she caught against her belly in the skillet and brought up into the clean air. 

In the kitchen the scullion was sitting up and rubbing his heels; when she set the skillet down he asked her who Emmet was.  So the water had left them that.  She sat on the floor beside him and told him what she knew, not letting go of his hand when the shame went through it.
