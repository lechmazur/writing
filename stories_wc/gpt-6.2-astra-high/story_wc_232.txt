Of the thirty children handed across the border that summer, Emil was the only one who went back to live in the town that had given them up.  He became an antique map dealer and kept a second chair by the stove, although nobody ever sat long in it.  People came to buy the kingdom as it used to be, in its old green borders, and did not much care to hear what it had been like to cross that green line in a flour sack. 

Then a woman his own age sat down in the chair and took off her left shoe.  On the sole, under a coat of clear varnish, was a little pointing hand and the number nine. 

"They sent me the shoe when my foster father died," she said.  "Did your mother write down who we were?" 

His mother had worked at the border foundry, where they cast letters in three alphabets until the king decided that one would do.  She had marked each departing child's shoe with a hot piece of type and copied the number beside a name in the paymaster's empty book.  Emil had seen her wrap it in oilcloth the night she sent him through.  "When the soldiers go, come fetch it," she had said, as if asking for a loaf from the baker.  The soldiers had outlasted the king, his mother, and most of the people who could have shown him the way. 

He showed the woman what had come back to him instead of a shoe: a type foundry matrix chip, the broken bit of copper in which that little hand had been cast.  He let the hollow fill with warm candle wax and peeled out a hand with a split thumbnail, identical to hers. 

"I can find the cavern," he said.  "What I never could find was the doorway of decisions." 

His mother had called it that because there, out of sight of either guardhouse, a parent could still take a child back. 

At midsummer the cavern bloomed; daylight fell through rents in its roof onto whole beds of pink campion, and bees went humming down into the earth.  They got there as the creek was beginning to rise with rain from the hills.  Water slid over the gravel on which he remembered the parents standing, and he saw the six low openings in the rock begin to glimmer like half-shut eyes. 

"I have tried them all in dry weather," he said.  "They pinch out." 

The woman took off both shoes this time and stood a while in her stockings, watching the flower beds shrink.  "You went through here in a sack," she said at last.  "Did they ever let it get wet?" 

Her way of seeing was empirically intuitive, like his knack for knowing from the drag of a fingertip whether a map had actually been folded on the creases inked across it.  Before he could answer she was climbing the bank on her knees, following ants home as they carried their white young out of the drowning flowers.  He saw a dark seam swallow the procession at the height of his shoulder.  From below it looked scarcely wider than a saw cut; from above, behind a lip of rock, he could put his whole leg in. 

There were three steps down on the other side, and on the third his fingers met the raised thumb of a tiny hand pressed into the mortar while it was soft.  Under the step lay a flat parcel the size of the space he had kept clear on his counter for forty years. 

Nine was Alma, whose mother had signed with a cross and whose father had signed with a second cross almost on top of the first.  Beside Emil's name, in the column where his mother had entered the addresses to which the children might someday be returned, she had written the foundry on Mill Street, upstairs, knock hard.  He had often wondered if she had been glad to see him go; he had never imagined her sitting up there listening. 

Back at the shop Alma put the kettle on while he turned the green kingdom over in the window and pinned a notice to its blank back.  "If you came through the mountain," he wrote, "come in."  He brought two more chairs down from upstairs.
