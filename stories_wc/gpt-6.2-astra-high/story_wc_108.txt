The morning I found out I wasn't the last person in a fall, a boy of nine asked me to scratch under his cast.  He had ridden up to my dressing room in the bundle of torn canvas the stagehands used for stuffing the landing bags.  I used the blunt end of my eyeliner, and he sighed with such extravagant relief that for a moment neither of us wanted to talk business.  Then he said, "Next time the horse goes down, could you get your hand out from under?" 

I had doubled for Lady Linn for sixteen years, through six horses and two changes of actress.  I feared heights, confined spaces, open water, and the particular sound a horse makes when it knows the floor is going.  My rule was that the fear stayed and the trick changed: widen the ledge, unlace the boot, let the horse see the hinge.  I had been rather proud of reaching thirty without breaking a bone. 

The boy took a whispering seashell half from his good ear and fitted it to mine.  I heard the little catch in my own breathing and, underneath it, a dozen children breathing with me.  Every stunt double wore the matching half against her breastbone; we were told the sea took what hurt we couldn't bear. 

"Lovely place, the sea," the boy said, and showed me the rust on his blanket. 

We lived and played in the old iron library, which had lost its roof when the town lost its foundry.  Under the stars the stacks made a fine theater, with galleries for the paying customers and the old book lifts to bring us up through the stage.  My father ran those lifts; it was he who had fitted my shell on my first morning, warming it between his hands.  I wanted to ask him nothing, which was how I knew I believed the boy. 

Tonight Linn was to fall from the astronomy gallery, catch a trailing banner, and swing out over the stalls.  "If I walk," I said, "they'll put another girl up there." 

The boy nodded and held out his cast, covered in what looked like smudged games of noughts and crosses.  "We're not allowed to know where the others sleep," he said, "but we can always hear the lifts." 

He'd been keeping score as the supper lift went round; grease from the dishes had blurred the tally marks into bars.  It took me until the first customers came in to understand that the crosses weren't tens; they were the clank when a lift went sideways at a junction.  I knew those clanks as other girls know the notes of a scale.  What he had written on his arm was the route to every occupied drawer in the library. 

My father watched the stairs when a performer had cold feet; he had taught me himself that a frightened creature runs for daylight.  I went down instead, into an iron box scarcely wider than my shoulders, and worked the foot treadle in the dark.  The cast bumped my hip all the way down.  At each stop I whispered a name the boy had given me, and a child climbed in, until there was hardly room for my ribs to move.  I told them what the matter was; they passed my hand from hand to hand so I could feel how much air there still was between us. 

When my father rang for Lady Linn, what came up was the sea. 

Some of the children wore slings and some had no visible injury at all, a fact the audience appeared to find immensely reassuring.  They clapped, uncertainly at first, and my father held out his hands to help the little ones offstage.  Then the boy with the cast asked me for the eyeliner.  On his white forearm he wrote the name of the horse that had fallen on us both.  I rolled up my sleeve beside him, and where he bore the weight of a horse I had a scattering of freckles.  Nobody clapped for that. 

"Tell them what else you're afraid of," my father said, not kindly, from the wings.  He knew exactly what it would cost me to lose the man who had warmed the shell. 

"Him," I said, pointing, and my finger shook so badly that the boy put his good hand under my elbow.  It was the steadiest gesture I had ever made on that stage.  Then the woman who played Linn stepped between my father and the children, and for once it was the star who covered the doubles.  She was still standing there, arms spread, when the last of us walked out through the stalls.
