The boy who had followed Inez home set a carrier pigeon's message tube on her supper plate, very gently, as though it still had a bird attached.  In the quiet courtyard at dusk the little click of brass on china carried all the way up to the loft, and the pigeons stirred.  He had come over the roof from the fever court; the soot on his bare heels left black commas on her flags. 

"Will you send it to the island office?" he asked.  "They still believe we're sick." 

Inez had been the port's storm recorder for thirty years, and the island observatory kept six of its homing birds in her loft against the days when the packet could not sail.  She was meticulous enough to enter even an uneventful day in ink, having once seen a week of calm invented afterward to fill a blank.  What she loved about the work was that nobody watched her do it; alone with a gauge, she could practice being honest without the pleasure of being thought so. 

She unscrewed the tube.  She had given his mother that dented tube to send back lists of what the court needed, rolled up tight against the soup spills.  This time it held a petition in one hand, signed in one hand, declaring all forty residents well and demanding the removal of the chain across the only gate. 

"Did forty people tell you this?" 

He considered lying, which she could tell because he sat down first.  "My sister wants to be married before she shows," he said at last.  "All I know is she's not got fever." 

The boy's mother had died in the first week of the sickness, when Inez herself had carried soup to that chain and been grateful for the iron between them.  Six months later she still set down the pot in the same spot and never asked who ate.  It occurred to her now to wonder who, exactly, had a key. 

They could hear the spoons on the other side of the wall, a domestic music so like her own that she had long ago ceased to hear it.  Along its foot ran the old milk hatches, sealed with thin boards when the building was divided into flats.  By progressing laterally from hatch to hatch, knocking with the cap of the tube, Inez could speak to each kitchen without anybody having to raise a voice.  At the third hatch someone knocked back with the old impatient double rap of her sister Luz, once the court nurse and now its keeper. 

"Nobody's been hot since Easter," Luz said, so quietly that Inez had to kneel.  "But the owners can put the widows out the day that chain comes down." 

The decree from the island had promised a bed and a ration to every soul inside a fever gate, and nothing at all to anybody outside.  What Luz had done was easy enough to intuit; Inez had only to imagine herself holding the key, with eight households depending on her grip.  Still she asked, and waited, because what a woman might do for eight households was not necessarily what this woman had done. 

"The doctor ordered us opened in April," Luz said.  "I asked the men to leave the chain to me." 

It was a rogue quarantine zone, then, these eight kitchens fragrant with onions and the cooling ashes of supper, and her soup had helped maintain it.  Heads were close to all the hatches now.  Somebody wanted the chain off tonight; somebody else wanted to know where precisely the boy proposed to put her bedridden husband.  Nobody asked for the petition back. 

Inez took the petition to her plate and turned it over, finding on its clean side just room enough for a weather report of the kind nobody had ever taught her to write.  She gave the date, the hour, the words she had heard from Luz, and the names of the families who said they would need beds if the owners came.  She did not certify a single person's health.  She addressed it to the island relief clerk, care of the observatory.  At the bottom she put her own address beside the bedridden man's name; her front room had a door wide enough for a carried bed. 

"Read it back," said Luz through the hatch, and when Inez had done so she heard her sister getting to her feet on the other side.  The boy held the bird while she fastened the tube, his sooty feet quite still now in the deepening blue.  After it flew they listened for the chain, which made remarkably little noise considering how long it had been there.
