After cutting off all contact with her sister, Ada kept the doorknocker ring because there was nowhere to throw it that would not make a noise.  She laid it on a pad of wool in the sanctuary, and for nine years it lay still.  On the night the last lintel began to sag, the wool jumped. 

The other shadow keepers had gone home by then, taking their shutters and tea tins and the little mirrors they held to the mouths of the suffocating.  Only Ada still had a patient, the narrow shadow her sister Ruth had shed getting out of the schoolhouse fire.  The child had got out through a window; the shadow, being flat, had gone on looking for a way under the door.  Even now it would encroach on every patch of starlight that fell between the ribs of the roof, feeling for that gap.  Ada cupped a hand behind its head so it would not keep knocking against the charcoal.  In all that ashen sanctuary hers was the one palm that had never left a print; she had learned to touch so lightly. 

Across the room the ring struck the floor and a woman's voice said, "There you are."  Ruth sounded like their mother now. 

Once, linking through scars had been as ordinary to the sisters as sharing a bed.  Each had caught hold of the same hot ring on the schoolhouse door before Ada thought of the window.  The brass still answered to either scar, no matter how much wool or how many miles lay between them.  Night after night Ada had called Ruth to lend the shadow breath, until one morning her sister fell asleep standing at the washtub and split her lip.  When Ada packed up the ring, Ruth had thanked her with a haste that still hurt. 

Now the fading threshold of the schoolhouse was hardly deep enough to cast a stripe across the floor.  At dawn the town would pull the ruin down to put up houses, and whatever was still inside would have no way out. 

"I came to fetch what belongs to me," Ruth said. 

Ada picked up the brass and the charcoal floor ran out from her feet in a narrow bridge to the place where Ruth lay in her distant bed.  The shadow crept onto it, belly down, then stopped about where the door would have been.  At once a terrible industry of breathing began at the other end, the grown woman laboring to fill the child, the child too full already to take any more. 

Ada felt for the matchbox in her apron.  Her old instructor used to say that a tender shadow keeper burns bridges carefully, a plank at a time, lest anything alive be stranded in the middle.  Nine years ago she had set a match to the whole span; she had been so sure there were only two choices, to call or not to call. 

"Let me be out of breath a minute," Ruth said. 

The silence that followed was as full of things Ada wanted to put right as their mother's workbasket had been full of socks.  She sat in it with the child.  Presently she noticed that the little back under her hand was as hard and round as a loaf, and had not once gone slack.  All those years of trying to teach the suffocating to breathe, and she had never asked the child to empty itself. 

"Here," she said, laying the cold ring flat against its mouth, as the keepers had once held their little mirrors.  "Make a cloud." 

She felt the small ribs give, and saw in the brass a pale coin of mist that she did not polish away.  On the far shore Ruth began to cry, a sound neither sister mistook for a summons.  The shadow stood up to walk the rest of the way.  Ada lit the bridge behind it plank by plank, waiting each time until she could see the sole of a small foot lift clear.  At the lintel the girl looked back, and Ada nearly told her not to be afraid.  Instead she held up the brass with its print of a single breath, and the girl went on into her own long body. 

At dawn the men who came to take the place down found Ada prying the strike plate from the schoolhouse door.  She put it with the ring in the pad of wool; between them they would make a serviceable knocker for her own front door.  She thought she might write to Ruth when it was up, not to ask for anything, just to tell her where to knock.
