The first thing the pilgrim did after she arrived was put out one of the two lamps, which cost her a perfectly good prophecy.  Sella could see what was coming only through overlapping shadows, and already she had glimpsed a white sail folding up like a napkin. 

"I know what you saw," the pilgrim said.  "It's what sails do when there's no wind.  Meanwhile my boy is out there in the milk with six other boys, and the tide doesn't need wind to take a boat." 

By milk she meant the white fog that sometimes came so far into the musical crystal cave that Sella could taste it on her pillow.  The pilgrim unwrapped something small and wet from her handkerchief and stood listening to the high, sweet notes the tide drew from the galleries below. 

"Once, people told me, you could make a little sound big enough to steer by." 

She held out a cave pearl, the kind that grew around a grain of grit wherever dripping water kept the grit in motion.  Sella knew this one by the nick in it from her own front tooth.  She had been eight when she dropped it down the well at the harbor mouth, conducting experiments her mother had expressly forbidden.  She had wanted then to magnify everyday magic: the scritch of a match becoming a bonfire of sound, a kiss on the pearl going down through stone to tickle a swimmer's feet.  Then the gift of sight had come, and people had begun to climb the headland on their knees, and nobody had asked for a kiss since. 

"My boy went down on a rope for it," said the pilgrim.  "He left it with me before they sailed, in case the fog came down and they couldn't find the channel." 

From the well to the channel up the middle of the reef was one long shelf of sounding stone.  Every fishing child knew to put an ear to the wet bottom boards and listen for the grinding of pebbles underneath.  Sella set the pearl in its old cup, a hollow worn in the crystal lip of her windowsill.  Out past that window a bright darkness had swallowed sea and sky; even the gulls were keeping their opinions to themselves. 

"What do I tell them?" 

The pilgrim looked at her almost with pity.  "Nobody wants to know what's going to happen, love.  They want to know where the hole in the reef is." 

Against the pearl Sella tapped the come-to-supper call that every mother in the port beat on her iron triangle at dusk.  The notes went down like coins, and for a long minute there was only the steadily unpredictable music of water finding a way into stone. 

Then someone knocked back.  Not from the reef; the knock came from directly under her feet. 

She almost lit the other lamp before the pilgrim caught her hand.  That was the hardest part afterward to explain: how much easier it would have been to see those boys drown than to stand there not knowing and work out what to do. 

The headland was hollow; at flood tide the sea came far enough inside to inhabit rooms no one with lungs had ever seen.  But this was ebb, and beneath her window the water was sucking out through a throat too narrow for a boat.  The boys had found the channel and come too far along it in the white. 

Sella stripped the braided cords off her prophecy curtains and tied them end to end while the pilgrim kept up the supper call at the window.  They pulled up seven boys, the last of them still gripping the tin spoon he had knocked with, before they heard the boat go to pieces in the dark. 

Sella had seen the sail fold but not this coming: seven cold mouths to feed and the astonishing appetite of her own body for the job.  She put the kettle on. 

Years later, when a stranger asked what unseen forces sang inside the headland, a pilot might still take him up to see the pearl.  But for the sound most of them remembered, you needed only a spoon, a pot, and somebody at the window who wanted you home.
