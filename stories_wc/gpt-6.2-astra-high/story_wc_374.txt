While the forests held their elections, my mother came up the lavender bluff with a small, private November soaking the shoulders of her good coat.  Above her the aurora shook out green pleats, exactly as it used to do on the wrapping of the peppermints she kept for election nights.  She had brought the peppermints, too, although by then she could no longer taste them. 

People called me their luminous skeptic, because I had the blue reading light in my cheek that all county auditors used to get, and because I would not certify a miracle.  I wanted to learn why some people carried weather with them before the county put my mother in a tiled room with a drain.  Her landlord had already sent photographs of the ceiling downstairs. 

On the bluff, the retired forest tellers were setting out folding chairs in their several patches of fog or sleet or insistent, almost horizontal sunshine.  Once a year they still came here to count a vote that the sky service now counted in the blink of a diode.  They had invited me for years, and for years I had sent a nice official envelope saying there was nothing left to count. 

My mother slipped her tin compass ring off her swollen finger and put it on mine with the stamped N toward my nail.  "That's where you put north when you find it," she said.  There was no needle, which as a child I had considered a pretty grave defect in a prize from a cereal box. 

Below us each stand of trees was voting with its roots for the place where the winter rain should fall.  The old tellers had sampled those votes in the air, turning as the pollen on their tongues went from bitter to sweet.  No single tongue could give a bearing, any more than one ear could tell you where in a house somebody was playing the piano.  It took a hillside of people calling out the changes and walking until their separate guesses began to converge.  I remembered that much from school, a little animation of an emergent compass made of children in pointed hats.  But in the film the children went home dry. 

My mother's mouth had gone sweet thirty winters ago and stayed that way until even peppermint was no more than a little cold stone.  That was the winter the service cut the bottomland willows off its maps because the reservoir was supposed to drown them.  Only the reservoir never got built. 

I asked the tellers if anyone had ever signed for the last bottomland tally, and one by one they shook their heads.  My nice official envelopes had been going into a file marked Refusals of Witness, which my mother now produced from under her coat. 

The old job was terrestrially cosmic, as my mother liked to say: you stood in sheep shit and something moved among the stars.  By my cheeklight I unfolded the paper chart she still carried against her breast, the ink miraculously intact under a skin of sandwich wrap.  Until a tally was delivered, said the fine print, the mirrors would keep each teller's allotment trained on the teller, for ease of transport.  The sky service had erased a destination without ever troubling to cancel the deliveries.  All those years I had looked for something wrong with her. 

I put a grain of willow pollen from her collar on my tongue and asked the others to give me north.  Then came the thin cries of sweeter and the low calls of bitter, the folding chairs left open like books as we went down.  At every fork I turned the little tin N toward the sweetest chorus so my mother could see by my cheek which way to step.  At the bottom, in a grove of willows as thick around as thirty years could make them, my mother found the old receiving post by touch.  I pressed my county seal to its tarnished eye, and the rain came off her shoulders like a shawl somebody else had agreed to wear. 

All over the grove lay little settlements of weather, already trading at their edges, and we stood there to see what they would make.  She unwrapped a peppermint and gave me half, the way she used to when there was still a bus to ride home on.  "Awful," she said with wonder, and ate the rest of hers.
