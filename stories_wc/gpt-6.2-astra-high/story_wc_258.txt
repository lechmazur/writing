After the cryptocurrency crashes, I came home to sell my grandmother's rain-beaded skylight loft and found a bride on the stairs with a pillowcase over her head.  She stood up and the case fell in folds about her shoulders, and there was the scalloped edge my grandmother put on all her work.  She wanted to know if it was true that my grandmother could get a wedding dress out of that much linen. 

People called the old woman the onyx-ink cartomancer because she worked in black, rolling her little printers' brayer across a piece of cloth and taking its impression on a card.  She read a future in the weave, never charging more than a woman could pay, and the one future she flatly refused to read was mine.  I went to school to map cultural patterns, which was a roundabout way of trying to find out what my grandmother did for a living. 

The bride was called Nell, and her mother had died promising her that dress.  I let her in.  My employer had paid us in coins that would no longer buy a loaf, and the loft was the only thing standing between me and the bread line.  Still, I had been steadily curious about those cards since I was six, and here at last was a question I could put to them. 

In the drawer under the cutting table we found a card as finely pitted as Nell's pillowcase, right down to a skipped thread like a tiny white eyelash.  On the back somebody had penciled three fractions and the word Mercy.  Nell knew Mercy, who had taught her mother to drive, and took me to the fish counter where she worked. 

Mercy read the back and wiped her hands on her apron before she would touch the card.  Three quarters of a yard of lawn, half a yard of lace, a quarter share in an ivory button, she translated, laughing at the last entry.  The things she had lent Nell's mother could have fitted into a hatbox, and after the wedding she'd taken them home again.  Her own card, printed with the herringbone of her confirmation suit, carried the names of the two women who had helped dress her.  Could she see if it was still in the deck? 

Reverse engineering my grandmother's readings turned out to be less like cracking a cipher than like unpicking a seam without tearing what lay on either side.  The deck worked by multiplying paths: a girl who knew only one woman could, through that woman's card, draw on the wardrobes of half a parish.  Nobody had to lend anything, Mercy said, and nobody needed to know why you had come up short. 

By dusk the loft was full of women shaking rain from parcels; somebody had tracked down the button, still attached to a gentleman's evening glove.  Nell stood on the cutting table in her stocking feet while I ran the measuring tape from her waist to her ankle.  Not one piece was long enough for the skirt she wanted, but pinned to a petticoat in overlapping scallops, they fell like the feathers on a dove's breast. 

When I reached up to pin the pillowcase over her hair, she bent to confide what her mother had actually promised.  Not that she would have a dress, but that on the morning she put it on there would be women there who remembered her mother young.  Mercy, threading a needle with one eye shut, was already telling us about the driving test and the policeman who had dived into a hedge. 

I sold the loft; there was no money to keep the rain from coming through that glass another winter.  But I took down the linen curtains before I went, and at the kitchen table in Mercy's flat I showed Nell how to roll the ink.  All those years I had gone to my grandmother with an empty hand, demanding to know my fortune as if there were such a thing as mine alone.  On the back of my curtain card Nell wrote one pillowcase, and underneath, in a hand so like her mother's that for a moment Mercy couldn't speak, her name.
