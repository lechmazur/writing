The morning Jo came to claim her son, the steps to the floating rock dojo ended six feet below the door.  By the time she got a knee over the sill, history had rewritten itself to include a landing, and the skin off her shin seemed an extravagant price for having arrived on time.  The referee gave her a square of lint and told her that her other self was already on the mat. 

It was the jar Jo noticed first, because somebody had put a knitted hat over the lid.  Under the brim she could just make out the word DUPLICATE, and under the glass the brain lay in its broth with a shoelace of bright electrodes trailing to a little tin speaker. 

"He made me that," the speaker said, in the voice Jo still expected to hear when she opened her mouth.  Ben sat between the red and white practice circles, unraveling the cuff of his sweater for more yarn. 

The rock had lifted free the night the valley split into two pasts, each with a perfectly good claim to having happened.  In one, the hospital had grown Jo a body around a copy of her brain; in the other, it had saved her brain but not the rest of her.  Which was the clone depended on when they'd printed the label.  Neither past had made an extra Ben. 

Up here, beyond the reach of the alterations, the old practice of settling disputes on the mat had acquired the force of law.  Down below, a court might change its ruling halfway through a kiss goodbye. 

The woman in the jar wanted Ben to live in the hospital quarters, where she could supervise everything from the stove to the locks.  Jo wanted him back in the house where the pencil marks ran up the pantry door.  She'd brought the door, in case the house had never had a pantry by sundown.  When the referee asked why she wanted custody, she turned it so he could see the little ladder of dates. 

"That's how tall I was," said Ben, who at eight had a way of slicing through pretense that no adult on the rock could match.  "He's asking what you want me for now." 

Jo looked down at the blood working through the lint, a sight that interested her precisely as much as the weather on a distant hill.  Between the taking of the impression and the waking in the new body, she had undergone what the hospital called ego death.  She had come out knowing whose shoes these were, whose boy this was, without the faintest twitch of gladness at the fit of either knowledge. 

"I thought if you came home I might start feeling things again," she said. 

Ben pulled the yarn so hard a whole row came loose.  From the speaker came a hiss like fat hitting a pan. 

"And I thought if I kept him where I could see him, I'd stop being afraid," said the woman under the hat. 

They had come prepared to prove which one was fit to be his mother, not to discover how badly each wanted him to be hers.  The referee asked Ben what he had come prepared to do. 

"Finish the ears," he said, and held up the hat to show two limp flaps, one still on the needle. 

Before the split, Jo had taught him how to vivify a rabbit with nothing but a stocking and the contrary motions of two fingers, one dipping as the other rose.  She slipped her hand inside the hat and the ears began their old indignant argument, taking turns being absolutely right.  The speaker supplied the squeaks, which Jo had forgotten were essential and Ben evidently had not, because he came in on cue with the sneeze that laid both ears flat.  His laugh did nothing to her heart, but for once she wasn't listening to her heart to find out how it was doing. 

"Could we live up here," he asked, "where you stay the same people even when I go out to play?" 

Jo glanced at the practice cells, barely wide enough for a cot, and at the socket by each bed where the visiting jars plugged in. 

"I can come off the hospital circuit," said the speaker, "if somebody will do the locks and stove." 

"I can do those," Jo said, and they asked the referee to put both names on the lease and the custody order. 

That evening she set the pantry door against the cell wall and took a pencil from behind her ear.  Ben put his back to it, then changed his mind and went out to play, and she let him.
