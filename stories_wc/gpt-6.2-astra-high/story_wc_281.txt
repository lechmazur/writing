The morning after the static shock, my mother agreed to sell the light painting studio, or I agreed and heard it in her voice.  That was the trouble that prompted the telepathy pill recall: a thought could go out on the wire and come back wearing the other person's coat.  A spark off the rug was enough to cross a wire that wasn't even there. 

Before the stroke she had made portraits by moving a lamp around a sitter in the dark while a shutter stood open.  Afterward she directed my hands, the pills dissolving under our tongues like the two halves of a communion wafer.  Now a builder wanted the place for its river frontage, and his offer would pay for somebody to come on mornings and wash her hair. 

My father, an affectionate skeptic, kissed the crown of my head when I told him what I had heard.  "Honey," he said, "in forty years I never heard your mother agree to a price without naming a higher one."  He took the contract off her tray and put her lamp there instead. 

I wanted to devise a test she couldn't fail just because the stroke had taken away the use of everything but her left thumb.  At the clinic they had tried to get answers through somatic markers, the little bodily events that happen before you know how you feel.  Her pulse jumped at the sound of my name, but it also jumped for the ice machine.  The lamp was better because she knew its weight and because you could leave the shutter open as long as it took.  We used to tell sitters we worked in darkness to illuminate hidden pathways; mostly what we meant was that everybody moved a little, even holding still. 

From the drawer of things that made good light I took a muscovite geode sliver, thinner than the white skin inside an egg.  She had used it to put scales on children who wanted to be fish.  I propped its broad end in a pinch of wax beside her hand and rested the tip on her thumbnail.  The least stir tilted the little mirror, sending a bead of lamplight racing halfway up the wall.  Her tremor made a fractally precise picture of the river in flood, each wandering line breaking into smaller wanderings when we enlarged the photograph.  It was beautiful and I very nearly called it an answer. 

My father sat down where he could see her face instead of the river.  "Ask her to do the one thing nobody ever asks a woman with a tremor to do," he said.  "Ask for more." 

She could not still the point, but she could give us a great wild slash of light, and after a rest she could do it again.  We stood letters on her tray in rows and ran a finger along them, one slash to pick the row and another to pick the letter.  To make sure it wasn't my own desire I saw blazing on the plaster, Dad showed her a word while I was outside.  I came back and took down TROUT, which was what he'd written, and then GRAVY, which was not.  He laughed until he had to sit down again; this had been the most notorious dish of their honeymoon. 

It took three mornings to get her terms for the sale.  She didn't ask for more money.  She wanted the builder to take the glass bricks out of the river wall whole, and she wanted enough of them set into her bedroom wall to catch the headlights crossing the bridge.  She'd been lying there a year watching cars go past on the ceiling in plain white. 

That winter the first green brick went in, with its old flaw like a willow leaf folded inside it.  When a truck came over the water the leaf opened and the whole room swam, and nobody had to tell anybody what to see.
