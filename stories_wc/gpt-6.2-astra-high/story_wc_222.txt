In the icebound spire above the clouds, where pilots came to die of the thinlung, my job was to put the right sky over every bed.  I was thirteen, an apprentice of wonder, trusted with the little lamps but not yet with the scissors that pricked stars in tin.  Each pilot chose a night to lie under; most wanted the night of a first crossing, a few the night before it.  When the sky is wrong, a pilot knows, even one who can no longer sit up to cough. 

So when Silas Reed motioned me down to his pillow, I went with my notebook open to amend the chart.  He had ordered the sky of the night he stole the duke's silver, and I had given special attention to the low red eyelid of Mars.  Silas slid a white pawn into my palm.  I left him my black one, just as I used to do on visiting days through the wide mesh of the prison wicket. 

The sisters allowed my father no private talk with me, since it was generally believed that chivalry ran in families and theft could be taught.  He had earned his bed by flying the duke's silver through a winter blockade to buy milk for the children of the fever camps.  Swapping hand-carved chess pieces with secrets inside had begun as a way to smuggle each other dreadful jokes under the prison chaplain's nose.  To my father a joke was a kind of grace, something freely given that made a hard mouth open in spite of itself.  This time the slip inside the pawn said only, I never saw those stars. 

I went back after rounds with the pocket sundial lid he had given me the day they brought him in.  On its brass inside he had scratched the course of that famous flight, so that whatever else they told me I could trace it for myself.  I tilted it into his lamplight and saw for the first time what ought to have been obvious from the start.  The long scratch followed the bed of a river; he had flown below the clouds, not above them. 

"Black as the inside of that pawn," he whispered, and for a moment we both shook with the effort not to laugh aloud.  Then he pointed past the tin sky at the shutters, sealed for winter with long strips of wool.  "I thought if you made that night beautiful enough I could go back there instead of going wherever this is taking me." 

His hand on the blanket made the little uncertain motion of a man feeling for a rung with his foot.  Beyond the shutters there might be stars or sleet or only the hateful white glare that sometimes came off the ice at midnight. 

"I'd rather see," he said. 

I knew how much cold those shutters held back; I had only to touch a hinge to leave a patch of skin on it.  I tucked hot bricks along his sides and rolled his bed clear of the others into the narrow bay where we kept the lamps.  The wool came out easily enough, but the latch was crusted solid and my little lamp had not the heat to melt it.  I slid the sundial lid under the latch and bore down on the river until the soft brass folded and the iron jumped. 

Cold walked straight through my apron.  Out went my lamp, and in the sudden dark the stars above the other beds shone on, every one where I had put it.  Over ours, a green light gathered itself in pleats, like a great skirt in the hand of somebody preparing to leap a puddle.  I had never seen the like and neither had my father, who had flown nearly everywhere there was to fly.  He opened both arms as far as the bricks would let him, and I nearly spoiled the thing by asking what he thought it was.  Instead I leaned into the crook of his elbow and let it do whatever it was going to do next.  By the time a sister came to shut the cold out he was asleep, and the bright hem was still lifting. 

In the book of approved skies I drew a little square after my father's entry and left it empty.  The sisters let it stand, and sometimes now a pilot lays a finger there.
