During the migration of clouds, when the long white banks moved south and rain came straight through the canvas market roofs, the islanders took their trading underground.  They poled punts into the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes and tied them gunwale to gunwale, so that a woman could step out for onions and come home with a haircut.  Lena, the island's firefighter, went from boat to boat setting the glass lamps in buckets of black sand from the volcanic beach.  She moved as her mother had on a dance floor, gracefully alert to the weight about to shift under her feet.  Nobody minded the lamps half so much as Lena, but nobody else was an arsonist's daughter. 

At the last punt Inez sat with a mandolin across her knees and a cardboard price tag tucked under its strings.  Lena had spent the summer learning how to graft a new neck onto that pearwood body, which still bore on its belly the print of a burning curtain.  Her father had set fire to the dance hall for the insurance, having first gone down the row of pegs and counted the coats.  Inez, playing for her own pleasure after the last dance, had come in a shawl.  The fingers of her right hand had healed curled around a little parcel of air. 

"I didn't know you meant to sell it," Lena said. 

"I don't charge admission to my lap," Inez said.  "That's the price of a cabin to Valparaiso, if you book before the rains stop." 

There was a sister there whose sons Inez knew only by the changing sizes of the socks she knitted.  Lena had pictured Inez playing the first dance when the stalls went up on dry land again.  She had wanted to prove that scars can sing, and here was a woman who wanted a lower berth near the lavatory. 

"Does it sound like what it costs?" asked a voice from the fish punt. 

Inez turned the tag over as if its blank back might have the answer the front lacked.  For three years Lena had washed this woman's hair on Sundays, neither of them mentioning that the visits from the insurance nurse had stopped after six weeks.  She knew the difference between Inez considering a difficulty and Inez waiting for someone to have the decency to look away.  She sat down to consider it too. 

From inside her blouse Inez drew a foreign coin drilled and strung on red thread, the first peso her sister had earned sewing buttonholes abroad.  She slipped the loop off her neck and crooked it around her thumb until the coin rested against the side of her fist. 

"Another turn," she told Lena, who took up the slack until the worn silver edge stood proud of the knuckle like a thumbnail. 

The first stroke caught so hard that everybody in the fish punt winced.  On the second Inez let her wrist go loose, and a chord unrolled across the black water, gathering its own soft echo from under the stone.  She played the tune they used to teach small children the names of fish, leaving room after every phrase for the ridiculous answers.  The fishmonger supplied these in a bass so solemn that even the eels lying in their wash of melted ice acquired a certain dignity. 

When he counted out the fare, he asked if the pick went with the instrument. 

"Not unless you want my sister too," Inez said, and held her fist out for Lena to unwind. 

Already the boats were rocking apart to admit a late arrival with a cargo of leeks.  Lena put the thread back over the gray head and asked whether she might come on Sunday as usual. 

"Bring the timetable," said Inez. 

Afterward, whenever anybody asked what had become of the mandolin, Lena sent them down to the fish stall.  What had become of Inez they could find out for themselves, if they cared to buy a stamp.  She had sent Lena a postcard of two boys standing barefoot in the Pacific, their good wool socks stuffed into the shoes they had entrusted to their aunt.
