"Do I put back the narrow door?"  The builder had his palm out for the piece of glass, but Ada kept it a moment longer.  Below the lighthouse orchard, perched on its shelf of basalt, ice floes shouldered past with bits of the old ferry house still on them.  A green table went by with a white loop of gulls above it, as if somebody had set out lunch.  By noon the first boat of the thaw would need somewhere to land. 

All the town could afford was a copy.  To reconstitute a thing cost only the memory of it, whereas to build from scratch took timber that had to come by sea.  The builder could raise a ferry house down to the last bent nail if Ada would give him one true image to work from.  He could not take the image himself; what the mirrors showed an ordinary eye was just an orchard full of crooked little apple trees. 

Marriage and money troubles used to come before a bench of six, but since the sea had taken most of the town they came before Ada, its only remaining shadow-laced peacekeeper.  Under the skin of her hands ran the shadows of those who had once laid their hands in hers to seal a settlement.  They tugged when a settlement had not held, which was how she knew, without looking, when her brother was in the crowd.  His was the settlement she used to teach the apprentices: since his chair could not pass the door, two porters would carry him through.  Everybody had shaken hands, and afterward he had stopped going across to the mainland for his paper on Saturdays.  He said the paper had gone downhill. 

In the weighted breath before her reply, Ada turned the broken crystal ball fragment against her thumb until its curved face caught the light.  The vintage store mirrors hanging from the apple boughs had once watched over racks of other people's clothes beside the landing.  Now they threw the lighthouse beam around the shoulder of the cliff, and kept to themselves what they had seen.  Through the glass in her hand she got glimpses in them, little and sharp as the pictures inside a raindrop. 

There went her brother down the gangway in the arms of a porter, his trousers riding up to expose a length of pale shin.  She did not linger over his face; she knew the look of a man doing his best to weigh nothing.  Instead she watched the woman who followed him with his chair, and saw that the woman was not looking at him either.  She was looking at the next building down the quay, where two wide doors stood open and a dress on a headless dummy shook in the wind. 

On a hunch Ada tipped the glass toward the oldest mirror, the one that had faced into the shop.  Mostly there were women turning to see how they looked from behind, but one wet Saturday a ferry captain in a dripping slicker came backing through the racks, paying out a length of rope.  She made it fast to the iron ring where the rag merchant used to tie his scow.  Then she turned the dummy's little price card over and wrote on the back with her indelible pencil: FERRY TODAY.  In came Ada's brother under his own steam, a little girl with a cello, a fishwife trundling a barrow of ice.  The captain counted their fares into the dummy's outstretched hand. 

Ada remembered the inquiry into that crossing; for leaving her appointed berth the captain had lost a stripe, and in the minutes Ada herself had called it an isolated incident.  She had meant that it would not happen again.  What a small, brave exception it had been, scarcely more than the width of a shopfront, and big enough to get a town through.  At her back she could feel her brother waiting to hear what she had decided would be good enough for him this time. 

The builder's hand was still out and the breath she had drawn was still there to spend.  She laid the fragment in his palm with the shop caught whole inside it, right down to the fare in the wooden hand.  "Put back the ferry house we had that Saturday," she said, and stepped aside so her brother could see the plan without having to ask.
