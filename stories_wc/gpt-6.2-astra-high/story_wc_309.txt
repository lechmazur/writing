The week the city automated the making of miracles, Nell lost the only examination she had ever stood a chance of passing.  She had been a candidate for the lesser orders of magic for sixteen years, and could at last keep a candle burning underwater.  The new instrument could compose a requiem for a drowned man and make the river sing it in his voice.  Nobody needed the candle, or so Nell understood until her father sent for her. 

He lived in the icebound spire above the clouds, where the city lodged its oldest citizens and where the instrument was undergoing its trials.  Across the ceiling of his ward went a procession of blue elephants carrying pavilions in which his boyhood friends sat playing cards.  One pavilion stood empty, awaiting him. 

"Very civil," he said, "but I should like to see what I am eating." 

His supper was an egg in a blue cup, and his hand kept passing through little luminous men on its way to the spoon.  Nell pulled the switch beside his bed; the elephants vanished but their light stayed, as the light of a window stays behind closed eyelids.  Only these eyelids belonged to the room, which had not been allowed a night in seven weeks.  The nurse said they could wheel him downstairs, but every room in the spire had been furnished with wonders except the cellar, and the stairs to that were narrow.  The other patients were reaching through the little men too.  One woman had given up and sat with her mouth open, waiting for a nurse to feed her. 

Nell remembered the quiet under the sham stone arch her father had built in the municipal garden, in the days when people still made their own unnecessary things.  There you could sit in full sun and count the hairs on the back of a bee without the dazzle driving tears into your eyes.  He never made a temple, but as a girl she had thought that if there was anything holy it ought to be possible to see it that well. 

"Where did they put your arch when they built the spire?" she asked. 

"Sold them the top stone," he said, and tapped the brass plate at the head of his bed. 

Nell had always taken the little arch stamped there for a rainbow.  Her father explained that a garden folly keystone accommodates impossibilities: it keeps up a ruin that has never fallen down.  The designers had bought his for the ward so a hundred different heavens could occupy the same space without the occupants having to agree on anything.  It had not occurred to them that anybody might want the space itself. 

He showed her the inspection hatch in the ceiling and lent her his spoon to turn the screw.  Inside, where warm pipes met the icy skin of the spire, there was mist enough for the one piece of advanced magic Nell had mastered.  Mist walking required neither strength nor invention, only a willingness to trust your weight to a succession of very small things.  She stepped from bead to bead until she reached the crown of the ward, where a belt of gilded leather held a wedge of cheap red sandstone.  The lingering luminance oozed around it, elephants worn down to tusks, tusks to crescents like parings of nails.  All she had to do was unstrap the stone and the whole expensive installation would go blind.  She had her thumb under the buckle when the woman with the open mouth said softly to one of the little men, "Play your ace, love." 

Nell let the buckle slip a single notch instead and fitted her candle between the strap and the stone, where the meltwater ran.  She lit it there, feeding the wick on spent light as she had learned to feed it on the drowned sunshine in a millpond.  Far below, somebody switched an elephant on and off, and this time its afterimage went up the wick in a small blue spurt.  The flame would need tending; she could think of no particular reason she should not be the one to tend it. 

At her father's bedside the egg was cold, but when he cracked the shell the white shone like a moon coming up through fog.  Across the aisle little men went on playing cards in a pavilion no bigger than a fist.  He salted the egg, offered her the first spoonful, and watched her face while she ate.
