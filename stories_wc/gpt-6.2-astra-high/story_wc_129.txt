Beyond the murmur of lost languages, when the crater no longer gave back even an echo, people there still had a way of getting the dead to move.  They wove them in reed and let the traveling sun draw their shadows across the ground. 

Eda, a marsh-reed sculptor, came up to the crystalline orchard perched at the edge of the shimmering crater to do this for her sister Linn.  On the glimmering obsidian arbor where the fruit pickers took their rest, she lashed down a figure of a girl plaiting her hair.  At noon she would dedicate it and go home. 

"She never did her own hair that I saw," said a picker with a long white plait.  Eda looked down from her ladder and saw that six or seven other women had come in off the terraces and stood considering her work.  "She did mine, though," the picker added, and ducked her head as if the hand at her nape might still be there. 

Eda had last seen Linn at fourteen, on the morning their mother sent one daughter uphill to earn the price of keeping the other on the bit of marsh that was their living.  She had come prepared for the difficulty of making a girl of fourteen out of dry stalks, but not for the embarrassment of discovering that she wanted to stay for supper with women who had known her sister at fifty. 

"There's a picture of her grown," the picker said.  "Little Wick drew it on a wall the year of the frost." 

Nobody had ever caught the elusive street artist at work, but you could find the drawings even when the hoarfrost came down by looking for the tag: a red wick with a bead of flame, always low enough for a child to touch.  The women fanned out along the shuttered packing sheds, rubbing the foot of each wall with their mittens, while Eda followed with a ragged scrap of reed mat that took the rime off clean.  At the first flame they uncovered a boy weighing fruit; at the second, a woman asleep with her mouth open and a pear still in her fist.  It was a boldly sympathetic portrait to have put on the wall of the very shed where you could be docked a day's wages for taking so much as a windfall. 

At the third flame the white-haired picker called out, and then they were all rubbing very gently with their bare hands.  It wasn't the face that made Eda sit down hard on an upturned crate.  It was the kink in Linn's little finger, broken once in a sluice gate, hooked through one of three thick locks while a girl in front of her shut her eyes for sheer pleasure.  One after another the women turned around to show Eda the same neat tuck where a plait began, just above the vulnerable knobs of the spine. 

"Keeps it out of the sorting belt," one said. 

Even the ones who had come to the terraces after Linn died wore their hair that way. 

Back at the arbor Eda cut the bindings at her figure's shoulders and bent its arms away from its head, extending them into the lovely empty air.  For the face she asked the women to split her some more reed, and together they worked a fine web of lines into the cheeks and the soft place under the jaw.  She saved the finest sliver for the crook in the finger; when she wetted it with her tongue it bent without breaking. 

"Linn," Eda said when the noon light laid the shadow at their feet, and that was all the dedication she could manage.  The picker with the white plait moved her crate until she sat just where the two dark hands could get to work.  Eda watched her tip her head forward and understood what it would take to graft herself onto this company whose webs of kinship didn't show until somebody turned around.  It wasn't another figure, though she thought she could make a much better one now.  She pulled the pins from her own tight bun and knelt, rather stiffly, between the white-haired woman's knees. 

"Would you show me from the beginning," she asked, "so that tomorrow I can do yours?"
