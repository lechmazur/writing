At the end of history, Ruth found her grandmother kneeling on a kitchen floor on Earth, looking under the table for a little girl.  The girl was eighty now and standing right there with a softcover dictionary in the pocket of her nightgown.  Over the stove hung a sampler bearing the last clause of the compact under which humanity had entrusted its passage through the dark to the stars: All shall be brought home.  At nineteen Ruth had translated that clause into the spare trade English the stars understood.  She had been paid by the line. 

Beyond the window other kitchens extended to the horizon, each fitted as neatly to its occupant as the velvet hollow in a violin case.  Her grandmother called her name into the dark under the table and listened with the terrible attention of somebody getting no answer.  Ruth had spent her working life unraveling cosmic illusions for people who couldn't bear to look behind them; now she had to pull herself away from those hands.  By the successive deaths of six senior clerks she had become the End of History Witness, empowered to dispute delivery.  She put her hand on the sampler and said she could not sign. 

Between one call of her name and the next the universe hiccuped, and across that infinitesimal break she saw lamplight on shelves.  She stepped through, taking with her the only book that the kitchen had seen fit to furnish from her adult life.  The ancient temple library where the compact had been drawn up had been lost in time rather than in space; its address was still on every copy.  At its center stood a reading desk, and from the desk a continuous shelf of depositions spiraled out to an open door.  Spiral arbitration, Ruth remembered: you followed the argument outward, passing the same point at each turn but obliged to consider it from a greater distance.  To appeal you had to go the other way inside yourself, journeying inward while walking out, until you got to the wish beneath the words you were disputing. 

She loosened the elastic that held her dictionary together, and its soft spine bent of itself at home.  On the first circuit she heard herself at nineteen insisting that the stars could furnish every last nail of a house if given good enough specifications.  On the second she heard an older voice, wanderingly exact, taking the long way round by the blue milk jug and the spoon with the chewed handle to arrive at a precise account of where the table stood.  Ruth had taken down those specifications on the dictionary's flyleaf at her grandmother's bedside, proud of how little the dying woman had forgotten.  On the third turn she heard what she had been too busy writing to hear the first time. 

"I used to have to knock on the edge of it," her grandmother said, "and you'd say I could come in if I wasn't too tall." 

All the way out to the door Ruth waited for the voice of the child, and there wasn't one.  That was what she had done in a line of English: given everybody a place to call from and nobody the liberty to answer. 

At the door two petitions lay side by side, the stars' petition to be quit of their long charge and her own not to be delivered into solitude.  She could unify them if she could find a word that asked of the stars only what stars could give.  She laid the dictionary across the petitions, tore out the flyleaf, and on the back of the meticulous inventory wrote a shorter clause: All shall be given passage.  Whether anybody would open a door at the far end was a matter on which the compact would henceforth be silent.  The stars signed in pinpricks that let lamplight through the paper. 

Then the hiccup passed and she was outside a kitchen, hearing in the hush the great hollow sound of her knuckles against wood.  Her grandmother opened to her, looked her over with some misgiving, and asked how tall she was.  Ruth took off her slippers to give them both a little leeway.
