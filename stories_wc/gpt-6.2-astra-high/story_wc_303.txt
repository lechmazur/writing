At midsummer the velvet observatory opened for one night, and the shutters stayed closed.  The velvet was there to swallow every glimmer except the light given off by an unfulfilled promise.  Mara, the imperial provenance researcher, had spent eleven such nights watching old obligations shine through the varnish of cradles and the tarnish of spoons.  This year she had brought her father's village in a refrigerated case. 

Inside lay a transparent violin made of ice crystal, with four silver strings and a brown stick wedged upright beneath the bridge.  The label, in her own best hand, said that the silk villages had given it to the emperor who evacuated them ahead of the flood.  She had been twenty when she wrote that label, proud to be trusted with the ink.  Her father had read it once, then gone outside to wash his perfectly clean hands. 

Now he was dead and the grandchildren of the evacuated still paid the palace for every mulberry cutting they set in earth.  There was no other stock; the royal nurseries stood on the only ground the river had spared. 

What had brought Mara back to the case was an accident with a pruning knife that spring.  While binding her cut thumb against a split pear branch she had heard, very distinctly, the pear's wish to grow over the wound from both sides.  Afterward even the onions in her kitchen troubled her sleep with their small pale ambitions.  Passing the violin one morning, she had felt three tight buds dreaming of leaves.  She knew then what her father used to mean by saying the village had sent its spring on ahead.  Tonight she meant to weigh a dream against an empire, and for the purpose had brought the chipped coffee cup in which her father used to read the future. 

The registrar set his seal beside the case and drew the last curtain.  Deep in the violin a thread of yellow kindled, illuminating the stick and the slip of paper wound about its foot.  Magnified by ice, six words appeared in her father's cramped schoolboy script: Keep cold until we have land. 

Her father had told her about the boats, how a musician might carry his instrument aboard but a farmer had to leave his trees.  He had never said which boy had stood on the deck with frostbitten fingers, pretending to tune the silver strings. 

"They have land now," Mara said, laying beside the seal the deed to the narrow yard where her father lay buried.  It had taken all his savings and most of hers to buy those six feet outright. 

The registrar peered at the three buds.  "Once I enter this as a deposit, whatever grows from it belongs to the villages," he said.  "But if it dies you can't put the music back." 

He had already uncapped his ink; it was kindness, not obstruction, and for that reason harder to bear.  Mara drank the coffee cold and turned the cup over on its saucer.  Reading the future in coffee stains was mostly a matter of not mistaking a hole for an absence.  All around the rim ran the familiar dark procession of women carrying rent silk up the hill to the palace.  Through three pinholes in their skirts she could see, variously, a strip of moldy bark, a green shoot bitten off by frost, and a child with purple teeth.  A perforated destiny, her father called that kind of stain; you could put your needle through, but you had to supply the thread. 

"Enter it before the promise loses its glow," she said. 

She lifted the lid and let the summer into the case.  One by one the strings came loose, sounding four notes so low she felt them in the wrist that held the cup.  She caught the stick as it fell, and the longing in it went quiet against the heat of her hand. 

To propagate a mulberry her father had always cut just below a joint; the tree remembered how even when she flinched at the knife.  In his yard she set two buds under the soil and left the third above, with his cup propped over it against the wind.  It took three years before there was wood enough to pass from hand to hand down the hill. 

By then the registrar had retired, but every summer until he died she sent him a square of silk, not fine enough for velvet, wrapped around a handful of fruit.  He always sent back the cloth unwashed, the stains on it dark and full of holes.
