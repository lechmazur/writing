The woman laid a patch on my desk, a white goose flying through a circle of blue flame.  I knew the bird from the old footage of the Tern coming down in the flax fields, its nose plowing a furrow straight as any farmer's.  I knew the two numbers on the back better than I knew my own address.  Sixteen, four hundred.  They had followed me through thirty years of the Chorus archive. 

My visitor sat brightly attentive, the way people do at an auction when they don't want to miss their lot.  She had found the patch in the lining of her father's suitcase after his funeral, stitched into a little pocket with her milk teeth.  She had always known he had brought her home from the Tern evacuation, but not what she was called before he called her Alice.  I turned my screen so she could see the goose on the wing of the broken ship.  She put a finger against it as if taking its temperature. 

My title was corporate memory keeper, which meant that when Chorus wanted to celebrate an anniversary I supplied pictures of strangers being good to one another.  On my own time I had been trying to find where deleted files go.  When the ship went down, Chorus was a social network where you sang along with somebody you would probably never meet.  For three weeks it was also the only place where the people scattered from those fields could look for one another. 

I was the girl on the help desk who told a woman that if she took down her appeal to correct the shelter address, I could put it back up with all the replies attached.  It was a lie, told because there were sixty people in line and she trusted me enough to move along.  By morning the replies were gone, the shelter was empty, and all I had of her was a missing persons case already starting to go cold.  What she had given me instead of a photograph was the laundry mark on the patch she had sewn inside her daughter's coat.  Sixteen was the ship's laundry and four hundred was her bunk. 

I told Alice her mother's name, which was Ada Lusk, and that she had been dead nine years.  She thanked me, pushed her chair back, then pulled it in again. 

"Was she the one who sang the numbers, then?" 

That was how Alice had known to come to Chorus.  Once you started listening for those numbers they were everywhere, in skipping rhymes and football chants and a love song that had played at her own wedding.  It was an old Chorus game to fit the words of one song to the tune of another, carrying over just enough of the last singer's voice to make the join audible.  I took her back across the echo of forgotten songs, from the wedding waltz to a harvest reel to a lullaby so slow the two numbers stretched halfway round the moon.  Under the lullaby there should have been nothing.  That was where the deletion was, a clean white bar the length of Ada's appeal. 

Beside the lullaby I kept eight other attempts to sing along with Ada, all from that first night, all with the same hole.  Each had caught a little of her voice through somebody's open speaker before a fiddle cut in or a baby woke up crying.  I had played them for Ada singly, year after year, until the thought of putting her through those tantalizing seconds again was more than I could bear. 

Alice wanted to know what would happen if we played them together.  Where one person had talked over her mother another might have been listening. 

We spent the afternoon piecing together eleven seconds in which a woman who had been a name in a folder became a woman out of breath, trying to find a tune her child would know.  The numbers were all anybody else had carried forward, but Ada had started with a name. 

"Little Nell with the eggs to sell," she sang, and the woman beside me opened her mouth on the next line. 

"Two for a penny unless you come empty, and then you can have them for free." 

I asked if she wanted me to put the appeal back up, so that anybody who remembered the song could hear how it had ended.  She shook her head and held out her phone to record those eleven seconds.  This time I waited while she played them back.
