On the day the two banks agreed to exchange their stolen children, a woman of sixty came to Linn and asked where she was to report.  She had brought a nightdress, an onion for the ferryman, and a little boy who had hold of her skirt with all ten fingers.  She was the first person to come of her own accord; the commissioners had expected to have to send for people. 

"They say my mother kept asking for me," she said.  "I would have gone sooner if I had known there was a way to go and come back." 

Linn, the chronicle keeper, had written the word exchange herself, delighted to have found a word that neither bank considered an admission of guilt.  She had pictured two boats passing in midstream; it had not occurred to her to picture the following morning. 

Together they walked down to the obsidian refuge along the river, built of black glass quarried on one bank and roofed with slate from the other.  Here, on the last night of the fighting, somebody had handed the woman as a baby across a sill still chipped by the buckles of other babies' slings.  From her nightdress she shook a well-worn origami swan, soft as a moth along its creases. 

"It came with me," she said.  "My father used to make it dip its head when I was ill." 

Inside one wing Linn saw a blue number in her predecessor's hand; inside the other, a brown thumbprint small enough to be the woman's own.  The number did not belong to any catalogue still in use.  When the boy held the swan against the window to make a shadow bird, the number shone through onto a flight of steps made by the cryptic folds.  Linn knew those steps; she had spent half her life going up and down them without ever thinking to lift the seventh tread. 

It took all three of them to shift it, and the boy, who could burrow like a vole, to clear the roots away beneath.  Below lay an overgrown library hidden in the catacombs of lost knowledge, as people called the chambers where the river had silted up one civilization after another.  Willow roots had drunk the paste from the book spines and let the pages fall like so much washed linen, but in the hollow of each spine a glass tube lay snug.  Across the drift of untold seasons the ink inside the tubes had kept the greasy shine of yesterday. 

Each held two drawings of the same infant, one by the mother giving it up and one by the stranger taking it in.  They found the woman's by laying the swan's little thumbprint over the prints pressed like pink shells into the wax stoppers.  Under her two portraits, which might have been of different species, the mothers had written to one another. 

"If she cries, stand where she can see a leaf moving," the first had written.  "If she lives, I will stand here every year on the day the swallows go." 

The other had put simply, "She is looking at the leaf." 

The woman sat on the bottom step for a long time; above her the boy tried to fold his swan back into being and got a squat little duck instead.  Linn did not record those minutes; a river needs its reed beds, and a life its stretches of regenerative silence. 

"My daughter still does that with her babies," the woman said at last, and Linn, who had been getting ready to offer comfort, shut her mouth. 

At the landing the commissioners were chalking a line down the deck of the ferry, one half for those being sent, the other for those being received.  They asked Linn which side the woman belonged on.  Ordinarily a chronicle keeper grips reality loosely, leaving room around any given fact for the next one, but Linn had let a single tidy word close like a fist.  She rubbed out the line with her sleeve and read aloud what she had written, substituting visits for exchange.  Both commissioners objected until the woman asked which of them proposed to tell her grandson she could not come home.  While both men considered this, the boy launched his duck; the ferryman scooped it out with his cap and accepted the onion as fare for three. 

On the far bank there was no old woman waiting, only a stand of poplars showing the silver undersides of their leaves.  The woman stayed there until she had seen them turn twice, then took her grandson's hand and went to find out who remembered.  In the column headed Returned, Linn entered only the time of the evening boat.
