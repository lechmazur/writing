In the hush left behind by a heated argument, Leon Vale could hear the campfire story stick rolling down the sloped floor of the decommissioned radar dome.  It came to rest against his good city shoes, whose little leather tassels had survived six winters of beer and sawdust.  Across the room Ada still had her fist closed around the place where the stick had been.  Neither of them bent to pick it up. 

Tomorrow the public would be admitted to the dome for the first time since the flood, and the first thing anybody would see was a white sheet stretched between two instrument racks.  On that sheet a thumb-high ferryman would put out his lantern while black paper water climbed the steps of the landing.  The boat had passed close enough for the people on the landing to hear its engine; by the time it came back, six of them had drowned.  Along the hem of the sheet would pass a strip of Leon's exquisite lettering: I never saw a light.  Ada had come up the hill to ask him what right he had to put her mother's words there again. 

The town had learned its history through subversive puppet shows in seedy bars, because for years the ferry company had owned both the newspaper and the only decent place to drink.  Leon, an urbane calligrapher who could order oysters in four languages, had followed a boy into one of those bars and stayed long after the boy was gone.  He liked the problem the puppeteers brought him: how to let people recognize a sentence without recognizing the hand that wrote it.  His uncommonly serene script, all even arches, let frightened witnesses take shelter in looking alike.  It had also made everything they said look settled. 

At the fires outside the relief tents, whoever held the stick could speak, and nobody else got to finish the sentence, not even if the speaker started crying.  Ada's mother had held it the night she said she never saw a light.  Leon remembered what came next, though the women who made the little ferryman had gone home by then.  She had kept her face inside her coat so her children would not see her terror; she would not have seen a burning ship.  She had given him leave to use the first part because she could not bear to have the second known.  He had mistaken the limits of her permission for the limits of his responsibility. 

Through a missing triangle of the dome he saw a meteor go by, so low it seemed extraordinary that there was no sound of something tearing.  He had always distrusted those half-lit epiphanies that came to a man alone at his desk and cost nobody anything until morning.  Now one came anyway: the words had never put out the light.  A little jointed wrist had done that, night after night, to a hiss from the drinkers who had lost somebody. 

He picked up the stick and offered it to Ada, who sat down on an empty cable spool with it across her knees.  Then he went behind the sheet and turned the crank in reverse until the water slid down and the lantern hung bright on its hook.  He took the puppet off his fingers.  In the wash of the projector his own hand appeared enormous and rather old, with a corn on the finger where his pen lay.  He let that hand take down the lantern and laid it on the table where Ada could see that its tiny yellow wick was just a shaving of boxwood. 

On the paper strip, after the sentence he had copied so often that it came as easily as his signature, he wrote another in his ordinary shopping-list scrawl.  We made the gesture that goes with these words.  He signed it, and for once his signature looked out of place.  Ada watched him run the two sentences through, the puppet standing idle and the great hand coming out of the dark.  Then she tapped the stick on the floor, the old signal that there was room now for another voice. 

"Leave your cuff in," she said. 

He had meant to roll it back out of sight, but she was right; anybody who had ever put a coin in the saucer would recognize that ridiculous inch of linen.  Tomorrow they could ask him to his face.
