The museum called me an esoteric realist because I painted the backs of famous things: the braces on the altarpiece, the darn in the flag.  When the last monarch butterfly began its migration, they offered me a month's wages for once to paint the front.  They wanted the orange wings spread against the green of the grotto where the insect would stop after crossing the pass.  I accepted because my grandmother had lived in that grotto and the museum owned nothing of hers, not even her name. 

I took her paint box, which still held, under a blister of varnish, one gray moth wing.  As a child I had thought it a sorry treasure for a woman who had seen the great orange processions when they still took hours to pass. 

Above the clouds the grotto was as green as the inside of a split pea, except where the dawn had entered and turned the wet rock pink.  There were six women on the lip, each with a bundle of willow rods, and nobody spoke above a murmur for fear of missing the call from across the pass.  It came at last, three strokes on a pan, and the women began building the little arches I had seen in the margins of my grandmother's account books.  They planted the rods in pockets of earth and bent their tops together, working to a tune that went two beats and then, just where you wanted the third, went quiet.  Into that interrupted cadence came an answering knock, so faint I might have taken it for the settling of a stone. 

"Somebody down there wants a look," the eldest said.  She made me get down on my belly and look over.  Through a rent in the cloud I saw a ledge full of washing and a woman building an arch out of two broomsticks and her apron string. 

"When we have her safe here we knot a cloth across the arch," the elder said.  "When she flies we take it down, and they do the same all the way to the dye works where there's no sky at all." 

I had supposed the arches were for guiding butterflies, and told her so. 

"They know the road," she said. 

The women who cut stone and dyed wool had devised the signals because they were paid by the piece and could not leave their benches to see the migration.  A watchwoman could leave because her pay for the lost hours came out of the common penny pot.  She lived up here for the length of the passage, transiently rooted as the willow that sometimes put out little white hairs before the arches came down.  For this last passage all six surviving watchwomen had come up together. 

"Your grandmother kept watch longer than any of us," said the elder, touching the moth wing on my box.  "That was the first thing she ever signaled, and she had to go the length of the valley apologizing when it flew up to a lamp." 

I pictured her keeping it, not the bright wing she could have had any autumn after that, but the little receipt for everybody's wasted minute.  Then something like a scrap of fired paper settled on the purple mint beside my hand.  Nobody cheered; there was just a passing flush from face to face as the women reached into their blouses for their strips of cloth.  I had my brush wet and the wings were open and it seemed to me a shame that the people at the dye works would never see so much as a speck of their astonishing color. 

"Does anything ever go down from hand to hand?" I asked. 

"Your grandmother used to send down mint for the vats when the stink got bad," said the elder. 

I painted on the square of linen I had brought to wipe my brushes, and when the wings closed I had got the black veins down and two unequal rows of white dots.  We sent it down from ledge to ledge after the arches fell, with a request that it be hung wherever the light was poorest. 

For the museum I painted six pairs of hands untying the cloths, with a bit of that blessed orange in the corner, just going out of sight.  They would not pay the month's wages for that, though they took it in the end for the price of the frame.  On the back, where somebody sooner or later would think to look, I put the names of the six women and the seventh who had once mistaken a moth for the beginning of a glory.
