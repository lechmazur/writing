After the mysterious package arrived, Nell slept for eleven minutes, her longest uninterrupted sleep that winter.  The bell that usually chased her from pillow to washstand was ringing inside the parcel instead.  She could just make out the old distinction between a thumb on the key and a fist.  Somebody down there was trying to be polite. 

Inside the brown paper lay a broken lighthouse lens, green at its thick end, with a library ticket pasted to the brass rim.  Nell knew the handwriting on the ticket; before she became an undertaker she had been a child entrusted to take things back.  It was her own name, in the labored capitals of somebody allowed out alone for the first time.  She remembered the lighthouse keeper asking for another week, and the sea coming up the stairs that afternoon.  Nobody had asked her about the lens in sixty years. 

The library had lent the citizens everything from christening gowns to the apparatus by which their harbor gathered light and sent it out again.  After the flood, the distress signals had gone on coming, little inquiries from people unwilling to believe there was nobody left in authority.  The county had buried its receivers in concrete.  Nell had taken the last one home because an undertaker was, in a modest way, somebody in authority.  She was inadvertently stubborn, a woman who only meant to stay up until the next shift came.  By the time she thought to ask who was coming, the county office was a shop that sold shoes. 

Now, lifting the lens to her tired eye, she saw not the room but the numbered hook where it belonged.  She put a finger through the glass and felt cold brass.  She had forgotten the way into the underwater city library, but her hand remembered and took her with it. 

Like everything the town could not afford a building for, the library had survived by occupying impossible spaces.  Once it had fitted between the platform and the train, so commuters could exchange a novel without missing their stop.  Now it occupied the gap between the two jagged faces of the lens, one here in her hand, one still turning in the drowned tower.  Through its high windows she saw cod go by in the dark like thoughts better left alone. 

Each turn of the distant fragment sent light across the returns desk, where a little girl stood beating on a key with her fist.  Behind her wound a patient queue, everybody holding something, a christening gown, a carpet sweeper, a chipped blue dish.  Nell had pictured people clinging to roofs; she had never considered what people might want once the worst thing had already happened. 

"I've had it long enough," the girl said, pushing a picture book across the desk.  "May I go home now?" 

On the book's flyleaf Nell found the same instruction that was stamped inside the lid of her receiver: acknowledge receipt and give address.  Every night she had answered with the address of her rented room, and every time she moved the poor souls had to start again.  She had been trying to give rest a permanent address by telling it where she happened to be sleeping. 

There was a stamp on the desk, dry as bone, and under it a pad the color of old tea.  Nell had no ink, but an undertaker never went anywhere without the soft black pencil that would write even on a wet label.  She rubbed graphite across the raised letters and pressed the library's address into the book, then onto the girl's outstretched palm.  The girl closed her hand and was gone before the light came round again. 

The man at the back smelled of lamp oil; brown parcel string had cut red grooves across his fingers.  Nell worked until the queue was gone and the only thing left to check in was the glass in her hand.  Putting it on the hook, she felt the old tug of duty discharged; she could walk out now with both hands empty.  But somebody on shore might yet have a return to make.  She signed for the lens again and found her way home by the thin gray light of her bedroom window caught in the glass. 

At home she set the glass upright on the sill, where anybody who needed to could find the way in.  It took most of the summer to revitalize the lending service; the first thing anybody borrowed was the carpet sweeper.  Sometimes a borrower lingered at the desk with a finger on a name in an old flyleaf, and Nell let the housework go.  She slept in the afternoons now, with the bell at her elbow, and when it rang it rang only once.
