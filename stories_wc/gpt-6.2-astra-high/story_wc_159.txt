In the opal library above the clouds, a day was as long as it took one patch of sunlight to travel across the Museum of Extinct Feelings.  At the cabinet of homesickness for ships, Iona opened the shutters; at the case of relief at hearing the all-clear, she closed them.  Between those two feelings the glass-orchid caretaker went about her gently systematic work, dusting one transparent throat at a time with a wren feather.  Each blossom held the sound of a forgotten name, not as a stranger might read it off a monument, but as somebody had once called it up a stair. 

Once the library had been only one of the marvels people came up the mountain to see.  Now the marvels were deserted, the chairlift carried nothing but weather, and the county wanted its cable back for the ferry.  On the morning the men came to wind it in, their apprentice brought Iona a small request folded into the official notice.  We would like to know what to call the ferry, it said in the careful hand of somebody accustomed to cutting letters into wood.  Underneath was a drawing of a woman with a foxglove tucked through a buttonhole, holding the tiller of the old river scow.  Nobody had needed to call her anything but Ma, the apprentice explained, until there was no Ma left to answer. 

Iona recognized the ticket from which he had copied the picture, right down to the dots the cheap press made of the bells of the flower.  As a girl she had sold those tickets on the wharf, where the river crossing had been the first leg of the ascent.  She had never once asked the woman who took them for her name.  Yet she knew exactly which orchid to try, for in those days every crossing had ended with the passengers singing their thanks to the pilot.  Orchids grew in the shape of whatever sound had brought them into bloom; the river bed was all tilted stems and wide, uneven mouths. 

"A crowd never quite sings together," she said, and took down a pot whose lip was purple as a bruise.  To light the little spirit lamp beneath it was the work of a moment; to make out anything in the din that rose from it was quite another matter.  There was the slap of tickets going down on wet planks, the chink of her own cashbox, even the derisive whistle of a kite.  Everything but the voices came back clear.  She had begun, with a familiar sinking of the heart, to enter the failure in her ledger when the boy put his ear to the pot's drain hole. 

"That's not your tickets," he said.  "That's her chime for when the boat was nearly in."  From his tool bag he took a length of bamboo, split from the hole for its string almost to the foot.  He had meant to fit the cracked wind chime tube above the new tiller, the only bit of the old scow sound enough to sail again. 

Iona set its hollow end against the drain hole and heard the wet slap again, but this time it was followed by another, softer and further off.  The far bank had been a sheer face of rock; what the tube caught was the sound coming back off that face, stripped of the bustle of the wharf.  With his ear to the crack the boy heard, by the echo of a long-forgotten song, what no one had thought to put on the ticket. 

"O Sella, carry us home." 

He sang it back with the stress on the second syllable, and Iona, who had been about to correct him, shut her mouth and listened again.  He was right; she had been hearing the word as one reads it. 

Light was creeping over the all-clear case when the men called up to ask if there was anything to go down on the last chair.  Iona wrapped the orchid in her apron and put it in the boy's hands, along with the lamp and the book of instructions she had kept for her successor.  "One at a time," she told him, "in the autumn, when you come up on foot."  On the back of the notice she drew him a map of the old path, marking the turn where the foxgloves grew so thickly that you could miss it.  Under the drawing of the pilot she wrote Sella, and over the a she put the little upward stroke his voice had made.
