In the first yawn of evening, when the palace let its kitchen girls out to court and its princesses out to pray, Ada took her daughter down to the sepia reading room.  Brown silk screened the windows there because daylight ate the ink of treaties, and out of the golden shadows the brass succession table shone like an ordinary supper gong.  Molly was seven, old enough to have heard what happened to a child under whose hand the sugar on that table failed to flower.  She had packed two nightgowns and a spoon for the convent. 

Ada had come to uncover a hidden truth, but the spoon, with its little rabbit finial worn down to a peg, made her want to break something instead.  Thirty years before, her own sister Nell had stood here in white stockings with a hole in one heel that Ada could see whenever Nell went up on tiptoe to watch the sugar.  Everybody else remembered the blank brass and then the rose that leaped up for Ada; what Ada remembered was the librarian rubbing his wrist.  Only that morning, watching Molly wind a toy duck and rub the very same place, had she thought to wonder why.  Such a small bridge of intuition to carry a woman back across thirty years, and yet here she was on her knees, feeling underneath the table. 

Her fingers followed a copper stem from the belly of the brass through a slit in the wainscot.  On the other side stood the librarian's desk, its one locked drawer eloquently silent amid a litter of keys for everything from the sugar tin to the privy.  Molly tried her spoon in the lock and achieved nothing; then, being seven, she pulled out the drawer above it and reached down.  What came up was a gramophone crank handle, patched with the sort of pink sticking plaster that the old librarian used to wind around his spectacles. 

Behind the kneehole a green curtain hid a machine with neither horn nor cabinet, its sound box joined instead to the copper stem.  The record on its spindle had just one groove that ran round and round to meet itself.  Ada fitted the handle and wound until the spring pushed back against her hand, then tipped the needle down.  Across the wainscot came a thin persistent A, the note Nell used to get by wetting her finger and running it around the rim of a wineglass.  Molly went to fetch the sugar. 

They watched the grains hurry out of the dancing parts of the brass and settle along the still lines, tracing through standing waves the six petals of a briar rose.  No touch was needed, not even that of a child. 

Ada could have stayed there all night to muse on who had paid the librarian, or whether he had simply liked the little girl who brought him pears better than the one who stole his plaster to mend her dolls.  She might even have found a receipt, and somebody's grandson might have gone to prison, and in the morning Molly would still have had to stand here in white stockings.  Instead she sent Molly out to the steps to call back the kitchen girls, whose young men came too, smelling of river mud and the hot iron of the tram sheds.  She asked the tram conductor to wind the spring, and a girl with a burn across her cheek scattered sugar, and the rose came up as sweetly for them as it had for anybody.  By midnight there was a queue to the steps, and what with all the laughing and the winding it was nearly breakfast before anybody thought to send for the guard. 

They called Ada the dynasty ender after that, although in her view a dynasty you could finish off with half a pound of sugar was nothing to boast of having ended.  She took Molly to the convent herself, with the spoon and both nightgowns, for it was a long way and Nell had never met her niece.  On the train Molly asked if her aunt would be glad to see them.  Ada started to say yes, as once she would have said it and had it printed in the papers, but the child deserved better than another pretty shape shaken out of sugar. 

"We'll have to ask her," she said.
