On the cylinder my mother left me there are six minutes of engraved quiet, which is a long time to listen to a dead woman not saying anything.  She made it to teach the roses not to finish people's sentences. 

Under the glass dome of our desert rose farm, every bush grew a single fat hip of green wax that turned slowly on its stem.  A thorn rode the turning hip and trembled to the sound of a voice; by winter you could pick a man's account of his wedding and play it to his grandchildren.  Unfortunately a rose loved a voice so well that in any pause it would give you back the sweetest thing it had ever heard.  Many a sober deposition in our district used to end with my father's tipsy rendition of Little Brown Hen. 

Inculcating plants with values was my mother's line of work, and she thought the beginning of decency was to let a person hesitate.  Each spring we played her cylinder to the seedlings and slipped a drop of honeywater to the ones whose thorns kept traveling through the silence without adding a sound. 

After the tower fell, people came to the farm to put on record who had gone into its cells and never come out.  All afternoon the dome held the color of a steady flame; outside, brick dust was settling on the dunes in little red terraces.  Near sundown my father came in carrying a scrap of an airship ballast sandbag, its faded red seam still gritty with the sand of some distant port. 

He had made his living collecting bounties for the tower, and the sight of that familiar coat emptied three benches as neatly as if he had swept them with his arm.  I had spent my girlhood refusing the pistol he wanted me to carry, but never until then had I understood why he liked to have it on him.  It must be convenient, when people turn away, to be able to make them turn back. 

He laid the cloth beside a woman who had been trying since noon to say her brother's name into a rose.  Stitched across it in blue shirt thread were thirteen names. 

"They weren't in the tower when it fell," he said.  "I loaded them onto the mail ship myself." 

The woman found her brother halfway down, in the small square letters he used for marking their hens' eggs before market.  She took up my pruning knife, and my father undid the catch on his holster.  I put my hand over his, although the hand I wanted to stop was hers. 

She set the blade between her brother's name and the next, as if she meant to take her share of a cake.  Then she seemed to notice that he had worked the whole list with one thread, carrying it along the back from name to name.  I saw her sheathe the knife in the leather loop on my apron as carefully as if she were putting a baby to bed. 

"Tell it here," she said to my father. 

Being senselessly perceptive about small things, I heard the tiny tick as the thorn took hold, but I missed entirely the moment when he decided what to do.  He gave the name of the ship, the hour of sailing, the pilot's habit of putting down at the salt works to water her engine.  Then came a silence of such duration that on any other farm the air would have filled with borrowed birdsong or some widow's vows.  I nearly lifted the thorn myself. 

"There was room for the others," he said at last.  "I only took the ones who could pay." 

The people who had got up from the benches sat down again, not beside him but within hearing.  He stayed until he had given the names of the ones he had left, as many as he knew. 

The woman went to the salt works that night, taking the cloth but leaving us a length of its red seam wound round the stem.  When the woman brought her brother home, he sat down on the ground by the red thread and began with the awful cold of the voyage.  By winter the hip had grown so heavy that I had to prop it up with my mother's empty chair.
