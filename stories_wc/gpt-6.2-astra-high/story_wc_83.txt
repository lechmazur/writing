They had offered Mina a recording of her daughter coming down the aisle, with the last eleven seconds cut off.  She had the first part in her head already, and the last eleven seconds too, though she had spent sixteen years trying to get them out.  What she wanted was to see Ada at sixty, an age at which nobody would think to call her a bride.  That was why she took the night shift on the floating observatory, though the diesel crossing made her sick and the pay scarcely covered it. 

Out on the lake, years that people had been prevented from living collected like cold water, heavy enough to draw a pontoon down an inch without wetting anybody's feet.  The hunt was for the deepest pocket, where an instrument lowered on a wire might bring a whole afternoon to the surface.  Clients paid for the ten likeliest minutes, cleaned of inconsistencies and printed on glass.  The rest went through resistors that heated the crew's bunks, and on winter nights Mina lay sweating under other people's Septembers.  When the resistors overloaded, the bunks went cold and there was a brief interval, while the circuit breakers cooled, in which nobody owned what came up. 

Mina had learned to locate the pockets through perturbation theory, a subject she had failed twice at college and grasped at once when it became a question of finding Ada.  You put an object with a known history on the balance, changed its position by a hair, and measured how much the deck tipped beyond what its weight could explain.  From three such small disturbances she could calculate the bearing of the mass of years in which the object had gone on being handled. 

She used the half geode Ada had given her the morning of the wedding, its purple crystal teeth still catching threads from the pocket of her good black coat.  Ada had carried the matching half in her dress instead of flowers, having once attended a wedding where two grown women came to blows over a bouquet.  After the drone strike a soldier had tried to give Mina back that second half, with bits of white satin caught in it, and she had made him take it away. 

All winter the bearings had converged on one patch of black water, and tonight she eased the pontoons out over it and paid out the wire until the bunk rails grew too hot to touch.  The needle fell against its stop; from below came the sweet little click of the breakers saving their own lives.  Mina took the raw line in both hands.  The manual called this an unfiltered awakening and advised against it because the body could not always tell which of several equally probable things it had felt. 

Something intangibly tangible, like the weight of a sleeping child just after you have put her down, settled into the crook of her arm.  Then she was sitting in a chair having her nails cut by a stout woman with a pleat between her brows and a towel spread over her knees.  She knew her daughter by the way Ada pressed each fingertip afterward, making sure she had not taken any skin.  At twelve she had done the same for the old dog, paw by patient paw. 

Mina wanted to ask about children, work, whether anybody had loved her well, but the mouth she had in that room was full of loose teeth and would not shape the questions.  She could only watch as Ada laid the clippers down and turned her face away and took one long, ragged breath before turning back.  Of all the things Mina had imagined giving her daughter by getting her those missing years, she had never once considered the privilege of watching her mother die.  It looked like hell and she wanted it for her with a ferocity that nearly tore the wire from its reel. 

From below came the thump of the first breaker resetting; already the pleat was smoothing out of the face, grief being one of the inconsistencies the clients paid to lose.  With the last of the cold in the wire she managed to turn her old hand over on the towel and let her daughter rest her cheek in it.  On the dawn launch Mina hung over the rail being thoroughly sick, laughing between spasms because Ada had grown whiskers on her chin.
