After the last forest burned, Nell took a job guarding the freight that could still have been timber.  She got it because she was dreamless; other guards fell asleep beside the divergent archive and woke to find their bunks full of the leaves they remembered.  Once a man dreamed of the fire instead, and there was a black square where his carriage had been.  No leaves for Nell, no little tongues of flame.  She slept like a spoon in a drawer. 

Her run ended on a glowing dune in bloom, where the sand flowers shone so brightly that the children in the company houses needed curtains to sleep.  On the other side of the dune, in the old freight tunnels, children slept three to a mattress in a dark so complete they could see the sparks behind their eyelids.  Nell had been one of those children, and knew what it was to wake up reaching for that light.  The flowers would not take below; her mother had carried one down in a teacup, and by the bottom stair it was just a wire of grass.  Yet on the nights the archive came through, pinpricks of green appeared in the cracks above the beds.  All her life Nell had wanted to cultivate the sparks in the unlit corners, to see what those shy little lights were trying to become. 

On her last run before the company sealed the archive inside its bright conservatory, she took aboard a stowaway.  Tess was nine, a tunnel child and a terrible chess cheat, who could put a captured knight back on the board with her toes.  She brought her grandfather's chess clock, frozen at midnight on the night the forest power station burned, its two buttons still taking turns to rise.  Playing in the dark, they used the click to tell each other it was safe to reach across. 

According to the freight manual, the archive traveled through wave functions, which meant that between inspections it was occasionally everywhere the train might have gone.  Nell knew this without the manual; as a girl she had felt the rails hum under her mattress although the switches down there were rusted through.  At the conservatory a clerk would open the freight door, fixing the delivery at a single address.  Tonight Nell meant to give somebody else first turn. 

Where the tracks forked she stopped the train and tucked Tess up on the mailbags with the clock between their faces.  She had thought it would be easy to charm a child to sleep with the tune her mother used to sing.  But the first thing Tess did when she shut her eyes was smell smoke.  The paint on the freight door began to blister.  Sleep, little cinder, Nell sang, and could not for the life of her remember what came next.  All the songs she knew went up to the lip of that night and broke off there.  From over the dune came the long impatient whistle of the clerk. 

Nell reached for the brake release, but a small bare foot got to the clock before she could move.  Click, and a cold knight pressed into her palm. 

"Your turn to shut your eyes," said Tess.  "Listen to who's on the other side, Nell." 

With her eyes closed Nell could hear something besides the whistle: knuckles tapping on the freight door, low down, at the height of a child's head.  As long as nobody looked, that door stood in the tunnels too.  She had brought Tess to dream a whole wood into being, and here were the children themselves, asking to be let in.  She lifted the latch and kept her eyes shut. 

Click went the clock, and small feet shuffled, and somebody said in a furious whisper not to take the thing by its roots.  Click again, and all along the train came the sound of hands reaching across.  When Nell finally looked, the carriage held nothing but a little spilled earth and Tess with a black smudge on her nose.  Down in the tunnels a thousand green lamps opened under a thousand pairs of grubby thumbs. 

Rest, little cinder; put out a leaf.  It came back to her on the long walk down, after she turned in her keys.  Under each lamp there were two leaves yet to unfold, curved toward each other like hands around a match.  She would have work enough.
