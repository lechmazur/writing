At nautical twilight the opal dock gave back just enough light to show a man where the boards ended and the water began.  In mist this was sometimes the only distinction left in the world, and Ada Bell, the lighthouse keeper, went down with a handbell to supply another.  She rang once for wood underfoot, twice for open water, a system of her own devising which the harbor commissioners had forbidden twice and learned to rely on. 

That evening the last boat from the island prison brought one passenger, carried ashore on a door.  Somebody had buttoned his coat wrong, but in its lapel he wore a dried Edelweiss boutonniere, every little woolly point intact.  Ada knelt to ask his name.  Instead of answering, he put the bell down for her, took her chin between his fingers, and turned her face until she was looking straight at him.  Then he folded his hands and waited, with the extraordinary patience of a man having his likeness taken when there is already a cramp in his neck.  He died like that. 

I was the receiving clerk, and it fell to me to write down what he had meant by it.  The boatman thought he had wanted a kiss.  Ada said we owed him better than our first guess, and showed up at my office next morning with two boiled eggs and a week cleared on her calendar.  In the softly persistent way that water finds the inside of a boot, she got us permission to investigate the island mail. 

For thirty years the prisoners had been allowed to send borrowed memories home in slivers cut from the same seam of opal that paved the dock.  You could not send the sight of your own face except by borrowing another person's sight of it, so the men had evidently come to an arrangement.  In every sliver a man sat against a limewashed wall, his hair wet and combed, his hands folded as if to keep them from doing anything that might worry his mother.  There were hundreds still unclaimed. 

I looked for the stranger among the faces; Ada made her discovery by observing gaps.  There was never a sitter at the hour when a long wedge of sun would have fallen across the chair, although the wedge crept up to one leg and receded from the other.  Whoever lent these men his eyes knew something about light.  There was a gap, too, between each man's top button and his throat, even on the stoutest men; somebody standing behind the chair had pulled a single good coat snug for everybody.  Once Ada caught a glimpse of a brown thumb brushing lime off the shoulder, and on it a crescent scar.  She touched the place on her chin where that thumb had rested. 

We went back through the slivers looking now for the flower.  On young men it stood up bravely, on old men it drooped, and once a boy who could not have been sixteen took it out and held it under his nose, forgetting that a dried flower has no smell.  From somewhere behind the borrowed eyes came a laugh so tender that I had to put the sliver down. 

"He wasn't asking me to look at him," Ada said at last.  "He was asking me to sit still long enough to lend him the looking." 

She fetched an unused sliver and laid into it what she had seen between the moment he set down her bell and the moment the life went out of his face.  We filed him with the others, not under Unknown, as the rules prescribed, but under Portraitist, which was how a person looking for him might think to ask. 

Nobody ever did ask, though families came for years and sometimes brought us things, a cheese or a pair of mittens, in gratitude for what that patient man had given them.  Ada always took them to his drawer before they left.  I have seen a grown woman look into that last scrap of evening, recognize the coat in which she had believed her father buried, and stay to the very end, so that the stranger on the dock did not have to hold his pose for nothing.
