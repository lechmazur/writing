In the cobalt library, with mist pressed white against its blue windows, Nell taught the after-school chess club how to lose a house.  She had marked each rook with the number of a condemned cottage, and on the other board stood the new estate, all its squares still empty.  By drawing lots the children could move everybody out of reach of the collapsing mine in under a minute.  Then Elsie put her finger on number six and said nothing.  Nell heard what she did not say as distinctly as the little tick of a rook settling on its magnet: Who will hear Gran knock? 

At home Elsie's grandmother knocked on the common wall when she needed help getting from bed to chair.  Nell had heard unspoken words all her life, but had learned early how people flinched if you answered them.  She asked Elsie to play the move she wanted instead.  The girl took two rooks in one fist. 

Above the returns desk stood a magnetic grotto under starlight, a little tabletop toy the miners themselves had made to show their children how the first rescue had been done.  Pinholes in its tin sky let lamplight through; a magnet on a runner drew a string of iron men along the one safe gallery.  Every year on Rescue Night the town watched them come out in single file on a screen above the square.  This year the housing board would draw the lots on camera immediately afterward, so that nobody could accuse it of favoring friends. 

That evening Nell stayed until the late shift had collected its children, as she had every practice night for seventeen years.  By then Elsie had worked out how to cradle two paper houses in a loop of wire and slide them together across the grotto's tin floor.  From outside, through the mist, their lit windows looked like the four holes in a button.  One such button in the broadcast might look like a plea for special treatment; a townful would look like a proposal. 

They had three days.  Nell spent them organizing synchronized window displays across town, starting with the butcher because his hands were steady and he owned the longest scissors.  He cut houses from the board that came under bacon and left the windows for the children to prick out with knitting needles.  Some people asked for three houses and some for one, and one woman whose brother drank asked for hers to travel in the opposite direction.  Nell had imagined herself going door to door to plant forests of second thoughts, but at that door she had to stop and grow one of her own.  She had wanted a town where nobody got left; that was not the same as a town where nobody could leave. 

On Rescue Night the camera always turned from the last iron man to the lighted houses climbing the hill: the descendants, the announcer called them.  Children crouched below the sills with baking sheets, chess magnets, and instructions to wait for the last man.  Up at the library the board chairman shook the drum of numbers, and Nell heard the thing he could not say into a microphone.  I promised them fair.  She believed him. 

The iron runner rasped to a stop.  In the hush where past meets prophecy, before a hand went into the drum, the camera turned uphill and the houses began to move.  Paired squares of gold glided sideways through the mist; three went abreast above the butcher's shop; a single square sailed clear and held its ground.  On the screen they were enormous, yet the only sound in the square was a bicycle ticking as somebody wheeled it to a stop. 

Elsie set her two rooks on the chairman's table, close enough for their chipped shoulders to touch.  "Draw for who gets to choose first," she said.  "Let people put their names in together if they want." 

The chairman did not call it fair straight off, which was the first thing Nell liked about him.  He asked what would happen to a single person if all the good places went to groups.  Nobody knew, so he put the lid on the drum and asked the camera operator for another night. 

By closing time there were six arrangements on the chessboards, each with something wrong that a child had spotted and an adult was trying to mend.  From the chair by the radiator Elsie's grandmother tapped the wall twice with her cane.  Without looking up from their work, half the room tapped back.
