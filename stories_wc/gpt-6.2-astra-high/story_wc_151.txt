At dawn the golden arcade was full of upside down suns, one in each brass bowl set out to catch the leaks.  I unlocked my stall under the clock and hung up the ebony clipboard that had been my mother's sign before it was mine.  On its black face a century of customers had scratched trial signatures, tenderly juxtaposing such names as Gas Giant and Little Spoon.  I was a pseudonym creator, which on the floating estates was a trade like any other, except that you seldom got to sign your work. 

The estates passed one another once a year, close enough to exchange sacks of surface mail by winch, and every crossing brought a fresh crop of would-be correspondents.  A child might want a name to grow into; an old woman might want one her grandchildren would never think to look for.  Some exchanges lasted longer than marriages, including the one that had outlasted mine. 

For thirty crossings I had written as Small Hours to somebody on the estate of Palermo who signed himself The Night Baker.  We had buried three parents between us, raised a daughter apiece, and disagreed about practically everything except the proper degree of ripeness of a pear.  Inside my apron lay his last letter, with its extraordinary proposal that this time, instead of writing back, I should step across.  My daughter had packed me a case; I had composed a carefully inventive refusal involving the imminent birth of a nonexistent grandchild.  I would mail it as soon as the sacks came down. 

I could take a year away from the stall, but I could not take the voice he knew along with me.  On paper I never stuck on a consonant until somebody kindly, murderously supplied the rest of the word. 

Below my counter two little girls were laying out a conversation in children's sidewalk chalk, the thick kind that wouldn't roll on a pitching deck.  They used the old pavement codes: a box around a drawing meant keep off, a tail on the box made it a kite, an invitation to add something.  There was a third sign I had forgotten, two uprights like the sides of a ladder with all the rungs missing.  One girl set it beside an unfinished bird and ran off to fetch another color; the other sat down on her heels to wait.  Not finished yet, it meant, which was different from keep off. 

All my life I had tried to discover meaning in the margins of what people told me: the right name was generally there, in the bit they thought they could leave out.  Yet until I saw those chalk marks I had never thought to juxtapose the Baker's first letter and his last.  I kept the first tucked inside my till, for luck, since he had been my first customer on the morning the stall became mine.  He had come across from Palermo on the plank, a boy with flour on his cuffs, and handed me a note asking for a name that smelled good.  Down one edge of that note were two pencil strokes; down one edge of the invitation, almost lost in the fold, were two more.  Between lay thirty years in which neither of us had ever had to ask the other for time to finish. 

Suddenly I remembered him waiting while I struggled to tell him the price, not looking away, not guessing, just peeling the backing off a stick of gum in one long white curl.  Perhaps all he remembered of me was a girl behind a counter; perhaps he remembered perfectly well how that girl spoke.  I had invented a man who would be disappointed in me, then invented a grandchild to spare his feelings.  Even for somebody in my line that was going a little far. 

The winch clattered and the suns in the bowls broke up into little gold coins.  I turned my refusal over and wrote my daughter a receipt for the loan of one stall, rent payable in news of the neighborhood, term one crossing.  Under it I signed my own name, with a kite around it so she could add her terms. 

By the time Palermo's plank came down I was at the head of the queue with my case and a stick of chalk I had bought from the girls.  Across the gap a man was eating a pear with the juice running down his wrist, which was exactly how ripe we both liked them.  He lifted his clean hand and waited for me to finish crossing.
