On the evening between kings, the palace of Babylon came apart with a noise like nine old men getting out of bed.  The cedar hulls beneath its courtyards nudged one another, and blue lions painted on separate walls touched noses across the widening seams.  From her roof, the astronomer Iltani heard the ferrymen on the conquered bank take up the sound and turn it into a song.  They had no boats left, but they still knew how to sing a crossing. 

At dawn the new king would have the hulls lashed together again, unless someone claimed them for an older use.  River law was plain: what joined the banks when the king took his oath belonged to both banks for the length of his reign.  Iltani knew the law because her wife had spent their last good winter measuring the river with a knotted line.  She had begrudged Adra even the price of the line; there was a perfectly serviceable one on her own sighting frame.  Iltani washed and reused her writing clay, trimmed lampwicks with hair scissors, and had observed three comets through a tube made from a broken flute.  When the king hanged Adra with the other boatwrights who had refused to build his troop barges, Iltani kept the line. 

That evening she used it to lower herself into the empty throne room.  Set into the floor was Adra's magic square tile, nine little pictures of the palace with a different number of stars above each.  The late king had won a good deal at dice by betting guests that any row they chose would yield fifteen stars, even on the slant.  Iltani had never looked closely at the buildings underneath until tonight, when she saw that the palace with one star had a crooked lion.  She had just seen that lion from her roof: it marked the first hull to swing free of the royal moorings. 

She followed the stars from one to nine with the tip of her finger.  Read across, the pictures were nine palaces; read in order, they showed one palace turning itself inside out.  Doors that opened onto other doors opened onto water, then onto land.  Adra, who could never draw a face, had left her a love letter entirely in plans.  Even at this scale there was the sag in the observatory roof where they used to sleep in summer.  Not for her alone, either; the last picture showed two mooring posts driven into ground where neither woman had ever set foot. 

Downstairs the palace oarsmen sat on the quay, waiting to see whose silver they would be rowing for next.  Iltani emptied her purse onto the tile, and the men passed both silver and pictures from hand to hand.  The youngest asked who would catch a line on the far bank, and she said she didn't know.  He took his oar anyway. 

By moonrise eight courtyards lay end to end across the current, their bronze door sills knocking out a slow accompaniment to the singing.  The ninth, her observatory, fell short of the farther shore by the length of a cedar trunk.  Adra had measured in winter, and now the river was in flood.  All her life Iltani had made a virtue of correcting the errors of people who believed what they wanted to believe about the sky.  Now the error in the work she most wanted to believe in was exactly the length of her sighting frame. 

She held the tile up to the moon to uncloak the last faint incisions, rubbed nearly smooth by the king's slippers.  In the ninth picture there was no tower.  She had been so pleased to recognize her own courtyard that she had supplied the tower out of her head.  At her direction the oarsmen took down the long cedar uprights, laid them from deck to mud, and spread her roof across them.  Somewhere over there in the reeds somebody had to drive the posts, or the current would have the whole expensive business by morning.  She could not see to whom she was tossing Adra's line.  For once she gave a thing away without so much as weighing what might come back. 

Two mallets answered, not quite together, like a pair of children learning a tune.  Iltani, who had expected to spend the morning mourning her observatory, lay on the planks and watched the last stars set.  For the first time in forty years she had a clear view to the west, where her tower used to stand.  At dawn the new king came down to take his oath and had to wait for a woman carrying eggs to clear his throne room.
