In the canyon of endless echoes, a shouted name could put a thousand birds to flight and leave a thousand supper pots empty.  So the netters who lived in the towers of bone had made a language for the hand, to be spoken on the wrist without taking an eye off the sky.  When the netters took their children downriver to pick peaches for wages, they left behind two old women who could still carry on a quarrel without a sound.  One of them was my mother, and the other had not touched her in forty years. 

My mother's trade was telling stories; mine was mending the little talking boxes that peach merchants used to dictate their accounts.  People called her the copper-veined storyteller because she had run fine copper wires under the worn leather of her artificial hand, and when she flexed it the wires rose like veins.  With that hand she was quietly articulate in ways that her river English, all prices and weights, could never be.  She wanted the grandchildren to know why it had been worth living up there, so she asked me to make a box that would translate texture into sound. 

I wrapped soft wax round a drum and had her speak on it as if it were my wrist.  To read the ridges I borrowed my aunt's flawed diamond, the one rumored to be cursed because it had cost my mother her hand.  The sisters had been girls together when they found it in the jawbone that bridged the deepest cleft.  That much I knew; what had happened on the bridge was the one story neither would tell.  The flaw divided the tip neatly in two, so that under the horn of my box one point could follow the thumb and one the moving finger.  Out came a dry rasp with a little sob at the end. 

My mother listened politely, then took me to the old netting ledge above the tunnel of echoes where the sisters had worked as girls.  A flight of teal was coming up through the tunnel, black against the white ribs of the roof.  She made me lay my good recording hand inside her copper one and watch the flock instead of our fingers.  As the leaders swung out around a dangling rib, her thumb rode forward; as the stragglers bunched and swept after them, her forefinger overtook it.  Through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight I began to learn what she had been saying to me all my life.  The thumb was the one who went ahead, the forefinger the one who followed, and the space between their tracks was the terrible instant in which you could lose each other. 

I had been trying to harness two voices to a single horn.  Back in my shop I split the mount along the fault in the diamond and gave each half its own horn, one a reed and one a bell.  I asked my aunt to come and hear what we had made.  She stood in the doorway with her coat buttoned and her gloves on while I turned the crank.  The reed went first, a small high girl running; the bell went after, gained on her, and stopped so suddenly that I put out my hand to catch something.  Yet the reed kept going, and only then did I hear, in the narrowing intervals between its notes, that it was going down.  There was clear air between those voices; the bell had never quite caught up. 

"You jumped," my aunt said. 

Mother nodded, and my aunt sat down hard on the doorstep, as if the missing plank had finally given way under her too.  "All this time I thought I pushed you," she said.  "For that filthy little chip you jumped?" 

My mother, whose hand had been crushed between two ribs halfway down, had the decency to look ashamed.  In her careful river English she said she had meant to tell the truth tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow.  Then she took my aunt's wrist and the two of them sat on the step until it was too dark for me to see their fingers. 

My aunt died that winter and my mother the next, and the grandchildren got the box and the stone that had once been worth jumping for.  When I play the record for them I don't tell them it speaks their grandmothers' language; there are questions they can ask it to which it has no reply.  But the youngest always reaches out at the place where the bell stops, and her sister always takes her hand.
