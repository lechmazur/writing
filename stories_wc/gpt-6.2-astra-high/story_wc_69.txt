Every autumn the velvet library came downriver, its red curtains furled while it traveled and let down when the boat tied up.  The curtains had once belonged to a theater; drawn around the shelves, they gave even a sneeze the intimacy of a confidence.  Children came aboard with yellow leaves stuck to their soles and stood under the little brass lamp to see what other towns had sent them. 

This year there was a zither from the town underneath the reservoir.  Its soundhole rosette was a street map cut in pearwood, with the river showing black through the middle and a hair of wood left for the bridge.  Alma, the choir teacher, knew it before she read the card.  As a child she had put a finger down on that map, sung a knock at a particular door, and waited for somebody to sing her inside.  No two households had answered a knock in quite the same way.  The card said that nobody living knew how to play the game. 

"Nobody asked," said Alma. 

She took her choir aboard after school, along with her mother and old Gil Sayer, whose hands had made the rosette.  The two old people sat as far apart as the width of the cabin allowed.  Gil had taken the water board's money and moved uphill early; Alma's mother had stayed until a man in boots carried her out of her kitchen.  It was Gil who had taken the board's petition from door to door, in exchange for a level plot where his sister could get about.  At school their grandchildren shared a desk without ever borrowing a pencil. 

Alma had spent years getting such children to sing together by subtracting noise, which usually meant asking the loudest child to listen for the softest.  It made for a harmonious choir and a teacher much in demand at weddings, but it had done nothing about the pencils.  She wanted them to have something they could make only by attending to one another, and could keep when the boat went away. 

She plucked the two worn strings that used to denote a knock, then touched the far end of the wooden bridge.  Her mother sang the fishmonger's welcome, a long slide down to the note on which he used to call the price of eels.  A child tried the bakery, and Gil gave the baker's answer through his nose, exactly the sound of a man bent over a hot oven.  Soon the children were knocking everywhere except at a little square beside the river where the pearwood was worn almost through. 

That was Gil's house.  His granddaughter held her finger above it, looking at Alma with the expectantly resigned face of a child waiting to be told that something plainly visible was not there.  Alma nodded, the girl knocked, and Gil opened his mouth.  Nothing came out. 

"I made the map," he said at last, "but you have to remember I was inside that door, not outside hearing how we sounded." 

Alma's mother pulled the zither across her knees.  She knocked and answered herself with three quick notes and a fourth so late that two children laughed when it came. 

"His sister had a bad leg," she said.  "Took her that long to get from her chair to the latch." 

Gil looked up at the fourth note, as if his sister had just crossed a floor that lay under sixty feet of green water.  "You used to wait for her," he said. 

"I wasn't always in such a hurry to get away from you," said Alma's mother. 

They did not settle the business of the water board that afternoon.  Over the next three autumns they sat a little closer, because the choir kept finding doors for which one remembered the knock and the other the welcome.  Before the boat left, Gil's granddaughter laid a sheet of paper over the pearwood and rubbed it with the side of a pencil she had borrowed at school.  She left the map aboard and took the rubbing home, where her little brother wanted a turn at every door.  By then Alma could walk uphill from the mooring in the early dark and hear, out of different kitchens, the town below the water letting people in.
