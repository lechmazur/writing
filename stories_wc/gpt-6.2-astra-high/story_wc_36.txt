After the guardianship ended, Ada kept the key because nobody else wanted to go down and tell the flute.  It lay in a cedar case at the bottom of the Thunder-echo range, where even in August the stone sweated cold water.  For thirty years she had been its keeper, oiling the hinges so that no note except the intended one should enter the canyon. 

Now the council wanted the gilded reed flute for a glass cabinet, with a card giving the number of people it had brought across.  Ada had asked for room on the card for the other number and been told there was no other number, properly speaking.  That was the trouble with losing a whole string of travelers between worlds: nobody at either end could swear how many had set out.  Under the terms of the guardianship no crossing could be attempted except to conduct a living soul; nobody missing qualified. 

On the morning of her last descent she took the case from its niche but did not uncase the instrument at once.  Instead she went along the dry wash arranging driftwood as directional markers, forked ends pointing home, peeled white bellies up.  A stone stayed in whichever world had made it, but wood that had ridden the spring flood belonged to both.  She had learned that from a boy who had crossed carrying his dead father's crutch, still dripping from the other side. 

At the last fork she opened the case and put her thumb in the hollow worn through the gold to the reed.  The crossing note was hardly music; it was the sound a woman makes blowing grit from a baby's eye.  The first echo brought up the wet canyon, its river running translucent green through the dust around her boots.  At each return of the note she stepped from one bank to the other, weaving between worlds as her mother had woven rush seats, always drawing the last pass tight. 

Then came the shelf where she had lost the string, and the air went avalanchingly quiet.  First her footfalls vanished, then her breath, then the little wet click her tongue made lifting off the reed.  Last time she had blown harder here until her lip split, as if the people behind her had merely fallen out of earshot.  This time she wanted to know what her noise had covered, even if all she brought home was a better account of how they died. 

She took the flute from her lips and felt, through the thin soles of her shoes, three knocks.  Below the shelf, in a narrow pleat of shade that neither sun had ever entered, somebody was hitting rock with a spoon.  She lay flat and saw the faces turned up like the pale undersides of leaves; not one had aged the eleven years she had.  The woman with the spoon mouthed something Ada knew from thirty years of talking to people over the noise of water. 

Not so fast. 

She saw it then: she had played to the feet of the quickest, and every echo had drawn the weaving tight across the knees of those still coming.  She could play them a road out, but not from up here where she could not feel their feet.  She slid down into the fold and gave the woman her wrist and laid two fingers of the spoon hand on the holes.  Together they made a wavering, ill-spaced series of notes such as no keeper would have let a pupil finish.  The white forks showed above them, first through water, then through air, and one by one the travelers climbed past, taking whatever time their knees required. 

When the woman climbed out, Ada followed the last faint gleam of wood with no note left to tell her if she would arrive.  Somebody caught her collar and hauled her into dust that smelled, wonderfully, of mule piss and sage.  She started to count heads, an old reflex, but the woman pressed the bowl of the spoon against her lips.  It held a little water from the crossing, enough to wet the place where her lip had split again.  Later they would sit together over the council's little card, turning it over to write on the back, then sending out for paper.
