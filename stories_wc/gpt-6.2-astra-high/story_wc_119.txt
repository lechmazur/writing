After last orders at the Lantern, I carried the till upstairs and found Uncle Ray trying to waltz with a display case.  He had it by two corners and was easing it across the old lamp room floor, one felt foot at a time.  Through its glass I saw a sheet headed SPYCRAFT: MICRODOT FILM SPECS, with a brown ring where somebody had once set a cup.  That sheet was the principal reason visitors paid a pound to climb the lighthouse on our little tooth of rock.  Nobody took the boat out for the view, which on nine days out of ten was of the inside of a cloud. 

Tomorrow the county museum was collecting the case, and Ray wanted the ring to show.  The curator had proposed covering it with a nice explanatory label about the island's clandestine wartime photographic unit.  There had been such a unit, but the ring was what interested Ray.  "Len always put his tea there," he said.  "If they want Len they can take the stain."  Len had been dead a month.  According to the island paper he had left no family.  I put down the till and took the other two corners. 

My trade is writing fortunes for the almond cookies my sister sells downstairs, six to a souvenir tin.  The trick is to tell people something they know in language that allows them to enjoy not quite knowing it.  That week I had written, What you turn over will ask you to dance.  I was thinking of a record.  But when we got the case flat on the tiles and lifted the loose back, Ray turned the sheet over.  There in Len's violet capitals were the steps of the Paul Jones, with a diagram for directing the flow of partners round a circle. 

"You see," Ray said, "we didn't all come here knowing how." 

I had known about the dances, in the way that everybody who had ever used the expression photographic club had known.  I hadn't known they had needed lessons.  It gave me an odd pang to imagine my uncle young enough to have two left feet and a whole evening to do something about them.  He asked if I could write one of my little riddles for the museum, to make people look round the back.  I got out my pencil, but everything I thought of sounded like an invitation to snigger. 

"What do you want them to know?" I asked. 

He thought about it for long enough that I heard the tide turn the loose stones over at the landing.  "How it felt when the whistle went and there he was." 

I wanted to give him what my sister could give a tune: a different note to start on, with all the dear old intervals intact.  Downstairs my sister was still settling the domino scores; on our island last orders and going home are quite distinct events.  I went to the stairhead and whistled up the people who had taught me to keep a secret by never telling me it was one.  Six came, then my sister with the old squeeze box from behind the optics display. 

Ray showed me how to orchestrate the business without fixing anybody up.  "Two rings going opposite ways," he said, "and don't look where you're going or you'll miss who's going by."  At my whistle they turned inward and outward, and whoever stood before you was yours for sixteen bars.  Once Ray got the empty space where I ought to have been, and held out his arms to it.  I nearly stopped the music.  Then my sister swung the tune from minor to major, a vulgar trick that Len had loved, and Ray caught me by the belt loop and hauled me in. 

"This isn't a seance," he said into my ear.  "I'm still here." 

Afterwards he wrote the label himself on the cream paper I cut my fortunes from.  "Len Mercer, who taught the photographic club to dance, and whom I lived with for forty years."  Below that he signed his name, not as a witness signs but with the big extravagant R he used on birthday cards.  We set the sheet upright between two panes and left the curator instructions to display it that way, so a visitor could see first how to make a great deal of writing very small, and then, by walking round, what all that saved space was for.
