The grain elevator had a magnificent bass voice because I was fourteen and could manage no other kind without squeaking.  When I crouched behind the model and said that, having served the town on its feet for eighty years, it would now like to lie down, even the mayor laughed.  Then Ada pulled a cord, and the four little wooden silos came apart into the long red planks of two bridges.  In the front row a woman stood up with a baby against her collarbone and asked where we proposed to put her bed. 

Up to that moment the grain elevator talks had been the greatest success of Ada's life.  As choral librarian she had spent thirty years putting the right words in front of people who otherwise had very little to say to each other.  She could find a third above the buzz of a wasp on the window and keep it company till somebody let it out.  Since the spring floods had sent both footbridges to sea, she had held her rehearsals in the astronomical clock tower on the island between the two channels, where the ferries met.  It was there she'd hit on the idea of taking down the empty silos instead of buying timber nobody could afford. 

Tonight, under the hush of borrowed starlight that the tower mirrors laid across our painted river, she looked at the model as if she had never seen it.  "Show me," she said, sliding it toward the woman. 

The woman lifted the west bridge and set its landing on the tiny square marked PUBLIC QUAY.  She laid the baby's mitten over the square, then turned to the window and pointed down at a blue roof just visible across the water.  The mitten and the roof were exactly the same shape. 

"Public down to the last nail," said the mayor, not unkindly, and the woman nodded as if she had heard that before.  Her husband mended the town nets there; after the flood they had been given leave to sleep above the drying shed.  I had painted that square myself, from a survey older than the baby and considerably younger than the shed. 

That was when I noticed the woman looking at the cuckoo clock weight on its silk cord beside my elbow.  It was an iron pine cone, the load with which we ended every performance, lowering it onto each span to prove our joinery.  "May I try something?" she asked, and instead of weighing down a bridge she laid the cord along its edge to take its length.  Holding that length between finger and thumb, with the pine cone for an anchor on the island, she swept an arc across the western shore.  It just grazed the church steps. 

"Six feet of stone to climb," I said. 

Her finger went on until the cord touched the low brick sill of the old choir school, where Ada kept the music that wouldn't fit in the tower.  The woman spoke in Portuguese now, too fast for me, and Ada answered by interspersing languages the way she did at rehearsal, giving each unfamiliar phrase a familiar echo.  "Porta, door," she finished, and set her two thumbs on the sill and tipped them outward, opening an imaginary pair of doors. 

Behind that window stood our shelves of part songs, and behind the shelves was the courtyard with its level way out to the street.  Nobody asked where we proposed to put thirty years of music.  I wanted to, but Ada had already given me the school key and was telling the mayor he could have our front room if he would pay to rehabilitate the rotten floor. 

The woman picked up the pine cone, lowered it onto the red planks, and got the applause that usually belonged to me.  I surprised myself by joining in. 

That winter we carried the scores upstairs in the tower, one part to each singer, and it was June before the first bridge reached the new double doors.  At the opening they wanted my old speech about lying down, but by then I could sing tenor and had lost the trick of making timber talk.  Besides, the baby had learned to walk.  We all shut up and listened to what those small hard shoes could get out of a board.
