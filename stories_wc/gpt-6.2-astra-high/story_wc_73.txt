Release was on the step below my brother's boots, and my brother was good at coming down a step.  The other way, of course, the staircase went up forever; it went down forever too, but the law had drawn a chalk line and called it home.  A warder rolled a pair of dice in a cup, then the walker rolled the same pair; if the walker scored higher he went down, if lower he went up, and ties didn't count.  One die was chipped where a walker had tried to eat it, but even a crooked pair gave the two men equal chances. 

I could pay his fine with my loom or let him walk for it.  He put out his hand, not for the loom, I think, but because he could see what it would cost me either way.  I remember the brush of the chalk on his cuff against my palm as I put my hand back in my pocket.  Up he went. 

From my loom I could hear the cup, then only the horn that announced a walker on the stair, then only the other looms.  We weavers were supposed to like the walk because it left poor people their tools.  The stair was lined on both sides with workshops, and after every throw somebody took over the cup, so no warder ever went far from his bench.  The Council called this its engine of perpetual motion: no fuel to buy and no prison to keep in repair.  Nobody counted the bread we left out on the treads. 

I am an optimist of the sort who lays out a shroud and a hot supper, and keeps the supper hot.  I put my earnings in a jar for his fine, although no fine could be paid after the first throw.  What got me away from the loom at last was wanting somebody else, just once, to feel that cuff.  A touch that light ought not to be able to rupture two lives; it was just a little grit that a woman with work to do might wipe away. 

The Council could be moved only through mathematical proofs, a language I knew from setting up patterns that must hold across any width of cloth.  I took them a strip of linen with a chalk line at one edge and a red thread at the other.  Between these I had woven ten stair treads, counting the red thread as the tenth. 

Suppose, I said, you could go free at either edge.  On each tread I had stitched the distance to the chalk multiplied by the distance to the red.  Each number was one more than the average of its neighbors, exactly as the time you must expect to walk includes one move besides whatever walking that move leaves you.  At either edge the number was zero, since you were done.  Their reckoner ran his thumbnail along the stitches and nodded; there was only one set of numbers that could fit those conditions. 

From the first tread, nine moves on average.  Put the red thread a hundred treads away and it was ninety-nine; put it anywhere you pleased and it still offered a mercy the real stair did not.  One step from home, and no finite average wait. 

The reckoner said this did not disprove the promise on the cup, that every walker came home with probability one. 

Except, I said, the ones who died. 

That was as far as I dared push him; I knew what a man would do to a pattern to keep from admitting he had miscounted.  I asked for no judgment on the walk itself, only that a family might buy a man out on any tread.  While they voted, the reckoner worried the cloth between his fingers until the chalk came off on him.  He rubbed his fingers on his coat, then looked down, as if somebody had touched him. 

They took my jar.  They sent the order up by horn, bench answering bench until the sound wore thin, and I went back to keeping his supper hot.  When he came down at last he laid one hand on every bench, as if to make quite sure he was allowed to pass it without stopping.  He laid his hand on mine, and this time I let the shuttle drop.
