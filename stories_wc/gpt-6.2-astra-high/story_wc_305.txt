The morning after she forgave the man who had sold her boys to the press gang, Nell woke up hungry, which was quite a disappointment.  She had expected contentment, the look she saw on the faces of the dead when they were ready to be born again.  For ninety years she had kept the umbral grotto above the clouds, washing its sheets, gathering its little black grapes, easing one frightened generation after another into sleep and down into waiting wombs.  It seemed unfair that she should know how to get cherry stains out of linen and not know where that look came from. 

At breakfast there was only one guest left, a woman with flour so deep in the creases of her hands that death had not got it out.  She had been at the table three mornings now, and the child meant for her was due before dusk.  The woman had not slept a wink. 

"I kept a bakehouse," she said when Nell sat down beside her, "and my daughter doesn't know how to scallop the lids on the grape pies."  "All that juice on the oven floor, and sugar such a price." 

Nell knew better than to tell the woman her daughter would manage, though in ninety years she had certainly learned that daughters did.  She took a gathering basket and went up the fig tree that grew slantwise out of the grotto mouth, carrying a grapevine on its back.  Climbing trees to think better was a habit left over from her days as a mother, when nobody thought to look for her above the wash line.  She settled in the fork, where the grapes hung warm in the sun and her feet dangled in the cool perpetual shade.  Below her the woman was absently pinching the tablecloth into waves, undoing each one with a little stroke like a caress.  Nell thought of the youngest boy buttoning his own coat for the first time and of how bereft her fingers had felt, standing by. 

She cut two bunches and climbed down.  "Show me," she said, and put the grapes on the table. 

For the first time the baker looked frightened.  "I can't remember my girl's name," she said.  "Get the flour before I lose the rest." 

Nell fetched the flour and the stone bowl and, since the baker kept looking round for something, emptied her apron pockets on the board.  The woman picked out the antique hairpin of hammered silver that once held her braid clear of her boys' breakfast porridge.  "My mother had one like this," she said, running a thumb inside the bent end, polished to a spoon shine by a dozen women's thumbs. 

Under her direction Nell rolled the lids thin, then used the loop of the pin to press half moons all round the joined rims.  Too shallow and the seam would open, too deep and the silver would go through both layers and the purple syrup find the hole.  The baker laid her floured thumb over Nell's until Nell could feel through the pin the precise instant the two damp skins became one. 

The pies baked while the long afternoon light crept up the fig trunk, and the only sound was a grape now and then letting go into the leaves.  When Nell lifted the pies out on the peel, there was not a drop of purple on the stone.  She looked round for the baker and found her asleep, her face in the crook of her white arm, with that look on it.  Even as Nell bent to kiss her, the sleeve went slack.  Somewhere below the clouds a girl was drawing breath, without so much as a name to carry over from the old life into the new. 

Nell ate a pie standing up; the seam held until her teeth broke it and then the hot sweet juice ran down her chin.  She had spent three days trying to give the woman rest, and it had never occurred to her that the woman might have something left to give. 

When the next guest arrived, a sailor so young she had to put down the peel and steady herself, she almost asked him the question she had asked every sailor for ninety years.  Instead she cut him a wedge of pie, sat down opposite him with a wedge of her own, and asked what he did best.  He could whistle through a blade of grass, he said, so softly as to bring a wren down on his hand.  There was grass enough at the foot of the fig, and plenty of light left to cut two blades.
