The first thing Ada tried was tearing linen close to the microphone, but it sounded like somebody getting a clean bandage ready, which was nearly the opposite of what she wanted.  She had until sunrise to capture the sound of trust breaking.  At seven the little radio station would broadcast her play about the night her father lost his boat, and the script called for that sound between the knock on the hull and the boots coming aboard.  As a moonshine runner's daughter she ought to have known what it sounded like when your best friend sold you to the law.  She had been six and asleep under the nets.  Her father had done his time; his friend Ben had never come home. 

All night she had worked her way through false starts, a snapped fiddle string, an eggshell crushed in a mitten, until there was only one place left to go.  The pirate market hidden beneath the rotting pier kept a truce of hush: you could buy back anything the river thieves had taken, provided you never asked who brought it in.  Her father's antique watch fob, a little silver pear he used to knock against the hull when his hands were full, hung above the tea stall.  She had passed it there on a hundred errands, too proud to pay for what was his.  If she could get the knock right, perhaps the silence after it would do. 

Ben's sister was pouring tea, one thumb over the chipped place in each cup so nobody would cut a lip.  Against the pilings the water made soft sucking noises like a child reluctant to wake.  Ada set down her recorder and put a week's wages on the counter.  Instead of counting the money, Ruth took down the pear and knocked twice on the wood, paused, then knocked once more. 

Ada put out her hands to be lifted.  She had done it before she knew she was standing up, and Ruth, curiously steadfast under the weight of a grown woman leaning across her cups, did not step back. 

"You took me off the boat," Ada said. 

Ruth nodded. 

"I thought I slept through it." 

"You slept here, on the flour sacks, after." 

"How did you get his pear?" 

The women gutting mullet at the next stall laid their knives flat.  Even the boy who sold loose matches knew what you didn't ask down here.  Ruth reached past Ada and pressed the red button on the recorder; the reels, which Ada hadn't yet set going, began to turn. 

"Your dad left it against a flask of my tea, like he did most nights.  Ben told me to take it and knock for you just before the customs launch came round the bend." 

"He really did call them, then." 

"He tried everything else to make your daddy leave you ashore." 

Ruth poured her tea and let her come to the rest of it by herself.  It was an old trick, taking a little girl aboard to thwart a search, and Ada had even written it into the play as a joke.  Everybody knew the part where she sat on the hatch and asked the nice man had he come to hear her sing.  In the script the nice man tipped his cap and nobody heard what Ben said afterward, because her father had never told her that part. 

From up on the pier came the clatter of the milk cart, the first of the noises that belonged to morning. 

"Ben gets the station where he lives now," Ruth said, and stopped the tape. 

All these years Ada's father had called him everything but by his name, and the people who bought their drink from him had done the same.  She wondered how many miles a man had to put between himself and home before he could buy his own sister a cup of tea. 

At the studio her father was waiting in his good suit to read the part of the customs man; it had struck them both as very funny yesterday.  She threaded the tape and handed him the headphones, keeping her hand on his shoulder through the knock that wasn't there and the voice that was.  He didn't ask her not to play it.  Only when the red light came on did he take his good hat off his knee and cover with it the page where the little girl sang. 

Into the space left for a laugh Ada fitted the sound of hot tea pouring, right up to the brim.
