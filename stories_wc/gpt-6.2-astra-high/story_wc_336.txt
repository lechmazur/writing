From the verdant parapet beside the waterfall, Ada could see the orchard in the other world ending one tree at a time.  First the water took the whitewash off a trunk, then the trunk went over without a splash that anyone on this side could hear.  At her elbow a boy stood very still, holding the last seed his father had put into his hand before passing him through the spray. 

Ada was the orchard pruner on this bank and had been the weaver of the crossing since her fingers were small enough to slip between two drops.  It was much the same work: you chose what to leave open so that something living could come through.  Now her fingers found only water where the crossing had been.  The far end had always been fastened to a pear tree, the one whose whitewashed trunk they had just watched go over.  Beyond the torn curtain she could make out the boy's father on the packing shed roof, taking off his boots to give his feet a better purchase.  Even at that distance it was terrible to see how carefully he set them side by side. 

The boy opened his hand.  In it lay the family heirloom, a pear seed wrapped in a twist of paper softened by the sweat of several generations.  Each new keeper planted the seed, and when the tree bore fruit, chose one pip to put back in the paper. 

"Can you make it be our tree again?" he asked. 

Ada, who could never pare an apple without trying to make the peel into some new animal, had already twisted three shapes out of the spray.  One had the pear tree's low fork, one its lopsided crown, one even the pale oval where a swing had rubbed the bark away.  Each came apart as soon as she let go.  Weaving between worlds was done by capturing essence, and here she was making portraits, like somebody trying to succor a hungry man with a drawing of bread.  What had held the crossing all these years was not the look of that tree. 

"Why a seed," she asked, "when a graft would give you the very same pear?" 

The boy looked almost offended.  "Then we'd know what color it was inside." 

All at once she remembered his father as a child at the parapet, bringing over the first ripe pear of his keeping.  He had waited until every last picker from both orchards was there before he cut it, and in the hush the two halves had fallen open on a pink so tender nobody had wanted the first bite.  His mother said it was nothing like the yellow she had grown, and looked so pleased that Ada had never quite forgotten her face.  To preserve the color of wonder, that family had gone to all the trouble of refusing to preserve a color. 

"Leave it wrapped," Ada said, closing his fingers around the paper.  "Show him what you have." 

On the roof the man saw the lifted fist, and his own hand closed as if it too held something not yet opened.  His mouth shaped a question that the boy understood without a sound. 

"We'll find out," he said, and upon the hush of budding hope that followed, Ada laid a single shining strand of water.  It held between those two shut hands.  She worked crosswise now, drawing out the bright slack in loops the size of a man's bare foot, leaving just enough room between them for a stride.  The father stepped into the first loop, then the next, coming through the lit mist with the grave, high steps of somebody entering a sleeping child's room in the dark.  When he reached the parapet the boy put up his free hand, and Ada let the whole lovely piece of work go back to being water. 

They planted the seed in a pocket of earth between the stones and the nearest row of trees, where a crossing might one day be wanted again.  Years later, when the boy brought Ada a pear on the flat of his hand, her fingers were too crooked to manage the knife.  She asked him to wait until his father came up from the lower orchard, and together the three of them watched the blade go in.  Out of the green skin came two small lamps of gold.
