At midsummer the rainwashed library stayed open until the clover on its roof had lost the last of its color.  That was the one evening when a borrower could return an hour of his life and have it shelved as local history.  After the heart attack, Nell's father had begun to worry about which hour the town would remember if he did not pick one himself.  He had picked the night he won their house back.  He was reading the racing form again by breakfast, but breakfast was outside the hour. 

Nell was a probability professor across the river, and every September some student asked if it was true her father had beaten the house.  She always wanted to tell them which house, right down to the little half window that had been her bedroom's one extravagance.  Instead she made them calculate the cost of playing until you won. 

On the returns desk stood a candle lantern with a mica pane into which her father, breathing hard, had pressed his thumb.  His hour lay in the mineral now, in leaves so fine that a hundred witnesses could fit behind one another without making the pane appreciably thicker.  Ordinarily only the donor's leaf was lit.  Nell had often objected to that rule in letters which the library board had as often thanked her for.  She was theoretically rebellious; in practice she had never put her own thumb anywhere near the flame. 

Her father slid the lantern across to her.  "See it before you sign," he said, for she had been there and the library required her leave. 

Inside the pane it was raining too, and she was nine, on the subway with her cheek pressed against his coat.  To traverse a borrowed hour you had to find the marks on which the donor's eye had come to rest.  Here they were cryptic scrawls on subway seats, and to go forward she had to interpret them as he had.  A fork in a stick figure's hand was no fork but a trident, the silks of the jockey he had backed.  The little ladder cut into the facing seat stood for the six races he had lost before the seventh came good.  She reached the end of the car by reading a lopsided square as the envelope in his breast pocket.  Through the wool she could feel the corners of the bills and, astonishingly, the beat of a heart that had once done its work without supervision.  She had expected triumph and found instead a tenderness so violent it hurt her teeth.  He was looking down at the part in her hair and thinking that tomorrow she would wake under her own ceiling. 

Back at the desk her father was studying his thumbnail, where a flake of mica shone like a fish scale.  "Well?" he said. 

Nell put her thumb beside his print and showed him what the square had been to her.  Not an envelope but the half window, which she had tried to draw from memory with the point of her barrette in case they never went home.  He sat quite still under the greenish light that came off the roof after rain. 

"I wondered how you broke that," he said. 

These were what the librarians called lucid contradictions: hold either leaf alone to the candle and every line was clear; light both together and you got mud.  Her father wanted to know if she meant to file her hour instead of his.  He had not stopped wanting people to know he brought the money home, and she was glad he did not pretend otherwise.  She asked the clerk for a divided frame, the kind used for maps before and after a river changed its course. 

"Can they open just my side?" her father asked. 

"They can close their eyes for mine," she said, "but there'll only be one latch." 

He tried the little brass tongue with his finger before he let her sign.  Outside, the clover had gone gray, and he took her elbow for the wet steps as if it were still an open question which of them needed the help.
