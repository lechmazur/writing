At dawn the shadowed library filled with women who had once known one another by the backs of their heads, bent over sewing machines.  Outside, the city's lights flickered after an unexpected outage; inside, the tall windows admitted a blue so deep that even the white mourning carnations looked dipped in ink. 

Under the floor, Emil folded his blanket to the width of a dictionary and waited for the reading of the names.  The reading happened every year at the hour the shirt factory's night shift should have come home.  Instead the starch loft had caught fire, and the only staircase had gone up like a wick.  He listened from the crawl space where he lived, knowing exactly which board would creak as the librarian stepped up to start the old machine.  He had already put a glass of water under every folding chair, having noticed that grief made some people cough. 

This year the board creaked twice.  A second pair of shoes, small and down at the heels, stood directly over his pillow.  On the reel, in the order in which they had come before the inquiry, the living named their dead.  Then the power dipped again, and a man's voice stretched like hot sugar before it broke on the first syllable of his daughter's name. 

In the sapphire hush that followed, the owner of the little shoes said, "It doesn't take electricity to finish saying Rosa." 

Several women finished it together. 

Emil felt again, against the pad of his thumb, the cold slot in the splicing block just after Rosa, where the blade had gone down.  He had been nineteen, the inquiry's sound clerk, entrusted with making a reel for the public record that included only claims against the company.  The women hired by the piece, who had worked at the very same tables, counted as their own employers.  With a razor and a pot of white pencil stubs he had made the distinction inaudible. 

Above him the woman said, "Now there's room for my sister, if anybody remembers her." 

His thumb still wanted to make the little smoothing stroke that came next, and with the stroke came a name he hadn't thought of in fifty years. 

"Alba," he said into the grille. 

The shoes turned.  "Who has her down there?" 

He had meant to cache the cuttings for a week, until the commissioners left town; then there had been a month, and then the difficulty of explaining the month.  When rent outran his pension he moved underneath the old inquiry rooms, near enough to hear if anybody ever asked the librarian for them.  People probably had asked, he realized now, at the desk upstairs, where a person could reasonably expect to find another person. 

He went along on his elbows, uncovering in the pipe chases, one hatch at a time, the square biscuit tins he had put away in alphabetical order.  When he lifted the first tin to the grille, the little paper sleeves shifted inside it with the dry whisper of skirts in a fitting room.  Each reel fragment lay in its own paper sleeve, each sleeve bore a name, and each narrow length of ferromagnetic tape had kept something no list could keep.  In Alba's sleeve, for instance, there was a woman trying three times to get air enough to say one word. 

He passed it up and heard the little shoes retreat.  For an instant he felt the sweet relief of having been useful without being seen.  Then the woman called down, quite angrily, "What am I supposed to do with this?" 

He came out through the atlas cupboard with the tins against his belly and the track of his pillow seam still red across one cheek.  The little shoes belonged to an old woman, of course, though he had been expecting the girl who had stood beside her mother at the microphone.  He showed her how he would join the pieces with blank leader between, enough for a breath, so that nobody would run into anybody else. 

"And if I've cut one short," he said, "you must tell me what to put back." 

She moved her carnations off the table to make a place for him right beside the machine, in full view of the door.  While the streetlamps wavered, he printed his name on a strip of white splicing tape and pressed it to the breast of his shirt.  Under it, so that even the last woman through the door would know whom she could ask, he added two words: Sound Clerk.
