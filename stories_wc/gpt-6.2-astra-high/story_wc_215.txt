The first morning the dead were visible, Amos set out six cups before he remembered why he had taken to eating alone.  In thirty years nothing had so astonished him as the readiness of his hands to have company again. 

Outside, the beekeepers stood among their empty supers, in that white dazzle where once a traveler could locate the oasis by the sound of bees in the tamarisks.  Salt had come up the well; the living had gone down the road; Amos had stayed because somebody had to mind the box.  It was a stained glass box made from the wreck of the chapel, with a slit in its lid just wide enough for a date stone.  As the keepers drifted toward it he saw that they had come back to vote. 

By noon a girl had arrived on a bicycle with strips of sacking wound around the tires against the hot stones.  She laid a square of paper on his table and weighted its corners with his unnecessary cups.  It was a drawing of a wall to be built at the settlement downriver; across the top someone had lettered the names of the twelve people who had died waiting for water at this well.  Beneath them were spaces for the names of the nine who had voted to keep the gate shut.  He recognized her grandfather's way of forming a capital G, a little bucket going down a well.  Her grandfather had kept the register of burials; Amos had sent him the tally, nine against eight, with nothing more to set beside the names. 

It had seemed a simple enough covenant when the oasis was green: the keeper of the box owed everyone the count and every voter the shelter of his closed mouth.  Now the dead were queuing in the sun with their fists out, and the girl had brought a pencil.  In some palms she could see a streak of white, the lime in which the stones for keeping the gate shut had been dipped.  Every stone looked like a coal sinking through fire behind the rippled red glass; that was why the keepers had chosen it instead of a jar.  She could learn it all by watching, and he could have pulled the shutters, but he let her gaze. 

There was her grandfather, who had dug twelve graves in ground that took the edge off a pick, stepping aside for a woman with a baby.  His palm was almost white enough to light the underside of his chin.  After his stone fell he stayed there with his hand cupped over the slit, as if the little clack it made might still be gathered up.  The girl did not write; she had seen that the old man held two stones and that a shutting stone would leave its dust even in the hand that kept it.  She waited until he went out under the tamarisks that were no longer there, and opened his fingers to let the other stone go.  Dark as a cockroach, it lay in his palm before the image guttered out. 

She printed her grandfather's name in the first space and sat with the pencil held still at the tail of the last letter.  He could have told her the old man had been afraid for her mother, then the baby at that woman's breast; it was what he had been telling himself about his own vote for thirty years.  But there was no way to put such words into a dead mouth without stopping it forever in that shape.  Instead he put down his own name in the space beside it. 

She studied the two names for a while, then turned the paper over and drew the wall again, with the twelve names, and beneath them nine pale stones and eight dark ones.  She did not erase what was on the other side.  Amos fetched the tin of lime, damped a pinch with water from his drinking jar, and pressed his thumb into the first of her nine circles.  When he set a cup before her this time she lifted it with both hands and drank.
