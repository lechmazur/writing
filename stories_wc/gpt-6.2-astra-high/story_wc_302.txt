The first letter was from a dead boy who had grown up to have rather nice handwriting.  He was coming home on the Sunday boat, and he wanted to know what I had told his mother in his name.  Please be exact, he wrote; she has had fifteen years to get used to that boy, and I have not. 

At seventeen I had been the Veiled Dreamer, a girl in black gauze who took dictation from the dead while her father passed the hat.  We never said a word aloud, for there is always somebody in a tent who knows what the dead sounded like.  We let the customers read their slips of paper and then burned them over a violet flame, a precaution which Father called discretion.  All I had left of him was a confidence man's watch, equipped with a little lever that could stop the second hand at the instant a spirit entered the tent.  It was a good watch when you left it alone. 

I put it in my pocket and took the ferry out to the glowing library in the mist, where Father had once worked binding catalogues.  He had learned about violet fire there; it was how the librarians collected writing that had never been given a voice.  He used to say that if anybody ever found the library of unspoken words, we would both have to get honest jobs.  I had laughed then, but it was an odd thing to say to a girl who wanted to grow up and bind books. 

Beyond the returns desk twelve long cases radiated from a violet lamp, each shelf brightening spine by spine when the lamp winked.  On the desk lay the catalogue I remembered seeing under Father's press, a thick concertina of paper ruled in twelve columns.  As a child I had been allowed to unfold it, never to mark it, and the smell of its paste still made me feel extravagantly trusted.  The entry under Violet Deposits was in an unfolding cipher: pull out one pleat and the halves of two letters met, pull out another and they parted to make others.  How many pleats to pull for each column was conveyed through propagation delays, the time the light took to travel the length of the corresponding shelf. 

I timed the first case and got three seconds; three pleats gave me LA.  At the second case I got nothing because I was watching the wrong end.  The pulses were cyclically reversed, running outward for one circuit of the room and inward for the next; what had been the finish was now the start.  Once I caught the rhythm I wanted to shout, but contented myself with doing a small, brisk dance in my wet shoes.  Twelve pairs of letters gave me LAST LANDING LIFT THIRD TILE. 

Beneath it were shelves of singed menus, lovers' quarrels, resignations, and one long run of the cheap blue paper Father had bought by the bale.  Each slip bore a date and the name of the customer, in my own careful schoolroom hand.  I found the mother's name and sat down rather suddenly on the tiled floor.  I had made the boy say he had gone swimming because she had sent him out to play, and that he forgave her.  Sixpence for the wound, another sixpence for the salve; no wonder we had eaten well that summer. 

I had meant to document only what the young man asked for, but around me were hundreds of small blue flames that had failed to go out.  Behind the returns desk I found a girl mending a dictionary and asked what it cost to reserve a copying table for a month.  She looked at the watch I laid down, tried the lever, and broke into a grin that showed a fine gap between her teeth.  She could use that, she said, when people kept her past closing time.  A notice in the ferry office would do for a beginning: Free Copies of Communications from the Dead, with Corrections by the Author. 

I wrote the second letter at my table while the mist was still too thick for the morning ferry to venture out.  I copied the slip exactly, then added my address and a sentence he could give his mother or keep for himself.  If she wants to look the person who wrote those words in the face, I no longer wear the veil.
