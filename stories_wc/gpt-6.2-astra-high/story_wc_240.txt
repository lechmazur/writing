At three in the morning Nell came back to the bioluminescent mushroom farm with her father's laser pointer in her pocket.  He had used it on tours because a flashlight washed the green clean out of the beds, leaving visitors staring at expensive brown fungus.  His thumb had worn the laser pointer button smooth, and lately hers had begun to find its hollow without looking. 

She was the intrusion detection specialist now, called in because something kept crossing between the growing bays without ever going through a door.  By breakfast the board wanted a name.  They had given her father the same job once, and he had given them his own. 

He lived quite happily now in a flat above a bakery and seemed neither to miss the mushrooms nor to resent the loss of his pension.  Nell resented it enough for two.  She had taken the job to understand the physics of letting go, as if somewhere in the building there were a scale on which she could weigh what he had put down against what he had saved. 

The bays occupied the spokes of an old railway roundhouse, divided by hanging screens that let the damp breathe but kept each tenant's harvest separate.  Along either side of every screen ran a narrow shelf for the full wooden flats awaiting collection.  In that perpetual twilight the tenants were familiar strangers, people who knew each other by the sound of a bad knee popping but might walk past each other in a shop.  Nell, spatially gifted in the particular way of children who have spent a lot of time being told to get lost, could stand in one bay and picture precisely what was happening three spokes over. 

She stood where her father had stood the night they accused him of taking a flat of silver bells.  Across the screen a woman was easing a full flat onto a shelf already crowded end to end.  Only now did Nell notice what held it up.  Nell sent her little red dot traveling along the long bow in the wood; two planks, one on either side of the screen, shared a single set of brackets. 

A bracket came loose with a small domestic sound, like a spoon dropped into a sink.  The woman didn't shout; she put her mouth to the mesh and said, "Hands, love," as if to somebody in bed.  Four hands came through the long darned slits at the foot of the screen and took the weight while she got her shoulder under.  From the office came the sweet double chime of an intrusion. 

"Once a night at least," the woman said, seeing the red dot dancing on her sleeve.  "Never had one hit the floor yet." 

Nell saw how help had traveled through permeable barriers all around that wheel, and how, seen from the hub where the board sat, it looked exactly like theft.  Her father hadn't confessed to stealing anything; he had signed the line that said he alone had crossed a boundary, a small distinction she had spent years refusing to hear. 

Before the echo of morning birdsong could reach the roundhouse and call the vans to the loading doors, she meant to have the whole wheel unloaded.  She showed the woman the bracket, then pressed the pointer into her palm and showed her how to make the dot travel along the sagging seam.  From bay to bay the little red coal went, and in its wake flat after flat came quietly down to earth. 

When the board arrived, a hundred tenants were standing beside their crops on the concrete, waiting for the landlord to do something about his shelves.  "You wanted a name," Nell said, and laid the lease open at the clause about common fixtures, with the landlord's signature underneath.  The cooperative that would revolutionize the valley's mushroom trade began with a very limited agreement: nobody was putting anything back on those shelves. 

By noon Nell was at the bakery buying two hot rolls, and by a quarter past she had told her father everything except that she thought she could get his pension back.  She was saving that for when she had it in her hand. 

"Where's my pointer?" he asked. 

She had forgotten all about it; probably it was still going round, passing from one warm hollow to the next.  She told him so, and felt the small surprise of having nothing to give him and nothing he wanted back.
