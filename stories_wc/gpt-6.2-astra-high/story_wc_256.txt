After the power grid stabilized, the first thing Ada lit in the laboratory of lost sounds was the carbide lamp her father had carried underground.  The bubble in its thick lens put a little dark thumb on the wall, just where she remembered it.  It was by that thumb that children used to know which of the lights coming down the switchbacks belonged to him.  Miners walked each midsummer from the pit to the bathhouse, the old men first, the young ones close enough behind to catch an elbow.  At the bottom Ada, the town's prism-song bell ringer, would draw a wet finger round the lip of whichever glass bell matched the color of the arriving light.  Each family kept a piece of colored glass in its lamp so that even before a face came clear you could sing somebody home. 

Now the road lay under a reservoir, and the laboratory had hired her to put the walk into a room the length of a railway carriage.  The brochure called it an artificial pilgrimage and promised that a visitor could propel the painted hills past a bench by pushing on its brass footboard.  From speakers under the seat came the scrunch of shale and, at the proper bend, a cough that Ada could have picked out of a thousand.  She had given them her father's last good summer on a spool of tape. 

Her mother came for the trial wearing the blue dress in which she had always waited outside the bathhouse.  She sat through two bends, heard the cough, and took her feet off the board.  The hill stopped moving but the cough went on down the valley without her. 

"He'd have stopped too," she said. 

All winter Ada had listened for the texture of connection in those tapes, as if enough passes of a ribbon across a head would wear away everything else.  She had assumed the cough came from his bad lung, because after all what else did an old miner cough for?  But why did it come at the same bend every summer, even on the earliest spool, when he could still run uphill?  She ran the tape back and asked her mother to listen not for him this time but for herself.  Out of the shale came two small clicks, which the laboratory had scrubbed from the version for the bench. 

Her mother reached in the pocket of the blue dress and brought out the bathhouse key.  "I'd knock on the rail and he'd clear his throat so I'd know it carried," she said.  "You could have rung all night, honey, but he couldn't hear your bells by then." 

She laid Ada's hand on the brass footboard and struck it twice with the key.  Against her palm the little blows were powerfully gentle, like a baby turning in its sleep against somebody else's back. 

Ada had been about to put the lamp back in its padded box, where it belonged according to the terms of her loan.  Instead she took out the lens and fitted it over the work light at the far end of the room.  The dark thumb fell across the bench, big enough now to lay a hand inside.  By opening and closing her fingers over the lens her mother could make the shadow wave.  Before the public opening they ran a single brass rail from the bench to the light and fixed her yellow bell to it by its stem.  Her father's recorded cough went back into its box; she would not have him answering a question nobody had asked. 

On opening day a boy who had never seen the valley sat on the bench while his grandfather went to the light.  The boy pushed as if he meant to win something, until a broad hand passed across the gold and stayed there.  He stopped, and after a while he bent his head and pressed his cheek against the rail.  At the other end his grandfather was rubbing the glass rim with an old man's deliberation, coaxing out a note too soft for Ada's ears.  She saw it arrive in the boy's face.  He knocked twice, waited for the shadow to lift, and took them the rest of the way down.
