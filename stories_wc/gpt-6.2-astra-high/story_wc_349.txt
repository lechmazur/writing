During the lull on her shop's very first opening day, Ada took the sheet off the mirror and looked at the light on her throat.  Green still, which meant the governor had not changed his mind about letting her work.  Glimmering parole, the children called it, and sometimes one would stand beside her just to see his teeth shine green when he smiled. 

She had registered as a clean slate vendor in the glass orchard library floating upon a swirling sea of clouds.  Here the trees bore thin square leaves on which anything from a marriage vow to a freight manifest might be scratched.  Ada bought obsolete accounts by weight, ground the writing off, and sold the leaves to people with something new to say.  It was respectable work for a woman who had once dropped sixteen tons of municipal brass into the weather. 

Her first three customers had bought slates for school.  Her fourth, a boy with one wet sleeve, brought a slate to sell. 

"My grandmother says don't clean it until you've felt the bottom." 

There was nothing on the face except the word ABOARD and the numeral nine.  Along the bottom ran a row of little bites, so shallow she might have taken them for the work of mice if mice ate glass.  She knew the nine; she had spent eleven months under lock listening to men say nine.  Nine adult fares on a ferry licensed for twenty, and yet Captain Ada Vale had cut loose a valuable tow on the pretext that her passengers were too heavy for the crossing. 

"Who was your grandmother?" 

"The one who took the money." 

From under her counter Ada got the bottled moonlight she used to detect old lettering before she guaranteed a surface clean.  She tipped the bottle on its side without uncorking it, and each bite cast a long blue shadow on the counter.  Not bites exactly: pairs of notches, one deep and one shallow, mindfully scattered so that no two deep cuts weakened the same stretch of glass. 

The boy laid his little finger in one.  "She had to incise the edge for every child that went out, then nick it again when we came back."  He considered his finger, which had grown.  "We didn't cost anything, so we didn't go on the front." 

Twenty pairs.  By quantifying absence, by adding up the glass that wasn't there, Ada could establish that twenty children had crossed with the nine paying passengers.  She remembered them sitting on the deck between adult knees, under coats, in the miserable soaking fog that had kept the inspector high and dry in his tower.  She remembered a small wet hand closing on hers after she cut the tow and the bow came up. 

"Why didn't she bring this to the hearing?" 

"She did."  He turned the slate over, and in the corner Ada saw the red wax seal certifying that the figures on the face had been copied into evidence.  Nobody had thought to turn it edgewise; nobody had needed to be wicked for her to go to jail. 

Ever since the hearing she had wanted to find the place where stories begin, imagining a particular mouth, a man she might someday stand in front of and oblige to take his words back.  Instead here was a grandmother keeping count in two different ways because one number was for the till and the other was for getting everybody home. 

"Is she asking much?" Ada said. 

"Just a clean one, so we can draw her a map to your shop." 

Ada wrapped the widest leaf she had, then unhooked the sign that promised delivery and turned it over to say BACK AT NOON.  At the governor's desk she would ask for a receipt specifying both faces and all four edges; first she wanted to walk the boy home and let an old woman count him in.  Out among the glass trunks he took her hand without looking, and this time she knew whose it was.
