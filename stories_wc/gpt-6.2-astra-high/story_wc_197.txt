My mother's occupation, according to the first history of the lagoon, was telephone operator; in the second she was a volunteer, and in the third there was no telephone service on the islands.  That was the edition the council proposed to teach in school. 

I set water glasses at the hearings, a job for which the chief qualification was that nobody minded talking in front of me.  Thus I knew, before the printer did, that the next edition would say the islands had been empty when the sluices opened.  I had a petition against that sentence and thirty signatures promised in pencil on my cuff.  By dusk on the evening of the vote I had thirty little gray smears.  The council had offered the survivors permanent tenancies on the mainland, and nobody wanted to find out what the word permanent meant in the fourth edition. 

There was another sort of signature, if I could get at it.  Islanders without a penny used to call the mainland on credit, signing the counterfoils my mother kept for settling up on payday.  On the night of the flood they had queued to telephone the ferry office, which later denied receiving a single call.  I knew because I had stood beside her sharpening the pencil. 

The booth stood on the island ridge, still beyond the reach of ordinary tides, but the causeway to it had gone soft as pudding.  All that remained of the telephone company was a brass key on my ring and its injunction, stamped on the bow, against making duplicates.  From the council steps I watched the mud take the evening sky.  Everywhere the ebb had gouged the flats, puddles caught fire; through their red reflections ran a crooked dark seam where the old raised road had drained dry.  I drew the turns on my other cuff, using the buttons for the two surviving willows, and borrowed the long pole that opened the council windows.  It was good to have a map that would come clean in the wash. 

By the time I reached the ridge the red was gone and the lagoon lay under starlight, auric with the minute gold sparks that rose whenever my pole disturbed the water.  The booth smelled of warm tar even in the cool, and for one foolish instant I expected my mother to reappear with her lunch wrapped in yesterday's rates.  She had lived six years after the flood and spent them waiting for somebody to settle those accounts.  The key turned nicely; I had oiled it every year on her birthday, a cheaper observance than flowers. 

In the credit drawer lay a mouse nest lined with strips of paper.  I sat on the operator's stool and put my head down on the counter where people's elbows had polished two pale moons.  There was half a signature caught on a nail, and it occurred to me that half a signature might do what a whole one couldn't.  I took only the loose outer strips, for there was a blind pink family in the middle that had no further use for history. 

The councillors were still arguing about the price of ink when I came back and began to set out the water glasses upside down.  Under each I placed a strip, curling side up so the glass spread it flat.  Nobody stopped me until I set the last glass in front of the ferry superintendent, who had brought his daughter to hear him speak.  She peered in at the scrap as if it were a fish and asked what a capital G was doing all by itself. 

The superintendent could have said it might be anybody's G, and I think I would have let him.  Instead he showed her how the hook turned back on itself and told her that her grandmother made her G that way.  Until that moment I hadn't known his mother had called him. 

People came up to the table and began putting the strips together, calling out a time or a name for the clerk to take down.  Not one of them had to sign a thing.  The printer, who had come for the approved text, set his copy of it on the table blank side up.  When the clerk ran out of ink I had a pencil ready, and when the pencil dulled the superintendent took out his knife.  The little girl stood between us, turning it carefully while he held the blade still, a job you could give a child when the line was long.
