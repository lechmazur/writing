At thirteen I put my hand on the guild mirror and saw myself ringing a bell, and everybody clapped except my father, who had already bought the wood for a boat.  At thirty I stood beside the same mirror with my hand on the rope and my little sister Tess at the head of the line. 

On the echoing terrace that spring evening, even the shuffle of a shoe came back sounding like somebody taking a second, more reluctant step.  The prism parapet after rain cast bands of plum and amber across the waiting children, who sang to keep their places in the dark.  By the time the bells rang, each would belong to the guild whose mark appeared above the silver. 

Tess turned her square little palm up to show me a blister shaped like a bean, earned that morning helping our father bend a rib of ash.  "If it shows a boat," she asked, "how will I know if I really want one?"  That was a question I had never put to myself, having spent seventeen years cherishing a shipyard where nothing ever leaked and no customer ever disputed a bill. 

In the mirror behind her a woman was wringing rainwater out of a brush, though on our side of the glass there was no woman at all.  I recognized the elusive mirage painter by the way she tested a color against the inside of her wrist, as if matching it to the blue of a vein.  As a child I had seen her paint a door on midsummer heat and hold it open for a funeral procession until the last mourner had passed into shade. 

Now she tapped the glass in time to the tune the children were singing, the one about a wren that rocked her young to sleep.  Listen with your teeth together, she mouthed, and under the tune I heard a hum that split whenever a child took breath.  Through coded lullabies in children's songs the old glassworkers had passed down instructions no guildmaster had thought worth learning from a nurse.  One note meant silver, two meant water, and the catch used to grace the end of each verse meant leave it wet enough to change.  I knew the words for those notes because my father used them still when he taught a child to paint a hull. 

"I can persuade mirrors to show possibilities," the painter said, "but I need a borrowed heartbeat to keep the silver running between one picture and the next."  Tess offered her wrist, and the painter shook her head: the hand that rang the bell was the one the mirror knew as its master.  She waited with the perceptive calm of someone who has noticed that a frightened animal will come closer if you look at something else. 

All this took place over the hush before bells ring, that pocket of time a bell keeper may stretch as long as she has the nerve.  I put my wrist to hers, and the pulse went out of my arm and came up under the glass like a fish knocking its nose against ice.  With her brush the painter lifted a band of amber from the parapet and laid it across my sister's reflection.  In that first wash of amber Tess stood ankle deep in curls of ash, planing a beam already too slender, her mouth set like Father's over a mistake.  In plum light she rang my bell, leaning so far out on the rope that the whole terrace gave a little gasp of pleasure at the swing.  Then the silver cleared and there we stood, and I understood that the painter had not undertaken to supply a happy ending in any trade. 

"Could I try the rope before I say?" Tess asked, and I let her take it though I knew what the first stroke would ordinarily mean.  The children kept singing as she pulled, lending the bronze their small, dusky harmony, and no mark settled long enough for a clerk to write it down.  One by one they went to the glass, and with each new face my heart knocked once in the mirror until it had seen them all.  By the time it beat again beneath my ribs, Tess had decided on a week with me and a week in the yard before she signed anything. 

The painter was gone, leaving a wet thumbprint where the guild mark ought to be.  Next morning Father found us at the bench with the bell rope between us, Tess teaching me the splice I had missed the year they bought me shoes for the terrace.
