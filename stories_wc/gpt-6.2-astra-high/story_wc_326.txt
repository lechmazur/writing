After the radio static clears, Nell hears her daughter ask a dead woman for another minute.  The dead woman has Nell's voice, down to the little click her tongue makes against a chipped front tooth.  From the doorway of the opal observatory, under snow so deep that the midnight sky is only a blue coin at the top of the access shaft, Nell says nothing.  She counts nine more heartbeats before the voice replies. 

"All right, love." 

They issued Nell's death certificate on the strength of a red mitten sticking out of the avalanche debris.  It took six weeks to find her in a survey hut across the ridge and ten months to get the certificate withdrawn.  In the interval her daughter Kit inherited the observatory, including its most popular exhibit, a digital ghost assembled from thirty years of Nell showing visitors how to look at things.  Kit could have shut it down when Nell came home.  Instead she comes here after closing and talks to it over the old guide radio, although its speaker is three feet from her stool.  Nell, who counts heartbeats even in her dreams now, has been trying not to count these visits too.  Tonight she has come to learn what she got wrong before she asks her child to stop. 

On the demonstration table Kit has set a porcelain shard over the split in the opal slab, making a bridge between its two halves.  When the observatory opened the quarrymen donated a slab too flawed to sell, and generations of schoolchildren have learned on it why the eye makes constellations.  Kit spills a thimbleful of ink onto the high side and tips the slab until a black tributary runs across the shard.  In the ephemeral patterns of the spilled ink you can see a heron become a hatpin become a pair of scissors before you find the words to say what you see.  That was the point of Nell's lesson: all the stars are moving and our pictures of them will go too. 

"I see the buckle on your good shoes," Kit says. 

Nell almost says that those shoes had buttons, not buckles.  The ghost says nothing for so long that Nell steps inside to check the terminal.  A blinking prompt reminds Nell how she used to defragment the disk every Sunday, so the guide wouldn't spend whole minutes hunting down the scattered pieces of its remark. 

"Is that all it is?" Nell asks. 

Kit looks up, and for a moment she is six again, caught trying on something that belongs to her mother.  "Don't fix it," she says. 

At the end of its long search the guide says that the southern sky looks upside down to people from the north. 

"It's been answering the question before last since Christmas," Kit says, and shifts her stool to make room. 

At home Nell has been making a project of teaching Kit everything the girl might need if the mountain ever takes her mother back.  How to change the aerial fuse, how to cook for one, how to listen for the small companion slide that follows the big one.  She has even been finishing Kit's sentences, as if words could get lost under snow.  It hasn't once occurred to her to leave a minute empty and see what her daughter might put there. 

She sits, dismisses the message without touching the disk, and sets her thumb beneath the low end of the slab.  "Show me the buckle," she says. 

Kit takes the shard by its dry corner and turns it a hair, diverting a thin black loop back into the pool it came from.  "Not yours," she says eventually.  "Mine, the pair I wore to your funeral." 

Of all the things Nell imagined her daughter might need to tell her, it was never that her feet had hurt that day.  She lets Kit tell her which heel bled, who found a plaster, how good the cold floor of the ladies' room felt through a stocking.  The guide finally identifies a buckle, just as the last of the ink slips off the bridge.  Kit laughs, and this time the voice that joins her comes out of a warm mouth, three feet from the radio.
