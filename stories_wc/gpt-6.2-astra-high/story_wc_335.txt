In the forgotten library under the playground, Ruth set down three cups without spilling a drop, then kicked the desk to wake the shard-mirror archivist.  Orra opened the one eye not printed with the weave of her sleeve.  "If you want him back when the swings go quiet, save your kicking foot for the stuck drawer." 

Ruth kicked, and out slid her father's afternoon shadow caught in glass, complete with the crooked little finger he had passed on to her.  She was fitting her finger over his when Orra tipped the first cup into a saucer.  All winter Ruth had been learning the geometry of tea leaves, how the points of the wet little triangles aimed themselves at whatever piece of the town was missing.  She had found a silver thimble and a set of false teeth in practice and was heartily sick of being told a person was different.  A person could decline to be found. 

The magistrates called the sentence reversible exile, though not one of them could have told Ruth where the library stairs were.  She had discovered them last October by following Orra, who was forever coming up through the nettles with bits of people's missing things stuck to her skirt.  Her father had driven the school bus into a wall, and Ruth, who sat behind him, had told the magistrates what was in his coffee.  Her friend Dot still had to take the swings standing up because it hurt too much to sit. 

Ruth poured the other cups at opposite ends of the desk and laid a ruler along each clot of leaves in turn.  The three lines crossed on the shadow's palm, where a message scratched with a pin would raise a white welt on the palm of a man no longer anywhere.  Orra moved to annotate the glass, then turned the pin blunt end up and put it into Ruth's hand instead.  "Don't write anything you'll have to live under like a low ceiling." 

Ruth scratched I LIED so hard that the I came out double.  Orra began walking backwards between the tables, which was what she did when she was nervous, and banged the backs of her knees on the stool where Ruth usually sat.  She sat down hard and said nothing, a kindness for which Ruth could cheerfully have brained her.  Upstairs somebody called the children in to supper, and Ruth could hear the familiar double thump as Dot landed first on her good foot and then on her stick.  Ruth dipped her sleeve in tea and polished the glass as Orra had taught her, until the letters closed like scratches in ice under a warm thumb. 

"I can't tell him it's all right." 

"I should damned well hope not," Orra said, and moved the lamp so Ruth wouldn't have to work in her own shadow. 

At the hush when the last empty swing drifted in the breeze, the old playground's shadow joined the one beneath her hand, and the little finger stirred.  Through the glass she could smell the spearmint he chewed on the mornings when he drove, which made her want to kiss his cheek and also slap it.  She wrote I TOLD THEM and underneath it I SET YOUR PLACE.  His finger traced each line twice before his hand curled up into a fist.  Ruth held perfectly still, although the fist was so much like the one he'd made in court that her knees wanted to run without her.  When it opened he had put a thing through the glass, a small brass key with the number of the school bus stamped into it.  Not until she took it did he haul himself up by the edge of the desk and stand dripping tea onto the floor. 

"You can take that back to them," he said. 

Her hand had already closed over it when she felt the pin still lying blunt against her skin.  "Take it yourself," she said.  She laid the key on his wet sleeve and waited until he picked it off for himself. 

At the head of the stairs he turned toward the depot instead of toward home, and she let him go without reminding him about supper.  Downstairs Orra was washing the cups, making the ordinary promising noise that meant she expected to want them again.  Ruth used her kicking foot to mash the nettles flat around the entrance so the next child wouldn't have to follow anybody to find it.
