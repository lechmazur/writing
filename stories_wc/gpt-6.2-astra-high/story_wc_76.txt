On Gull Island the first sign of hunger was not an empty plate but a face on the lighthouse wall.  Under the slow exhale of low tide, a black slab emerged from the rocks below the tower and shed the light it had gathered underwater.  Up through a shaft that nobody remembered cutting came the faces of the fishermen who would not be home for supper.  They called the slab the hunger stone, and until she was nine Mara had thought it was the stone that wanted feeding.  Then her father appeared with a split lip and a little silver bubble lodged in one eyebrow, and she learned whose cupboards would go bare. 

Mara grew up to make counterclockwise clocks for the barbers on the mainland, who liked to give their customers the time in the glass.  She was neatly inventive in the way of people who cannot afford to spoil good brass; even her filings went into an hourglass.  Each spring she spent her earnings on a week at the lighthouse, taking apart the few things its vanished builders had left behind.  She did not propose to stop the sea from killing people, as her aunt rather bitterly supposed.  She wanted to know why her father had been looking so hard at her. 

Of all the things in the tower only an ancient yoyo, small enough to close a child's hand around, was made of the same black stuff as the slab.  Its puzzle was a practical one: no matter how carefully she wound its cord, the toy would go down but never come up.  Yet on the descent she sometimes saw a point of gold inside it, the latent radiance of her lamp caught in the stone. 

Her breakthrough came the spring she stopped trying to mend the yoyo and built its exact reverse in brass, as if the original were a clock in a barber's mirror.  Working backward from the worn place on the axle, she discovered that the cord had never been knotted there; it had been doubled into a loose sling.  With a sling the stone could hang spinning, and the slightest upward twitch would initiate its return.  When it climbed, the spot of gold sprang out and shone on the lamp.  She snuffed the wick with her thumb and tried again, and for one giddy second a little yellow flame burned cold on her nail. 

That afternoon, when the ebb bared the stone, Mara was waiting beside the shaft with a loop of sailcloth slung around its idle pulley.  She had always taken the pulley for the remains of a hoist, although what cargo might go up a shaft no wider than her wrist had baffled her.  A boy appeared on the wall with his cheek pressed flat against planking; behind him she saw, upside down, the patched thwart of her aunt's boat.  It was her aunt's hired boy, who ordinarily had a great deal to say for himself and was saying nothing now. 

Mara ran the sling down, felt it catch the spindle inside the shaft, and pulled just hard enough to earn a blister.  The wall went dark, and when the face came back the boy was shading his eyes.  She had sent him a patch of whitewashed wall, the last thing the shaft had seen.  In the soot from her lamp she wrote WHERE on the plaster, then pulled again.  The boy mouthed something she could not read, considered this difficulty quite calmly, and drew a bell in the air with his finger.  The bell buoy marked the old quarantine anchorage, three miles from the grounds where anybody would think to look for a capsized boat. 

By the time the tide covered the stone they had the boat righted and the boy wrapped in somebody's trousers, there being no blankets in the rescue skiff.  Her aunt did not come up.  All the way home Mara rowed with that blister opening and the boy's hard wet back against hers, and could have howled for the joy of his weight. 

People afterward wanted to know whether her father had seen her too, and to this she would give no answer she could not stand by.  She spent the winter engraving little charts of the sound on brass plates, to be nailed inside the hull of every boat, where a cold finger could point.
