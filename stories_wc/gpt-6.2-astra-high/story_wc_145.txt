After the falcon returned to the ledge above the spillway, the three mayors took off their chains of office and went down to open the rivers.  It was a courtesy their grandfathers had extended to a nesting bird: no hammering at the rusted outpost by the frozen lake until she had chosen her ledge for the year.  By now her return had come to herald the irrigation season, and the mayors took considerable satisfaction in obeying a creature they did not pay. 

Nell Vale, editor of the provincial atlas, followed them carrying her mother's phrasebook and a demand from the revenue office.  She had been sent to find where the rivers began.  Under a new law, a village that owned a river's source could charge the villages downstream; already at the baker's in Nell's village there were two prices chalked up for the summer loaf. 

"Odd," she said, ducking under the lintel, "how much geography a baker has to know." 

None of the mayors laughed, although one held the door for her.  Inside, three wheels stood beneath three windows, each overlooking a dry stone channel descending toward a different valley.  Behind the wheels was a tiled wall and behind the wall, according to every edition Nell had put to bed, was the lake. 

The mayors turned their wheels together.  Outside, water slid over the stones without a splash, as though someone were unrolling three bolts of gray silk.  Nell had expected the ice beyond the spillway to groan or settle; not even the falcon looked up. 

Her mother had interpreted here in the years when the three valleys still needed a polyglot to agree upon the price of a hinge.  The phrasebook had a single dog-ear, polished nearly through at the tip, marking what Nell had always supposed was a page of toasts.  Now, with her thumb on the fold, she noticed that the three versions of "To your health" were followed by three versions of "Your turn to breathe." 

Not a toast, then.  She laid her cheek against the tiled wall and felt cold striking through one square more fiercely than through its neighbors.  When she rubbed away the whitewash with her damp cuff she found thick inspection glass, and behind it something too dark to distinguish from its own reflection. 

"Give me a breath," she said to the oldest mayor. 

He knew where to put his mouth.  That was what hurt her afterward: not that he knew, but that until she asked he had been willing to let the baker set his price.  The other two cleared the neighboring squares and breathed there, and Nell watched the pattern of condensation on glass that had once been a single pane before the plasterers framed it into tiles.  The mist vanished almost at once except where running water kept the glass cold; there three pale tracks lingered, joining in one round palm of white. 

"Three outlets," Nell said. 

"Inlets," the oldest mayor said, barely loud enough for the others to hear.  "My father laid the left one." 

They took her around the back and lifted the service cover, and there was the singular convergence her mother and the other builders had contrived: three hillside springs delivered through clay pipes into one cistern, from which each valley drew its share.  The lake had lent the works its name and not a drop of water.  In drought a stronger spring had always fed a weaker one; underground, nobody had been obliged to call it charity. 

Nell sat on the cover to amend her proof.  She gave each spring its village name, inked in the cistern, and made the mayors witness the direction of the arrows before their breaths had quite dried on the glass.  There would be hearings, but no man could claim the source of his neighbor's river now without admitting that his neighbor owned a piece of his. 

On the way home she stopped for bread.  The baker heard her out, wiped away the higher price, and wrapped her loaf in the damp, floury quiet before anybody else knew.
