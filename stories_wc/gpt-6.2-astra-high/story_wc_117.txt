My father used to say that if you dug down beneath the hush of dreaming roots you would find next spring wide awake and cracking her knuckles.  That was how we got through February on Sage Ridge: I held my ear to the thawing ground and he made small pops with his thumbs behind my back.  Since his stroke he had only the one good thumb, and my daughter Alice, at nine, was getting wise to the ways adults tried to produce a little ordinary magic. 

At work I was the mellow music therapist they sent for when somebody flung a tambourine; at home I could get pretty grim about having a lovely time.  For the partial eclipse I had written a duet you could play with one thumb apiece on the copper pipes that hung outside Dad's kitchen.  He had cut them to length years ago so that opening the screen door played the first three notes of Three Blind Mice.  I wanted Alice linked to him by something besides the Sunday trip up the ridge to sort his pills.  I wanted her, at his age, still to be putting her ear to things.  It didn't occur to me that she might have to put it to something I hadn't furnished. 

On the porch I tucked the music stand under the eave out of the wind and handed round the cardboard eclipse glasses.  Already the sun had a little nick in it, like a dinner plate my mother would have turned to the wall.  Alice took one look, then put her glasses in her pocket and studied the page of notes as if there were a grade attached.  At work my playing was softly adaptive, following a breath or a tapping shoe until the other person discovered he was leading.  Now Dad kept striking his chest on the offbeat and I kept dragging him back to my count. 

It was Alice who saw that he wanted something from his breast pocket.  She fished out the card he had carried there for going on forty years, a keepsake baby hair curl stitched to shirt cardboard with blue thread.  Underneath, in Dad's carpenter capitals, it said MAGGIE BEFORE SHE TURNED RED.  All my life it had embarrassed me how readily he would show a cashier the one thing I'd accomplished before I could do anything.  It used to make my mother laugh that the card was bald on the corners and still had hair in the middle. 

Dad wanted to show Alice the back, where the old needle had made a ragged little horseshoe of holes from his first attempt at stitching.  Or so I thought, until he nudged my shoe with his own and pointed at the floorboards.  Alice had been turning the card over and over; at each turn something winked on the blank back of the sheet music that had blown off its stand.  Dad steadied her wrist with his good hand and there on the paper lay a horseshoe of tiny bitten suns.  Nobody seemed willing even to breathe on them.  Then Alice whooped, startling a magpie out of the sage, and clapped her free hand over her mouth as if she could put the bird back that way. 

How long had Dad known about this?  He shrugged his one shoulder when I asked, and Alice said, quite crossly, that maybe he had just this minute found out, like us.  She lifted the card farther from the paper until the little suns grew fat and fuzzy, then closer until they sharpened to nail parings.  Dad knocked once on the lowest pipe when he wanted her to stop.  Without a word they worked out a second signal for go on, two quick taps that set the high pipe singing against the low one.  I sat on the step and held the music flat for them with both hands. 

After the last nick healed, Dad gave Alice the card, but she shook her head and tucked it back inside his shirt.  On the drive down she asked if I had a needle.  She wanted to try stars, letters, the outline of that magpie with its wings flung up.  I nearly told her it would be years before she got those bitten shapes again.  Instead I told her which drawer, and all the way home she kept one finger on the dusty window, trying to get the wings right.
