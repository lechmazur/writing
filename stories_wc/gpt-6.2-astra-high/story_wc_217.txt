After rain the cobalt lagoon looked hard enough to take a scratch, and my father sat trying to scratch it with a drake's tail feather.  At least that was what I saw from the path above him, the little black curl traveling back and forth across an acre of blue.  My brother Kit, who had spent the night helping him to the toilet, saw a man who ought to be indoors.  Both of us had forgotten the game was his. 

As children we had fought so bitterly over what we saw from that bank that Father invented a verse for two soreheads.  In its simplest form I said what the morning looked like and Kit said what work it meant, and Father took a line from each of us in turn until he had a poem.  He called them woven dawns, and even when I was earning my living writing verses for boats at launching and people at burial, I had never made anything half so good.  Being quiet was an asset in my trade; people usually told you the thing worth setting down just after you would ordinarily have spoken.  Only with Father since the stroke did I keep barging into the silence, as though an old man in want of a noun were a house on fire. 

That morning I had brought our old ledger down to the bank because the printer wanted a last woven dawn for the little collected book.  Kit had brought a blanket and his daughter Nell, who was flying the brown paper kite her grandfather had taught her to mend with onion skin.  We were each there to tend to something and each secretly certain that the others had chosen wrong. 

I held the ledger on Father's knee and asked what the water looked like, though I already had that lovely scratchable blue in mind.  Kit told me to leave him be, and in the little burnished hush that followed, the kite string snapped with the intimate sound of a hair pulled through a comb.  Nell ran along the bank looking up, though the glare had already swallowed the kite.  Father caught her skirt with his good hand and pointed the feather down.  On the water the kite was perfectly visible, a brown lozenge with one luminous pane where the onion skin let the sun through.  His little curl followed its reflection until the kite itself settled in the rushes not ten yards from us. 

Kit went in up to his knees and came back with the ribs and a sodden brown fringe.  Nell took the wreck without thanks, being eight and in company and determined not to cry.  Father turned the ledger to a clean page, set the feather on it, and with his forefinger spun it until the quill pointed to Nell.  She was still holding the spool, and it was to the spool, not to any of us, that she said her piece.  It went on pulling, she said, after there was nothing there. 

The feather turned to Kit, who said, a little gruffly, that a person could do something with two sound sticks.  I wrote the lines across from one another, the tug on the empty string against the solid feel of sticks, and waited for the quill to come round to me.  Instead Father turned it on himself, and we waited so long I could hear the water draining out of my brother's shoes.  He lifted the feather by its tip and let it fall between the lines, where it rocked on its curl and presently lay still.  Now he pointed at me. 

I had my mouth open to describe the fall when I saw he was pointing at the pencil behind my ear.  I traced the shadow of the feather where it lay, leaving a break where the curl lifted clear of the paper and the shadow thinned to nothing.  Father tapped the break twice with his nail and shut the book.  We printed that page exactly as it stood, down to the small muddy thumbprint Kit had put beside his line. 

That night I took my turn getting up with Father, and in the dark on the slow walk back from the toilet he stopped at the window.  We stood there looking out until his hand on my arm gave the slightest pull, and neither of us had to say what for.
