At twilight the barge canal was so still that Nell could see, upside down in the water, the lettering on the bow of the orphan boat.  It said FATHER VALE, the name under which a man who had never been photographed had raised nineteen children on freight money.  Nell had been an errant legend seeker for thirty years, going wherever somebody had seen a saint or heard a hill singing, but this was the first legend that had ever paid for her shoes.  Her mother had been the youngest of the nineteen. 

At daybreak the boat would come apart under a crane, except for the captain's cabin, which the canal museum had agreed to salvage.  Once the morning radio finished reading out the claims on the timber, the operator would cut his carrier and the crane driver could begin.  Nell had until that silence to decide what should go on the little card beside her mother's antique pocket sundial.  The curator had penciled in the obvious: Father Vale's watch. 

In the cabin she opened its brass leaves and raised the little thread that cast the shadow.  The ring on its case had worn almost through, yet there was no corresponding wear inside, not even at the notch where the thread caught.  By training her instincts on hundreds of lovingly preserved frauds, Nell had learned to notice where a thing had been held before she read what had been written about it.  She slipped a finger through the ring; it was easier to pass the dial that way than to drop it into somebody's palm. 

Down on the towpath her Uncle Kit was taking bolts out of a bench for which he had put in a personal claim.  She leaned over the rail and held the dial out to him without a word.  He took it on his finger and stood up straight.  For an instant the old man's shoulders made a shape she knew from watching her mother argue freight rates with men twice her size. 

"Who's asking for the master?" he said, and then, seeing her face, sat down on the mud. 

She had come to understand the architecture of lies, and had spent years looking for a single crooked beam, a signature or a grave that wasn't his.  Now in the tender light that comes up off water after the sky has gone dark, she saw what had held the whole thing up.  Every child old enough to go ashore had been Father Vale. 

Kit said the first six had inherited the boat from a woman whose actual sons would have sold it for scrap.  The port required a master's signature, but at the freight window, set low enough for a seated clerk, all anybody ever saw was a hand. 

"We thought a master ought to have a watch," he said.  "That was the nearest thing in the pawnshop." 

Afterward they had taken on children the way other boats took on paying passengers, except that nobody ever had to get off.  Her mother had always been thoughtfully clear with strangers, giving the depth at the sill to the inch and never volunteering the name of the pilot.  What had looked in childhood like good manners came clear in hindsight as a lifetime's practice in keeping a crew together. 

Nell asked what the museum would get if it took away the cabin. 

"A privy with a desk in it," said Kit. 

He tipped the bench on its side.  On the underside of the seat, in pencil, in ink, in scratches made with a nail, a single handsome signature emerged by degrees from a thicket of uncertain ones. 

Nell sat with him until the radio began its morning litany of things that people could still use.  When the operator called for amendments she took the handset and asked the museum to exchange its portion for Kit's, if he was willing.  Her uncle ran his thumb along the wood where a little hand had driven a nail too deep on the downstroke. 

"I'd rather have the privy," he told the operator, with such dignity that nobody on the working channel laughed. 

By the time the radio fell silent the dial lay on the bench, and Nell had written a card that Kit had read and signed.  It said: We carried this watch when we went ashore to sign the name we learned to write here.  In the name beneath, the capitals wobbled, but the final e ran out level and easy as a stretch of open water.
