Seven years after I went off the grid, a girl found me by asking along the coast who still mended stoves for cash.  She spread a faded tapestry fragment on my bench, and there was my mother with a tack hole through each eye.  "Gran says you can put her back," she said.  I recognized my sister in the way she kept a thumb on the cloth as if my whole trade were making things disappear. 

The mainland papers called me the lending library arsonist, which was accurate except for the suggestion that I had meant to burn the books.  I had meant to burn the list of islanders who borrowed primers in our forbidden language.  Also burned were the deposit slips for the island treasures our people had entrusted to the library when the sea came through the wells.  The mainland salvaged the treasures and exhibited them in what it called the Lost Civilization Archive, although it cost three living islanders a day's wages to go in. 

"Gran says she didn't lend them her wedding coat to be buried in something off a rack." 

That was how I learned my mother was dying. 

The girl paid both our admissions with coins sorted into twists of pharmacy paper.  In the entrance hall hung the vandalized self-portrait my mother had woven for the lending desk, a woman at a loom with a neat square of dirty wall for a face.  I had cut it out myself at the reopening ceremony, furious that the museum should portray her as the last weaver when she was across town doing piecework.  Apparently she had tacked it up over her bed. 

I held the scrap in place; the eyes looked down, not at the shuttle as I remembered but at the little red fish worked into its handle.  My niece was already at the case containing the actual shuttle.  "There's another one in its mouth," she said. 

Not a fish but a boat, small enough to sail on a thumbnail.  I knew that boat; my father had carved it as a cradle for the first baby born in the resettlement camp.  We followed the trail of things our people had made, from cradle to painted oar to wedding coat, each maker having tucked the next maker's work inside his own.  It had been a game for children waiting at the lending desk, back when the coat hung where a child could reach it.  At the end you got to put your hand in the pocket. 

My niece flattened her palm against the glass.  "Gran said you always knew where a thing had broken just by hearing it run.  Well, where's it broken?" 

The coat bothered me the way a stove does when the flame burns blue but the kettle stays cold.  All those years of little fists should have dragged the pocket crooked; instead it lay stiff as a patch.  I asked the guard to fetch a conservator, and when he wouldn't, I told him who had cut the face out of the tapestry.  That got us a conservator and two policemen. 

With all three watching I offered the face back in exchange for turning the pocket inside out.  The conservator eased it inside out over gloved fingers and there in laundry ink, on a square of linen stiff with age, was the end of the game.  On it, in the mainland librarian's block printing, was the promise I had thought I'd burned: every piece to go home on request, even if home was now a rented room.  Below stood the names of the lenders in the order a child would find their treasures. 

While I stood shaking, my niece photographed every name.  She had our people on the phone before the conservator could tuck the names away.  When the policemen took my address I made them wait while I wrote it out a second time for her. 

The coat took nine days to come home; since my mother only had eight, my sister kept her on ice a day against the priest's advice.  I wanted to go down to the archive and take the windows out with my bare hands for that one day.  Instead I warmed my hands at the stove before I dressed her, as if there were still some chance she might feel the cold.
