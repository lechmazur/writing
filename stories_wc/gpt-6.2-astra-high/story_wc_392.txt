I investigate preventable deaths, which mostly means arriving after somebody has had time to put the guardrail back.  In the subway maintenance tunnel under Mercer Street, the new rail still had its price sticker on it.  Below it was the pump well where Isaac Bell had broken his neck on the last night of his mother's film.  His sister June had brought me the one thing their mother would not let the city impound. 

It was a tin lantern from the Underground Railroad, made by a cooper who had punched the air holes with the same square nail he used to mark his barrels.  The Bells knew this because the cooper was theirs; they still had his barrels, his awl, and the account book in which he charged nobody for the oil.  His wife had carried the lantern down to the river to meet people who had crossed in the dark. 

June's mother had waited through six years of drought for enough water to film that crossing in the actual place.  When the drought finally ended, the river took the landing, the footpath, and most of the bank.  She rented the tunnel for its long ledge beside running water, and made a river there instead. 

On her last piece of footage a little constellation of squares traveled steadily away from the camera until it dropped out of sight.  Isaac had been walking backward, carrying the lantern in one hand and feeling for the rail with the other.  His mother said that she had called a halt well before he reached the gap; June remembered hearing that call in her sleep.  She also remembered the rail knocking under Isaac's wedding ring, although he had never been married.  That was their father in an earlier film, taking the same backward walk along the deck of a ferry.  All her life her mother had used the children to stand in for somebody gone. 

I had learned not to try to pull entangled memories apart by asking people to remember harder.  I asked June to stand where her brother had stood and listen while I called from the chalk cross that marked the camera position.  She stayed watchfully calm, one palm spread on the new rail, until I opened my mouth.  Then she flinched, not at my voice but at the sound of the pump starting below her feet.  From my end I could hear my own words reverberate off the curved brick and come back surprisingly tender, like somebody calling a child indoors.  At her end, she said, she could see my jaw working.  That was all. 

We traded places and got the same result.  The pump was audible on the footage too, a thin mosquito whine at the camera that down here shook the fillings in my teeth.  Then she handed me the lantern and had me hold it high, the way her mother had wanted it, close beside my cheek.  With the flame there I couldn't even see her jaw. 

June took off her coat and showed me the white dish towel she had been twisting around her wrist under the cuff.  She shook it once and I could see it from a hundred feet away.  Her job that night had been to stand beside the camera and wave the towel if anything went wrong.  She had done it on the ferry when she was nine.  On the last night her mother had taken it out of her hand because the white kept showing up in the water. 

That was what June had come to tell me, but first she'd needed to know whether a voice alone might have been enough.  Until today the only way she had ever found out what happened on a shoot was to sit beside her mother and watch the cut. 

In my report I put the missing rail first; nobody should have had to wave anything to keep a man from falling through a hole.  June read that sentence twice before she signed her own account.  Her mother called while we were packing things away, and out of long habit June started to tell her where we were standing and what she could see.  Then she stopped and said she would come over if her mother wanted company, but the two of them weren't going to work on a statement.  She tucked the wick down with her thumbnail, a small competent gesture she must have learned at home.  She asked me to keep the towel with her statement.
