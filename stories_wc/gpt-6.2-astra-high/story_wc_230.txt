The first time the hanging orchard lost a tree, everybody heard the cable ticking and nobody admitted hearing it until the pears went down like a string of yellow lamps.  After the empathy virus, a direct question drew an honest answer the way a tap drew sap, but until somebody asked you could keep your mouth shut.  Mara knew why the pickers kept theirs shut, because she had conducted the inquiry into the loss of that first tree.  She had asked her brother when he first heard the ticking, and he had answered that it was while he was kissing Pavel in the pump house.  She got the hour out of him eventually, but not before everybody on the platform had learned something Pavel had not yet told his father. 

Mara was a truffle forager by trade, the sort who weighed what she dug from under each hazel and marked the weight against hours of sun.  She disliked an explanation that left no dirt on the scales.  Yet she worked her way through rumored cures in discredited textbooks, looking for a village where anybody had managed to keep the good of the sickness without the shame.  Three books described a clinic across the ridge where a boy holding the doctor's glass eye had been able to refuse him an answer.  All three spelled clinic with two n's, which brought the tally of witnesses down considerably.  Still, she made the crossing with her last white truffle wrapped in damp wool. 

The boy, now gray at the temples, kept his father's instruments in a cabinet along with a nest of antique glass eyes, each a slightly different blue.  He picked out the one whose iris had faded to the color of skim milk.  She offered him the truffle, though taking a relic out of the quarantine parishes could cost them both their crossing papers. 

"Tell me how it worked," she said, mindful of the difference. 

"He took it out when he wanted me to understand that he wasn't looking," the man said.  "He put it in my palm and turned his chair around and waited for me to talk." 

Mara came back with the smuggled eye in the damp wool and her scales still balanced: one truffle for one credible account of mercy. 

That night the storm got down between the walls of the ravine and stayed there, beating the suspended beds against their stays.  By morning a whole terrace of hazels had begun to ride crooked, and the pickers stood in the pump house listening to nuts roll off into the dark.  The steward wanted a name before he would send anybody down to look; he had no intention of trusting a man to inspect his own work.  Mara felt her brother beside her stiffen for the question.  The steward drew breath, and Mara dropped the glass eye into the tin cup at his elbow.  The small hard noise was enough. 

She gave them the rule as if it had come with the eye, though she was making it up in terror as she spoke.  Anybody could occlude that painted pupil with a thumb, and until the thumb was lifted there would be no questions, not even from the steward.  She tipped it out and put her own thumb on it. 

"I loosened a strap to get at a white truffle," she said, "and I know I put it back but I can't swear I put it back right." 

Her brother took the eye from her.  From the pump house, he said, you could see that strap was straight; what you couldn't see from anywhere else was the far stay sawing into the lip of the ravine.  The steward looked at the man's white knuckle a moment and then sent two women out along the upper walk with a roller and a jack.  They lost the end hazel, the one that gave Mara her earliest truffles, but by noon the rest rode level again. 

By supper the eye was back in the cup, and by winter there was a cup on every terrace, some with nothing more remarkable in them than a plum stone.  No one mistook this for a cure.  Mara's brother still did not come to her house, though one morning he left a pear on the step, bruised on one side from the long ride in his pocket.  She ate it without asking what it meant.
