At Saltwick the morning fog saxophonist stood with one shoe in the sea, since a dry sole missed the lower notes.  On the day her daughter sailed, Ruth could feel the little bones of her wet foot stirring to the voice of her own mother.  It was twenty years since she had heard that voice anywhere but here, and here it never said a thing she wanted it to say. 

People waiting on the quay saw only the roof of the listening room, a prism atrium in mist, green at the edges like the thick bottom of a tumbler.  Underneath was a radiant grotto in mist of its own making, where the still warm breath of the sea struck the cold skirts of the land.  As dewdrops form on a glass held over a sleeper's mouth, so they formed on the inner faces of the roof, and at the joining of the first two drops the stone began to talk. 

It recited the names on the sailing list in the voice of the listener's dead, a quietly vivid roll call, right down to her mother's habit of giving French names an apologetic little lift.  The prophecy was spoken in code, through negative space: the stone left out the names of those who would never come home.  Ruth had to put those names to music, one note for each letter, for the people up above. 

In the old days the families had been allowed to keep those notes inside the hill.  They called this right the vested hush, because it belonged not to the musician or the harbor board but to the family from the instant a name went missing.  All you had to do was whisper into the speaking tube beside the ticket window, and the musician, alone below, would know to pass your silence on.  Nobody had done it in Ruth's working life.  Better the terrible knowledge, people said, than setting a place for supper every night until your wrist grew crooked lifting down the extra plate. 

But Ruth's father had heard his name played, and although he had only been going across the sound to have a tooth pulled, his sisters had hauled him bodily off the boat.  He had lived another thirty years without once setting foot outside Saltwick, a distinction the stone had apparently not troubled itself to make. 

That morning the stone read straight from Fenner to Finch, leaving out the name of Elsie Field, who was bound for the mainland to learn to mend pianos.  Ruth set her mouth to the reed and saw, in the watery light, her father's forceps hanging on their nail above the sink.  He had taken his own tooth out and kept the instrument for the rest of his life, though nobody had ever asked to borrow it. 

From the tube came the creak of a coat sleeve against tin.  Then somebody whispered, and for the first time that morning Ruth heard a voice with an actual warm mouth at the other end of it. 

"Not for me," Elsie said. 

Ruth had imagined a hundred times what she would do if the stone skipped her child, but never that her child might claim to be her own family.  She had imagined getting up those steps somehow on one wet shoe and one dry one, and arriving in time to be forgiven later. 

She took her mouth off the reed.  Relieved of the work of spelling, the pale and pearly keys felt absurdly big under her fingers, like the buttons on the coats of very small children.  Into the bell of the saxophone she whispered the thing her mother used to say when somebody went out for bread or lamp oil or a pint of winkles. 

"Take your time," she said, and let the horn keep what little sound there was.  Then she played the three plain notes that meant the list was finished, and above her the gangplank rattled in. 

When she got up to the quay the boat was gone, and on the sill beside the speaking tube lay a small square envelope with her name on it.  Inside was the key to Elsie's piano, with a request that she lift the lid now and then, since mice preferred a place where nothing ever happened.  That evening she lifted it, and the evening after, and on the third evening she sat down and tried to remember a tune that was not anybody's name.
