After the search party disbanded, eleven women came into the rain-washed foundry and laid the zodiac coin on my desk.  They wanted to redeem it before the grocer shut, and the twelfth woman, my sister Linn, was somewhere under the river. 

I had apprenticed myself to the old Balanced Alchemist to find out what a name weighed in good gold.  Now it was my job to keep the gold on one side of the payroom scales exactly equal to the promises on the other.  As long as the beam hung level, the owners could neither take back a penny nor be made to give one up.  Once I had been rather proud of that arrangement. 

The twelve women had cast the great observatory dome, working in pairs on the curved bronze plates that would carry the constellations.  It was the last plate, swinging loose above the wharf, that had knocked Linn into the water.  Instead of cutting twelve separate dies for their pay tokens, I had cut one wheel with a sign for each woman.  Each had pressed her thumb into the soft wax below her sign, and when the coin was cast their prints had come out clear down to the little scars.  Linn had chosen the Crab because it walked the way she did carrying a full ladle.  Put all twelve thumbs back in their hollows and the till would open. 

"We tried my little boy's," said Bet, who had worked under the Fishes.  "His thumb went in all right.  Nothing happened." 

I set the coin on the promise pan and put a month's gold on the other, less one woman's wages.  The beam stayed down on the side of the coin.  I tried the full sum, then half, then a single shaving from the edge of a crown, working from either limit the way my master had taught me.  Whichever pan held the coin stayed down, even after we turned the scales around and changed the pans themselves.  Across the middle ran the one name I had entered in the ledger: Dome Crew.  I had given twelve people a single name, and no eleven of them could answer to it. 

While I was staring at the beam, Bet of the Fishes took the cracked tea bowl off my shelf and turned it slowly in the light.  Linn had mended it with lacquer dusted with gold, a piece of kintsugi finer than anything she had done for wages.  She had even left the nick in the lip where I liked to rest my spoon.  Bet laid the wheel in the bowl, and I saw what she was looking at: the gold lines divided the clay without dividing the vessel. 

"Can you do that backwards?" she asked. 

We could have broken up the coin, but the pieces would have been scrap, just as the clay before Linn mended it had been so many crocks.  What we wanted was a break that knew what it was meant to save. 

Bet held the wheel steady while I cut between the signs, stopping just short of the rim each time.  Into each groove one of the women hammered a thread drawn from her own wedding ring or earring or the little gold ball on her Sunday hatpin.  For the last seam I used the wire from my badge of office.  When we were done the wheel looked like a small sun coming up through rain. 

Each woman spoke her name and pressed her thumb to her sign, and one after another the wedges came away along the soft bright seams.  On the scales each piece weighed exactly one woman's wages, neither more nor less, right down to the odd penny for the night they had worked late.  The till opened eleven times.  Bet went out to catch the grocer, who had children of his own and might be persuaded to take his shutters down. 

I was left with the Crab, and under its claw the little white dash where Linn had nicked herself opening oysters for our father's supper.  I did not try my own thumb, though it was near enough in size and I would have given a great deal to hear the drawer open once more.  I left her gold on the scales and set the Crab beside it, so that whoever came on duty next would know whose it was.  I filled the bowl with tea and went down to the river steps, where I sat until dark drinking from the nick my sister had seen no reason to mend.
