In the mirror world, where even a puddle had another country standing in it, the orchard house was the one place with a window you could look through.  The window was mica, peeled from the hill in the days when light came up like water if you dug deep enough.  Now it showed only the alchemist's pear trees and, beyond their black lace, the endless night.  At the sill a woman was trying to remember how to swaddle a pear. 

She got as far as tucking one corner of the cloth under its round bottom before her hands went blank.  Somewhere across the river a fishwife would be standing with her hands crossed just so, unable to say why.  That was what it meant to be the scattered memory keeper: other people got to keep what Lio wanted most.  She had given each departing orchard worker a piece of herself on the night the great furnace broke, so that if the greenhouse fell there would still be somebody who knew its work.  Nobody had been able to tell her how to call the pieces back. 

While she hesitated, the last pear on the last living branch grew a little cooler against her wrist.  Inside its skin lay the fine hot filaments from which the workers used to make their woven dawns.  Pick it bare and the threads would spring apart in one magnificent, useless flash.  Leave it till it fell and the realm would never see anything by its own light again. 

Lio put her cheek to the fruit and heard herself humming the tune they had used to time the wrapping.  It had been a ridiculous song about a fish that stole a baby's shirt.  She had never in her life sung it past the word shirt; that was where the woman at the next tree always came in.  Without much hope she pushed open the mica window and sang what she had into the street.  Every surface that reflected that open window became, for the space of a breath, open too. 

From the rain barrel at her feet a fishwife looked up, a gutted pike still on her board.  She sang the line about the baby's bare behind, and Lio's hands came together under the pear.  Across the river the pike slipped to the floor. 

For a moment Lio had the whole quick use of those other hands, down to the old knife nick that hurt in cold water.  The fishwife had none; she stood looking at her fingers as if they were so many pale little fish on a string.  Lio could have gone on, drawing the scattered workers through forgotten songs until she was the woman who knew everything again.  She had lain awake for years wanting exactly that and imagining no particular cost.  Instead she let the corner slip and asked the fishwife to show her. 

Freed of Lio's grip, the fishwife's hands demonstrated on the pike, which she wrapped in yesterday's news.  Lio copied her, and the pear came away whole in its muslin, staining the weave with a thousand lines of gold.  The next verse went out by way of a washbasin to a village where nobody had troubled to get dressed in a month.  There a boy still in his nightshirt showed them how to draw a thread without drawing all the rest after it.  One by one the dark towns lit their little squares of water and polished tin as somebody recognized a cue.  By the time the cloth was empty there were people in six valleys holding the ends of light, waiting for Lio to tell them what came next. 

That was the part nobody in the orchard had ever sung, because that was where the furnace used to take over.  Just past the trees she could see its iron mouth waiting to be fed the work of all those hands.  She looked instead at the fishwife, who was passing her strand from bank to bank as casually as if she were lacing a boot.  Where the strands crossed they held. 

By breakfast there was a sky you could hang washing under, though it sagged a little over the river.  After that, whenever a village felt the dark coming on, somebody there would begin the song and wait at the washbasin for an answer.  Lio generally let the others answer first, for the pleasure of hearing what the fish had stolen this time.
