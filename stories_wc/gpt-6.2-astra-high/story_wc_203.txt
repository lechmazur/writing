All her life Nell had taken lifts apart to find out what ceilings hide, and the one ceiling she cared about was twelve feet up in an abandoned circus ticket office.  Her father had told her there was a second circus there, for children who couldn't pay.  At his funeral her brothers had put this down with his other tactful inventions, like the Christmas the landlord took their front door away to paint it.  She had laughed with them, which was what brought her back. 

Nell was the elevator repair technician the city sent to disconnect the old cash lift before the building came down.  She had traded a week's worth of clean hospital calls for the job.  Beyond the counter, cracks in the facade ran through the painted ringmaster's cuffs, making both white hands look as if they had been tied with string.  As a girl she'd have watched those hands; now she watched where the ringmaster was looking, at a boy sitting on the tile with his knees poking through his trousers. 

She saw ghosts often enough in her work, mostly porters waiting for cars that no longer stopped on their floors.  None had ever had her father's trick of pinching the loose skin on one elbow while he waited.  He didn't look at her, even when she sat down beside him.  From down here the two cracks met above the ticket window, at the corners of a long slot somebody had papered over with gold stars. 

She went behind the counter and killed the power to the lift.  When the overhead fluorescent buzz died off, she heard the boy whistling through the gap where his front teeth ought to be.  It was the tune her father used to whistle for the kettle, with little gaps of its own, as if to let somebody else come in. 

By torchlight she studied the dust on the sill of the cash shaft, the way she'd learned to read a machine when its operator was gone.  Even under years of gray fluff the black grit had kept its pattern: two fans shed by the lift chains and a third, much finer fan off to one side.  Above it her fingers found a little brass door.  Inside sat a horologist's mainspring barrel, beautiful work among the big greasy sprockets, with a pawl that let it steal a little wind each time the cash box went upstairs.  All day the paying customers had wound it without knowing. 

She found the square arbor and turned it with her key until she felt the pawl engage.  Then she slit the brittle paper between the stars and lifted the latch that had once been within reach of whoever sold the tickets.  Out of the slot rode a lady no bigger than a spoon, her tin slippers resting on a wire stretched taut by the thin steel arm that bore her.  She advanced across the ceiling in a series of small recoveries from disaster, first her red parasol tipping and then her whole body tipping to catch up.  Each time she found her balance a bell sounded, just one note in the gaps in the boy's tune. 

Nell had expected, if she ever found the thing, to feel the weight of having laughed at him.  Instead she wanted what he must have wanted then: somebody else under there before the lady went away.  She took out her phone, but on its little screen the wire was just a scratch and the lady's brave corrections looked like faults in the recording.  Nell put it away. 

On the city's salvage sheet she struck out CASH LIFT, SCRAP and wrote TICKET WINDOW WITH WORKING THEATER, REMOVE WHOLE.  There was a box for the weight and another for where it ought to go.  She could work out the weight tomorrow; in the other box she put the waiting room of the children's clinic, where she serviced two elevators and knew exactly how high the ceiling was.  When she looked up the boy was gone and the little lady was tipping into silence on the journey back.  Nell pursed her lips and took the part that wasn't bells.
