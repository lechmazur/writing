After remembering began to hurt, people came up to the mountain bookstore to sell their memories rather than carry them another mile.  The bookstore occupied the whole mountain, and its aisles followed seams of softer rock, so that you could walk all day toward a voice and come no nearer.  At its heart lived Ada, the mercury-glass seer who bought the memories and bound them in blue cloth.  What she paid was a bed among the shelves and one night in seven back in the valley before the dam went up. 

She substituted reality nightly, one aisle at a time, laying her silver palm on a volume until its boards swung apart like shutters.  On baking nights the smell of rye reached even the atlas gallery. 

Then a girl came to the counter with a burn along her wrist.  In her grandmother's kitchen there was always a hot loaf, but no matter how early the girl got up there was never any dough.  She had reached into the oven looking for the moment before the bread was bread.  Her grandmother had died that week.  Could Ada please turn back a page? 

Under the counter Ada's hands made the old movement of folding a wet mass over itself, something they had not done since they were flesh.  She had taken the silvering in order to understand the shape of home, to see all six sides of the houses the rising water took.  Now she saw what a perfect likeness of a loaf could cost. 

Leaving the girl to mind the counter, she took the spiral down to the buried stacks, where the blue books ended and the gifts nobody had thought worth buying began.  Here were the knotter's speed, the cooper's ear for a bad stave, her own knack for knowing just when rye would stand another folding.  They had come in with the memories like seeds in the hems of coats. 

At the entrance hung the lost language stone, the oldest book on the mountain, an index to these volumes without words.  Its face was worn smooth except for the impression of a single flight feather.  As a girl Ada had swept flour from the kneading board with a goose wing; she knew what the impression lacked.  From the quill behind her ear she wiped the ink and laid the feather in its bed.  As she stroked it from tip to root the little barbs parted, each with a catch like the faintest click of a lock.  Down in the stacks something answered, a mutter like thunder on the other side of a hill.  One barb dragged against her thumb exactly as dough did when it wanted more water.  She held it there, and a narrow book slid out against her knee.  On its cover was the scar her mother's wedding ring had printed on her knuckle the day they learned to knead together. 

That night the girl watched Ada open the kitchen to the waiting families, then slip the narrow book beneath the blue one to seed the likeness with something it could not keep still.  The hot loaf vanished, and for one terrible moment all they had was their hunger.  Then Ada rolled up her sleeves and asked somebody to fetch the sack of rye from the real storeroom.  Silver came off in the first lump, which they threw away, and by the second her hands were brown and sore and useful.  The girl spoiled two loaves herself before she felt the drag Ada meant. 

At dawn the kitchen folded up around them; its window went first, with the drowned orchard still green beyond the sill.  The bread stayed on the atlas shelf where they had set it out to cool.  Nobody asked Ada for the orchard back that morning, although one old man sat with his face in his hands until the girl brought him the heel.  By noon somebody had remembered where there was clay for an oven, and somebody else was down among the buried books, listening with a feather for the weight of a brick.
