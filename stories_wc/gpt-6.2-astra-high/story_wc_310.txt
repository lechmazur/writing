After the universe contracted and the first few miles of space opened up again, the sky looked like the inside of a shut eye.  On the ambered observatory deck, Linn could just make out two wakes in the dust, diverging like the legs of a pair of scissors.  One belonged to the orchard tug on which her sister grew pears; the other belonged to a school barge whose name she had learned that morning, Attendance.  Neither pilot could see the narrow passage out of the dust, and once the observatory fired its guide flare down one wake, the other ship would have to fly blind. 

It was Linn's office to cast lots in matters for which nobody could offer a decent argument either way.  She kept a collection of foreign coins for the purpose, each tested for balance, each from a place now smaller than its own small change.  Ask her about any one and she would quietly bring out her rubbing book, her eyes brightening as she showed you which fish on which little silver piece had once bought an actual fish.  Today she put the silver fish in the tossing cup and told both ships that fish meant orchard, net meant school. 

Before she covered the cup, a burst of static came through the speaking grille, and a sound like a girl drawing breath after a dive.  Linn had spent the long inward fall learning to understand those bursts.  Around the hinge of time, a radio signal sometimes reached the receiver before it left the throat, and its syllables arrived last first.  The trick was to distill sense from the hiss by listening for the little cuts in it where a pilot keyed her mike, then running the tape backward between cuts.  Out of the grille came her sister's voice, matter of fact as ever: "I can see your light." 

Linn set down the cup without shaking it.  Here was the argument the rules allowed for: one pilot was going to see the flare anyway, so why not aim it at the other?  She got as far as dialing in the school's wavelength before she caught herself trying to bargain with something that had only ever given its word backward.  On the inward fall she had heard her father say he was warm and taken it for a prophecy of spring, when what it meant was that somebody would find him a blanket.  Seeing a light was not the same as being given it. 

She cleared the dial, slid the lid home and shook until the coin stopped making separate knocks and sang.  When she lifted the upturned cup there lay the net, knotted by a dead engraver who had even thought to mend a hole in it.  Linn had always understood her calling as a matter of getting her own wishes out of the way, as if a person could be tuned to the wavelength of a purpose and left there.  Yet the little net lay there doing nothing, and the children would go on flying blind unless she did the rest herself.  She dialed in Attendance again, called the call sign twice, and felt the deck jump under her feet as the flare took flight. 

Her sister said the words Linn knew, and then the ones she hadn't known: "Is that all those things are when you see them from the side?"  Against the enormous darkness there was a scratch of green, hardly enough to steer a bicycle by, let alone a school.  Linn wanted to tell her about the fish and the net, how evenly they weighed, but her sister knew the weight of that coin as well as she did.  Instead she asked what the pears were like this year. 

"Hard," her sister said, with the particular disgust of a woman who had put a year's work into pears you could break a tooth on.  The school sent its passage signal, a single clear note, and Linn entered it in the book with one hand while she held the grille to her ear with the other.  From the orchard came three small, deliberate crunches, in the right order, in the only time that either of them had.
