After time became visible, the dead of Orison returned most thickly in bad weather, when the slow gold sediment of afternoons rose from the floors.  Orison was a baroque asteroid, a mile of nickel carved into swags and scallops, and it had scarcely a flat yard where the dead could stand without looking grand.  This was a considerable inconvenience to an archaeologist. 

Iven had been a poet before he became a revolutionary, and now that his republic was defunct he earned his keep lettering the labels in its museum.  What he wanted, for the time that remained to him, was to reimagine an entire civilization without first having to find a rhyme for chain.  The words still came in pairs, like prisoners. 

The first label they gave him concerned the Hall of Lilies, lately uncovered above the pit where three hundred cutters had died behind a pressure gate.  In the weather pictures a woman in court dress knelt there amid ranks of identical women, all raising crystal vases as if to drink.  The excavators called it a funeral rite; the cutters' grandchildren called it the supper at which the court had toasted the closing of the gate.  Iven himself had given that supper its name in a marching song, and a good many decent people had gone to prison singing it. 

When the diggers put a crystal vase shard in his palm, his first unworthy thought was how handsomely it would take the light beside his manuscript.  Then he saw the little crescent chipped out of its rim, and went to look at the pictures again.  Every vessel had that nick; in every other vessel it was on the left. 

His mother had made a practice of covering mirrors during storms because she could bear one appearance of his drowned brother but not a roomful.  With a bolt of the black stuff the excavators used to wrap their finds, he thought he could devise a test.  He strung curtains across the mirrored scallops of the hall, with a separate cord for each, and waited through six days of perfectly steady starlight. 

On the seventh, as the sun flung copper fire across their orbit, he took the cords in his left hand and the shard in his right.  One by one the lifted arms went out, until the last court lady knelt alone in a puddle of tarnished gold.  She wasn't lifting her vase but lowering it, mouth down, onto the rock, and what had looked like a jeweled collar was a row of pins held between her lips.  She touched her ear to the foot of the vase; then she took out a pin and scratched something on the floor. 

Beside Iven the youngest digger dropped to her knees and began to ease the dust out of a lily with a camelhair brush.  The scratches ran along the veins of the leaf in the old pit shorthand, which any cutter's child could read because it was still used for knocking through walls.  Water at our ankles, the first leaf said, and the next said, Water at our knees.  It took them the rest of the storm to clean the floor, and all that while the woman crept from lily to lily, taking dictation.  The last marks were names, not three hundred but thirty, and one man had spent his breath spelling out that the dog was with him. 

What neither party to the dispute had bargained for was that the people on the other side of the gate might have left a record too.  He could have made a ballad of the woman, a saint out of a single evening, and he felt the old lift in his ribs at the thought of an audience drawing breath together.  But he did not know whether she had begged for the gate to be opened or had herself given the order to shut it. 

He lettered the thirty names on thirty cards, leaving room on each for whatever else might yet be learned, and laid them in the cases usually reserved for ducal silver.  For the shard he cut a little cradle of black cloth that held the nick at the exact height of a child's eye.  When his mother came he found her standing by the card about the dog, tapping on the glass with her wedding ring.  It was the miners' customary acknowledgment, three taps that meant, on the other side of any thickness of stone, I have heard you.
