On the orchard planet of Pome, a blacksmith could go a lifetime without making a wheel, but there was always work shoeing a floating arch.  The stone bows hung low in spring, and wherever two rubbed shoulders, somebody had to rivet on a plate or let the orchards shake each other to pieces.  Mara did the riveting, and if a grower called her "boy" from the far end of an arch she seldom troubled him with a correction.  She was less timid about the fit of a rivet.  She would make a man late for his own wedding rather than let him cross on a shoe that clicked. 

That spring the arches descended over a valley full of broken stone, all that remained of the terraces where the miners had grown their food.  The growers voted to sell the valley families plums on credit for another year.  Mara knew what that meant, having just finished paying for the plums she had eaten when she was ten.  She waited for a silence in which to suggest another arrangement, and went home still waiting. 

At home her mother was packing the blue silk in salt against the moths.  It had been a magician's silk before it became an orchard tool, back when people would pay to see a rose fall asleep in one fist and a thistle open in the other.  Now every grower bartered awakenings, putting a tired old stock to bed for a season and bringing on some promising young graft in its place.  Grafters called the first hour of thaw the doubtful hour: a tree understood that it must wake, but could still be confused about which wood was its own.  The silk had to touch both. 

Under the broken terraces, Mara told her mother, there was still the stump of the blue damson on whose spreading roots all the miners' trees had once been grafted.  She had struck it driving a roof bolt, sound wood that rang through the iron.  A root that had lost its crown had no way of knowing that winter was over, even if a hundred winters went by.  Given one waking, it could send up suckers through a whole hillside of rubble. 

Her mother stopped folding.  "You've found somebody with a season to spare?" 

"I've got the tree Dad left me." 

"And I've got your dad's debts," her mother said, but she took the silk out of the salt. 

At first light Mara went down through the abandoned mine shafts, trailing the silk from the lowest root of her father's plum, which hung from its arch like a bell rope.  Like all good stage silks it would go on coming out of a sleeve as long as nobody looked inside.  Her father had drawn the workings on the back of his deed to the tree, and she knew the blind turns as well as she knew his signature.  Even so, by the time her fingers found the familiar hard grain overhead, the silk in her other hand had begun to stir.  Up on the arch her tree was waking. 

She had only to close her hand, but for the first time she saw that the debt was not quite hers to risk.  Her mother would have to eat too.  She held still until she heard a second pair of boots gritting along the gallery. 

"You could have said no," she called, which was the nearest she had ever come to asking her mother for anything that mattered. 

"Could still," her mother said, and laid her hand over Mara's and closed their fingers with deliberate care.  There was no flash, only a smell like the stone floor of the forge when somebody spilled the cooling bucket. 

They ate on credit that year, like everybody else.  But all summer blue shoots kept nosing up between the stones, and when the growers came down with their ledgers the miners showed them where to put their feet.  By the third summer the damsons were thick enough to stain the children's mouths a blue that no amount of solemn licking could remove.  Mara's mother took the first basket up to the arch, not in payment but to sell, and this time Mara went along to name the price.
