After the final petal dropped from the old rosebush, I could take down the mirrors without my father accusing me of murder.  I was the fire escape gardener of Gannet Court, a title acquired by growing one rose in a pickle tub where no rose had any business living.  Its whole summer depended on my ability to reflect light into dark corners, first from a square of glass on the laundry roof, then from a shaving mirror clamped to our rail.  Now I wanted the mirrors for the bedroom, and the bedroom was where my father had begun to die. 

He had once been lockmaster of the little basin where the fishing boats wintered, an office that came with a cottage until it came with a pension instead.  In the cottage, when weather kept the boats in and me underfoot, he had put on shadow plays with the contents of his pockets.  A lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth, broken off the brass tool he used to check the depths cut into keys, became a fox with one ear laid back.  The fox was always trying to get passage out of the basin without paying.  I had forgotten every trick he tried, which seemed to me an emergency now, though it had not seemed one in twenty years. 

I set my telephone on a pile of folded towels and angled the mirrors to bring the harbor lamp onto the wall above his bed.  Without getting up he could see our luminous harbor by moonlight framed in the little glass, and on the plaster beside it a stage as yellow as butter.  I put the brass tooth into his good hand. 

He looked at the telephone.  "Who else is here?" 

Nobody, I told him, which was true except for the woman I expected to be that winter, sitting on the edge of an empty bed, pressing play.  He used his thumb to make the fox's ear twitch, and for a second I was six again and could smell the wet wool of his uniform steaming on the stove.  Then his hand shook and the little animal went to pieces in the light.  Into a quivering hush that followed came the minute, industrious sound of the telephone refocusing. 

"I'd rather you forgot it," he said. 

I nearly told him what a small thing this was to ask in return for changing his sheets, and the shame of nearly saying it made me rough with the towels.  I put the telephone in the linen drawer and shut it on the light.  On the wall the fox lay still. 

After a bit my father asked whether the boatman intended to keep a paying customer waiting all night.  There had never been a boatman before, just a voice he did through his nose.  I picked up the teaspoon from his saucer and set its shadow under the fox. 

"Fare's an ear," I said. 

The fox considered this with the wistfully pragmatic air my father used to assume over the price of anything beautiful in a shop window.  Then he turned the tooth on its side and the ear disappeared. 

"Cost me half that coming home," he said, and my laugh rocked the spoon so hard we nearly lost our passenger.  He steadied my wrist with his bad hand, the one he usually hid under the blanket when anybody came.  All his life he had tried to instill in me a respect for tools, which included letting the tool tell you what it could still do.  We moved our two bits of metal with the rhythm of oars in water, pulling and resting, pulling and resting, until our shadows crossed the bright square and ran into the harbor in the glass.  He fell asleep holding the tooth. 

That winter I did forget the sound of the boatman's voice, though it was my own, and for a while the loss was as hard to bear as I had known it would be.  But the next summer, when a child from downstairs asked what I did with the mirrors after the rose was finished, I told her to come back at dark and bring a spoon.
