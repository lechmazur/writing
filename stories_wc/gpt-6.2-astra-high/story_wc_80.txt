After the drawbridge rose to admit the salvage crane, Nell went down the ladder into the submerged pavilion.  Behind the cofferdam the pumps had lowered the water just enough to uncover the ballroom floor, and the setting sun burned without heat in every puddle.  As shore surveyor she had the last word on what the crane could safely take before the pumps stopped.  She had already said no to the chandelier, twice, the second time to the mayor.  It would bring the rotten ceiling down on the men who went to sling it.  She let him think she felt nothing about the little glass pears his father had blown. 

The museum wanted the compass rose from the middle of the floorboards, but Nell had come for the scratches.  Before the pavilion acquired a bandstand it had been a mould loft, where boatbuilders drew hulls at full size so they could lift the shapes of their frames straight off the boards.  Her mother had worked there with her sister Kit, who could wrest a fair curve from a batten that looked fit only for kindling.  Every boat they finished got a brass number; every boat they failed to sell survived as a few cuts in wood, crossed out by the next keel.  As a girl Nell had wanted to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams, a profession she imagined required a spoon and unlimited time.  Now she had twenty minutes and a crowbar. 

Kit had taught her where to look by placing notes in foundation repairs, a builder's courtesy to whoever next had to take the work apart.  Beside the compass rose Nell found a short board pegged instead of nailed, directly over the head of a replaced pile.  Under it, in a bed of pitch, lay the antique traveling inkwell that used to stand beside her mother's plate at supper.  Its screw cap had once kept ink off a gentleman's shirts on coach journeys; it had apparently done as well at keeping the sea out.  Inside were two tight rolls of tracing linen, one in each woman's hand. 

Kit's note gave the stations of a shallow hull, broad enough for cargo but drawing so little it could have gone all the way up the tidal creeks.  Her mother's was a list of shelf lengths, all different, and a calculation of the weight of six hundred books.  Nell set her lamp on the floor.  Low light drew shadows out of cuts that had been invisible from the height of a standing adult.  Between the long sweeps sprang little flights of straight lines, like stitches holding two edges of cloth together.  Separate them and you had a boat without a cargo and a library without walls; follow them together and you could walk from the tiller to the children's shelf.  She knew that shelf by its height, the height of a windowsill on which she had learned to read while her mother shelved returns in the town library.  All her life she had thought that job was what her mother had settled for when the yard shut down. 

Above her the crane operator knocked on the dam with a shackle, asking for his cut marks.  The rose was lovely, but it was an ornament you could take almost anywhere and tell almost any story about.  With her rule and the two scraps of linen she could give the museum the whole plan, but she wanted one piece on which the women's hands had actually met.  She chalked a rectangle at the turn of the bilge, where three shelves stepped inward to make room for the narrowing bow.  Then she took the trouble to go up and tell the mayor herself. 

"I can give you the pears too," she said, "if you'll take them broken."  From her jacket she took three she had picked out of the silt, snapped at the stems but still whole enough to cup a little light.  He held out both hands.  While the crane lifted its curious harvest of scarred wood, they stood passing the pears between them, rubbing the mud away with their thumbs.  She would set the panel low in the museum, with the lamp down at one end, and let children find for themselves where to put their feet.
