At twilight the wind chime on Midnight Ridgeway gave one last knock and fell silent.  Below the lookout there were two steeples now, one coming up out of the reservoir and one going down.  A swallow skimmed the seam where the two were joined.  Levee warden Ada Venn watched the hands on the lookout rail rather than the steeples; it was when the hands let go that somebody was likely to try the mud.  She caught her brother's sleeve before he got one leg over. 

"Not even for Mam," she said, and felt him go heavy in her hand. 

Ben had spotted the brick shell of their mother's boardinghouse, where the kitchen door had served as a register of heights.  The last mark beside his daughter's name would have been at Ada's waist; leukemia had seen to that, not the flood.  He had a tape measure in his pocket.  He didn't want the door, he said, only the distance from the floor. 

Thirty years of telling people where to stop had made Ada precisely humane.  She never said later when she meant no, and she never sent anybody away with nothing if there was something to give.  Somebody might have a picture, she told Ben; Mam had fed half the county in that kitchen.  In the quiet that followed she could hear the dry slip of album leaves. 

Ever since the first chimney broke water, people had been bringing pictures up there to settle which chimney it was.  Mostly what Ada wanted from those arguments was the moment when two people who remembered different towns discovered they had lived next door.  She cleared the oilcloth on the long supper table and asked them for kitchens. 

First came a bride with her veil hitched up to wash dishes; behind her was the door, unmistakable even in black and white because an obliging lodger had cut a crescent in its foot for the cat.  Then somebody found the crescent sticking out from under a mattress on a removal truck.  Across that print Ben laid another, of their first supper on the ridge, eaten outdoors with the mattresses piled under canvas and the bedding ropes serving as washing lines.  No crescent, but there was Ada, already a grown woman, crouched under the trestles with something brilliant blue in her hand. 

She remembered the taste before she remembered the job.  A sapphire-hued liqueur label had come loose in the ice bucket, and Mam had sent her under the table with a mouthful of drawing pins to tack it over a keyhole that was dripping gravy on somebody's trousers.  Iron on her tongue, cold gravy on her wrist, and overhead the laughter she had taken for unkindness because she couldn't see the faces.  All these years she had told the story of that supper as if the great thing about it was that nobody cried.  What came back to her now was Mam's stocking twisting in her fist and Mam standing quite still, letting her hang on. 

Ben was on his knees now, easing back the oilcloth where it hung almost to the floor.  A little blue square, faded everywhere except under the heads of four rusty pins, showed against the wood.  They turned the table over together. 

On the underside were a hundred dates and names in pencil.  Someone had tried to expunge the first mark beside his girl's name, then written in apology below it: Shoes on.  Ben found her last mark by touch; he had scored it with his watch buckle when she wouldn't stand still long enough for him to find a pencil.  From the bottom edge, not the top of the cat hole, she was forty-one inches.  He ran the tape up the front of his coat and held his palm flat there for a while.  Nobody asked him to move his hand. 

Then the bride from the dishpan wanted to know if her boys were on there, and somebody fetched a lamp, and Ada took down the names as they came back to people.  Out on the water the two steeples had gone the same shade of gray.  When they set the table upright again, they left the lamp burning underneath.
