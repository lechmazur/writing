On the Xenial Trellis above the clouds, a place was always kept for whoever fell up.  For the hour each spring when gravity inverts, the trellis puts out nets as a tree puts out leaves, and for a week beforehand people climb the mountain to help mend them.  My father went up to build a bunker for the net crews after two men froze at their posts.  I went to collect the flavors of different silences. 

At thirteen I had a notebook fat with them: the silence before bread is cut tastes of fennel, while the one that follows a bad joke is greasy as the rind of ham.  I had begun the collection in Father's bunkers because he paid me to sit inside while he tested the seals.  He thought that by limiting exposure, ten seconds today and eleven tomorrow, I might come to bear a door closing at my back.  The seconds only stretched, but in the space they made all sorts of queer tastes began to sprout.  The one I called the gilded hush came just before he let me out: honey at first, then the cold flat taste of the spoon. 

Father was proud of that name and painted it in gold above the door of the new crew room.  He gave me the last scrap of leaf to mend the chipped slipper of my tiny porcelain doll.  When I was little we had passed her back and forth through the test bunker's speaking tube, the only part of me small enough to get out. 

When the mountain bell rang and the loose stones went streaking up like larks, he invited me inside to see what he had made.  The crew lay snug against the padded ceiling, each man watching through a pane of glass for a telltale twitch among the red net cords.  Some had taken off their mittens for the first time in twenty years of service.  I stayed on the sill with my belt hooked to a staple and my feet dangling into heaven.  For a long while nothing came but a drift of washing, still pinned to the line, billowing out into enormous empty bodies and then collapsing into shirts again. 

Then my doll kicked me.  She lay on her back against the roof of my pocket and I felt the tap of her heel through my shirt.  Tap tap.  I held her to the sill and felt her skip.  Somebody was knocking on the trellis, too faintly for flesh to feel without a little bone of fired clay to carry the news. 

Inside, every eye was on the nets.  None twitched.  The gilded hush lay intangibly tangible on my tongue, a spoon at the back of my throat that would choke me if I tried to swallow.  I spat, which was easier than speaking, and my spit went sailing past my eyebrows.  Father saw that and came to hold my head, as he always did when I was sick.  I put the doll between his fingers and the sill. 

Together we followed her little dance along the iron ribs to a place where the woodbine grew thick and the net crews had never thought to look.  There under a platform a woman lay flat on her back with one arm clamped round a boy and the other quite free to knock but not to haul them clear.  She had been beating the planks with her wedding ring; her knuckle had swelled over it like risen dough.  Father gave a red cord one hard twitch, and the crew came tumbling out with a sling already knotted in a rope.  We slid them sideways into the sling with our bare hands because nobody had stopped to put his mittens on.  The bell struck.  The stones went home.  The rope held. 

Afterward Father sat with the woman and counted with her the minutes she had knocked while the men lay watching their red cords.  All summer he cut speaking tubes into the bunker, one to each iron rib, with little cups of porcelain at the listening ends.  On the lintel the gold letters stayed, though he offered to scrape them off for me.  I liked them better now that the hush inside was something you kept in order to hear.  Next spring Father fitted a hook to hold the door open one finger's breadth, which turned out to be exactly how much sky I needed.  Under fennel in my book I made a new entry: the silence of people listening together tastes like the first bite somebody cuts for you while the loaf is still warm.
