Every night since her coronation, Ada had dreamed of her mother picking at a piece of lace with the single long nail she kept for peeling oranges.  By morning the finger was bloody and the lace intact.  The priests called this a blessing, as they called everything that happened to a queen too young to contradict them.  Seven summers had made a disenchanted queen of her, but it was the smell of oranges on her pillow one morning that finally made a daughter of her again. 

She sent for Linn, who had washed her mother's body and had not been invited back to court. 

"Does she ever look up?" Linn asked. 

Ada shook her head, and Linn went to fetch two climbing coats from the peg where she had hung them seven summers ago. 

They began the climb after the final warning, when even the priests had taken shelter from the annual shower.  Overhead the stars had opened like little cuts, and wherever a drop fell on the path it preserved the shape of what it struck.  Ada stepped over a silver impression of a field mouse, fine down to the whiskers; the mouse itself had gone about its business.  It was this property of bleeding starlight that had first given some bereaved fool the idea of keeping the dead. 

At the ember grotto on the ridge, Linn lit a taper from the coals that porters kept banked there against the murderous cold of the upper galleries.  Here they had warmed the little queen's feet on the night she came to have her mother's last hour fixed in stone. 

"You might have stopped me," Ada said. 

"I wanted her too," said Linn, and pulled the larger coat tighter about herself, so that Ada saw whose shoulders had worn it smooth. 

They went up through the mountain until they came out in a cobalt grotto above the clouds, blue even at midnight from the mineral that gave the royal tombs their color.  In its open side hung what looked like a curtain of frost, each crystal a minute of somebody's life, each glittering scallop an hour brought back entire.  Beyond it her mother sat in bed with an orange in her lap, picking at the lace on her sheet. 

"Look where the thread goes," said Linn in the softly commanding voice with which she used to get a whole sickroom quiet enough for sleep. 

Ada followed the thread past the hem of the sheet and into the fist of a child kneeling beside the bed.  She remembered being that child; what she had forgotten was how fiercely she had held on.  The priests had said the dead kept the hours turning; Ada could see now that the taut thread ran clear through the child's palm and into her own. 

"They told me to choose an hour when she was happy." 

"She was," Linn said, "but that didn't mean she wanted it to last." 

Ada put her hand through the curtain to take the thread, and the girl by the bed put hers out too.  The cold ran up to Ada's elbow before Linn snatched her back.  It was not her mother who needed help letting go. 

"How did the hour end?" Ada asked. 

Linn tipped her head toward the open side of the cave, where something small and green was laboring up from the dark pines through a rent in the clouds.  All at once Ada remembered how the three of them had blown out the bedside candle to see those lights better through the hospital window.  For one precious minute nobody had been required to be dying, or brave, or nine. 

Linn held up the taper between them, and with a shared exhale they put it out, spotting the first fireflies of summer just as the child inside the curtain spotted them.  Her fist came open; her mother had not been trying to undo the lace at all, but to free one small finger after another without hurting her.  This time, when the hour ended, Ada let it end, and the bed stood empty save for a shallow dent in the pillow. 

On the way down she asked Linn what they had done with the orange. 

"Ate it on the stairs," Linn said, "though you wouldn't take any of the pieces with pips." 

That had not been kept in stone anywhere, and for the rest of the descent they took turns remembering things that hadn't.
