After the town went silent, we got to quarreling about the noise its last streetcar had made.  Some remembered a shriek on the bend by the baths, others the soft complaint of a finger traveling around the lip of a glass.  Nobody disputed the bend, so we put it in. 

Up on the mountaintop, where the water board had given us land in exchange for our cemetery, we laid out the streets between benches of potted trees.  By the time the reservoir had filled, you could walk a blooming labyrinth of bonsai forests and get lost precisely where you used to get lost on your way to school.  I was the gilded-leaf stationer who made the little address cards that hung from the branches.  In the shop below, my stock in trade had been invitations, and the habit of inviting people somewhere was stronger than the evidence that there was nowhere left to go. 

I slipped a battered notepad into my apron and went among the benches to collect the textures of different times.  Under my thumb the paper for a winter morning ought to feel quite unlike the paper for that same morning recollected in July.  I was earnestly studious about this, testing scraps against the inside of my wrist, although I was perfectly willing to supply invented histories when nobody could remember who had lived behind a particular door.  People took pleasure in correcting me, and thus by degrees the cards came right. 

Or so I told the girl who came looking for the room where her father had been born.  She had his address written out on the back of a bus ticket, including the words Above the Baths.  We found the baths, a grove of quinces whose scarlet flowers were just beginning to drop onto the gravel.  There was my card about the blue tiles and the brass spout shaped like a perch, both details that had survived correction for eleven years.  She turned it over as if the people who had lived upstairs might naturally be found on the other side. 

We had made a mountain on which nobody lived above anybody else. 

I had begun the notepad, in fact, by charting the faint hum in a fluorescent lamp at dawn, waiting in those baths for our turn on the evacuation bus.  The drawing was still there, a line of little scallops interrupted at regular intervals by a tall ragged tooth.  Somebody walking overhead had set the loose metal shade to rattling, six paces out and six paces back, as regular as the turns of a shuttle.  I had written under it, Bad lamp, good sound. 

The girl counted off six paces on the path and stood considering the distance with her hands tucked under her elbows.  "My gran took in hems," she said, "and Dad says she had to lay a wedding dress right out on the floor and walk around it." 

She didn't say it had been her grandmother walking, or that on the last morning before a flood anybody would have been letting down a dress.  It was I who wanted to say it, who could already see the gold needle on the card and the bright thread running through its eye.  Instead I got out the thin stuff I used for interleaving, through which you could see whatever was written on the sheet beneath. 

She copied the scallops while I held the paper still, and with the blunt end of my pen I pressed them up from underneath so that no gold was needed to distinguish them.  Then she wrote what he remembered on another slip, leaving room under the last line in case he remembered more.  I made the holes the way I always did outdoors, letting my reading glass refract the sun to a single white point until a brown eye opened in each sheet.  We hung the slips one above the other over the baths, high enough that you had to lift your chin to read them. 

I let her take the notepad home on the bus.  Up and down the paths that evening people kept stopping under trees they knew by heart, looking up into all that unclaimed room.
