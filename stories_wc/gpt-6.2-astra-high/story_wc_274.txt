At the dedication of the Foundling Room, the mayor drew back a square of velvet and found, instead of the brass tablet she had paid for, an envelope addressed to Mama in her own childish hand.  Her eyes widened, and in the blink that follows surprise the library had time enough to do its work. 

Dora, its cryogenic archivist, lived from one such blink to the next, since only behind closed lids could visitors bear to see at the speed of the cold.  In that interval the echoing library under the snow acquired a kitchen table, with a little cave of shade beneath it where a child could sit unnoticed.  The mayor sat there now, a substantial woman of sixty trying not to knock her head. 

Up on the table lay the letter Dora had written to the mayor's mother on the morning the ferries stopped.  It began with the practicalities of feeding a little girl who would not touch an egg if she could recognize its shape.  What made it a dangerous letter was not the liaison it disclosed but the arrangement the lovers had made for the following Tuesday.  At four, when the tide uncovered the old telephone junction, they would clip a wire to it and let their daughter talk to whichever mother she wasn't with. 

The dry towns had offered beds to the children of the drowned, with money for any household that would take one in.  Dora, already employed up north among the frozen collections, had registered her own daughter as a foundling and herself as the foster parent.  She had done much the same for forty other children whose parents could not afford to bring them north or bear to let them go to strangers.  The calls on Tuesdays had gone on for years. 

Beside the mayor under the table, Dora sat down, and a hundred feet above them the floorboards of the dedication platform returned the small creak of her knees.  She had forgotten that knees made a noise; it had been thirty winters since she had possessed a pair. 

"You used my envelope," the mayor said. 

"It was the only thing I could think of that would make you stop and look." 

The tablet credited the town with taking forty-one nameless children to its heart, which would have been a pardonable piece of vanity if the names had not been on file directly underneath it.  Those contraband truths had put bread in forty-one mouths so long as they stayed contraband. 

"Do you know who made that tablet?" her daughter asked.  "The girl who used to hold the wire when your hands got too stiff." 

Now Dora could see the awful weight of the thing, the years of wanting to say thank you and the single word, nameless, that had allowed them all to say it together.  She had been prepared to scandalize the town with her letter, less prepared to put that girl back beside the river with a wire cutting into her palm.  Dora folded the letter shut, leaving the names of the other subscribers where neither of them could see them.  What she wanted, after all, was to provoke an unspoken conversation, not force forty other people to join it. 

From her apron she took the little brass plug she had once carried everywhere, even to bed on a Monday in case the tides were early.  Through dormant networks in the ice, along the buried impressions of voices that the archive had never discarded, she placed a call to just one number.  Nobody answered, of course; the river had taken its time, but it had taken the junction, the quilt mill, the woman who lived upstairs from it.  But the receiver had kept the sound of a finger tapping twice against it, the signal that meant somebody else was listening and they mustn't use names. 

In the dizzying quiet that followed, her daughter reached up and tapped the underside of the table twice, so softly Dora felt it rather than heard it. 

"Keep the tablet," Dora said, and felt the table go, and the knees, and the lovely weight of the child grown old against her shoulder. 

The mayor opened her eyes and lifted the envelope, and there was the brass underneath after all, bright enough for the people in the front row to read.  She set the envelope beside it, still sealed, and under the childish Mama wrote out the full name of the woman who had answered on Tuesdays.  Then she borrowed the engraver's sharp point and wrote it again, on the brass, where there was room for forty more.
