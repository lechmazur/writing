I was the distress signal sender on the salvage ship Mercy, which meant that in an emergency everybody but me was allowed to babble.  My job was to listen until I could unsnarl what had happened from what people feared had happened, then send the few words that would bring the right help.  I was good at the first part even on my days ashore, as my wife had once observed with considerable bitterness.  When she died, I had a whole shelf of things she had told me and very little she had heard me say. 

These were actual shelves, actual bottles.  On our coast we know how to keep a gathered hush, the particular silence made by two or more people listening together.  Open the stopper a hair and it touches memories on both sides, so that for an instant each listener hears with the other's ears.  Even death does not spoil it, though there is no putting back what escapes. 

The one I carried next to my skin was a crystal perfume vial from our wedding, filled in the interval after the fiddler laid down his bow and before our vows.  Only four of us had been in the room; the fiddler was also the magistrate, and the witness was my wife's father, who captained the Mercy.  Once a year I let out enough to hear the little ivory buttons on my cuffs clicking together as she had heard them when my hands shook.  Her father never mentioned what he heard. 

We found the forgotten underwater temple when our anchor went through its roof and came up wearing a gold wreath.  The captain lowered three of us in the bell to see what else the sea had kept.  Through green glass I saw a flight of steps descending between trees of beaten silver, each leaf lifting separately in the current as if a small wind were making its lonely way to the altar.  Then the lip of the broken roof peeled off under our cable, and the bell tipped and came to rest with its open mouth against the steps. 

The speaking wire went dead.  They began hauling at once, and through the glass I saw what nobody above could see: the cable had caught beneath a silver branch as thick as my thigh.  Every turn of the winch was rolling us farther over, spilling our air in great bright coins that went spinning up where they might easily be mistaken for a sign of life. 

The two riggers were shouting different prayers; I had the cork out of my vial before I quite knew what I meant to do.  At first I heard the familiar clicking, and once again my whole ambition was to catch that little noise and keep it from going where she had gone.  But under it was the winch pawl on the Mercy, a bigger, slower click, and a man drawing breath between each stroke as if he were the one going under. 

I could have stoppered it then and saved a little for another year.  Instead I upended the vial against my palm as I used to see her do before a dance, to get even the last drop of scent.  Into the ears of the man at the winch I said what no drowning man ought ever to have to say. 

"Lower away." 

The clicks stopped; then came the soft whir of the brake paying out.  As the bell settled upright the younger rigger ducked out through its mouth and lifted the slack loop clear, and I had just enough of our wedding left to whisper, "Now." 

We rose past the silver leaves without stirring them.  On deck the captain sat with his back against the winch, rubbing the dent its handle had left in his palm.  He said, "She was trying not to laugh at those buttons, you know."  He could see her face from where he'd stood, which I couldn't, having been too busy looking down at my hands. 

I sat by him until the riggers got the gold wreath off the anchor.  Between us on the planks lay a silence I might once have thought worth keeping.  I put the empty vial away and asked him what else he remembered.
