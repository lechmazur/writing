From either country the gate onto the parapet opened easily, but from the parapet itself there was nothing to take hold of except wet oak.  Lea knew this because she had once watched her sister try to get her fingernails between the boards.  That was the year the treaty sent all captured nurses home, including those who had married where they worked.  The eastern escort had already shut the gate behind Bess when the western escort took her elbows.  Lea, the prisoner exchange negotiator, had put a neat tick beside her sister's name.  By the time she understood what Bess had been trying to do, the tide was over the causeway. 

They still lived together, in the long season between apology and forgiveness, buying coal by turns and taking care not to run out of milk.  Once a month Lea wrote east to ask permission for Bess to go back to her husband.  Once a month the answer came that the causeway was open only for exchanges.  Now his name had come up on the eastern list: not among the prisoners, but as the surgeon who would certify them fit to travel.  For the first time in four years Bess asked to come along. 

Neither republic would acknowledge the other's calendar, so the escorts kept their appointments by synchronizing through moon phases, meeting on the morning ebb after each full moon.  That gave Lea three weeks to persuade the captains to let the nurses coming through this time open the gates for themselves.  They agreed so readily she knew neither had understood what she meant. 

She spent the last evening looking for a pair of handles in her father's shed.  Bess came out with a screwdriver and the bottom drawer of their mother's linen press.  Each antique drawer pull was a brass pear on a hinge, worn hollow underneath by three generations of women looking for a clean sheet. 

"You'll split the oak with those fat screws," Bess said, and took an auger from its hook. 

At dawn they had to untether the tool chest from their cart and carry it up the windblown steps.  On the storm-worn parapet the gray sea mist blew level with the stones, so that the two women seemed to be kneeling in shallow running water.  Lea bored where Bess pointed, and Bess fitted a pear to the bare face of each gate.  Among the boots Lea heard one foot dragging, then a little double knock.  Bess laid down her screwdriver. 

"That's his bad leg and his stick," she said. 

Four nurses came in from the east and four from the west, and the gates swung shut on them.  Lea could see the captains' caps above the boards.  She told the nurses that here on neutral territory nobody would put a hand on them, and showed them what the pears were for.  They were free to go through either gate. 

Seven went where the lists said they belonged.  The eighth, a western girl with a red groove across her nose from her spectacles, stood quite still. 

"I've a practice there," she said at last, pointing back east, as if a practice were a rather flimsy thing to set against a country. 

"That's five against three," the western captain called when she lifted the eastern pear. 

Lea had prepared a speech about the difference between exchanging prisoners and exchanging people, but the girl was already through and Bess was standing up. 

"Four released on either side," Bess said, "and that's what you have to sign for." 

The furrow between the captain's brows stayed deep, but nobody broke the hush while he did the sum on his fingers.  Then came the scratch of his nib. 

The eastern surgeon came through to countersign, took one look at Bess and sat down hard on the tool chest.  Lea turned away, ashamed to have forgotten, in all her planning, that the man would need somewhere to sit.  But Bess had closed the lid and spread her apron on it before he came through.  As a nurse she'd been uncommonly thoughtful about the difference between offering help and having it ready. 

Lea collected the tools and the papers and kept both escorts busy until the tide licked the lowest stone.  She could not give them back four years, but she could give them the whole ebb. 

At home that night Bess threaded tape through the empty holes in the linen drawer and knotted it into loops.  "He'll come every moon," she said, testing them with a tug.  Next time, she told Lea, they ought to take a proper chair.
