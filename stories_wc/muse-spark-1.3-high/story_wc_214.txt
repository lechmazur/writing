<story>Sal was a cosmic janitor who swept orbits clean between midnight and dawn. [13] He worked at a shooting star tracking station perched on a basalt ridge in the desert. [29] The station had been forgotten through the hush of an overlooked era when telescopes turned elsewhere. [45] Sal did not mind the quiet because he was spiritually curious about endings and beginnings. [60] Each night he polished the great dome and collected dust from dead satellites in his bin. [76] Above his workbench hung a lamp fitted with yellow lantern glass that glowed like held sunrise. [92] The old keeper who trained him said the glass had survived three fires and one meteor strike. [109] Sal believed it remembered light and lent him courage when the sky felt too vast. [124] His task was simple and strange to log meteors by tracing the path of falling stars across paper charts. [143] He would watch a streak split the black, mark its arc, and whisper where he thought it landed. [161] One autumn night a signal blinked red and the radio crackled with a tired voice. [176] A transport pilot was lost above the dunes, her engine dead and her beacon fading. [191] Sal felt the familiar pull to find strength in vulnerability instead of hiding from fear. [206] He answered calmly and asked her to describe every light she could see. [219] She said she was spinning slowly and the stars looked like nails shaken loose from the dark. [236] Sal closed his eyes and tried to empathize with her breathless drift between worlds. [250] He imagined cold metal around her, the smell of ozone, and the weight of silence pressing in. [267] Under his lamp the charts spread wide, inked with years of patient lines. [280] He moved with primal elegance, bare feet silent on stone, hands sure among dials and paper. [296] He asked for her last three meteors and she gasped out directions and colors. [310] A green one to the left, a white one straight ahead, a long orange one behind her right wing. [329] Sal plotted them quickly, his pencil finding her place where the three paths crossed. [343] You are over the salt flat, he said, drifting east toward the old array. [357] She laughed in relief and fear, asking how falling rocks could map a living woman. [372] Because they fall true, he said, and we can learn from things that let go. [387] He kept her talking while he calculated wind and descent with a slide rule. [401] He told her stories of the station to keep her awake and steady. [414] He spoke of mopping moonlight off the lenses and feeding sparrows that nested in the antenna. [430] He spoke of learning flourishing stillness from watching dust settle in sunbeams. [442] Stillness is not empty, he said, it is where small lives gather strength. [455] The pilot grew quieter, her voice thin as her craft sank toward the dark earth. [470] Sal lit his lamp higher until the yellow lantern glass threw a warm circle on the charts. [487] He carried the lamp outside and raised it on the ridge as a beacon for her eyes. [504] The desert wind tugged his coat and the stars burned cold and near. [517] He radioed steady counts and told her when to pull her failing chute. [530] For a long minute there was only static, then a thump and a cough and a laugh. [547] She had landed hard in sand a mile out, alive and swearing with joy. [561] Sal grabbed his broom and first aid kit and ran without feeling his feet. [575] He found her tangled in bright fabric beside the smoking hull, grinning through tears. [589] I followed your light, she said, that little yellow sun on the hill. [602] He bandaged her ankle and swept sharp debris away while they waited for dawn. [616] They sat together in flourishing stillness as the sky paled and the last meteors faded. [631] She asked why a man with such grace cleaned floors instead of flying ships. [645] Someone must tend the ground for those who fall, he said, and honor both dust and fire. [662] When rescue came he returned to his dome, tired and strangely lifted. [674] He logged the night with clean lines and marked her path as a bright star. [689] He understood then that sweeping and saving were the same gentle work. [701] At sunrise he turned off his lamp and swept the steps in peaceful silence. [715]</story>
