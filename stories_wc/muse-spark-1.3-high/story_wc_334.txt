<story>Mara Ellis had always been a hands-on folklorist, the kind who learned stories with soil under her nails. [18] She was chronically curious since childhood and could never pass a locked gate or an unfinished legend without prying it open. [39] When she was nine, her grandmother rowed her each October to the sunken orchard greenhouse ringed by shipwrecks. [57] The glasshouse had settled deep into the estuary, its apple trees rooting through cracked panes while old hulls stood guard around it. [79] Inside, in the damp light, her grandmother taught her to listen for orchard ghosts and to leave cider for the wreck crews. [101] One autumn they found a perfect circle of pale caps growing through the flooded aisle, a fairy ring mushroom colony breathing mist. [123] Her grandmother warned her never to step inside the ring, for it kept what it was given and returned it changed. [144] Mara stepped in anyway, laughed at the cold tingle, and made a wish to remember everything forever. [161] She understood decades later that was the day her memories began to thin. [174] She became a scholar of coastal tales, recording shipwreck ballads in notebooks that never quite filled the gaps in her own past. [196] Names of childhood friends faded, her grandmother's voice flattened to script, and whole Octobers went missing like pulled teeth. [215] Doctors called it stress, but the old women in the harbor said the ring had taken its price and held her Octobers hostage. [238] Mara returned to the estuary when habits break, in that raw spring after her grandmother died and her morning tea went untasted. [260] The ferry no longer ran, so she poled a borrowed skiff past the leaning masts toward the drowned glass walls. [280] The greenhouse sagged lower now, apples bobbing among shattered roof beams and barnacled ribs of wrecked schooners. [297] Salt had eaten the door, but the fairy ring mushroom circle remained, wider and whiter, pulsing faintly in the brackish water. [318] Her motive was simple and fierce, to recover stolen memories she had traded unknowingly as a child. [335] She did not pray or dig, but set her lantern down and prepared to detect what the ring had buried. [355] Above her the greenhouse roof had collapsed into jagged panes that caught starlight even at dusk, a map of broken constellations. [376] She would work by studying broken constellations in the glass, matching each shard to the harbor charts her grandmother once drew. [397] A crisp nostalgia settled over her as she recognized Orion's belt snapped across three panes above the central apple tree. [417] The tree was older, its bark tattooed with initials she did not remember carving but knew in her bones were hers. [438] She pressed her palm to the letters and felt the tingle return, sour and sweet like unripe cider. [456] Kneeling at the ring's edge, she logged each mushroom cap, each spore print, each tide mark in her field book. [476] Practical as ever, she tasted salt on a fallen apple, rubbed moss between fingers, and whispered the old offering rhyme aloud. [497] The water stilled, and in the glass shards she began to detect faint movements, not reflections but replays. [515] A small girl in a yellow slicker stepped into the mushroom circle while an old woman laughed and raised a lantern high. [537] Mara watched herself vanish for a breath, then reappear with eyes bright and strangely empty, as if rinsed. [555] Each shard showed another stolen October, her grandmother rowing, singing, tying red yarn to the apple branches for luck. [574] She understood the bargain now, memory for wonder, and how the ring had fed on her brightest seasons. [592] With steady hands she harvested one outer mushroom, thanked the circle, and set it on the central root like an offering reversed. [614] She spoke her grandmother's name into the water, listed every ballad she had saved for others, and asked for her own song back. [637] The ring shivered, the panes flared, and the broken constellations aligned into a clear path of light across the flood. [657] Memory rushed in with the smell of cider and kerosene, the cold oar handle, the yarn rough in her small fist. [678] She wept without sound, not from loss but from return, as Octobers stacked warm and crisp inside her chest. [697] At dawn she poled home lighter, her field book damp but full, carrying spores and stories together in sealed jars. [717]</story>
