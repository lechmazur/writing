<story>Tomas Reyes was a thoughtful gravestone carver who listened longer than he chiseled. [13] After the river road collapsed and took eleven neighbors in one night, the town asked him to discover the shape of collective grief. [36] He did not know how grief could have a shape until he began to sit with each family and hear what remained unsaid. [59] Fiercely meditative by habit, he woke before light and breathed with his hands flat on cold stone. [76] For epitaphs he relied on a family custom of trading favorite poems at dinner, asking each mourner to bring one line that the dead had loved. [102] In that way no stone carried only his voice. [111] Across the butterfly migration that autumn, orange wings settled over Greyrock like slow ash. [125] The survivors could not sleep, and Tomas could not carve fast enough to keep pace with their need. [143] Each evening he walked to the ancient greenhouse at dusk, where glass panes sagged and vines held the roof together. [163] Inside, the air stayed warm and damp, and thousands of monarchs hung from the iron ribs in thundering calm. [182] He went there to retrace the last walk his brother had taken before the collapse, step for step, breath for breath. [203] His brother Mateo had tended the greenhouse and known every cracked pane by sound. [217] On the third evening, as light thinned to copper, Tomas heard a tapping that matched his own hammer rhythm. [236] In the far row, half hidden by milkweed, stood Old Ana, the carver who had taught him letters before she died in spring. [259] Her apron was dusted with marble powder, and her eyes were patient as ever. [273] You are counting stones instead of listening to them, she said without moving her mouth. [288] Tomas did not startle, because grief had already thinned the wall between living and dead. [303] What began that night became his spectral mentorship, a season of lessons held only when the light failed. [321] Ana taught him to read a name until the letters stopped being orders and became weather. [337] She taught him to leave blank space for what families could not yet say. [351] She taught him to carry a woven ribbon roll and let each mourner choose a color for remembrance. [369] He tied a length to every temporary marker, so wind and wings moved with the names. [385] Red for anger, blue for lullabies, yellow for kitchen laughter, white for unspoken apology. [399] Soon the field of fresh graves looked like the greenhouse interior, fluttering and bright against gray earth. [416] Tomas brought the poem lines to Ana each dusk and read them aloud among the butterflies. [432] She corrected nothing but asked which line trembled most in his chest. [444] That line, she said, belongs nearest the name. [452] Midway through the migration, a mother named Ruth could not choose any words for her twin sons. [469] She sat silent through dinner while others traded verses, clutching an empty cup. [482] Tomas remembered Ana and did not rush her, only tied two white ribbons to the small wooden crosses. [500] He returned to the greenhouse and confessed he feared his chisel would lie. [513] Ana touched his forehead with cold fingers and told him silence was also an epitaph if given shape. [531] The next day he carved a single shallow wave under their names and left the rest bare. [548] Ruth wept and pressed her palm to the stone, saying it looked like breath held between waters. [565] Others came to see, and soon many families asked for less, not more. [578] Tomas understood then that collective grief was not a monument but a migration, many fragile lives moving together without touching. [598] When the last monarchs lifted from the greenhouse glass, Ana did not appear. [611] He waited through dusk into dark, listening to thunder roll far beyond the hills yet feeling utterly still inside. [630] On the workbench she had left her own hammer and a fresh length of yellow ribbon. [646] He tied it to the greenhouse door, and the wind carried its bright end toward the quarry where new stone waited. [667] Tomas lifted his tools and walked home steady, ready to listen again.
