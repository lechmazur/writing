<story>Three years after the empathy virus burned through the cities, Ilsa kept to the ravine. [15] Storms had carved its walls steep and slick, but old farmers had hung an orchard there on cables and terraces. [35] Apple roots clutched the air, and mist moved beneath the fruit like a slow river. [50] Ilsa had not come for apples, but for the black truffles that swelled in the damp soil below. [68] She was a rational truffle forager by training, a woman who weighed soil pH and spore counts before prayer. [87] The virus had left every survivor involuntarily truthful, unable to soften even the smallest cruelty. [102] A mother would tell her child his singing hurt her head, and a lover would confess disgust while still holding hands. [123] Ilsa loved truth, but she had watched honesty flay her camp raw until no one slept. [139] She wanted to cultivate psychological safety, a place where candor did not have to wound. [154] So she built low canvas shelters under the hanging orchard and taught the rule of pause. [170] Anyone shaking with a hard truth could lift a hand and be granted silence instead of reply. [187] In her pack she carried an antique glass eye, pale blue with a cracked vein of gold. [204] It was a smuggled relic from the flooded museum at Lowell, said to be the eye of Saint Oda. [223] Ilsa did not believe in saints, but she had carried it out under her coat when the guards burned books. [243] The old woman who gave it to her whispered that Oda could hear confessions without flinching. [259] At night in the orchard, Ilsa set the eye on a wooden box and let frightened people speak toward it. [279] Talking to glass felt safer than facing another raw and bleeding face. [291] Meanwhile she worked on the deeper problem, searching for a chemical mercy for overlit nerves. [306] She followed leads through rumored cures in discredited textbooks, mildewed mycology guides the council had banned. [322] One text claimed that black truffle extract, steeped with orchard bark, could blunt the virus speech fire. [339] Another swore that darkness itself healed, if a patient sat blindfolded through a storm. [353] Ilsa tested both claims with scales and notes, refusing hope without measurement. [365] She dug truffles at dawn, her pig snorting through leaf rot while cables swayed above. [380] When the wind rose, whole rows of hanging apples swung and knocked like dull bells. [395] Rain found every seam in her coat, yet she marked each find with steady hands. [410] The crucial trial came when young Tomas blurted that he wished his injured sister dead. [425] He wept and tried to swallow the words, but the virus would not let him lie or stop. [443] His sister stared, and the camp held its breath against the coming break. [456] Ilsa knelt and gently placed her palm to occlude the glass eye, closing its stare. [471] She told Tomas to speak the rest into darkness, where no gaze could sharpen shame. [486] With her hand shading the relic, she brewed the truffle tea by lantern light. [500] Then she did what the banned book hinted and used cloth to occlude the lantern, dimming the glare to amber. [520] In that soft fracture of light, Tomas admitted exhaustion and fear, not hatred, and his sister softened. [537] The tea did not cure truthfulness, but it slowed racing hearts and loosened jaws enough for kindness to enter. [556] Ilsa recorded the dose, the pulse, the exact words, refusing to call it miracle. [570] Over weeks the orchard ritual held, and even storm nights felt almost graceful, broken but tender. [586] Newcomers learned to cover the eye when shame burned, and uncover it when they could bear witness. [603] No one lied, for lying was still impossible, yet pauses and dimmed lamps gave truth room to breathe. [621] Ilsa kept foraging, rational as ever, testing each truffle bed as if the earth might answer gently. [638] She never claimed the relic held power, only that shared attention could hold people. [652] When another storm battered the ravine, the cables sang and apples fell like offerings. [666] The camp gathered below, truthful and trembling, and found in fractured grace a way to stay.
