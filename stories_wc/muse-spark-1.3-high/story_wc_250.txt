<story>Mara came to the cobalt dock after rain because she could not sleep through the quiet in her apartment. [19] The water held the sky without rippling, and that stillwater tension made her footsteps sound too loud. [36] A man sat at the far piling with a lantern turned low, known along the row as the silent canal guardian. [57] He did not speak when she approached, but nodded once toward the empty cleat beside him. [73] Mara sat because her knees were shaking, and she stared at her hands instead of his face. [90] She had come to say something about her brother Tomas, but the words stopped within the edge of a half-spoken secret. [111] "I told him I would meet him here," she started, and then could not finish. [126] The guardian did not ask for more, and his silence settled around her like a blanket that did not need to be warm to comfort. [151] In his quiet she recognized silent empathy, not pity and not curiosity, just company that left room for grief. [170] He was spiralingly calm as he worked, his hands moving in slow circles while his eyes stayed on the water. [190] From a canvas roll he took reeds and cord, and began to weave a small flat basket for the lantern. [210] Mara watched his fingers cross the damp fibers over and under with the patience of someone mending tide. [228] Across the canal the old ice warehouse stood dark, its tall shutters closed against the night. [244] The guardian lifted his lantern twice, and a boy on the far roof answered by opening one shutter a crack. [264] Light cut across the water in a thin gold line, then vanished when the shutter closed. [280] Again the signal came, and again the answer, creating patterns with shutters that broke the black surface into rhythm. [299] Mara understood slowly that they were practicing, not signaling, learning how to let small lights survive large dark. [317] Her brother had loved that warehouse and had planned to photograph the canals by starlight alone. [333] He had drowned in August trying to swim out for a better angle, and she had told everyone it was an accident. [355] The truth she had not spoken was that she had dared him to do it, laughing, thinking he would refuse. [375] Now the half sentence hung between her and the water, and the guardian let it hang without reaching for it. [395] Instead he drew from his coat a small dental mirror on a long steel stem, pitted with age. [413] Mara frowned, because it was a strange tool to carry beside cord and reeds. [427] He angled the mirror to catch the lantern flame, then tilted it up toward the clouds tearing open above the roofs. [448] A star showed through, then another, small and sharp after rain had washed the air clean. [464] He caught each point in the little glass and laid it gently on the dark water, moving the bright speck as if setting a stitch. [489] Mara realized his purpose was to teach darkness the value of stars, not by banishing night but by showing what it could hold. [512] Tears came without sound, and she did not wipe them because the guardian did not look away in embarrassment. [531] He handed her the mirror, and its handle was warm from his pocket. [544] "Try," his eyes said, though his mouth never moved. [553] With shaking hands she caught a star herself and wove its reflection into the gold shutter lines crossing the canal. [573] The light did not bring Tomas back, but it made the secret bearable to carry another hour. [590] She whispered the rest to the water, not to the man, and the still canal took it without echo. [609] "I dared him and he went." [615] The guardian bowed his head and kept weaving the basket, finishing the rim with a knot pulled tight and even. [635] He set the lantern inside, and the reeds glowed amber, throwing soft squares onto his weathered cheeks. [652] The boy across the water opened three shutters wide, and the canal filled with trembling light like a held breath released. [673] Mara held the dental mirror tight until the edge hurt her palm, then loosened her grip and let the star go back to the sky. [698] She would return tomorrow with food to share, and perhaps in time she would forgive the girl who had laughed on the dock in August. [723]</story>
