Mara had been a vivid mural artist for ten years before she ran out of daylight. [16]
She was quietly driven, the kind of woman who sanded walls at midnight and remembered every crack by name. [35]
In late October she took a commission to paint the inside of an amber pavilion in autumn, a small wooden hall in the botanical garden that glowed like a lantern when leaves fell. [68]
The director wanted seasons on four walls, but Mara wanted something truer, so she began to understand why flowers bloom at night. [90]
Her grandmother had left her a collection of old postcards, each one showing moonflowers, evening primrose, and white jasmine opening under streetlamps in different cities. [115]
On the backs, in fading ink, were notes about scent getting stronger after dark and about grief softening when no one watched. [137]
Mara pinned them above her scaffold and worked with an umbral tenderness, shading petals in blues so deep they felt like held breath. [160]
By day she ground pigments the color of honey and bark, and by night she walked the garden to watch the real night bloomers lift their heads. [187]
November stripped the maples, and the pavilion stood amber and empty while cold pressed against its thin windows. [205]
A priest from the old cathedral on the hill came to see the work and told her the north window held secrets no restorer had explained. [231]
He said that through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window, a painter could see how light had been taught to linger. [256]
Mara followed him up the frozen path the next morning, carrying her postcards in a canvas satchel for luck. [275]
Inside, dust moved in shafts and the saints looked tired, but the north window burned with night flowers trapped inside the glass. [297]
Layer upon layer, the glaziers had etched moonvines and moths, each pane set behind another so the image deepened as she shifted her stance. [321]
She pressed her forehead to the cold lead and understood that the flowers in the glass only appeared whole at dusk, when outer light failed and inner lamps woke. [350]
That evening she returned to the pavilion and painted until her hands ached, adding moths with dusted wings to the east wall. [372]
Winter came early, sealing the garden paths, and the commission was paused until spring, which left her restless and awake. [392]
She rented a small room near the cathedral and spent her days studying the window and her nights writing down what the postcards had never said plainly. [419]
She watched the light change a fraction each morning across the melting of winter's last snow and learned patience from the glass. [441]
The priest brought her tea and asked why she cared so much about flowers that most people never stayed awake to see. [463]
She told him her mother had gardened at night after long shifts at the hospital, humming while jasmine opened, and had died before Mara could thank her. [490]
The postcards had been her mother's, sent from cities where she had hoped to rest one day, all unsent and kept in a shoebox. [514]
Mara had to reckon with the fact that she had mistaken work for remembrance, and that paint alone would not bring anyone back. [537]
In March the thaw softened the garden, and the director called her back to finish the pavilion before the spring opening. [558]
She returned with new eyes, scraping off a whole wall of timid blossoms she had painted in autumn fear. [577]
Instead she painted a long dawning hush, that blue hour when night flowers hesitate and day flowers have not yet decided to open. [600]
She layered thin glazes like the cathedral glass, so visitors would have to move to see the moths and the half closed buds emerge. [624]
On the final night before the opening, she left the pavilion lamps low and invited the priest to see what darkness could hold. [647]
They stood together in silence as the painted jasmine seemed to breathe, and Mara felt her mother close without pain for the first time. [671]
She wept, not from sorrow but from relief, and the priest rested a steady hand on her shoulder until the lamps buzzed warm. [694]
At dawn visitors entered to find moonflowers glowing against morning light, proof that night and day could share one wall. [714]
