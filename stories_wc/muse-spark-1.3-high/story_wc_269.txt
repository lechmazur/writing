<story>Twenty years after the fossil fuel ban, Lena kept watch at Station Nine, a sea level monitoring station anchored off the drowned causeway to Funafala. [25] She had come as a coral bleaching witness, one of the last divers to log the white surrender of the reef before the heat eased. [50] Now her work was quieter, measuring a sea that rose out of habit rather than hunger. [66] The station was moored but never still, drifting a meter on its chains with each tide and settling back by morning. [87] Lena loved that contradiction, feeling both held and unheld, as if time itself had tied a loose knot. [105] On the wall of the instrument room hung an old atlas with a page torn out where her home atoll should have been. [128] Storms had taken the paper years before she arrived, and no one replaced it because digital charts had replaced need. [148] Yet Lena kept the gap, for it reminded her that some paths disappear not because they never existed but because we stop looking. [171] Her particular path was the reef walk her grandmother used at low spring tide, a coral road from hut to islet that no satellite now found. [197] Lena wanted to discover why some paths disappear, whether water alone could erase memory or whether people let them go. [217] Each evening she practiced tapping rhythms on surfaces, knuckles against pipe and palm against hull, listening for change. [235] The habit had started during bleaching surveys, when divers tapped tanks twice for okay and three times for ascend. [254] Now she tapped the station into speech, learning the periodically eternal music of tide and metal. [270] High water sang sharp on the upper struts, low water answered dull on the keel, always returning yet never the same. [291] In late August the gauges began to assemble a strange pattern, a pause at mid-flood that held for eleven minutes each day. [313] Lena first thought the sensor was fouled, and cleaned barnacles with a wire brush until her arms ached. [331] The pause remained, precise as breath, and her taps on the moon pool rim echoed back a fraction late. [350] She called old Tui by radio, who had fished these waters before the ban and remembered every stone by name. [370] He arrived with his grandson in a solar skiff, carrying tools and dried fish and suspicion of machines. [388] Together they began to assemble the old mooring logs, the handwritten tide books, and the stories Tui carried in his head. [409] The work was slow, salt-stained pages spread on the deck while gulls watched like auditors. [424] Tui tapped his own rhythm on the fuel drum, two quick and one slow, the signal for reef close beneath. [444] Lena recognized the pattern from her pipes, and her skin prickled despite the heat. [458] At the next low spring tide they took the skiff out to where her grandmother once walked, following numbers and memory. [479] The water dropped to reveal a spine of coral rock, pale but alive with new polyps, rising exactly where the pause occurred. [501] Lena gasped and slipped overboard with mask and slate, kicking down as a coral bleaching witness returned to testimony. [520] Below she saw not death but patchy recovery, brown and green fingers gripping the old path and slowing the flood. [540] The reef road had grown upward after the ban, learning to keep pace with the sea without fuel smoke to choke it. [562] Its crest tripped the incoming tide for eleven minutes, the exact delay printed on her gauges. [578] She surfaced laughing and crying, tapping three times on the skiff hull so all would know she was safe and amazed. [599] That night back at the station, Tui showed her why the atlas mattered after all. [614] From his oilskin he pulled the atlas page torn out, brittle and stained, saved from the instrument room trash decades ago. [635] On its back, in faded pencil, a former keeper had sketched the reef walk and written walkable at spring low, watch the coral heads. [659] Lena understood the delayed revelation at last, that knowledge waits for water and will to align. [675] The page had been missing when she most needed direction, and returned only when she had learned to read the sea herself. [697] She did not tape it back into the atlas, but pinned it beside the tide screen where work lived. [716] The station kept its drifting moored tone, swaying in place while the world moved on toward repair. [733] Lena kept tapping, not from habit now but from conversation, asking the reef how high it could climb. [751] And the reef, periodically eternal, answered in growth and pause, in loss and return, teaching her which paths vanish and which wait. [773]</story>
