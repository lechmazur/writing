<story>Mara had been called a secretive conjurer since she was twelve. [11] She never denied it because secrecy felt safer than explaining the hollow place her brother left. [27] Jonas had wandered into the cavern orchard nourished by dripping stalactites and had never walked out. [43] No body had been found and no farewell had been spoken which left her with ambiguous loss. [60] Psychologists used that term for grief without proof and Mara hated how accurate it felt. [75] She was gently inventive by nature and built small remedies from wire and peach pits and rain. [92] Her motive had always been to understand the mathematics of emotion. [103] If grief could be measured then perhaps absence could be solved like an equation. [117] The orchard itself was her laboratory and her chapel. [126] Rows of pale trees grew in darkness with roots drinking from mineral pools. [139] Stalactites hung above like slow needles and fed each leaf drop by drop. [152] A shaft in the roof let in the moon on clear nights. [164] Mara visited only after the orchard grows silent beneath the moon. [175] That was when the bats settled and the water stopped echoing and she could think. [190] On the path lay a mosaic stepping stone left by her grandmother. [202] Its blue tiles were cracked and its yellow sun was missing three rays. [215] Mara had memorized the damage and learned to read messages via the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone. [234] Each chip marked where Jonas had stepped or stumbled or paused as a child. [248] She kept a taxidermist's glass eye pair in her coat pocket for luck and for focus. [264] The eyes had belonged to a stuffed owl that once watched over their bedroom. [278] After Jonas vanished the owl had fallen and shattered except for its eyes. [291] Mara carried them to remind herself that seeing was an act of will. [304] That night she placed one glass eye on each of two chipped hollows in the stone. [320] Moonlight caught the glass and threw two thin beams across the tiles. [332] Where the beams crossed she marked a point with chalk. [342] Night after night she repeated the ritual and recorded angles and distances in a ledger. [357] She wanted the scattered sightings of Jonas to dovetail into a single clear path. [371] A farmer swore he heard whistling near the lower pools. [381] A child claimed a boy shared peaches with her by the third row. [394] Mara plotted each story but the lines refused to meet. [404] Her ledger filled with numbers that described longing more than location. [415] She began to understand that emotion did not obey addition. [425] Loss multiplied when unconfirmed and divided attention and subtracted sleep. [435] Still she returned because the work gave her hands something honest to do. [448] One cold night the beams aligned perfectly with two cracks shaped like arrows. [461] Following their direction she found a narrow cleft behind the oldest tree. [473] Inside lay a small cairn of peach pits and a rusted harmonica Jonas had loved. [488] No bones and no clothes and no final answer waited there. [499] Mara sat down hard and held the glass eyes until they warmed. [511] She realized he might be alive somewhere or gone forever and both could be true in her heart. [529] Ambiguous loss would not resolve but it could be carried differently. [540] She placed the harmonica and the glass eyes together on the stone to dovetail past and present. [557] She would leave fruit for whoever wandered and keep recording the moon and the drips. [572] Somewhere beneath grief she felt a buried optimism taking root like a pale seed. [586] The orchard dripped on and the trees breathed and she was no longer entirely alone. [601] She walked home slowly with chalk on her fingers and peace in her chest. [615] The mathematics had failed but the love remained measurable in every step. [627] Morning would come and she would plant another tree in his name. [639]</story>
