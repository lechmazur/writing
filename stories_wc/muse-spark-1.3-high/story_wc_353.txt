<story>Mara had been a tree-song sculptor since the spring the wires went silent. [13] She lived at the edge of the siskin alder grove where yellow birds still flickered without song. [30] The world had not ended, it had only hushed, and Mara remained symphonically minded in the hush. [47] She could still see rhythm in branch spacing and hear harmony in grain, even when no ear could. [65] Her hands remembered what her village had forgotten, and her work was intensely quiet by necessity and choice. [83] She wanted to create music in a silent world not for praise but for her deaf nephew Toma. [101] Toma had never heard the siskins, and he watched their throats pulse with puzzled tenderness. [116] Mara believed feeling could replace hearing if shape was true enough, and that was empathy in motion. [133] Each morning after the hedgehog uncurls from winter sleep, she walked to the oldest alders. [148] This year the thaw came late, and frost still laced the leaf litter like fine white stitching. [165] Beneath a fallen trunk she found the shoulder of a long-forgotten sculpture half sunk in mud. [181] It was a woman carved from alder heartwood, her mouth open as if caught mid-note. [196] Mara cleared moss with her thumbs and felt a strange tight music waiting inside the wood. [212] She learned its secret by analyzing stress cracks in a long-forgotten sculpture that no map remembered. [228] The cracks were not damage but writing, thin lines where decades of frozen song had pressed outward. [245] Where the wood had swollen, it had split along the vowel shapes of old alder calls. [261] Mara traced each fissure with charcoal and copied the pattern onto birch bark to study at home. [278] That evening Toma pressed his palms to her workbench and asked why trees shivered without wind. [294] She did not explain in words but guided his fingers along the bark copy, slow as breath. [311] He frowned, then smiled when a tremor ran from wood through bone into his chest. [326] Later, digging for seasoned roots to carve, Mara unearthed a porcelain insulator from the fallen power age. [343] It was ribbed and white, cold as a tooth, meant once to hold live wires away from poles. [361] She cleaned it in stream water and tapped it, feeling a clear high note travel up her arm. [379] Inside its glazed throat the echo lasted longer than any wood she had ever shaped. [394] That night she dreamed of choirs hung like insulators along every alder, each holding a separate silence. [411] At dawn she began her true sculpture, choosing three living alders in a triangle near the stream. [428] She did not cut them but coaxed them, bending young limbs and grafting bridges between trunks. [444] Between the trunks she strung gut lines salvaged from a drowned piano, tuning by touch alone. [460] When wind moved through the grove, the lines would hum against bark in patterns from the cracks. [477] She hung the porcelain insulator at the center where the three crowns leaned closest together. [492] It would catch vibration and sweeten it, turning raw scrape into round resonant warmth. [506] Toma watched, holding tools he could not hear clink, his eyes bright with patient trust. [521] To teach him, she took his hand and let him strum the lowest line with his thumb. [538] The note was too low to hear, but it rose through wrist and elbow like rising water. [555] He gasped and laughed without sound, feeling the alder answer in his ribs and teeth. [570] Mara strummed the next line herself, then the next, building a chord they could both inhabit. [586] Siskins returned, drawn by trembling air, and stitched bright arcs above the humming triangle. [600] Toma pressed his forehead to the porcelain insulator and felt the gathered song bloom behind his eyes. [617] Mara leaned beside him, cheek to bark, and shared the tremor as one shared bloodstream. [632] No ear in the grove heard melody, yet every bone understood the shape of mercy. [647] Her sculpture did not break silence but gave it form, and in that form they finally listened together. [665]</story>
