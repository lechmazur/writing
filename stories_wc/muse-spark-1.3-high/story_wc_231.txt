<story>My uncle called Tomas Harrow the eccentric lighthouse keeper of Grey Widow Point. [13] He lived alone in the white tower for thirty one years and talked to gulls by name. [30] People said his strangeness was harmless and kept him supplied with oil and bread. [44] I worked the ferry landing that autumn and saw his witnessed isolation every evening. [58] He would come down to watch the last boat even when no cargo was due. [73] He was softly persistent with questions about passengers he would never meet again. [86] On the last Thursday of October it happened at nautical twilight on a cold tide. [101] The sun was twelve degrees below the horizon and the world had no shadows. [115] We stood on the opal dock in mist that turned the lamps into pale moons. [130] The ferry came late with one stranger standing alone at the bow rail. [143] He was thin and gray and breathed like paper tearing in his chest. [156] He wore a dried Edelweiss boutonniere pinned crooked to his wet coat. [168] No one on this coast wears mountain flowers in late autumn for any ordinary reason. [183] Tomas stepped forward and offered his hand with his usual awkward courtesy. [195] The stranger did not shake it but pressed the flower into his palm instead. [209] Then he touched his own chest and pointed to the lighthouse beam above the fog. [224] Then he sat down on a bollard and died before the lines were tied. [238] That was his final gesture and none of us understood it at first. [251] Tomas closed the man's eyes and kept the crushed flower clenched in his fist. [265] After the doctor left he told me he needed to decode a stranger's final gesture. [280] He said the flower had woken borrowed memories of mountains he had never climbed. [294] His younger brother Ansel had left for the Alps forty years before and never returned. [309] Ansel wrote letters full of white slopes and small starry flowers that survive frost. [323] Tomas had read them so often that the Alps felt like his own childhood. [337] That night he asked me to help him investigate the stranger without disturbing the peace. [352] He did it by observing gaps more than facts, the way keepers watch fog. [366] The manifest listed no name for the bow passenger, only a blank line in damp ink. [382] His coat pockets held a train stub from Zurich, a hospital card, and three smooth stones. [398] His wallet held a faded photograph of two boys beside a lighthouse door. [411] One was Tomas at twelve, squinting, the other was Ansel with a chipped tooth. [425] On the back in pencil was written forgive the mountain and tend the light. [439] Tomas stared a long time and his hands shook around the dried petals. [452] He remembered now that Ansel had married late in Switzerland and had a son. [466] The hospital card bore the name Peter Ansel Harrow, aged fifty three, heart failure. [480] The gaps began to close as Tomas laid each small fact beside the silence. [494] The nephew had carried his father's last flower home to the only family left. [508] Touching his chest meant I am Ansel's blood, and pointing to the light meant keep him. [524] Or so Tomas believed, and belief was enough to steady his breathing. [536] He placed the boutonniere in his logbook between storm notes and gull counts. [549] He lit the lamp himself that night and kept it bright until dawn. [562] I sat with him in the gallery and watched the beam turn through thinning mist. [577] He no longer spoke of borrowed Alps as if they were his own loss. [591] He spoke of Ansel as a separate man who had loved and climbed and forgiven. [606] At dawn we buried Peter beside the cliff path with the three stones on his chest. [622] Tomas kept one stone and set it by the lamp where he could see it. [637] He remains alone but his solitude feels tended now rather than abandoned. [649]</story>
