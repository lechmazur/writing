<story>Tomas Alden had worked for eleven years as a labyrinth maintenance worker at Harrow Estate. [15] His duties were unremarkable and seasonal, involving trimming yew hedges, raking gravel paths, and fishing lost coins from the central fountain. [36] Visitors walked the circuits to grieve, to think, or simply to get lost without consequence. [51] Over time Tomas began keeping a small notebook of what they left behind. [64] He called it his ledger of quantified sorrow. [72] On each page he recorded tissues dropped, minutes spent weeping at the center, and whispered names overheard among the hedges. [92] He did not do this from cruelty but from a need to discover what trees witness. [108] Ancient oaks ringed the labyrinth, older than the estate itself, and their roots lifted his carefully leveled stones every spring. [128] He felt certain they absorbed something from every footstep and every stifled sob. [141] His grandfather had been an astronomer, and Tomas kept the old man's astronomer's broken sextant in his tool shed. [160] Its mirrors were cracked and its arc was bent from a fall down stone stairs. [175] It could no longer measure stars, so Tomas used it to measure shadows between hedges at dusk. [192] One October evening a storm rolled over the hills while a thin moon held its ground. [208] Lightning lit the hedges in white flashes, but moonlight persisted, and Tomas climbed to the stormlit tower by moonlight to check for fallen branches. [232] The tower stood at the labyrinth entrance, housing a narrow exhibit corridor lined with maps and dried wreaths. [250] Inside, the wind dropped so completely that at the moment a pin is heard in a silent corridor, he heard his own bootlace snap. [274] He paused, embarrassed by the loudness of his breathing in such stillness. [286] That night the cut across his left palm, earned that morning from yew wire, itched strangely. [302] He stepped onto the open platform and held his hand in the pale light without thinking. [318] The torn skin knit before his eyes, and he watched because it heals in moonlight whenever storms clean the air. [338] He had noticed this before with blisters and nettle stings, and he called such events mundane miracles. [355] They were not grand resurrections, only small repairs the night offered freely. [367] From the platform he could see the whole labyrinth, its circuits glimmering with rain and moon in alternating bands. [386] Lightning flickered far to the east, silent now, turning the wet gravel to scattered silver. [401] Below him the oaks tossed in the wind, their crowns flashing silver and black. [415] Visitors had told him their dreams while he worked, of lengthening hallways and kitchens reached by impossible stairs. [433] Tomas had begun by mapping scattered dreams on tracing paper, overlaying their paths onto the labyrinth plan. [450] Each dream, when drawn, resembled a broken circuit, a wrong turn taken at grief. [464] He realized the labyrinth no longer released people but stored their sorrow in its dead ends. [480] His ledger proved it, for the numbers of tears and lingering minutes grew each year. [495] He descended to the shed and retrieved the astronomer's broken sextant, not as an instrument but as permission. [513] If his grandfather could chart vanished stars with broken tools, Tomas could chart invisible burdens. [528] He walked the outer circuit in the rain, letting moonlight and lightning take turns lighting his way. [545] At the second turn, where the oaks pressed closest and roots rose like knuckles, he stopped. [561] This was where weeping lasted longest, where dreams clustered thickest on his map. [574] Here the hedges trapped wind and voices, and even daylight felt heavy. [586] He understood then that to maintain the labyrinth honestly, he must deconstruct a single wall. [601] Not destroy the pattern, but open a door the original designer had never intended. [615] With shears and a pruning saw, soaked to the bone, he cut a narrow arch through yew. [632] He braced the opening with fallen oak limbs, forming a rough portal toward the trees. [647] Immediately the trapped air moved, carrying the smell of wet leaves and old rain through the circuits. [664] He sat beneath the largest oak and pressed his healed palm against its bark. [678] For a long moment there was only rain dripping from leaves and thunder dreaming in distant hills. [695] Then sleep took him, brief and deep, and he dreamed of all the walkers moving easily outward. [712] When he woke, the moon stood clear above the stormlit tower by moonlight, steady and unremarkable. [728] The gravel paths were already draining, and a tissue dissolved into pulp near his boot. [743] He added no numbers to his ledger that night, choosing instead to leave one page blank. [759] In the morning, visitors found the new arch and stepped through it without instruction, touching the oaks as they passed. [779] They smiled without knowing why as Tomas resumed raking, a labyrinth maintenance worker watching yew sap seal like skin. [798]</story>
