<story>Maya had been called a pocket-sun alchemist since she learned to bottle noon in copper jars. [78] Tonight the air trembled, because the Council had decided to revalue sunlight and sell it by weight instead of wonder. [98] Maya knew what that meant, that her jars would be priced as fuel and her villages would go dark by spring. [119] She pushed through the steam to her old stall, where a cracked lens and a coil of wire waited like loyal dogs. [141] Joren was already there, her childhood friend, mending a lantern with hands blackened by oil. [156] He looked up and grinned, though his eyes held the tired arithmetic of someone calculating loss. [172] They had planned something reckless, born of directed spontaneity rather than any formal rebellion. [226] Maya was scientifically romantic in the way only field chemists can be, measuring miracles while still believing in them. [245] She had discovered that the curl could amplify a pocket sun if the light was passed through it at dusk. [265] It was not just brightness she wanted, but to capture the density of presence that makes a room feel inhabited. [285] A bare bulb could illuminate, but only layered light could convince a lonely child that someone was still awake nearby. [403] One shade for mending nets, one for reading letters, one for sitting quietly without feeling alone.
