<story>Ellis had been the wistful station pianist for eleven years before the tides learned his name. [16] The platform sat inside a tidal arbor above the clouds, where silver leafed vines drank mist and bells marked the breathing of the sea in the sky. [43] Every afternoon the tide rose through the lattice and made the whole station sway like a held breath. [61] Ellis played through it, because music steadied the arrivals and kept the departures honest. [75] On the piano lid rested a broken anniversary clock, its hands frozen at half past seven, its dome cracked from a fall no one admitted. [100] It had belonged to his wife Lena, who once tuned his courage the way she tuned middle C. [118] She died on a night train three winters ago, and since then time had refused to move forward in his chest, though the world insisted. [143] Above the keys hung flickering compasses, a hundred brass dials salvaged from lost airships, all spinning and stuttering whenever the tide surged. [165] The officials said the compasses proved the arbor was drifting off course and would soon tear loose into the blue. [185] The travelers believed it too, and they crowded the waiting room with a steely longing that made Ellis play softer and lower. [207] He was strategically creative by training, a man who once arranged storms into sonatas and rewired signal lights to keep time. [228] Now he studied the compasses between songs and wrote their stutters into his left hand, turning error into rhythm. [247] Late one ebb, a girl slipped from the last car carrying a canister labeled in pencil and eyes too old for her face. [270] She said her name was June and that soldiers had burned the lower archives and were coming up the vine to finish the work. [294] Inside the canister was a gray film reel, heavy and cold, smelling of vinegar and rain. [310] June said the reel held the true charts of the arbor, spliced by her grandfather who built the first tide engine. [331] He had hidden the escape course through hidden sections in a black-and-white film reel so censors would see only a love story. [353] June needed refuge, not for herself but for the memory of the garden routes, and she asked Ellis to project it once. [375] Ellis knew the station master answered to the soldiers and would surrender any map by midnight. [391] Yet something in the broken anniversary clock caught the lamplight and threw it across his hands like a cue. [410] Lena had always said fate loved a rehearsed ending, and the only reply was to surprise fate with an unplanned encore. [431] So Ellis agreed to play until the horizon invites night, when the light would be low enough to run the projector unseen. [453] He seated June inside the piano shell where the strings hummed, and told her to count his false notes as signals. [474] Then he began a long program of departures, waltzes stretched thin to fill the hall with patient smoke and chatter. [494] The soldiers arrived at dusk, boots loud on wet wood, demanding manifests and turning the flickering compasses to the wall. [514] Ellis nodded politely and played louder, folding their orders into a march that made children laugh despite the guns. [533] Between pieces he whispered the count, and June threaded the film through a hollowed music lamp behind the ivy curtain. [553] Frame by frame the wall behind him bloomed, disguised as stage light, showing tide channels and root bridges no official chart admitted. [575] The crowd watched without watching, memorizing turns the way people memorize choruses, humming to cover the click of the reel. [595] A sergeant grew suspicious and ordered Ellis to stop, but Ellis smiled and said a station pianist never stops mid farewell. [616] He launched into Lena anniversary song, the one whose chorus always stuck at half past seven, just where the clock had broken. [638] This time he refused to stop at the fracture and forced the melody past it, inventing a bridge in a higher key. [660] The extra bars told the room which vine to climb when the tide fell and which bell would hide the sound of feet. [683] June wept quietly inside the piano, because her grandfather's music had finally been heard correctly. [698] When the last frame burned white, Ellis closed the lid gently over her, lifted the broken anniversary clock, and set it ticking with a flick of his nail. [726] It was not repair but defiance, and as the soldiers turned, the whole arbor slipped its moorings and sailed on his encore into dark. [750]</story>
