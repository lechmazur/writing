<story>After the great fire, the city kept breathing only through its ruins. [12] In the ancient plaza beneath a shattered sky, ash settled like slow snow over broken fountains. [28] Mara walked there each dawn because she was known as a gentle radical who refused to carry a weapon. [47] Her arms were steel, forged by the relief corps to lift beams and carry the injured, and she had vowed to cradle empathy in arms of steel. [74] She was retrocognitively trapped, able to see the last hours of any place she touched, and the plaza hurt her most. [95] When her metal palm pressed the cracked pavement, she felt children running, merchants shouting, and then the roar as the sky split open. [118] The council called her gift a sickness and ordered her to report only useful memories for trials and claims. [137] Mara disobeyed quietly, bringing soup to squatters, mending coats with wire, listening to the dead without profit. [154] Her aberrant mercy had already cost her rank, rations, and the trust of her brother Joren, who enforced the new law. [175] The law was simple and cold, that no one who had guided the arsonists could be sheltered, fed, or mourned. [195] On the seventh morning, she found a boy asleep behind the fallen minaret, his face black with soot and hunger. [215] He clutched a bright blue fragment, a minaret mosaic tessera pried loose from the tower wall. [231] He told her his name was Samir and that his mother had lit a signal flame that spread beyond control. [251] She felt pity rise in her like heat under ice, a warm coldness she could not explain or refuse. [270] If she turned him in, the council would use him to name others and then cast him to the pits. [290] If she hid him, she would break the oath she had sworn as a rescuer and lose her steel arms. [310] She lifted him gently, feeling how light he was, and carried him to the shelter of the arcade. [328] There she set him down upon a mosaic stepping stone worn smooth by ten thousand feet. [344] She needed to learn what the boy had truly seen, not through questions but via the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone. [367] She pressed her steel hand beside his small bare foot and let the past flood through her. [384] She saw a woman on a rooftop holding a lantern toward the harbor, meaning only to warn fishing boats of soldiers. [405] She saw wind take the flame to dry awnings, saw Samir drag buckets of water, saw his mother push him from the burning stair. [429] She saw the woman kneel in smoke and beg forgiveness from empty air while bells cracked above her. [447] The vision broke, leaving Mara shaking, for the mother had not meant to burn the city, only to save her own. [468] Boots struck the plaza, and Joren came with two wardens, his face gray with duty and lack of sleep. [487] He said the tessera the boy held proved his family had sheltered inside the minaret, which was forbidden to civilians. [507] He ordered Mara to state the name of the child and the place of his mother, so judgment could be recorded. [528] Mara looked at Samir, at his burned hands and his bright blue fragment, and chose to rename him in that moment. [549] She said clearly that he was Nilo, her nephew, lost in the fire and now found, owing no debt to any flame. [571] Joren stared at her, understanding the lie, and warned her that lies to the council meant the stripping of her arms. [592] Mara knelt and set the minaret mosaic tessera back into the dust, as if returning a star to a shattered sky. [613] She answered that her steel was given to lift the fallen, not to point at children, and she would not point today. [635] For a long breath no one moved, while ash fell and the broken fountains held their silence. [652] Then Joren did a strange thing, for he wrote the name Nilo in his ledger and closed the book. [671] He told the wardens the plaza held no fugitives, only kin of the corps, and ordered them back to the gate. [692] Later, when Mara asked why he had spared them, he said her mercy had infected him like a fever he welcomed. [713] She held Samir close as the sun broke through cloud, and the ancient stones seemed to warm beneath her knees. [733] She knew the council would come again, yet she would keep choosing the small living spark over the great cold law. [754] In that ruined place she had found a better use for memory than judgment, to shelter the future. [772] She understood that life after the great fire would demand more courage than survival alone. [787]</story>
