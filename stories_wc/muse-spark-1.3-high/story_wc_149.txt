<story>The alchemical orchard greenhouse shrouded in endless night had not seen dawn in forty years. [15] It stood over the hush of winter-cracked fields where frost held every furrow like held breath. [31] Old Tomas kept its copper boilers alive and its grafted trees dreaming in glass jars. [46] He had inherited both the endless dark and a debt he could not name. [60] Then came the ashen-veil ghost bride, first as fog on the panes, then as a woman. [76] Her veil was grey as cold ash, her wedding ring green with orchard copper. [90] She could not stay, so she spoke through ephemeral cameo appearances in kettles, mirrors, and rain barrels. [107] She would ghost between the rows when Tomas blinked, leaving wet footprints that smelled of orange blossom. [124] Around her throat hung a single carved stone bead, a plum pit etched with two names. [140] Tomas recognized one name as his grandmother's, the orchard's first grafter. [151] I have returned to repay a debt across lifetimes, she told him, her voice thin as ice on glass. [170] Three generations past, his grandmother had hidden a poisoned bride and called it medicine. [184] The bride was her, Elara, wed and widowed in one night of bitter almonds. [198] Her husband's family had been lovingly venomous, kissing her cheek while dosing her wedding wine. [213] Tomas's grandmother had drawn the poison into moonfruit trees and saved Elara's soul but not her body. [230] That was the debt, a life carried forward like a seed. [241] Now Elara taught Tomas the resonant awakenings, songs that hummed in the roots until the trees remembered spring. [259] Each night she appeared a moment longer, humming low beside the alembics. [271] The glass lemons swelled, the copper apples blushed, and buds cracked their wax seals. [285] Tomas learned to answer her hum with his own cracked tenor, feeling his ribs vibrate. [300] With every chord the greenhouse grew warmer, though outside the night never thinned. [313] He worked in mournful elation, grieving the dead bride while rejoicing that fruit might feed the starving village. [331] The village council feared the poison still slept in the sap and demanded the trees be burned. [348] They came with torches that guttered blue in the sunless air. [359] Elara stood full-bodied before them for the first time, veil lifting like smoke. [372] She pressed the carved stone bead to the oldest trunk and sang the deepest awakening. [387] The bark split and poured light, not sap, and the poison rose as white moths. [402] The moths circled Elara and lifted her veil, showing a bride no longer grey but golden. [418] Tomas wept, for he understood repayment meant release. [426] She smiled, lovingly venomous no longer, only loving, and placed the bead in his palm. [441] Keep the song going, she whispered, and do not let the night convince you it is eternal. [458] Then she thinned to mist and did not ghost back, though Tomas called until his throat bled. [475] He planted the bead beneath the oldest roots where the light had pooled. [488] By morning the council's torches had gone cold and their anger had turned to hunger and shame. [505] Tomas harvested moonfruit that tasted of honey and clean rain, with no bitter almond beneath. [520] He carried baskets over the hush of winter-cracked fields to doors that had forgotten hope. [535] Children bit into light and laughed, and old women wept into their shawls. [548] In the greenhouse the trees now hummed without him, keeping the awakenings alive in wood and wire. [565] On still nights Tomas still sees her cameo in a kettle's curve, a bride veiled in ash and starlight. [584] He sings, she answers, and for a breath the endless night feels like dawn about to break. [601]</story>
