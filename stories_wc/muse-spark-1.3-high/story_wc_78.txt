<story>The Leeward tide prophet woke before dawn inside an orchard afloat on a circle of driftwood in open seas. [19] She interprets cloud movements the way other sailors read compasses, and that morning the sky warned of crosscurrents. [37] It was at the blue hour, when water and air share one bruised color and sound travels farther than light seems to allow. [60] Her trees were salt stunted apples rooted in lashed barrels, their branches hung with buoys and bells and broken rigging. [80] She had vowed long ago to turn obstacles into instruments, and every wreck the sea offered became part of her orchestra. [101] Splintered masts became xylophone rails, coiled ropes became drum skins, and hollow gourds became echo chambers for the wind. [120] In her hand she always carried a xylophone mallet worn smooth by years of striking wood and bone and storm glass. [141] That morning a tangle of drift blocked the eastern gap, threatening to spin the whole orchard toward the reef. [160] Most prophets would have prayed or cut it loose, but she heard a low restless euphony humming inside the jammed timber. [181] She climbed barefoot across the slick circle, tapping each log and listening for sketched resonance beneath the bark. [199] Where others heard dull thuds, she heard unfinished scales waiting to be tuned, mapped, and set free. [216] She dragged the largest spar to the center, lashed it between two apple barrels, and strung it with salvaged piano wire. [237] Then she began her method of fractal generating, splitting each tone into smaller branching echoes that multiplied as they faded. [257] She struck once, listened to the overtone divide, struck again softer on the divided node, and taught the wood to remember. [278] Gulls tilted in the gray light, confused by music rising from what had been a hazard minutes before. [296] The tide pushed hard against the driftwood ring, groaning and testing every lashing while she worked without pause. [314] Her younger brother Jonn had drowned doubting her forecasts, and she still played to prove the sea could be answered. [334] Grief made her restless, and restlessness made her precise, because stillness on open water meant being taken. [351] She chalked quick arcs on the spar to mark the nodes, a score that looked more like tide charts than music. [372] When the wind shifted, she climbed a barrel to study the clouds boiling pink over the eastern swell. [390] Low scud ran fast beneath high mare tails, a sure sign the flood would turn early and twist the orchard. [410] She needed a deeper bass to pin the rhythm, so she chose to dub the spar tone with a barrel drum. [431] She smeared fish glue on hide, stretched it tight, and tuned it until it answered the spar a fifth below. [451] Then layer by layer she dubbed and redubbed, building a rolling loop that held steady against the pulling sea. [470] The orchard steadied as the new music gave the crew of one a cadence for rope and rudder and pole. [490] She poled the jammed drift outward plank by plank, striking each piece before releasing it so none left silent. [509] Each freed log sang as it spun away, carrying its tuned note into the fog like a message buoy. [528] By sunrise the eastern gap breathed clear, the circle rode lower but true, and the apples shivered with spray. [547] Exhausted, she sat among roots and wires with the xylophone mallet across her knees, listening to the whole structure hum. [567] It was not triumph she felt but continuity, the knowledge that obstacle and instrument were only tides of the same water. [588] Above her the clouds thinned to gold, confirming her reading, and the sea eased into a patient shining calm. [607] She lifted the mallet once more and tapped a quiet lullaby to the trees, to the lost brother, to herself. [627]</story>
