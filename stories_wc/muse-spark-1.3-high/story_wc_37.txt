<story>I came awake in the mirror world with my name in pieces around me like fallen leaves. [17] The others called me the scattered memory keeper because I kept everyone else pasts and lost my own. [35] Above the beds of my alchemical orchard greenhouse shrouded in endless night, the glass roof showed only stars that never moved. [56] No sun had risen here for nine generations, and the trees had learned to drink lamplight and old stories instead. [76] I tended copper apples and mercury pears that glowed faintly when I hummed, and I told myself they were enough to rekindle a dying realm. [101] Each evening I polished the mica window set low in the western wall until it caught the greenhouse lamps and threw pale sheets across the soil. [127] The old growers had fixed it there to watch for true morning, and its cloudy layers held the last daylight ever seen on this side. [152] When I pressed my forehead to its cool surface, fragments of other lives flickered behind my eyes and would not settle. [173] A grandmother teaching names for clouds to grandchildren who had never seen a cloud lit from below. [190] I carried those borrowed memories in baskets like fruit, and the weight made my hands shake. [206] The villages beyond the greenhouse had stopped speaking to each other, for grief makes people silent and silence makes them strangers. [227] My work, as the elders taught me, was the craft that knits communities together when nothing else will. [245] I did it through forgotten songs learned from the mica itself, for every layer rang faintly if touched in the right order. [267] At first I only sang to keep the trees alive, coaxing sap up with lullabies no living throat remembered composing. [287] Then one lamp dark night I understood that light could be woven like cloth if the singing was exact enough. [307] I began to save each glimmer the mica window released and to twist it with breath and sound into woven dawns. [328] Every dawn I made was small enough to swaddle in both arms like a newborn, warm at the core and trembling at the edges. [352] I would swaddle it tight in undyed wool and carry it down the black paths to a village that had forgotten morning. [374] The first time I unwrapped one over the well in Gray Hollow, people wept without knowing why. [391] Color returned to their faces pink and gold, and the water showed sky for the first time in living memory. [411] They sang back to me then, a verse I had never taught them, and the mica window in my mind cleared a little. [434] Night after night I wove and walked and unwrapped, and each woven dawn lived only an hour before it thinned and tore. [456] Yet while it lasted, neighbors stood shoulder to shoulder in its light and remembered how to ask for help and offer it. [478] I gathered their returned songs and stories and planted them under the orchard rows, where the roots drank them eagerly. [498] The trees grew stronger and bore heavier fruit, and the fruit held more light, and the next dawns I wove burned longer. [520] My own scattered past remained broken, and that was the price I had accepted long ago without bitterness. [538] One cycle, as I polished the mica window, I saw not other lives but my younger self singing to a green orchard under a real sun. [564] I understood then that I had once lived before the darkening and had chosen to shatter my memory to feed the greenhouse lamps. [587] Grief rose in me sharp and clean, not for what I lost but for how long I had carried it without knowing. [609] That night I wove the largest dawn yet, gathering every stored glimmer and every village verse into a single burning cloth. [630] I knew I could not carry it alone, so I called the villages to the greenhouse and asked them to lift it with me. [654] They came with lanterns unlit, doubtful and hopeful, filling the aisles between the shining trees in silence. [671] Together we lifted the woven light and pressed it upward toward the roof, singing all the remembered verses at once. [691] The glass cracked, the mica window sang aloud, and the endless night thinned like ice in spring. [708] For one full hour a true pale morning lay over the orchard and the paths and the upturned faces, gentle and complete. [730] It faded as all my dawns had faded, but this time the lamps were unnecessary because people held the light in their eyes. [753] I felt my borrowed memories loosen and lift from me like seeds, each returning to its rightful owner at last. [773] What remained of me was light and empty and free, a bound release like a kite still held by a loving hand. [795]</story>
