<story>Mara Voss had served eleven years as the bewildered curator of Station Selene before she admitted the archive was lying to her. [22] The station clung to barren rock, save for the astral orchard blossoming on a lonely asteroid, whose silver branches drank starlight and remembered every hand that pruned them. [50] Her duty was simple, cataloguing seed pods and dead letters between the first and last page of the Founders Log, a metal book that supposedly recorded everything in order. [79] But the trees fruited out of sequence, dropping blossoms that recalled grafts not yet made, and Mara began to pursue forbidden knowledge about why inheritance skipped and doubled back. [108] Botany called it nonlinear inheritance, a pattern in which traits arrived from grandchildren instead of grandparents, and the orchard kept it written in sap and pollen. [134] Her predecessor had left one warning carved inside a drawer, do not inquire what the roots remember, and Mara read it every morning until curiosity outweighed obedience. [161] That spring by ship time, one tree bloomed with glass fruit, and inside each globe Mara found a pistachio shell cameo blank waiting to be carved with a face that had not been born. [195] The blanks were simply complicated, smooth as eggs yet etched with microscopic branching lines no tool of hers had made. [215] She cleaned one in the washroom behind the herbarium, and like always she ended up having philosophical breakthroughs in bathrooms where the pipes hummed and the mirror refused to fog. [245] Watching her own clear reflection, she understood the orchard was not growing forward but sideways through time, borrowing futures to repair pasts. [267] The Founders had engineered the seeds to survive radiation by letting descendants donate resistance backward, so a child tree could save its parent from blight. [292] It was brilliant and terrible, because every rescue rewrote the gardener, and no curator could keep a straight catalog when causes arrived after effects. [316] Mara brought a blank to Doctor Ilsen, the orchard geneticist, and demanded to know whose profile the shell was prepared to carry. [338] Ilsen turned pale under the grow lights and whispered that the cameos were meant for the first colonists, carved posthumously so the trees would recognize their makers and obey. [367] Without carved faces, the orchard chose its own heirs, and recent blossoms smelled faintly of Mara, of sweat and citrus and unsent letters. [390] That night the air in the dome felt bright with a brightened disquiet, cheerful lamps buzzing while shadows pooled too thick beneath the grafting benches. [415] Mara could have burned the blanks and filed a tidy report, but the unwritten face pulled at her more than duty ever had. [438] She locked the herbarium door, set a blank under the carving laser, and asked the orchard what it wanted her to become. [460] The branches tapped the dome in a rhythm like a pulse, and pollen drifted down to settle in the etched lines of the shell. [484] Instead of a Founder, the dust revealed her own younger eyes and a mouth set in stubborn wonder, a portrait from a future she had not lived. [511] She realized the orchard had selected her as donor and recipient both, sending hardiness backward into wood that would one day shelter her arrival. [535] Fear rose clean and bright, not dark, as she grasped that carving her face would bind her to tend the trees forever, generation after generation. [560] She fled to the narrow bathroom again, where cold water steadied her hands and the old hum gave her room to think without the orchard listening. [586] There she decided not to destroy the blank nor to surrender to it, but to carve it halfway and plant it with an unanswered question. [611] She etched one cheek, left the other smooth, set the shell among the roots, and spoke aloud her terms for coexistence and care. [634] The orchard shivered, accepted the partial gift, and bloomed in strange new colors that the Log had never named nor numbered. [655] Mara updated the catalog in her own hand, writing that some inheritances move in circles and that stewardship can be negotiated, not imposed. [678] When the next glass fruit ripened, it held no blank but a seed shaped like a small bright key, humming faintly for whoever dared to ask next. [705]</story>
