<story>Kael had been a chivalrous thief for ten winters, stealing only from hoarders who starved the valleys below. [18] He climbed toward the icebound spire above the clouds when the sky is wrong, when green auroras tangled at noon and compasses spun like frightened moths. [44] The spire held the Clockmother, a blind oracle who traded prophecies for memories sealed in wood and bone. [62] Kael carried no gold, only a satchel of hand-carved chess pieces with secrets inside, each hollowed king and pawn holding a folded confession or map. [87] His plan relied on swapping hand-carved chess pieces with secrets inside for her own set that guarded the summit door. [107] He moved with cautious abandon, testing each frozen rung before trusting his weight to the singing ice. [124] Inside his coat, wrapped in rabbit fur, he kept a pocket sundial lid, the only relic of his dead sister Mira. [145] The lid could not tell time without its base, but its etched constellations glowed faintly when truth was near. [164] The Clockmother greeted him by name without eyes, her voice cracking like thawing lakes. [178] You come to amend what you stole, thief, or to steal again, she asked, sacredly playful in the way of old gods and tired grandmothers. [203] Kael bowed low, snow shaking from his cloak, and admitted he had once robbed her pilgrims on the lower pass. [223] I wish to amend that theft, he said, not with coin but with wonder returned. [238] She laughed and set her ancient chessboard between them, its squares frosted and humming with trapped storms. [255] Her pieces were black ice shaped as beasts, and each move she made shifted the wind outside the tower. [274] Kael offered himself as an apprentice of wonder, willing to learn awe instead of cunning if she would teach. [293] Teach me to embrace the unknown with open arms, he said, and I will trade my best secrets for your forgiveness. [314] The Clockmother, sacredly playful still, proposed a game where every captured piece must be opened and its secret spoken aloud. [334] Kael agreed, feeling fear and delight braid together in his chest like rope. [347] He opened with a pawn, setting his carved offerings onto her board one by one as the storm thickened. [366] She answered with a wolf, and the tower groaned as snow struck the crystal walls. [381] By midgame Kael was losing, his kings exposed, but his pocket sundial lid grew warm against his heart. [399] He realized the lid was reacting not to victory but to honesty, brightening whenever he spoke a secret without flinching. [419] When she captured his queen, he opened the hollow base and read aloud how he had abandoned Mira in a blizzard to save himself. [443] Shame burned him, yet the lid flared gold, and the Clockmother nodded with something like kindness. [459] Then she sacrificed her own beast, revealing inside a tiny brass key and a folded note in Mira's handwriting. [478] Kael trembled, for the sky outside split violet and silent, and the sundial constellations aligned with stars that should not exist. [499] Your sister came here before you, the Clockmother whispered, and chose to become wind rather than die unforgiven. [517] The key, she explained, fit the sundial base hidden in the spire, which could turn back one frozen hour if powered by truth. [540] Kael understood his theft could never be undone, only transformed, and he chose wonder over rescue. [556] With cautious abandon he placed the pocket sundial lid upon its base, poured his remaining secrets into the light, and turned the key. [579] The spire shuddered, ice blooming into flowers, and for one hour Mira stood laughing beside him, solid and warm. [598] He did not beg her to stay, but asked forgiveness and told her all the valleys he had fed in her name. [620] When the hour ended she dissolved like breath, and Kael wept without regret as the sky righted itself to honest blue. [641] The Clockmother returned his empty chess pieces, now light as shells, and named him apprentice of wonder in truth, not in bargaining. [663] He descended from the tower no longer merely a thief, but a keeper of sacredly playful mysteries who taught children to trade secrets for stars. [688]</story>
