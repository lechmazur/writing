<story>Lena Reyes had worked three years as an ardent ferry deckhand on the evening run between Harborlight and Cinder Quay. [20] She knew every commuter by coffee order and by the tired rhythm of their footsteps on steel. [37] She stayed resiliently positive through fog and engine trouble and budget cuts that threatened the line. [53] The Meridian was an old vanishing commuter ferry in harbor gossip long before Lena saw it happen. [70] On a clear October night the radar showed open water where the bow lights should have reflected. [87] The engines hummed on but the city skyline simply failed to arrive. [99] Passengers laughed at first and then went quiet as the same buoy passed for the third time. [116] Lena found the captain staring at the wheelhouse clock with his mouth open in disbelief. [131] Both hands trembled sideways across the face when clocks run sideways between tides. [144] She tried to radio Harbor Control but static answered in layered voices that almost formed words. [160] In the lost hour she remembered the empty hourglass her grandmother had left in her locker. [176] No sand had ever moved inside it no matter how often Lena turned it over. [191] Her grandmother had called it a keeper for time that refused to be measured. [205] That night the glass felt warm and strangely heavy in her coat pocket. [218] Beyond the starboard rail a narrow opening glowed inside the cliffs north of the channel. [233] No chart listed a cave there yet water poured calmly into a spectral grotto under starlight. [249] Starlight clung to wet stone and made the arch look breathed into existence from memory. [264] The Meridian drifted toward it without engine command as if remembered by the tide. [278] Lena felt no fear only a sharp professional duty to keep her people calm and counted. [294] She walked the deck with her flashlight and called every passenger by name. [307] Old Mr Chen held his transfer slip like a prayer while twins from the cannery shared earbuds. [324] Lena told them the ferry had missed its approach and help was already moving. [338] Inside the grotto the water held two reflections of every light and every face. [352] She saw another Meridian overlapping their own with a crew that moved half a second ahead. [368] On that other deck she stood too older and tired and reaching outward in warning. [383] Lena understood she needed to merge parallel timelines before the channel remembered only one. [397] The answer came not from instruments but via interconnected patterns she had memorized unknowingly. [411] The commuter footsteps and gull cries and tide slaps formed a rhythm that matched the star flicker. [428] The empty hourglass pulsed with that same rhythm against her ribs. [439] She lifted it high so both crews could see the useless beautiful thing. [452] Then she turned it on its side to honor sideways time and set it on the rail. [469] Light from the grotto bent through the glass and threw paired lines across the fog. [484] Lena shouted for passengers to step in time with the slapping water and to breathe together. [500] The twins clapped and Mr Chen tapped his cane and the engine idle joined the beat. [516] The overlapping hulls shivered and slid toward each other like hands finding a rope in darkness. [532] For one bright instant Lena faced herself and felt grief and hope pass between them without words. [549] Her other self mouthed the name of the brother lost on the last vanished run. [564] Lena nodded and held the line of rhythm steady while tears blurred the paired lights. [579] The grotto flared and then released them into ordinary night with harbor bells ahead. [593] Dawn found the Meridian docked and logged as merely late with every soul accounted. [607] Harbor officials called it fog and faulty radar and Lena let them keep their neat story. [623] She knew commuters live as permanent transient people rooted by motion and ritual farewells. [637] She kept the hourglass on its side in her locker as a private compass.
