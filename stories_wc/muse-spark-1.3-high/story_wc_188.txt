<story>Mara had spent six years walking from town to town as a wandering fireworks designer. [15] She chants while walking, a low counting song her teacher taught her for timing fuses on windy hills. [33] She reached the blooming fortress at dusk, where ivy and wild roses had split the old ramparts into gardens. [52] The gatekeeper warned her the people inside had stopped celebrating, but Mara carried her mortars anyway to defy the gravity of hopelessness. [74] She answered his warning with serious playfulness, promising gloom a fair fight and then a nap. [90] In the courtyard a fire spinner practiced, and Mara traded two red comets for his spare fire spinner's kerosene rag knot. [111] She needed the knot not to burn but to smell, because soaked rag remembered every show it had survived. [130] She could filter the fortress air through ambient awareness, reading damp stone and dinner smoke for how high her shells would lift. [152] Her chant steadied as she walked the wall, marking echoes between the rose towers. [166] Children followed at first, then mothers, then old soldiers who had not looked up in months. [182] Mara let them help, giving the smallest girl the job of sorting willow shells by size. [198] Near the chapel steps she stopped, for a note in mosaic blinked from the floor like a dropped star. [217] It was a single cobalt tile shaped as a musical note, set wrong among saints and vines. [234] An old mason saw her stare and said his daughter had hidden it there before the siege to mark hope. [254] Mara knelt and pressed her palm to it, feeling the town's old vow under dust. [269] She decided then that her show would answer that hidden note with fire. [282] She worked until dark, packing charcoal stars and measuring lift with the spinner's knot held to the wind. [300] The spinner stayed to watch, twirling unlit chains to calm his hands. [312] She chants while walking the firing line, checking each mortar angle twice. [324] The crowd gathered in the rose court, quiet in a way that felt heavier than fear. [340] During the hush of a night with no stars, clouds sealed the sky and even dogs stopped barking. [358] Mara loved starless nights, because her work could lend the heavens back for an hour. [373] She lit the first volley from the mason's daughter's tile, kneeling to touch fire to fuse. [389] Green peonies broke above the fortress, dripping light onto blooming walls. [400] The children gasped, and the old soldiers lifted their chins as if remembering drill. [414] Next she fired a willow curtain that hung long enough to silver every rose. [428] She timed the pauses by chant, letting silence filter back through ambient awareness before each burst. [444] The spinner swung his kerosene rag knot at last, drawing a burning ring around her mortars for luck. [462] Mara laughed with serious playfulness and bowed to him mid count without missing a beat. [477] For the finale she fired seven shells together, each tuned to bloom where clouds were thinnest. [493] Gold and blue split the dark, and for a moment the missing stars seemed less like loss. [510] A boy shouted that the mosaic note was singing, and the crowd laughed and looked down then up. [528] Afterward the mason wept, saying his daughter would have loved the noise breaking the hush. [543] Mara gave the girl who sorted shells a burned out star case for courage. [557] She packed her tools before dawn, leaving the knot cleaned and oiled for the spinner. [572] She left the mosaic note uncovered and swept clean, so feet would polish it brighter. [587] On the road out she chanted louder, carrying the fortress echo inside her song. [601] She did not cure sorrow, but she had loosened its grip for one starless night. [616] And that, she thought, was enough work for wandering hands. [626]</story>
