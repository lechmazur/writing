<story>June had lived three summers in the crown of the old sky orchard. [13] The station was a nocturnal arbor above the clouds, woven from blackwood limbs and salvaged wing frames. [30] By day she slept under canvas, but by night she became a midnight pattern seeker tracing slow lights across the dark. [51] Her work was simple to describe and hard to carry, for she held the watcher's burden of every glider below her. [72] Far beneath the cloud sea, the valley towns trusted her lantern codes to guide harvest balloons and night ferries safe through the upper winds. [96] She was attentively inventive, always mending lenses with sap and wire, always finding a new way to keep the lamps lit in wet wind. [120] On the last night of the storm season, the radio hissed and cracked for hours with no clear voice. [139] She waited on the platform with her knees wrapped in a blanket, listening for a break. [155] Then after the radio static clears, a thin voice came through calling for descent vectors. [170] June answered calmly, giving heights and drift lines while her hands moved across the chart table by remembering through muscle memory. [191] Her fingers found the right pegs and strings even in darkness, because she had set them a thousand times before. [211] Above her the stars burned cold, and below her the clouds lay thick and silent as a held breath. [230] She tracked seven lights moving east, each one a slow pulse against the black. [244] Six held formation, but the seventh wavered low and wide, falling out of rhythm. [258] The pilot's voice shook as he reported a torn harness and a failing left strut. [273] June told him to hold steady, to preserve trust under pressure she kept her own fear out of her tone. [293] She promised she would revise his path to a softer air lane over the arbor's lee. [309] Working fast, she replotted angles with chalk, erasing and redrawing while wind tugged the paper. [324] The great signal shutter that flashed guidance to pilots had jammed the week before, and only a makeshift catch held it. [345] That catch was a parachute buckle, brass and worn, left by the keeper before her. [360] June climbed the wet frame to the lamp house, the buckle cold in her pocket where she had placed it for luck. [382] The wind pressed her coat flat and tried to pull the wrench from her hand. [397] She wedged her boots into the familiar notches and reached for the shutter housing without looking. [413] Frost had swollen the hinge, and the old clasp would not seat. [425] She breathed once and let her hands work the metal as if they remembered an older winter. [442] With a click the parachute buckle locked, and the shutter swung free again. [455] She flashed the new vector, three long and two short, toward the lost glider. [469] For a moment there was no answer, only a soundless echo in her headset where his reply should have been. [489] Then his light steadied and began to climb toward the safer lane she had marked. [504] Tears blurred her vision, but she blinked them away and kept flashing until all seven lights passed overhead. [522] When the last glider slipped beyond the arbor, the night felt suddenly empty and kind. [537] June logged each crossing in the bark book, closed the lamp, and sat listening to vines tick in the cold. [557] She understood then that watching did not mean stopping every fall, only staying awake so others could find their way. [577] She touched the brass edge in her pocket and thought of all the keepers who had waited here before her. [597] Dawn would come soon, and the valley would wake never knowing how close the night had come to loss. [616] But trust would hold, quiet and whole, because someone had stayed to watch.
