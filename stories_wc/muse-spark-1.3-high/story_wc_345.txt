<story>On Marrowlight, an orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches, the harvest never ended because the trees remembered every hand that had picked them. [24] We arrived as evacuees from burned stations, strangers crammed into seed barges, and we stayed because there was nowhere else to fall. [46] It was only after strangers become family that we began to hear the ghost. [60] She had been Orchard Keeper Wren before the blight took her lungs, and death had not cured her of tending things. [81] Unlike the bitter echoes in the fermentation cellars, she was altruistic ghost and patient gardener, strangely trustworthy even to those of us raised to fear the dead. [108] Each night she walked the rows where apple suns glowed amber through leaves, humming to roots to keep the floating arches steady. [130] When the drought came and the sky wells cracked, our new family began to argue about who deserved water first. [150] Wren wanted to orchestrate collective effort, not charity and not command, so she taught us to reexamine thirst as a shared clock. [172] We did not see her at noon, only through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn, white arrows and watering tallies that faded by midday. [197] My brother Tomas laughed at first, saying chalk could not carry water uphill to the high grafts. [214] Then he found the butterfly garden watering bell hung on her old hook by the nursery gate, still dented from the evacuation drills. [237] It was small and brass, etched with moths and monarchs, and when tipped it sang a thin note that called real butterflies from the clover. [262] Wren had loved that bell, and Tomas, who had lost two children to fire, wept to hold something gentle that still worked. [284] That morning the chalk read: ONE BELL, MANY HANDS, TAKE TURNS, and beneath it a map of the thirstiest trees. [304] So we formed lines at dusk, passing the bell from orchard row to cistern, measuring sips instead of gulps, singing while we waited. [327] The children carried drops to seedlings, the elders primed pumps, and even sour old Jax, who once hoarded rations, chalked encouragement for the dawn crew. [352] After a week the floating arches stopped groaning, and the leaves uncurled like small green fists opening. [369] Wren appeared then, faint as pollen, standing where the irrigation lines crossed under the largest arch. [385] She showed us why the high roots were failing, pointing with light through bark to the knotted channels within. [404] We learned to prune and graft together, Tomas lifting, Jax cutting, my daughter Liora binding wounds with wet cloth. [423] The work hurt and healed in equal measure, a brambled hope that caught our sleeves even as it held us up. [444] On the last night of drought, the sky split open above the arches and revealed the nebula of respite. [463] Astronomers had named it for its soft pink calm, a nursery of stars where dust settled instead of burning. [482] Under its light, sap rose again, and the orchard exhaled a perfume so sweet the butterflies returned in clouds. [501] We filled the bell to overflowing and poured it at Wren's empty grave, not from duty but from kinship. [520] Her voice came thin through leaves, asking us to keep the chalk and the turn-taking after the rain returned. [539] Tomas promised first, then Jax, then Liora, who hung the bell higher so smaller hands could reach it. [557] When storms finally broke, we did not scramble for barrels but moved as one body, guiding water to roots and neighbors alike. [579] Now each dawn someone chalks the paths, not because Wren commands it but because we remember how thirst taught us to belong. [601] And if you visit Marrowlight when the arches float low, you may hear a brass bell and smell apples, and feel watched over by love that refused to leave. [630]</story>
