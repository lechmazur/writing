<story>Tomas had been a gloom-hill coachman before the war took the horses and left only the hill. [17] After the reef dies, the bay at Marrowgate turned to white bone and stinking mud. [32] He returned each evening to the blooming jetty at twilight with a sack across his back. [48] The sack held his collection of dried seed pods, each labeled in a hand that shook from old driving. [67] He carried them to let a garden reclaim a battlefield where his last passengers had fallen. [83] The battlefield lay beyond the jetty where the artillery had churned the dunes into black glass. [99] No one else came there because the air still smelled of iron and rot. [113] Tomas, who speaks dead languages learned from his grandmother and from gravestones, muttered old planting blessings as he walked. [132] The words felt right in his mouth, narrow and salted, made for soil and rain. [147] On the third evening, he found a girl waiting where the planks met the shore. [162] She watched the gulls and terns wheel low over the exposed flats. [174] She told him she knew where the guns had buried the dead too shallow. [188] Tomas listened because she guided him by understanding bird calls better than any chart. [202] When the kittiwakes cried short and high, she said, the mud would hold his weight. [217] When the cormorants went silent, the tide was turning back through the dead coral channels. [232] He learned to time his work by understanding bird calls, stepping only when they allowed passage. [248] Together they carried buckets of crushed shell and rotted kelp to fertilize the poisoned sand. [263] To fertilize such ground took patience, for the salt had burned every root for three summers. [279] He broke the pods open with his teeth and worked the seeds into the dark mixture. [295] The girl asked why he kept the names of the dead in Latin and in Old Marrow speech. [313] He answered that a garden needed memory as much as rain. [324] Beneath the low water they found the remains of the submerged concord that officers had sunk in an iron box. [344] The two commanders had signed it the week before the reef dies, promising to share the harbor. [361] Fear had drowned the promise faster than the tide, and the box had rusted shut for years. [378] Tomas pried it open and read the blurred ink aloud without surprise or anger. [392] The girl laid the paper flat under stones so the wind would not take this submerged concord back to sea. [412] That night the jetty flowers opened white and pink around their feet, sweet against the stench. [428] Standing on those narrow boards above the endless flats, Tomas felt a narrow vastness open inside his chest. [446] The planks were only wide enough for one man, yet the sky and ruined water went on forever. [464] He understood then that grief could be both small and boundless like that place. [478] Through autumn they planted beach pea and pine and salt rose where trenches had been. [493] Gulls dropped fish bones and droppings that helped fertilize the rows without any order from men. [509] By spring, green shoots split the black glass and softened the outlines of helmets and wheels. [525] Travelers on the hill road stopped to stare at color where nothing had grown for years. [541] Tomas no longer drove the living to die, but walked the jetty each dusk to tend what lived. [559] He hung his old coach lamp on a post to mark the rows for night birds. [575] In winter storms the garden held, roots gripping ash where tanks had turned. [588] The girl carved the names from the submerged concord into driftwood markers among the pines. [603] Visitors left stones and shells, and even soldiers returned to weep without shame. [616] Tomas knew the reef would not return, but the land could learn a different mercy. [631] Each dusk he blessed the seedlings in dead tongues while gulls answered in living ones. [646]</story>
