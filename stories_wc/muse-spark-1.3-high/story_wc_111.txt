<story>Silas had tended the estate maze for forty years before the Ministry began issuing luck readings like weather reports. [19] He was a withered vine dresser with knuckles like root knots and eyes pale from watching seasons turn. [37] After luck becomes measurable, people stopped praying and started investing, but Silas kept pruning. [51] His maze of yew and climbing rose had once hosted weddings, and now it hosted gamblers hoping to bend their odds by walking lucky paths. [76] Silas was wisely pragmatic about the new science, neither scornful nor awed, only attentive to what roots would tolerate. [95] To prove that gardens know their gardeners, he told his granddaughter Wren, a hedge remembers the hand longer than a man remembers hunger. [118] Wren laughed and rolled her collection of marbles, cracked from years in her pocket, across the potting bench. [136] Each glass held a frozen swirl, blue or amber, and each crack caught the light like a small lightning held still. [157] Silas would polish one every evening while Wren read his luck score aloud from the government post. [174] His numbers ran low that spring, a thin red line, and the estate manager suggested replacing him with automated shears. [194] Silas answered by commemorating small victories elaborately, as his mother had taught him during blight years. [210] When a rose bud opened despite frost, he rang the old brass bell and chalked its name on the shed door for all to see. [235] When Wren cleared bindweed from a single turn of the maze without tearing roots, he baked honey cakes and invited the sparrows in verse. [259] The gamblers thought him foolish, but they paid to enter because his maze bloomed while other estates withered under forced fortune. [280] In truth Silas had practiced a mended deception for months, quietly trading his better luck rations to Wren. [298] The Ministry allowed transfers within families, and each morning he pressed his thumb to the scanner and gave his green surplus away. [320] His own vines yellowed at the edges, his hands shook more, yet Wren thrived and won a scholarship her scores could never have earned. [344] He told himself the lie was mended because it healed more than it broke, like grafting a weak stem onto strong roots. [366] Wren discovered the transfers in June when she found the receipts folded inside his seed tin beside her marbles. [385] You are withering on purpose, she accused, tears cutting lines through dust on her cheeks. [400] Silas did not deny it but led her deep into the garden maze where the oldest vines twisted into a living chapel. [422] There he showed her a graft he had made decades ago, a blooming rose fused to a withering stock that should have died. [445] Look, he said, the top blooms because the bottom endures, and no meter can measure which part is luckier. [464] Wren held up a cracked marble to the sun and watched its flaw scatter color across his weathered face. [483] If gardens know their gardeners, she said slowly, then mine must learn I will not grow from stolen light. [502] That night she reversed the transfer, returning two seasons of luck in a single trembling press of her thumb. [521] Silas woke with his score blooming green and found Wren pale among the rows, her marbles arranged in a careful circle around her. [544] You foolish child, he whispered, kneeling with a groan as thorns caught his sleeves. [558] She smiled tiredly and said some victories should stay small and uncelebrated, or they rot like unpicked fruit. [576] Together they polished the marbles one last time, rubbing oil into each crack until the glass glowed warm and whole. [596] In autumn the maze produced its strangest harvest, roses on blighted wood and grapes sweet despite sour forecasts. [614] Visitors swore the air inside felt both blooming and withering, joyful and mournful, as if time grew there like ivy. [634] Silas retired that winter, leaving Wren his shears and his bell, and the garden kept his shape in every tended path.
