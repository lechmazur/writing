<story>Ilya called herself an esoteric realist, which meant she believed in moss and mathematics in equal measure. [17] She lived transiently rooted in a verdant grotto above the clouds, where a warm pocket of air kept ferns alive on a mountain that had forgotten summer. [44] It was during the migration of the last monarch butterfly, a single orange ember crossing a white sky, that she began her work. [67] She was building temporary structures as signals, small towers of fallen basalt and willow, to honor overlooked histories no archive would keep. [89] Each tower marked a story the lowlands had paved over, a beekeeper in Lisbon, a school garden in Ohio, a moth collector in Kyoto. [113] She worked in blushing stillness, dawn turning her cheeks pink as she moved without speaking among dripping leaves. [131] The air was thin and sweet, and every sound fell softly, as if the world were holding its breath for the butterfly. [153] Ilya charted the wind with knotted strings, learning how gusts curled around the cliff before she stacked a single stone. [173] On the third morning she found a moth wing caught in the moss, barred brown and gold, perfect despite the frost. [194] She lifted it gently, and its lightness reminded her of all the night pollinators whose names had never made the news. [215] For her next signal she chose to knot red thread around two bent willow rods, fixing the moth wing at the center like a sail. [240] The structure shivered whenever the monarch passed overhead, and the wing trembled as if answering a distant kin. [258] Ilya played a small wooden flute to test the wind, but her tune kept breaking into an interrupted cadence that never resolved. [280] She liked the incompleteness, because extinction felt like that, a song stopped one note before home. [296] By noon she had raised seven towers along the ridge, each tuned to hum at a different pitch when the wind rose. [318] Travelers below would see only clouds, but pilots and geese and drifting seeds might read her meaning. [335] That was enough for an esoteric realist, who trusted that real things worked even when no one clapped. [353] In the afternoon the last monarch landed, exhausted, on the tower holding the moth wing, folding its orange sails. [372] Ilya knelt and did not breathe, feeling the old blushing stillness deepen into something holy and alert. [389] She remembered the Kyoto collector who had pinned three thousand moths before burning his labels in shame, and the Ohio children who had planted milkweed in cracked asphalt. [417] She remembered the Lisbon beekeeper who sang fado to empty hives through one long winter. [432] Tears came, quiet and warm, and she let them fall onto the basalt without wiping them away. [449] The butterfly rested for an hour, tasting salt from the stones, while the moth wing turned slowly in the breeze beside it. [471] Day and night, living and dead, migrant and resident, seemed transiently rooted together on that narrow tower. [488] When the wind strengthened, Ilya lifted her flute and played again, deliberately leaving the final phrase open. [505] The interrupted cadence floated across the grotto, and the seven towers answered with hollow humming. [520] The monarch shivered, opened its wings, and rose, catching an updraft that smelled of fern and rain. [537] Ilya watched until orange vanished into white, then carefully untied the red thread and freed the moth wing. [555] She buried it beneath the central tower with a seed, so memory and growth would share one dark place. [574] Wind would topple her signals within a season, and that was the point, for only temporary things are honest about loss. [595] She stood among ferns as clouds closed below like a slow sea, feeling small and useful and unafraid. [613] Above her the sky kept its blushing stillness, pink and quiet, guarding the path where the last monarch flew on. [633]</story>
