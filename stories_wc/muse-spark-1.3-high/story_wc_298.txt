<story>She had always been a reckless idealist, planting seeds where no light should reach. [14] Her name was Ilsa, and she tended the catacomb orchard where tree roots snake between bones. [30] She worked within the glow of half-lit corridors, carrying water through tunnels lined with quiet dead. [46] Above Paris the living argued about wars and wages, but below she listened to roots drinking marrow-dark earth. [64] She was fiercely gentle with the saplings, hoping to patch holes in the soul of the city. [81] Her method was simple and strange, inlaying meaning everywhere like mosaic tiles pressed into mud. [96] She tied ribbons with names of the forgotten dead to low branches, whispering their stories back into sap. [114] In her pocket she kept a burned tarot card, the Star, its edges blackened by a fire she refused to explain. [135] It had survived the chapel blaze that killed her brother, and she read its scar as proof that hope could char and hold. [158] One dusk the wardens brought a man in chains, a cartographer accused of selling maps to smugglers and priests alike. [178] His name was Tomas, and his eyes were tired in a way that made Ilsa want to unmake his sentence. [198] The law would trammel his feet with iron and his future with exile, unless someone bought his debt. [216] Ilsa offered the wardens her winter apples and her spring water rights in exchange for three nights of his labor. [236] It was the old custom of the tunnels, bartered destinies sealed with salt and shared breath. [252] They spat on salt, pressed foreheads together, and for three nights his punishment would hang upon her shoulders. [270] Tomas laughed bitterly and asked why she would risk her clean record for a man already condemned. [287] She answered with dusky resolve that no one was only the worst map they had ever drawn. [304] He worked beside her in silence, hauling buckets past femurs and skulls stacked like cordwood. [319] On the second night he noticed the ribbons and mocked her for decorating death with nursery tales. [336] She did not argue but showed him the ledger where she recorded each grafted name and its small return. [355] Old Mathieu, remembered, had yielded pears that soothed fevers, and little Annette had coaxed white blossoms after frost. [373] Tomas traced the burned tarot card she used as a bookmark and asked if it told her future. [391] She said it reminded her that even burned guides can point, and that she chose to walk anyway. [409] On the third night the roots broke through a sealed niche, exposing a child's skeleton clutching a wooden bird. [428] Tomas knelt without orders and freed the tiny ribs from the grip of wood with surprising care. [445] Ilsa watched his rough hands turn tender, and understood the exchange was changing them both. [460] He confessed he had sold false maps to feed his sister, leading three families into quarry pits. [477] She asked the name of his sister, wrote it on blue cloth, and tied it to the newest sapling. [496] He wept then, not for his chains but for the weight of being known and not turned away. [514] At dawn the wardens returned, and Ilsa felt the iron of his sentence settle cold around her own ankles. [533] Tomas begged to refuse the bargain, but she shook her head and pressed the burned card into his palm. [552] Take my star, she said, and draw a true map for once, one that leads your sister back to you. [572] He left with her apples gone and her name stained, while she accepted exile from the upper markets. [590] Yet in the orchard that spring the sapling bearing his sister's name bloomed twice, red and white together. [608] Tomas returned months later, no longer chained, carrying honest charts of water and root and stone. [624] He had walked the quarries to patch the harm he caused, and his hands were scarred from digging survivors free. [644] They planted a new tree together over the child's niche, setting the wooden bird among its roots. [661] And when the lamps burned low, they sat among bones and blossoms, two debtors forgiven by green growing things. [680]</story>
