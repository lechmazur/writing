<story>After making first contact, humanity did not celebrate but grew quiet and homesick for unvisited places. [16] Mara Ellis was a luminous skeptic who studied attachment the way other people studied weather. [31] She was academically rebellious enough to publish that longing had a molecular signature and a postal address. [48] Her grant required her to walk the new artificial pilgrimage built by Meridian Corp to understand the chemistry of homecoming. [68] She did not believe in engineered transcendence, but she believed in data. [80] The route ended at a rainwashed tower in spring, tall and forgiving under low clouds. [95] Pilgrims were supposed to arrive by shuttle, but Mara came on foot via dusty footprints left on an overgrown sculpture path. [116] The sculptures were saints of obsolete technology, their shoulders soft with moss. [128] Each footprint ahead of her was shallow and deliberate, as if someone had walked while looking up. [145] She followed them because science had taught her that trails converge for a reason. [159] Near the third arch, she found a boy sitting beside a vendor cart. [172] He sold memory objects sealed in resin, and held up a fly amber glowing like bottled sunlight. [189] Inside the gold, a single fly hung with wings spread, forever interrupted. [201] The boy said his grandmother caught it on the last summer before the towers learned to listen. [218] Mara wanted to dismiss it, but her throat tightened at the small preserved weather of another time. [235] She bought it not as a souvenir but as a control sample. [247] Further on, the path widened and other walkers began to converge from side trails without signs. [263] An old nurse carried peonies wrapped in newspaper. [271] A retired satellite engineer carried his dead wife's walking shoes tied together by laces. [285] A teenage girl with shaved hair carried nothing but sang low to keep herself company. [300] No one spoke of the signal that had arrived two years earlier from the cold edge of the system. [319] The signal had only repeated prime numbers and a weather report from a world without oceans. [335] Still, people wept in observatories and wrote letters they never sent. [346] Meridian had monetized that ache with certified routes, stamps, and completion certificates. [358] Mara had come to prove the feeling was mere dopamine and altitude. [370] By noon, the tower appeared above wet birches, its concrete skin streaked clean by rain. [385] The door stood open, and inside the air held a bronze quiet that softened every footstep. [401] Hundreds of paper tags hung from wires, each with a handwritten address of home. [415] Some named childhood kitchens. [419] Some named countries that no longer existed. [426] Some named people. [429] Mara climbed the spiral stair with the fly amber warm in her pocket, annoyed by her own pulse. [447] At the top, a circular room opened to the sky where rain had entered and then forgiven. [464] The nurse, the engineer, and the singing girl stood there already, wet and unsurprised. [478] They had arrived by different roads, yet their breathing fell into the same slow rhythm. [493] The engineer untied the shoes and set them facing east, toward the direction of the signal. [509] The nurse laid peonies in a puddle and watched petals hold rain like small dishes. [524] The girl stopped singing and whispered an address Mara recognized as her own childhood street. [539] Mara felt a sudden chemical bloom behind her eyes, salt and iron and cut grass. [554] She understood then that homecoming was not return but recognition, triggered by shared air and attention. [570] Her instruments would later show lowered cortisol and synchronized heart rates, but numbers missed the softness. [586] She took the fly amber from her pocket and held it to the light, seeing her fingerprint magnified through time. [606] The fly had died far from home, yet here it taught strangers how to stay. [621] Mara hung her own tag on the wire, writing not her lab address but the kitchen where her mother hummed while peeling apples. [644] Descending, she left her certificate unstamped, choosing instead to record the footprints washing away outside. [659] Her final report called the artificial pilgrimage real, not because the tower answered but because people did. [676] Skepticism, she wrote, is also a kind of luminosity when it lets light pass through other lives. [693]</story>
