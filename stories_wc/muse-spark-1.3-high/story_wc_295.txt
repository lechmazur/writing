<story>Mara Reyes kept her post at the celestial mapping observatory long after the town below went dark. [17] She was a lucid dream chronicler, paid to sleep in the dome and record what the night showed her. [36] The directors had hired her to decode temple inscriptions found carved under the old telescope pier. [52] No scholar could read the stone language, but Mara could enter it through variable stars. [67] Each pulse of Beta Lyrae opened a door, each fade of Algol taught her how to listen. [84] On the last night of summer she dreamed the temple as it had been, bright with lamps and low chanting. [104] Priests mapped the sky on the floor while children pressed daylilies between sheets of bark paper. [120] A younger Mara walked among them, carrying a daylily-pressed telegram she did not remember receiving. [135] The paper smelled of sun and iron, and its message was only four lines in her own hand. [153] It said that grief becomes lighter when two people agree to witness it together. [167] Mara read it twice, and when understanding dawns the temple bell rang without any hand softly. [183] The priests turned to her, their faces tired and kind, and asked her to stay and record their names. [202] She took up her brush, for she was one who heals with words when medicine cannot reach. [219] Name by name she wrote them down, and each name eased a line of pain from their shoulders. [237] An old woman showed her the star charts etched on the altar, charts that matched the dome above the observatory. [257] The charts proved the temple had once been an observatory too, built for shared burdens rather than lonely prayer. [276] In the dream Mara understood that the priests had mapped heaven not to own it but to share its weight. [296] A boy with ink on his fingers sat beside her and whispered that he feared forgetting his mother. [314] Mara wrote the mother name in large clear script and promised the boy the stars would keep it. [332] Outside the dream the real dome hummed, the telescope tracking Delta Cephei through thin clouds. [347] Her sleeping hand moved across paper, copying temple signs while her breathing stayed slow and even. [363] Near midnight Tomas, the night assistant, climbed the stairs to check her pulse and found her crying in sleep. [382] He did not wake her, for he knew her work required a gentle midnight solace and not alarm. [400] Instead he placed a blanket over her shoulders and sat to witness the quiet labor of her pen. [418] When Mara woke before dawn, the pages were full of copied inscriptions and translations she had not consciously made. [437] The telegram from her dream lay beside her, real now, pressed with a fresh daylily from the hill. [455] Tomas admitted he had left it there weeks ago, after her sister died and words had failed him. [473] He had pressed the flower himself, hoping the small gift would speak where his voice could not. [490] Mara opened it and found his plain note asking her to let him share the long nights of watching. [509] She realized then that her task was never solitary, that decoding required living hearts as well as charts. [527] Together they compared the dream translations to the stones below the pier and found them true. [543] The inscriptions were not prayers for kings but records of ordinary people who brought sorrow to the stars. [561] Each line ended with the same vow to carry one another, to divide night fears into smaller portions. [579] Mara felt her sister loss shift as Tomas read the names aloud in his low steady voice. [596] His reading was its own healing, a quiet proof that language can hold what hands cannot. [612] By morning they had finished the first wall, and Mara added her sister name to the archive with care. [631] She understood at last that the temple, the dreams, and the observatory were one continuous kindness. [647] She filed the telegram with her charts, the daylily still bright, a sign that shared burdens endure. [664]</story>
