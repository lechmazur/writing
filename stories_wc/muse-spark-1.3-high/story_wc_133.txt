<story>Mara had not slept in eleven nights when the ferry left her on the tidal island with a canvas roll and a tired heart. [24] It was three years after the water wars, and the causeway still showed each day only for a narrow hour at low tide. [47] She was an insomniac painter famous in the inland camps for portraits of people who had lost their wells and rivers. [68] She had come here to thresh meaning from experience, to sort the grief she had painted but never fully understood. [88] The islanders gave her the old coastguard hut, whose walls were damp and whose single window faced the drowned mainland. [108] On the first evening an old woman named Sela knocked and offered salted fish in exchange for a small painted stone. [129] Mara laughed, wryly earnest, and said she had no stones yet, only sleepless eyes and too many brushes. [147] Sela studied her face and pinned an amber brooch to her coat, saying it would keep her visible in the fog. [168] The brooch held a trapped bubble of ancient air and a tiny fern, and Mara felt its weight like a promise. [189] That night the rain came hard, and she could not sleep, so she walked the black rocks by following rain trails. [210] The trails were thin silver lines where water ran down to the sea, marking paths the daylight never showed. [229] She followed one trail around the northern cliff and found steps cut into stone, half buried under kelp and mud. [249] At the bottom lay a flooded chapel whose roof had collapsed long before the wars, exposing a floor of colored tile. [270] In the center, where rain and tide met, there was an unearthed convergence of old paintings, tools, and bones. [289] Broken frescoes leaned against rusted pumps, and fishing nets tangled with frames, as if two histories had collided. [307] Mara stood in cold water to her ankles and stared with haggard awe at beauty that refused to stay buried. [327] She understood that the island had been a holy place and then a fortress, and now it was both at once. [348] For three sleepless nights she returned at low rain, sketching the tiles and the way light moved through broken arches. [368] Her hands shook from want of sleep, yet every line felt clearer than anything she had done in the camps. [388] Sela found her there on the fourth morning and did not scold, only waded in with bread wrapped in cloth. [408] You look for ghosts, Sela said, but these dead left instructions, not warnings. [421] Mara showed her sketches, and the old woman touched the amber brooch still pinned to the wet coat. [439] That belonged to my mother, Sela said, and she painted this chapel ceiling before they took the water south. [458] Mara felt shame rise hot in her throat, and she tried to return the brooch with shaking fingers. [476] Sela closed her hand over it and proposed another exchange, one painting for one true story. [492] So Mara painted through the rising tide, using chapel mud and rainwater and the last of her blue pigment. [511] She painted an older woman high on scaffolding, brush in mouth, while soldiers below capped the wells. [528] She painted herself sleepless among the tiles, small and wet, learning at last how to listen. [544] When the water reached their knees they climbed the steps together, carrying the wet canvas between them. [561] On the cliff top the wind tore at their clothes, and the amber brooch caught the pale morning light. [580] Sela told how her mother hid brushes in the chapel cistern when the water patrols came to draft artists. [599] They wanted beauty for their reservoirs, Sela said, but she gave beauty to the thirsty instead. [615] Mara listened and felt her long insomnia loosen, not into sleep but into purpose, steady and strange. [632] She would stay until the causeway opened again, then carry the chapel story inland as both debt and gift. [651] That night she finally slept for an hour, the brooch on the table beside her, glowing like held rain. [670]</story>
