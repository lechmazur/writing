<story>Mara Ellison worked as a contact tracer for hearts in the mist-clad atrium at twilight. [15] Her shift began only when the dead call, during the twenty minutes when cooling fog met failing light. [33] She was persistently curious, the kind of woman who still asked nurses for maiden names and old addresses. [51] She had taken the job to protect vulnerable voices from being forgotten or fooled after sudden loss. [68] She did the work by tracking faint pulses in a decades-old hearing aid left behind by her father. [86] The aid whistled and warmed whenever a recent death still tugged at a living heart nearby. [102] In her coat pocket she kept the last photograph of her brother Tomas, smiling beside a hospital bed. [120] Tomas had died alone during the spring fever, with no one to name who loved him most. [137] Mara had promised herself no other heart would go untraced like his. [149] That evening the atrium filled with mist and relatives waiting for a whisper. [162] A small boy named Jonah sat apart, clutching a plastic phone to his ear. [176] He said his grandmother called every night and told him to meet her by the river gate. [193] Mara moved to assure him, but the pulse in her aid beat too fast and too cold. [210] A true echo felt warm and uneven, like a hand searching for another hand. [224] This signal was perfect and patient, repeating its invitation without breath or pause. [237] Mara followed the sound across the tiles, pretending to check names on her clipboard. [251] The mist thickened near the old fountain, and the air dropped into reeling hush. [265] There she found a woman in a gray shawl holding three different phones to grieving visitors. [281] The woman smiled and whispered details only the dead could know, then asked for gate codes and account numbers. [300] Mara recognized the trick at once, for con artists haunted the calling hour to harvest trust. [316] The shawled woman was not channeling spirits but intercepting pulses and reshaping them into lures. [331] Jonah stood and started toward the outer doors, his eyes bright with obedient hope. [345] Mara stepped between him and the mist, kneeling so their faces were level. [358] She told him his real grandmother stumbled over words and always sang off key. [372] She played the true pulse through the hearing aid, faint and crackling with love. [386] Jonah listened, frowned, and then pulled the plastic phone away from his ear. [399] He began to cry, not from fear but from recognizing the difference at last. [413] Other visitors turned, heard Mara challenge the woman, and lowered their own phones. [426] Security arrived as the mist thinned, and the shawled woman fled without her devices. [440] Mara had meant only to comfort one child, yet she had shielded a dozen grieving hearts. [456] Later the director called it incidental heroism, but Mara shook her head and filed her notes. [472] She logged each pulse, each name, and slipped the last photograph back into her pocket. [487] Outside the atrium the night air was clear, and the river gate lights blinked amber in orderly rows. [505] Jonah waited by the bench with his mother, no longer holding the plastic phone. [519] Mara sat beside them and explained how grief makes ears eager and signals easy to counterfeit. [535] She promised to help them trace the true cadence of his grandmother through hospital records and letters. [552] The mother thanked her with shaking hands, and Jonah offered her half a river stone for luck. [569] Mara accepted the stone and felt the old aid cool against her skin as the calling hour ended. [587] Walking home through thinning fog, she thought of Tomas and how curiosity had finally become care. [603] She was no guardian angel, only a tracer of missing love, and that was enough to keep listening. [621]</story>
