<story>Ila had not picked a lock in eleven years, not since the night the moon reddened and swallowed her tools. [20] She had been the lunar-eclipse locksmith of Grayport, trusted with vaults and chapels and tired sea captains' boxes. [38] Now she climbed the ice stair to the frozen library above clouds, carrying only a cracked lantern and an old ache. [59] It was under the soft hush of a distant morning, when the sky was pale and the wind held its breath. [80] She came to learn the rhythm of repair, because mending brass had once been easier than mending herself. [98] The door remembered her hands and opened without a key, sighing like weathered boats returning home. [114] Inside, frost furred every shelf and ladder, and the air smelled of paper and winter apples and salt. [132] A driftwood nostalgia settled over her, soft and gray as tideworn wood, for she had shelved books here as a girl. [153] Her father had taught her locks among these stacks, saying every tumbler keeps time like a heart. [170] Now the central reading room had become a hall of mirrors, tall panes leaning between shelves and reflecting snowlight. [189] Each mirror showed not her face but her hands at different ages, small and quick, then scarred and steady. [208] She moved slowly through scraps of prophecy hidden in a library's corner, where torn pages fluttered like moths. [226] The scraps spoke of a locksmith who would return when the moon was thin, and of a clock that had forgotten its song. [249] On a stone table beneath the mirrors lay a clockmaker's gear assortment, brass wheels and springs arranged in careful rows. [269] A note in her father's hand read that only patient fingers could uncap the past without breaking it. [287] The great clock of the library stood silent against the far wall, its face hauntingly bright with trapped moonlight. [306] Its hands had frozen at eclipse time, and frost had sealed the winding arbor like a closed eye. [324] Ila understood then that the prophecy was not magic but maintenance, a reminder left by those who tended time. [343] She set down her lantern and spread the gears by size, listening for the faint click that meant a true fit. [364] In the mirrors her younger selves watched and copied her movements, learning steadiness from her present care. [381] Her fingers remembered oil and winter, the weight of a pick, the patience of turning without forcing. [398] She cleaned the arbor with breath and cloth, eased the rust, and chose a toothed wheel no wider than a thumbnail. [419] When she fitted it, the mirrors chimed softly, as if distant Ila approved across the years. [435] She wound the spring one quarter turn, then paused to hear whether the metal accepted the tension. [452] The clock shuddered and ticked once, a lonely sound that folded into the hush like a footstep in snow. [471] Tears blurred the mirrors, and for a moment every age of her wept together without shame. [487] She steadied herself and fitted a second gear, smaller and brighter, aligning its teeth with tender precision. [504] The tick strengthened into rhythm, and the hauntingly bright face softened as moonlight released its hold. [520] Pages stopped fluttering, and the scraps settled, their warning fulfilled not by spells but by work. [536] Ila laughed, a rusty sound, and her reflections laughed with her, young and old and middle-weary. [552] She oiled the escapement and listened until the beat matched her own pulse, steady and forgiving. [568] Outside, clouds parted and sunrise touched the ice, turning the frozen shelves to gold and rose. [584] She realized repair was never about returning to newness but about honoring wear and allowing motion again. [601] She closed the clock's glass, returned the spare gears to their felt, and bowed to her mirrored selves. [619] Descending, she carried no prophecy, only the quiet knowledge that hands can learn to mend what hearts break. [637]</story>
