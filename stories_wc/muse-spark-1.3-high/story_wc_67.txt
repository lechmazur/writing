<story>The vanishing point gallery was a long white hall where all the floorboards and wires converged toward a single dark door. [21] Mara Ellis worked there as a charcoal portrait ash scatterer, drawing visitors in black dust and then burning each likeness at dusk. [43] She did not keep the drawings because she wanted to crystallize a fleeting emotion rather than preserve a face for years. [64] Each morning she arranged a small exhibit around a mask made from shattered porcelain shards that had once belonged to her mother. [86] The mask caught the skylight in bright fractures and made every stranger pause with a somber marvel at its broken beauty. [107] Mara sat her subjects in an old oak rocking chair and told them the portrait would keep them safe from forgetting. [128] It was the mirage of safety she offered, warm and convincing, and people leaned into it gratefully. [145] She weaves truth carefully, telling each sitter only what the charcoal can hold and never what fire will take. [164] She learns their names and sorrows in brief interviews and then draws fast while the chair still rocks. [182] When the sketch is done she lets them look once, just long enough for recognition to soften their mouths. [201] Then she folds the paper away and promises the image will live in the gallery archive forever. [218] That promise survives through incomplete information, because no visitor stays to watch the evening burning. [233] At closing she carries the day's portraits to the back courtyard and sets them in a rusted barrel. [251] She lights the edges and watches the faces curl, then she will powder the gray flakes between her palms into finer dust. [273] The ash she scatters along the converging floorboards, where footsteps blend it into the grain of the wood. [291] She believes the dust holds something human that varnish and glass could never protect. [305] One autumn evening a boy named Tomas came with his grandmother and asked for a double portrait. [322] The old woman settled into the rocking chair with Tomas on her knee, both trying not to smile too widely. [342] Mara drew them with unusual care, catching the way his hand gripped her shawl and her chin rested on his hair. [363] As she shaded the grandmother's eyes, the woman whispered that her memory was thinning like wet paper. [380] Tomas said he wanted something to hold onto when her stories stopped making sense. [394] Mara felt the familiar ache of her own task, because her mother had worn the porcelain mask to hide a burned face. [416] After the accident her mother had claimed the mask made her invulnerable, and Mara had believed her as a child. [436] Only later did she understand that porcelain could cut the skin it pretended to shield. [451] She finished the drawing and showed it to them, and the grandmother wept quietly at the likeness. [468] For once Mara hesitated to burn such tenderness, and she hid their portrait under her stool instead of folding it away. [489] All night she debated whether mercy meant keeping one face intact while turning all others to air. [506] By morning the gallery felt heavier, and the mask seemed to watch her with cracked and accusing eyes. [524] She seated the next sitters and tried to work, but her lines felt dishonest and her hands shook with charcoal dust. [545] She realized that keeping the portrait would not save Tomas from loss but would only delay his understanding. [563] Safety was not in the paper but in the act of sitting together and being truly seen for a little while. [584] That evening she carried the hidden portrait to the barrel with the others and unfolded it last, as the old rocking chair finally slows to a stop in the empty hall behind her. [617] She struck the match and watched the grandmother and boy darken and lift, feeling grief and wonder rise together. [636] She scattered their ash down the center aisle toward the dark door where all perspectives end. [652] In that quiet scattering she finally understood that love outlasts every image made to contain it. [668]</story>
