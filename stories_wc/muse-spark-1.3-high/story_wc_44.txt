<story>Mara had come to the nocturnal observatory on a floating isle as a budding rune scholar with more questions than glyphs in her satchel. [24] She was quietly deliberate in everything, from how she tied her boots to how she copied a broken circle without lifting her pen. [47] Her teachers at the low colleges had sent her upward to find where shadows go at noon, half as a joke and half as a test. [73] It was an old child riddle, but in the years after spoons bend reality it had become a serious problem for navigators and farmers alike. [98] During the Bending, kitchen spoons had curled like ferns and clocks had run backward, and since then light did not always obey straight lines. [122] At noon on the ground, shadows sometimes stayed put instead of shrinking, pooling under chairs like spilled oil. [140] On the isle, which hung three thousand feet above the wheat sea and turned slowly to follow stars, noon left no shadow at all. [164] The observatory worked only at night, its great brass dome opening like an eyelid to map constellations that had shifted after the warp. [187] Mara was assigned to dust the archives and relearn the first hundred runes, because the old meanings no longer held. [207] A hearth rune that once meant warm now meant stuck, and a river rune meant echo if drawn counterclockwise. [226] Her master, Old Pell, told her she could only correct them by closing loops, finishing each broken circle with her own breath steady. [249] He gave her a clockwork automaton key of tarnished brass, its teeth cut like tiny stairs, and warned her never to turn it idly. [273] The observatory was kept by twelve sealed automata, tall figures of wood and copper left sleeping since the founders fell. [293] Pell called their sleep sealed awakenings, for each one dreamed a piece of sky lore and would wake only when asked rightly. [315] Mara spent her first month tracing light charts and learning why noon shadows climbed uphill toward the isle instead of falling down. [337] She measured angles, copied star fixes, and interviewed a cook who swore his ladle pointed north whenever a shadow lingered. [357] At night she practiced runes, closing each loop in one motion until her wrist ached and her lamp smoked. [376] She learned that a loop left open leaked meaning, while a loop closed too fast trapped fear inside the line. [396] In the third week, the isle drifted into a cloud bank and the instruments lost the pole star for six nights. [417] The brass dome could not turn without guidance, and Pell grew thin with worry that they would miss the solstice alignment. [438] He admitted the automata remembered the old sky, but waking one wrongly could shatter its dream and darken the lenses forever. [459] Mara asked permission to try, not because she was confident but because her noon question needed a witness that remembered straight light. [481] Pell studied her for a long moment and set the smallest automaton, a child sized keeper named Tock, before her desk. [502] Its chest held a keyhole rimmed with nine runes, all open loops waiting to be finished by a steady hand. [522] Mara cleaned the plate, breathed slow, and closed each loop with ink mixed from soot and starwater, whispering the new meanings as she worked. [546] Warm became warm that holds, river became river that returns, and shadow became shadow that waits to be called home. [566] When the ninth loop closed, she fitted the clockwork automaton key and turned it twice to the left, as the margin note instructed. [589] Tock shuddered, unfolded its copper eyelids, and spoke in a voice like rain on a roof, asking for the date of noon. [611] Mara told it about lingering shadows and her old riddle, and the automaton tilted its head with something like sorrow. [631] It explained that shadows never vanish at noon but slip sideways into folded light, and after the Bending the folds had stuck shut. [654] To free them, one must open the folds by closing loops on earth that mirror loops in heaven, rune for star. [675] Together through the cloudy nights they turned the dome by hand crank, Tock calling directions while Mara redrew the alignment runes. [696] On solstice eve the clouds parted, noon light far below released its trapped shadows all at once, and the wheat sea rippled dark and bright. [721] Mara watched from the rail and understood that she had not solved childhood wonder but given it a working grammar. [741]</story>
