<story>Elias Wren mended broken clocks in a shop perched over forgotten tunnels. [12] People called him the broken-clock tinkerer, and he answered to it with tired pride. [26] Every timepiece he touched had stopped at 3:07, though each had failed years apart. [40] At first he dismissed it, but when coincidence becomes pattern a craftsman pays attention. [54] His ledgers were full of careful plans that ash and water had always ruined. [68] After his wife died in the greenhouse fire, he resolved to befriend uncertainty in planning rather than fight it. [87] He was uncertainly certain that control was a story clocks told to comfort people. [101] Beneath the ashes of the old botanical house, he believed, something patient was waiting. [115] The city had sealed the ruins, but wind still pushed leaves through the cracked glass roof. [131] One October evening Elias slipped inside through the swirling leaves in a neglected greenhouse grown wild since the blaze. [150] Vines choked the iron ribs, and charred benches stood in rows like blackened pews. [164] Near his wife's favorite orchid table he found a brass disk half buried in soot. [179] It was the admiral's lost compass rose, famed in harbor songs and missing for eighty years. [195] Its face showed thirty-two points, each engraved with tides instead of mere directions. [208] The needle spun freely, then fixed not on north but straight down through the floor. [223] Elias cleared soot and lifted a grate his wife had once used for drainage. [237] Cold air rose, smelling of stone and rain, and he heard water falling far below. [252] With rope, lantern, and the compass rose in his pocket, he descended into darkness. [266] The shaft opened onto a cavern where a subterranean waterfall thundered over mossy ledges. [280] Mist hung in the lantern light, and the pool below glowed faintly green. [293] All around the pool lay ship bells, sextants, and clock gears fused by time. [307] Elias understood that smugglers had once used the falls to hide harbor loot. [320] The admiral must have chased them, lost his compass, and lost his name to legend. [335] Elias set his lantern down and laid his three stopped clocks in a row. [349] Each showed 3:07, and beside the roaring water he finally heard the reason. [362] The falls pulsed with a deep rhythm that shook loose gears every night at that hour. [378] It was no curse, only vibration traveling up through rock and old foundations. [391] He laughed aloud, because careful planning could never have predicted such a humble cause. [405] His rope was frayed and his lantern oil was low, so he had to improvise a bridge. [422] He lashed broken benches from the greenhouse with smugglers planks to cross the pool. [436] In the center, on a stone worn smooth, lay a watertight tin his wife had sealed. [452] Inside were orchid seeds, a note in her hand, and one brass gear marked for repair. [468] The note read that she planted for a future she could not plan or promise. [483] Elias wept, understanding that love, like water, carves its path without permission. [495] He placed the compass rose beside the tin as thanks, choosing to leave treasure behind. [510] Then he climbed back, carrying seeds and gear, guided by the roar fading above. [524] In his shop he sowed the seeds in pots and fixed the gear into his own clock. [541] He no longer promised customers certainty, but taught them to wind and watch and adapt. [556] When clocks stopped now, he listened first for hidden waterfalls shaking their foundations. [569] And beneath the ashes of his old grief, something green and unplanned began to grow. [584] He kept the compass story for rainy evenings, reminding all that mystery outlasts maps. [598] His plans grew looser, kinder, open to chance and to second chances. [610]</story>
