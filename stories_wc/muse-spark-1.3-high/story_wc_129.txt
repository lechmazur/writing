<story>Sela had come up from the low marshes with bound reeds on her back and mud still caked in the lines of her hands. [24] She was a marsh-reed sculptor by trade, and the hiring council had laughed gently when she applied for work in the high orchard. [47] No one wove reeds this high, they said, not in a crystalline orchard perched at the edge of a shimmering crater. [68] The trees here did not grow leaves but slow glass fruit that rang faintly in the wind coming off the crater light. [90] Sela rented a loft above the cistern and spent her first season watching, listening, learning how to belong without pushing. [110] Her true motive was simple and raw, to graft oneself onto community when one had been cut loose too young. [130] The marsh floods had scattered her kin, and she carried their names knotted into her belt like small dry seeds. [150] In the orchard, people spoke of roots and branches as if families were orchards too, and Sela believed them. [169] She was boldly sympathetic by nature, quick to sit with pickers through night frost and to mend a child's broken kite. [190] Yet kindness alone did not make her kin, and the old growers still called her marsh girl with fond distance. [210] Everything changed on the morning the first black mark bloomed on the trunk of Grandmother Tree. [226] It was a looping tag in reflective ink, there and gone with the light, signed only with a hollow circle. [246] The council called it vandalism, the children called it magic, and Sela recognized it as an invitation. [263] That evening she proposed a shared search for an elusive street artist's tag before the council scrubbed every trace. [282] We will map where it appears, she told the gathered pickers, and we will learn who tends us in secret. [302] The old keeper Joren snorted, but his granddaughter Liss tugged his sleeve until he agreed to walk with them. [321] Night after night the unlikely crew wandered the rows with lanterns, comparing whispers and chalk marks on their palms. [340] Sela taught Liss to twist reeds into snares for light, and Joren taught Sela which crystal limbs could bear weight. [360] Slowly their separate stories tangled into webs of kinship stronger than blood or contract. [374] They found the tag etched inside hollow fruit, painted under ladders, and once high on the crater railing where no ladder reached. [396] Each mark held a tiny record of orchard life, a lost scarf, a wedding, a frost that never came, rendered in quick bright lines. [420] Sela understood then that the artist was archiving them, holding their fragile present for a future beyond the murmur of lost languages. [442] When even words failed, she thought, these bright marks would still speak of hands that pruned and laughed together. [461] On the seventh night Liss vanished near the crater rim, and the search turned urgent and afraid. [478] They found her small lantern flickering by a fresh tag larger than any before, a wide arching gate of dark shining lines. [500] Beneath it stood a shy boy from the cistern crew, his hands black with ink and his eyes bright with terror. [521] He was no vandal but an orphaned pruner who had taught himself to draw so the orchard would remember his dead mother. [543] Sela knelt in the cold glass grass and held him while Joren lifted Liss, who had only followed to watch him paint. [565] Do not scrub his work, Sela pleaded at the dawn council, her voice shaking with a luminescent ache no one could dismiss. [587] Let us instead dedicate a place where memory and craft can meet without fear or shame. [603] The council voted at noon, moved less by argument than by the sight of pickers standing shoulder to shoulder behind her. [624] Over the next season Sela wove her marsh reeds through frames of dark crater glass to raise a glimmering obsidian arbor. [645] It stood at the orchard gate where crater light pooled brightest, half reed and half glass, fragile and enduring at once. [666] The boy, whose name was Temo, was given inks and wall space, and his hollow circle became the arbor seal. [686] On the opening night, Sela hung her belt of knotted names from its center and spoke her kin aloud for the first time in years. [711] Joren answered with his own lost names, Liss added a reed star, and Temo painted them all into one branching tag. [732] No one called her marsh girl after that, for she had rooted herself not by birth but by tending. [751]</story>
