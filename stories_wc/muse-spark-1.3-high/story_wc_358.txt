<story>Mara Kessler had worked as an accident investigator for eleven years before the port authority called her to Bergen. [19] Three delivery drones had fallen in one week inside the towering labyrinth of shipping containers in a port city that never slept, and no one could explain why. [47] She arrived after the town goes silent, when the cranes stopped and the gulls settled and fog blurred the sodium lights. [68] In her kit she carried laser measures and logbooks and a worn flight engineer's patch that had belonged to her father. [89] He had told her that every wreck held a lesson for those willing to look with hazy precision instead of easy blame. [111] Mara wanted to cultivate foresight in action, not just file reports that no pilot would ever read. [128] Her assignment was to survey the container corridors and find the pattern behind the failures. [143] The official theory blamed software, but she suspected air. [152] Her method was documenting wind patterns between buildings, because steel walls could turn a harbor breeze into a weapon. [171] At midnight she walked into the canyon of red and blue boxes with an anemometer on a pole and chalk in her pocket. [194] Fog hung low and her breath made small clouds that the instruments cut through with steady numbers. [211] She marked each corner with chalk arrows and times, recording gusts that shifted direction every few seconds. [228] The containers were rapidly adapting to the night, cooling and contracting and singing faint metallic notes as the temperature fell. [248] Mara shivered and pressed the patch in her jacket for luck, remembering her father tracing airflow over a wing with his fingertip. [270] By two in the morning she had filled six pages with numbers that seemed random until she mapped them. [289] Four corridors funneled wind inward from different sides of the yard toward one central intersection. [304] There the streams collided and rose in a sudden column that could flip a light drone like a leaf. [323] It was a singular convergence, a single point where separate currents became one violent agreement. [338] She stood at the chalk cross she had drawn and felt the pull on her coat from three directions at once. [359] A loose manifest fluttered past her face and then shot straight upward, vanishing into the dark. [375] Her radio crackled as the night supervisor asked if she had found anything worth grounding flights for. [392] Mara stared at her map and understood that the crashes were not spaced in time but aligned with tide changes. [412] When the water rose, the cold air over the harbor grew heavier and pushed harder through the steel canyons. [431] The port had stacked two new rows last month, unintentionally completing the trap. [444] She photographed the intersection from four angles and tied a ribbon to mark the danger zone for the morning crew. [464] Her hands were numb, but her mind was clear with the kind of focus her father had prized. [482] She did not want another pilot to lose a craft, or worse, to trust a chart that lied by omission. [502] At dawn she presented her findings to the tired managers with coffee stains on their jackets. [518] She showed how the winds met and lifted, and how a small change in stacking could break the pattern. [537] They listened because her numbers were exact even though the fog still clung to her hair and boots. [555] The chief agreed to remove one container from the north wall and to reroute all light flights around the crossing. [575] Later, while the yard filled with engines and shouting men, Mara walked back to the quiet crossing alone. [593] The wind now passed through without rising, brushing her cheeks like a normal sea breeze. [608] She touched the patch once more and felt she had turned warning into practice. [622]</story>
