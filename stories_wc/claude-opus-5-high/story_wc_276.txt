The sound wave garden opened only when the patterns broke themselves, and Marda had waited eleven years for that night. [20] She came down the old grade with a rail spike in her coat pocket, cool as the river she fished. [40] Fishers in the valley were superstitious people; Marda was simply patient, which looked like the same thing from a distance. [60] Above her, the constellations had begun to sleepwalk. [68] The Ox drifted out of its yoke of stars; the Ladder folded its rungs and staggered west; the Nine Sisters wandered apart like children let out of a schoolroom. [97] On such nights the garden woke. [103] It lay in the bowl below the dead railway: terraces of stone troughs and copper reeds where sound grew instead of vegetables. [125] Ripples stood up out of the troughs like cabbages of air. [136] A hum of bees that were not bees moved along the rows, and every wall gave back a voice a little older than the one that struck it. [164] Marda walked the rows in a zigzag. [171] Straight lines were patterns, and patterns, tonight, were breaking themselves; anything regular would come apart under her boots and take her ankle with it. [195] Her grandmother had taught her the crooked step before she taught her knots. [208] Her grandmother had also taught her the letters. [216] Marda transliterated emotions into script; it was a small trade and a difficult one. [230] Give her your grief and she would write it out in the old spiked alphabet, letter by letter, until the feeling sat on paper instead of in your chest. [259] Villagers paid in fish and silence. [265] Silence was the part she saved. [271] At the lowest terrace she knelt at the sluice where the goddess had gone under. [286] Ilesh of the Crossing had been a river once, and then a woman, and then, when the railway drove its line across her water, only a sound going away. [315] Marda's grandmother had heard the last of her; Marda had heard nothing, all her life, and had come to catch the echo of a goddess before the garden closed again. [345] She took out the rail spike. [351] It had been pulled from the crossing ties the year the line failed, and it still carried the weight of what it had pinned down. [376] Iron remembers; that is the whole of the fisher's craft. [386] She set the spike upright in the trough as a hook, and for line she used the only thing an echo will bite. [409] She began by voicing silence. [414] It is not the same as saying nothing. [422] Saying nothing is a hole; voicing silence is a shape you push out of your throat with the breath held behind it, the letters of the unsaid pronounced as themselves. [452] Marda transliterated her own want into the spiked alphabet and spoke each character without sound, mouthing iron shapes into the copper reeds. [474] The garden took them and gave them back grown. [483] What came out of the walls was a lullaby with its teeth showing. [496] It was feral, that song: a mother's tune gone a generation without a house, hunting along the terraces, sweet and low and hungry. [519] Overhead the sleepwalking stars slowed and leaned down to listen, the way sleepers turn toward a sound they half know. [539] The spike rang once. [543] Marda did not grab for it. [549] A grabbing fisher loses the fish; she kept her hands loose and her breath even and let the ringing climb the iron into her palm. [574] The echo came in zigzag, like any living thing that does not want to be caught, and she let it come crooked. [596] Then it was in her hand: three syllables of a woman's voice, saying a name that had been drowned under a railway. [618] It was her grandmother's name. [623] Marda sat down on the wet stone and did the only honest thing a fisher can do with a catch that is not food. [647] She transliterated it. [650] She wrote the echo down on the back of her tally sheet, character by character, until the goddess's voice sat on paper and her own chest was quiet enough to hold. [681] Then she pulled the spike from the trough, because iron left standing makes a pattern, and patterns were still breaking. [701] She climbed the terraces in her crooked step while the constellations shuffled back toward their old shapes, half awake, faintly embarrassed, the way anyone is who has walked in their sleep. [732] Behind her the feral lullaby thinned out into the ordinary noise of water. [745] At the top she looked back once, cool-headed as ever, and said the name aloud so the garden could keep a copy. [767] Then she went home to fish. [773]
