Ilve came up the crater road with forty pounds of dried marsh reed on her back, three generations beyond the murmur of lost languages. [24] Nobody in the orchard could name the tongue her grandmother had hummed; nobody could name theirs either, which was the only thing they had in common at first. [52] The crystalline orchard stood on the rim of the shimmering crater, eighty trees of grown glass, their branches ringing faintly whenever the wind came off the glowing basin below. [81] At the center, where the eight founding families had once knelt, rose the glimmering obsidian arbor: a black arch of poured volcanic stone, threaded all over with knotted cords. [110] Those cords were the webs of kinship: every marriage, adoption, and debt forgiven was tied into the arch, so that reading the arbor meant reading the whole town. [138] Ilve had no cord in it, and she had come to make one. [151] She was a marsh-reed sculptor, and the orchard had sent for her because glass cannot be mended but it can be braced. [173] For a season she wove reed cradles around cracked limbs, and the townspeople paid her, fed her, and did not take her in. [196] Kinship there was not given for good work; it was given for shared trouble. [210] The trouble that year was the tag. [217] Someone was marking the glass trunks at night with a sigil of crushed crater-light, a spiral closing into a knot, and by morning the mark glowed inside the crystal where no hand could scrub it. [252] The council called it vandalism; the children called it the town's only honest map; everyone, secretly, wanted to know who. [272] So Ilve volunteered for the night watches, and that was how the search became shared. [287] She walked the rows with Danel the glasswright, with old Suhn who had buried two sons, with the twins who mapped every fresh spiral on a slate. [314] Ilve was boldly sympathetic, the kind of listener who asks the second question, and in the long cold hours the orchard told her things it had never told itself. [343] Suhn admitted he had stopped visiting his brother's tree. [352] Danel admitted the cracks were spreading faster than he reported. [362] The twins admitted they had once tried to cut a cord out of the arbor, to unmarry their parents, and had not been able to find which one it was. [392] Ilve began to notice that the spirals appeared only on trees whose families had gone silent with one another. [411] The tag was not a signature. [417] It was an accusation, and it was also an invitation. [427] On the ninety-first night she caught the artist: Suhn, seventy-four, with a jar of crater-light and hands that shook. [446] He said he had been marking the places where the webs of kinship had frayed, because he could no longer speak the words that would mend them, and because someone had to keep a record. [481] Ilve could have turned him in and earned gratitude, which is not kinship. [494] Instead she spent the winter doing what a marsh-reed sculptor does: she grafted. [507] Reed by reed she wove a second arch inside the obsidian arbor, a living lattice that carried a new cord for every spiral Suhn had drawn, each cord tied to the two names it had accused. [543] She worked in the open so that anyone could watch, and the watching turned into helping, and the helping turned into argument, apology, and eventually bread carried out to the rim at midnight. [576] At the spring ringing, with the crater shimmering below and the glass trees chiming, the town gathered to dedicate the reed arch. [598] They dedicated it to Suhn, who wept, and to the unnamed grandmothers whose languages had gone under, and then Danel took Ilve's wrist and knotted her cord into the obsidian beside her own work. [632] It hurt more than she expected, that luminescent ache of being finally held, the way a graft aches where the two living things are learning to be one. [660] The spirals stopped appearing. [664] The reeds went on glowing faintly, fed by crater-light, and Ilve stayed to tend them, since kinship, like glass, cannot be mended, only braced, patiently, by hand. [691]
