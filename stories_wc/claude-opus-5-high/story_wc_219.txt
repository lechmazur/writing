The visitors never spoke, but in the spring after first contact they built a road. [14] It was not a road for cars. [21] It was a sculpture path, eleven kilometers of bronze figures set in a wet meadow, ending at an old water tower that the rain had washed the color of a coin. [52] Nobody was told to walk it. [58] People walked it anyway. [62] I walked it because I had been thrown out of everywhere else. [74] My name is Ines Bardal, and for nine years I was the department's favorite embarrassment, the chemist who kept publishing on the wrong questions. [98] Homecoming, mostly. [100] I wanted to know its chemistry: what molecules a body makes when it comes back to a place that knew it. [121] The committee called this academically rebellious, which is a polite way of saying unfundable. [135] I carried my evidence in my coat pocket, a lozenge of fly amber my mother took from Gdansk when she left it for good. [159] Resin, terpenes, a fly caught mid-flight forty million years ago, still pointed somewhere. [172] She used to say it smelled like the house she grew up in when you warmed it in your hand. [192] The path was overgrown by April, sedge and nettle swallowing the bronze up to the knees. [208] You found your way by the dusty footprints left on the statues themselves. [221] Pilgrims climbed the figures to see over the grass, and their chalk-pale prints stayed on the shoulders and outstretched arms, a dotted line of human dust running north. [249] I followed it for three days in a bronze quiet, the kind of silence that has weight and a little warmth in it. [272]

On the second day I met Teodor Vey. [280] He glowed. [282] Everyone who had stood inside the visitors' first landing field glowed now, faintly, a cold green under the jaw and at the wrists, and Teodor hated it. [309] He was a luminous skeptic, lit up by contact and refusing to be moved by it. [325] "This is an artificial pilgrimage," he said, spitting nettle seed off his lip. [338] "No saint, no relic, they poured a route and we obeyed it like ants." [352] I told him that ants are chemists too, and he laughed for the first time in a hundred kilometers. [371]

We walked the rest together, converging with others at every crossing. [382] A ferry mechanic from Ostrava. [387] Two sisters who had not spoken since their father's funeral. [397] By the last morning there were perhaps four hundred of us in the wet grass, and the rainwashed tower stood ahead with rain still running off its rivets, smelling of iron and thawed ground. Inside, the visitors had left nothing but a floor of pale resin, soft and warm, that took our footprints and held them. [453] Teodor understood before I did. [458] "It's a collector," he said. [463] "They want the end of the walk, whatever we make when we arrive." [476] He was right, and it was the answer to my nine wasted years. [489] You cannot study homecoming in a laboratory, because nobody comes home to a laboratory. [503] So the visitors had built a destination out of nothing, an arrival with no origin, and let us supply the rest: the sweat, the breath, the cortisol falling, the strange sugars of relief. [536] They were doing my experiment at the scale it required. [546]

The sisters were holding each other by the elbows and crying. [557] The mechanic had sat down in the resin like a man in his own kitchen. [572] Teodor's light had gone the warm color of a lamp. [582] I took the fly amber out and warmed it, and the old resin smell rose, pine and dust and my mother's hallway, and I set it down on the new resin floor, which closed over it slowly, the way water closes. [623] A sample, freely given, forty million years old: a body that never arrived, preserved by the same chemistry that was reading us now. [646] Teodor watched it sink. [650] "You still think it means something," he said, not unkindly. [660] "It means they had to build a home to find out what one does to us," I said. [678] "That's the saddest thing I've ever heard, and I'd like to publish it." [691] We stayed until the light left the rivets.
