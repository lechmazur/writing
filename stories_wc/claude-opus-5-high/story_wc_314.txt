The waterfall was not water so much as light learning to fall, a kaleidoscope hung on the cliff, and Sabra stepped through it the way she had every solstice since she was nine. [33] Behind it lay the orchard that no map would admit: forty trees, ancient and new at once, bark grey as old scripture, leaves wet with a green that seemed invented that morning. [65] She had come for the last measure of the Work, and she had eleven hours. [80] Her auburn hair was tied back with copper wire, which the apprentices took as proof that she loved instruments more than people. [102] They were wrong, though she let them believe it; in her left sleeve she carried the last note her master Oren ever wrote, folded so often the creases had gone soft as cloth. [135] She had never once shown it to anybody. [143]

The crucible she set on the flat stone at the orchard's heart, and the fuel beside it, and the frozen dew crystal last of all, unwrapped from its sheath of moss. [174] It was a single bead of dawn dew from these trees, caught by the waterfall's cold breath and held in ice that would not melt in her palm. [202] Oren had called it the orchard's first opinion of the morning, which was the sort of phrase she had spent nine years pretending to find unscientific. [228]

Everything was ready, and nothing was, because the books disagreed about the moment. [241] Twelve distillations were done; before the final transformation, the crystal had to enter the flame at the one instant when the mixture was neither rising nor settling. [268] Sabra had calculated that instant forty times, and gotten forty answers, each defensible, none true. [283] Logic, Oren used to say, will carry you to the door and then stand there arguing with the hinge. [302]

She struck the flame at the seventh hour, and it caught low and blue under the crucible. [319] The orchard filled with an odor of hot stone and cold apples. [331] Then, as the light behind the falling water broke into its evening colors, the starlings came. [347] They rose off the far hedges in a single body, a thousand birds thinking one thought, and the thought kept changing shape above the trees. [372] Sabra had watched them here as a child and had wept once, for no reason she could name. [390] Now she watched them as an alchemist, and saw what she had been too trained to see. [407] The flock rose, hung, and folded: rising, settling, and then a held beat between the two, a pause the whole sky agreed upon. [430] It was the same interval her mixture wanted, drawn in birds instead of numbers, and it came around again and again, patient as breathing. [454] She had not derived it; she had recognized it. [463] Wonder had touched her first, and the mathematics had only afterward consented to agree. [477] That, she understood, was the whole secret Oren had never managed to write down. [491] The Work failed for those who only counted, and burned those who only dreamed; it asked instead that the two be married, that the measured hand obey the astonished eye. [521]

She took out the note and read it once more, though she had long known it by heart. [539] It said: When the arithmetic runs out, look up. [548] Sabra laughed, and the sound surprised her more than the birds had. [560]

On the next held beat, she dropped the frozen dew crystal into the flame. [574] The fire went white, then went quiet, and the crucible sang one clean note like a struck glass. [592] What lay in it afterward was not gold, and she had suspected for some time that it would not be. [612] It was a small clear stone that held, whichever way she turned it, a shape of birds mid-turn. [630] The final transformation, Oren had warned, is never of the metal. [641]

She sat in the old orchard until the water went dark, holding the stone, letting herself be, for once, exactly as sentimental as she was. [666] In the morning she would go back down and teach the apprentices the ratios, every one of them, correctly. [685] And then she would bring them up through the waterfall, and say nothing at all, and let the starlings do the rest. [707]
</story>
