Mira had not heard a sound in eleven years, and neither had anyone else. [14] The Hush had come the way frost comes, quietly, thoroughly, until air refused to carry anything but light. [32] So she became a tree-song sculptor, which in the old world would have been a joke. [48] She shaped trunks and limbs so that motion could be read as music by anyone patient enough to watch. [67] Her grove was a siskin alder grove on the ridge above the drowned substation, and the siskins there were her orchestra. [88] They swung on the alder catkins in small yellow arcs, and Mira, symphonically minded since childhood, assigned each arc a voice she could no longer hear but could still feel. [118] Feeling was the whole art. [123] Empathy in motion, her teacher had called it: the discipline of watching a body move and letting your own body answer. [144]

That autumn Mira came to the grove to solve a problem she had failed at for three years. [162] Her sculptures moved, but they did not sing. [170] Wind went through them and came out unchanged, and the villagers who climbed the ridge to watch left with the same flat faces they had brought. [196] The answer, she suspected, lay in the ruin at the grove's center: a long-forgotten sculpture from before the Hush, a tangle of alder-wood ribs some ancestor had left to weather. [226] She worked by analyzing stress cracks in it, mapping every split with charcoal and thread. [241] Cracks were memory: each one recorded a force, a direction, a season of pushing. [255] If she could read the cracks, she could read how the old thing had once vibrated, and if she knew that, she could build a shape that vibrated in the range people could feel through their feet and teeth. [294] That was her motivation, plain and stubborn: to create music in a silent world, even if the music had to be made of skin and bone instead of air. [323]

Among the roots of the old sculpture she found the porcelain insulator. [335] It was a fat white mushroom of a thing, threaded, brown-glazed at the collar, fallen from the dead telegraph line that once crossed the ridge. [360] When she set it against her jaw and tapped it, something happened behind her ears that had not happened in eleven years. [382] Porcelain held the shiver. [386] The Hush ate air, but it could not eat stone. [396]

She did not move for a long time. [404] A hedgehog had curled at the base of the ruin when she arrived, a tight brown fist of fear, and the rule of the grove was older than the Hush: you do not work while a frightened animal is watching you. [445] So she waited, breathing slow, letting her shoulders drop into the same rhythm as the alder catkins. [462] After the hedgehog uncurled and went nosing off through the leaf litter, she began. [476]

She cut nine alder saplings and bent them into ribs, and into each rib she seated a shard of the insulator, porcelain teeth set in green wood. [503] The cracks in the ancestor's ruin told her the angles; she copied them faithfully, curve for curve, failure for failure. [523] Where the old sculpture had split, she let her ribs split too, because a crack was only a place where the wood had finally agreed to move. [550]

It took until the first frost. [556] Then she called the village up the ridge and had them lie down with their cheekbones against the roots. [575] She stood at the frame and drew her hand across the nine ribs, a slow strum, the way a harpist greets an instrument. [598] The porcelain shivered; the wood carried it; the roots carried it; the ground carried it into forty skulls at once. [618] Nobody made a sound, because nobody could. [625] But Mira watched an old man's hand open on the frost, watched a child's spine arch like a bowstring, watched the siskins lift together off the catkins in one startled yellow chord, and she understood that she had not brought sound back. [667] She had brought back the thing sound was for. [676] She strummed again, and the grove moved, and it was intensely quiet, and it was a symphony. [693]
