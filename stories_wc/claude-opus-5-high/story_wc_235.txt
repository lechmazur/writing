The first rule of the craft is that you cannot trust a dream to stay in order. [17] Ives Arbenz had chronicled eleven thousand of them, and every one had arrived out of sequence, like mail from a country with no calendar. [41] She wrote them down anyway, in ink boiled from alder cones, on birch bark scrolls she peeled each autumn from the trees behind the mountain ranger cabin. [68] The cabin had been hers since the district stopped sending rangers, and it suited her: one stove, one cot, one window facing the pass where the weather came through like an argument. [100] Her work was simple to state and impossible to finish: to distill chaos into narrative. [115] Dreams arrive as weather; she boiled them down to story, the way you boil forty gallons of sap for one of syrup. [137] After thirty years the wonder had gone jaded in her, though it had not gone out. [153] She could still be astonished; she simply expected it, the way one expects rain. [167]

The trouble began in October, when she walked down to the old burial ground at Kettle Bend to copy epitaphs for a scroll about the dead who visit. [195] Third row, under a wind-bent larch, she found her own grave. [206] IVES ARBENZ, it said, and beneath the name a death date three winters gone. [220] The lichen on the stone was older than the lichen on the stones around it. [235] She stood there a long while, feeling the jaded part of her shrug and the wonder part of her kneel down to read it twice. [260]

Walking home she could not say whether the walk was happening now or had happened, and by the time she reached the porch she had lived that October four times. [290] After that she was chronologically confused in the clinical sense, though there was no clinic within forty miles to say so. [311] Tuesday came before Sunday. [315] Her fire burned down before she lit it. [323] The scrolls kept order when she could not, so she wrote harder, three a night, stacking them in the woodbox like kindling against the dark. [348]

And in the dreams, her past selves came. [356] Ives at nine, barefoot on the ridge road, who could soar over the pass simply by leaning into the wind and deciding to. [379] Ives at twenty-six, who dreamed in color so violent she woke with a headache and called it a gift. [398] Ives at forty, who had not yet learned that a chronicle is a cage you build around something to keep it from leaving. [421]

She found herself getting jealous of past selves, and the jealousy had teeth. [434] It was not the soft, sepia ache of missing a thing; it was feral nostalgia, a wild animal that had once been hers and now circled the cabin at night, hungry, remembering her smell. [468] She would wake with her jaw clenched because the nine-year-old had flown and she had only written about flying. [487] In one dream she snatched a birch bark scroll out of the girl's hands and the girl bit her. [506]

She woke with the mark on her wrist, and that was when she understood the grave. [522] The lichen had been honest. [527] Three winters ago the stove had gone out in a January that took the whole valley, and she had been dreaming ever since, lucidly, doggedly, chronicling a cabin she had built out of habit and alder ink. [564] That was why time would not stack. [571] A dream has no yesterday; it has only rooms, and she had been walking through hers with a pen, taking inventory, insisting it was a life. [597]

A lesser chronicler would have panicked. [603] Ives put water on for tea she could not taste and considered the problem professionally. [618] The jealousy was the error. [623] She had spent three posthumous winters envying children who could fly, when the whole point of lucidity is that you are permitted to fly too, provided you stop taking notes long enough to try. [657]

So she wrote one last scroll, and made it a story rather than a record, because chaos deserves an ending and not merely a tally. [682] It said: a woman lived above the pass, kept the trees' skin for paper, and died warm in her sleep, which was the truest sentence she had ever boiled down. [712]

She left it on the cot, opened the door, and went out into the blue hour where the feral thing was waiting. [734] It was only a girl, barefoot, unimpressed. [741] "You took too long," the girl said. [748] "I was working," said Ives, and took her hand, and leaned into the wind off the pass, and was astonished, and expected it. [771] They soared. [773]
