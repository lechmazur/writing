The kettle went on the burner at four o'clock, and Marta set the ash wood carving blank on her knee. [20] Rain beaded the skylight above her loft, each drop holding a little rust-colored streetlight, and she thought, as she always did, that the roof looked like a sheet of hammered iron left out too long. [55] Teodor had called that light rust-hued peace, back when he was alive to name things. [70] He had been a lion tamer for thirty-one years, and she had been his wife for twenty-nine of them, and now she was his widow, which was a shorter job and harder. [102] People called her hauntingly practical. [107] She had sold the cage bars for scrap the week after the funeral and bought this loft with the money, because iron that had failed once should at least become a roof. [139] She had kept one thing only: the trick he taught her, which he swore he had learned from the lions. [159] A lion, he said, lives inside a single second and makes that second enormous; a tamer must learn the same, or he will be too slow. [185] So Marta breathed out, and the boiling water stopped climbing toward its whistle, and the minute went hard and clear around her like quartz. [209] Inside crystallized time there were facets, and each facet was a day she could stand in as long as she needed. [230] This was how she carved. [235] The blanks were always ash, because ash bends before it breaks, and there were eleven finished pieces on the windowsill already. [256] A tent with its guy ropes snapped. [263] A folding stool. [266] A lioness named Beatrix with one ear chewed short. [275] A whip coiled like a sleeping animal, which had taught her that authority is mostly rhythm. [291] That was the point of the work: not to mourn, but to harvest lessons from loss, the way a farmer walks a burned field for seed. [317] Every figure had cost her a day inside the stone of a stopped minute, and every figure had given her back one usable sentence. [341] She had never carved Teodor. [346] Tonight she meant to, because the blank on her knee was the last of the ash he had cut himself, and there would be no more. [372] She turned the minute until she found the right facet: the last morning, the ring smelling of wet sawdust, Teodor buttoning his red coat wrong and refusing to fix it. [402] "Applause is here and gone," he told her, as he told her every morning, "so don't stand in it." [421] She stood in that morning for what felt like nine hours, watching him, and she did not follow him into the cage, because she had learned in her second year of widowhood that you cannot contain a man by watching him harder. [463] You can only contain what he knew, and that fits in a fist of wood. [478] So she carved him the way he had stood, weight on the back foot, chin down, one hand open and low. [499] The open hand was the lesson; it had taken her four years to see it. [514] He had never gripped anything, not the whip, not the chair, not her. [527] He held things the way you hold water, and that is why the lions loved him, and that is why the one bad afternoon could take him so easily and leave her standing in the sawdust with her hands full. [567] The kettle whistled. [570] Time softened, and went liquid, and ran on, and the rain resumed its small work on the skylight. [588] Marta poured one cup, because one was accurate. [596] She set the twelfth figure on the sill with the others, facing the door, and she did not cry, and she did not pretend the ache was gone. [624] She simply drank her tea in the rust-hued light and thought about the open hand, and about how she would need to buy ash from a stranger now, which was fine. [655] Everything is here and gone; the trick is to carve fast, and to keep the hand open while you do. [675]
