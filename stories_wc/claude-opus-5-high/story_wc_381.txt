Ilse had been the valley's only summoner for nine years, and in that time she had learned that silence has geography. [21] Her haven stood where the alders leaned together beside a quiet brook, a house of lamplight and paper that woke only after dusk. [44] She worked at night because the brook was loudest then, and because what she hunted could only be heard against running water. [66] The oracle of Marrenwell had died in her arms two winters ago, mid-sentence, and the sentence had not stopped. [85] It went on travelling, a prophecy spoken in code, its meaning hidden not in the words but in the gaps between them. [107] Anyone who solved those gaps would learn how the world ended, and Ilse had met enough people to know that knowledge would become instruction. [131] So she had chosen to hush it. [138] You cannot burn a prophecy; you can only give it somewhere quieter to live. [152] Her method was mapping silence. [157] Each night she sat on the flat stone at the water's edge with charcoal and a ledger, marking every hollow in the sound: the held breath between two owl calls, the pause where the brook slid over moss instead of gravel, the half-second lull when wind changed its mind. [206] Over two years the map had grown terrestrially cosmic, a chart of local quiet that matched, hollow for hollow, the spacing of stars. [229] That was the discovery that made the work possible: the gaps in the oracle's sentence were the same gaps, and a silence anywhere could be persuaded to hold them. [258] The vessel lay in her lap: a paper crane the oracle had folded on her last clear evening, its body soft with handling. [281] On the inside of the crane's origami wing, visible only if you unfolded it, the code was written in the oracle's cramped hand -- and Ilse had never unfolded it, because reading it aloud was how the thing spread. [319] Tonight the map was finished. [324] Visitors had come that week, three of them, scholars from the coast who talked amid whispered secrets, trading fragments of the prophecy over her table as though it were gossip. [354] She had fed them, listened, and recorded the shape of their whispering, which gave her the final hollows. [372] They left at dawn, carrying pieces they could not assemble, and she began. [385] A summoner alone has no chorus, no circle to steady her, and so she used what she had: the brook, the map, and a single candle. [411] She set the crane on the water and let it flow. [422] The current took it under the alders, and as it turned she read her ledger aloud, not the code but the quiet around it, calling each hollow by the name she had given it. [456] What answered her was not a creature but a condition. [466] The candle flame stretched tall and stopped moving, burning without flicker or heat -- a tranquil blaze, the kind of fire that has nothing to consume and no reason to hurry. [496] Light lay on the brook like a second surface. [505] Ilse kept reading, and the hollows she named began to join, edge to edge, until the whole valley held one continuous absence instead of a thousand small ones. [533] It was a smoothed hush, and the prophecy, which lived only in unevenness, had nowhere left to stand. [551] The crane spun once in an eddy, opened along its folds, and sank. [564] Ink lifted off the paper in threads and dissolved, letter by letter, into water that made no sound at all. [584] When the brook spoke again, it spoke evenly, and Ilse found she could not remember any of the names she had spent two years inventing. [609] The ledger in her lap was blank. [616] She was not sorry. [620] The candle guttered back into ordinary fire, and she carried it inside, where the paper walls glowed and nothing waited to be told. [643] Somewhere on the coast, three scholars would compare their fragments and find that the pauses no longer matched anything. [662] They would call it a forgery, or a failure of memory, and go home. [676] Ilse slept past sunrise for the first time in nine years, in a haven beside a brook that had, at last, nothing to confess. [700]
