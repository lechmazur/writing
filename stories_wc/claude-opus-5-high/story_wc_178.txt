The old king was dead and the new one had not yet been chosen, so for eleven nights Babylon belonged to no one but the sky. [26] Iltani liked it that way. [31] She climbed the rebuilt palace stair with one lamp, filled halfway, because oil cost silver and silver bought clay. [50] The palace itself had been raised twice: once by Nebuchadnezzar's masons, once again by restorers who had guessed at the missing courses and glazed them in a blue slightly wrong. [80] Iltani approved of the wrongness; it was honest about being a guess. [92] On the roof, her instruments waited: a water clock she had repaired with bitumen, a sighting rod of reused cedar, and a floor of sixty-four tiles she had laid herself, one per month of widowhood. [127] Sixty-three were plain. [130] The sixty-fourth she kept under a square of linen, and did not uncloak it for anyone, not for the priests who came asking whether the interregnum was lucky, not for the general who wanted a horoscope cheap. [167] Under the linen lay a magic square tile: four rows, four columns, sixteen numbers pressed into the clay while it was soft. [189] Every row, column, and diagonal summed to thirty-four. [197] Thirty-four was the age at which Marduk-shuma had died, coughing, in the third year of the old king, having spent his last coherent hour explaining to her why the moon's speed was not constant. [231] The sixteen numbers, read as syllable values in the old scribal key, spelled a sentence she had never said aloud while he lived. [254] This was her economy: one tile, one grief, no waste. [264] Tonight, though, the tile had a second use, and Iltani had waited eleven nights for it. [280] Between regimes, the sky had no royal owner. [288] No king's name was read into the omens; no god had been assigned the month's accounts. [304] Whatever moved the moon unevenly moved it without a patron, and Iltani intended to make a covenant with that, with the unnamed thing her calculations kept touching and failing to name. [335] She was aware of the irony, and empirical about it: an astronomer who refused to buy incense because smoke could not be measured was now preparing a costly offering. [364] But an offering of the only kind she trusted. [373] The unknown had no name, so prayer was useless; it had no ears, probably, so song was worse. [391] It had, however, geometry. [395] If anything in the world could be addressed to it, it would have to go through schematic representations: diagrams, ratios, the clean bones of relation. [420] So she had drawn, on a fresh tablet she could not really afford, the lunar anomaly as a zigzag: rising columns, falling columns, the moon's speed shown as a shape rather than a story. [454] Beside it she drew the magic square. [461] Two figures, one confession. [465] The sun went down behind the glazed lions and the air turned the color of a fig skin. [483] Below, the city hummed without shouting; between regimes, everyone kept their voices low, and the sound that rose to the roof was almost a chord. [508] Iltani set her lamp down and waited for the moon to reach the position her zigzag predicted. [525] It arrived early by a third of a finger. [534] She wrote that down, because a covenant built on flattery was not worth sealing. [548] Then she knelt and uncloaked the tile. [555] Moonlight came across the sixteen numbers, and thirty-four answered from every direction at once, which was, she thought, exactly what devotion should do: hold the same value however you approach it. [586] "I do not know what you are," she said to the anomaly. [598] "I will not pretend a name. [604] I will give you what I have: measurements, honestly taken, for as long as I can take them." [622] She pressed the tablet with her zigzag face-down against the magic square tile, clay to clay, and left it there. [642] Nobody would read the sentence underneath, which was the point; the devotion stayed encrypted, and the diagram on top would be legible to anyone who could count. [669] Two languages, stacked. [672] One for the dead man, one for whatever had no ears. [683] In the morning a new king was proclaimed, and the priests came up to the roof to reclaim the sky for him. [705] They found an astronomer who asked for no raise, only for the tiles to remain undisturbed, and for a standing order of cheap clay. [729] They thought her thrifty. [733] She was simply already paid. [738]
