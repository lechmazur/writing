The last wish was granted on a Tuesday, and by Friday Lanternfall was a ghost town. [16] Sevric Oam stayed. [19] He was the alchemist of the cove, and his three apprentices had left him the way tide leaves a boat: gently, completely, overnight. [42] They had taken the copper stills, the ledgers, and the good lamp oil, and left the amber bottle sitting on the workbench because the amber bottle was worthless now. [71] It had no opening. [75] That was the whole of its famous design: a vessel blown shut, seamless as a plum, warm to the touch, holding a thousand years of wishes that could only ever come out and never go back in. [112] Pilgrims had come for nine generations, and the cove had grown fat on them, three hundred houses, a fish market, a street of paper-lantern makers whose work hung over the water so thickly that sailors called the bay a second sky. [153] Then a tired woman from the interior asked for her son's fever to break, the bottle grew cold, and the age of wishes ended without ceremony. [179]

The mayor's council had a plan, and they brought it to Sevric before they fled. [194] Counterfeit the bottle, they said. [199] Blow a dozen amber vessels with no openings, seed the pilgrim roads with rumor, and let the faithful keep coming; hope was an economy, and nobody audits hope. [227] Sevric refused. [229] He had spent forty years distilling things down to what they actually were, and he would not spend his last years selling people a warm lie to sleep on. [258] Comfort that was false, he told them, rots the floor you stand on. [271] So they called him stubborn and went south, and he was left alone with an empty cove and a bottle that could not be opened, refilled, or broken. [299]

For a winter he did nothing but walk the emptied streets, and in walking them he began to conceive something enormous. [320] Lanternfall had never been a town, he realized; it had been a waiting room. [334] Every trade in it existed to serve people passing through toward a miracle. [347] If it was ever to live again, it could not be revived; it had to be reimagined from the keel up, an entire small civilization redrawn around the fact that no one was coming to save it. [384]

He had no paper. [388] The apprentices had taken the ledgers and the archives had gone to the interior with the priests, so Sevric built the new civilization the only way left to him: through working memory, holding it all at once behind his eyes, revising it nightly the way a man turns a stone in his palm. [441] Twelve families to start, because twelve was the most he could hold with all their debts and grudges intact. [460] A salt works, since the flats were already there. [469] A school in the old shrine, its first lesson being the difference between a thing that works and a thing that consoles. [491] A law that no one may sell a cure he cannot explain. [503] He kept the paper-lantern makers, but turned their craft outward: navigation markers, signal floats, cheap light for people who fished at night. [525] Each evening he lit one lantern on the pier and recited the whole design aloud to the water, because a thing held only in one skull dies with the skull. [555]

In the second spring, a boat came in: salt traders, lost, following a light. [569] They asked if this was the wishing town. [577] Sevric said no, and showed them the bottle, and told them plainly that it was finished, a beautiful sealed dead thing, and that they should not stay for it. [606] Then he told them about the salt works, and the school, and the twelve families, and the law about cures. [626] Four of them stayed. [630] By the fifth year there were sixty people in the cove and the lanterns over the bay had begun to thicken again, though they pointed at shoals now instead of at heaven. [662]

Sevric died in the ninth year, and they buried the amber bottle with him, unopened, unopenable, at his instruction. [681] The school kept the recitation, every evening, one lantern, the whole design read aloud to the water. [698] Visitors sometimes asked the children whether anything in the cove was magic. [710] The children had been taught to answer honestly, which is to say: not yet, and not ever, and look what we did without it. [734]
