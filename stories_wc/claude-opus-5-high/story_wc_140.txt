The clerk's pen slipped in the spring of a wet year, and a woman who was still breathing became, on paper, a corpse. [23] The death certificate named a cause she had never tasted: oxalic acid, poisoning, accidental. [37] The name on the certificate was hers, and after that the name belonged to the dead, so she left it at the registry office like a coat she could not afford to claim. [70] Without records she could not rent, could not work, could not argue; the erased have no shelf to stand on. [90] So she went down to the port, where the containers stood in a towering labyrinth, stacked nine high, rust-orange and sea-green, and she learned to live in the gaps between them. [121] The dockers called her nothing at all, which suited her, because a nameless thing is hard to delete twice. [140] She kept one document: the label she had peeled from the poison bottle on her mother's shelf, the bottle the coroner's assistant had photographed and misfiled into her life. [169] Her mother had used the acid to clean brass; the label showed a skull and a crossed spoon and, in faded ink along the margin, a price in an old currency. [200] It was the only object that agreed she had existed, though it could not tell her where that house had stood. [221]

The trouble was that being erased had done something to the inside of her head. [236] Her own memories had gone smooth and frictionless, like photographs of photographs, and she found she filtered reality differently now: she could no longer see a past, only feel for its texture, the way a blind hand reads a wall. [276] A real memory, she discovered, had grain: burlap, wet rope, the nap of a coat collar. [292] Hers had none, so she resolved to build a past she could actually touch, by collecting fragments of overheard stories. [312]

The labyrinth was good for this. [318] Sound crawled along the steel corridors, and the crane operators, the night welders, the stowaways waiting for a hold to open, all talked as though the containers were walls and not ears. [350] She worked across the rhythm of tides, because the tides ruled the port and therefore ruled the talk. [368] At high water the ships came in and men told lies about money. [381] At slack water they grew honest and told about their mothers. [392] At low water, with the mudflats stinking and the gantries idle, they told the things they had never told anyone, and those were the fragments with grain in them. [421]

She took a girl's memory of a burned kite from a Ghanaian bosun. [434] She took the smell of a flooded basement from two customs men arguing about a bribe. [450] She took a grandfather's cough, a wedding where the electricity failed, a dog that swam out past the breakwater and did not come back. [474] Each night she would layer them, one thin sheet of borrowed life over another, the way the port layered containers, and by morning the stack held. [500] She wrote nothing down; she filed each fragment against the poison label, and if it did not contradict the label, she kept it. [523] The label was the foundation stone: a mother, a shelf, brass, an old currency, a house. [539] Everything she added had to fit inside a life that could contain those five things. [554]

In the second winter, a woman came looking through the containers with a folder, a clerk from the registry, older now, apologetic. [576] The error had been found. [581] They could restore the name, the taxes, the school records, the whole thin official history. [596] She asked the clerk what her mother had used oxalic acid for. [608] The clerk turned pages and said the file did not record such details. [621] "Then your history has no texture," she said, "and mine does." [632]

She took the folder anyway and carried it to the water at the turn of the tide, and she read every page, and she let the pages go one at a time. [664] She kept one thing from the folder: her mother's address, three streets from the dry dock, which she had not known she wanted until she read it. [691] That was a fragment with grain in it, and it did not contradict the label. [706] She went up the ladder of the labyrinth to the ninth tier and sat where the wind came off the harbor, and she laid the address down over everything else, the newest sheet on the stack. [742] Below her the tide went out, taking the paper name with it, and she found she did not need one. [762] She had a house with brass in it, a burned kite, a flooded basement, a dog past the breakwater. [781] In the morning she would walk three streets from the dry dock and knock. [795]
