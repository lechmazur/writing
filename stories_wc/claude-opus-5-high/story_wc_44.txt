The observatory on Hesper Isle only opened its shutters after dark, because daylight on a floating isle has nowhere to fall. [21] Ilva Sarn arrived at moonrise with chalk in her pockets and a question she had carried for three years. [40] She wanted to find where shadows go at noon. [48] Everyone born after spoons bent reality had grown up with the absence and stopped noticing it. [64] At noon, on the isle, nothing cast a shadow: not the railings, not the bell tower, not Ilva's own hands held up against the sun. [89] The elder scholars called this a residue and told her to study something useful. [103] She was a budding rune scholar, third year, with no patron and a habit of being quietly deliberate about everything, including disobedience. [125] The Bending had ruined the old rune grammar, so every scholar of her generation had to relearn the craft from broken tablets. [147] What they had recovered was a single principle: runes hold by closing loops. [160] A stroke that returns to its own beginning binds; a stroke left open leaks, and leaking is how spoons learned to bend. [182] In the observatory's lower vault stood the sealed awakenings: nine clockwork automatons, wound and ready, each one stopped mid-breath by a binding rune across its chest. [208] They had been built before the Bending to survey the sky, and they had been sealed during it, when the first of them opened its eyes and reported that the sun had two undersides. [242] Ilva had spent two winters copying the vault's inventory and had noticed, in the margin of a water-stained page, a line about a key. [266] The clockwork automaton key hung on the archivist's ring, a brass spiral that ended where it started. [283] It was, she understood at once, not a key for a lock but a closed loop in the shape of a tool. [305] Turning it in an automaton would not open the seal; it would complete the seal's loop through her own hand and let her ride what the automaton saw. [333] She borrowed the key on a night the archivist slept, and she did it slowly, without hurry, the way she did everything. [355] In the vault she chose the first automaton, the one that had spoken. [368] Its chest rune was a broken spiral, an open stroke, which meant the old sealers had bound it wrong and it had held only because nine of them leaned on each other. [400] Ilva knelt, set her chalk to brass, and closed the loop. [411] Then she fitted the key and turned it once. [420] The automaton did not wake. [425] Instead the vault filled with noon. [431] She stood in white light with no shadow of her own, and she saw the isle from beneath, from the side the sun never shows, where every shadow the world had ever cast at its zenith was folded and stacked like laundry. [473] Shadows at noon do not vanish; they go under, into the isle's undersides, because the Bending left the world one surface short. [495] The automaton's voice came from inside her ear, patient, as though it had been waiting three hundred years to finish a sentence. [517] The automaton said that there were two undersides, and that one held the shadows while the other held the spoons. [537] Every bent spoon in every kitchen on the night reality slipped had been an open loop, and the world had been draining through them ever since. [563] Noon was simply the hour the drain ran fastest. [572] Ilva turned the key back, and the light let her go. [583] The automaton stood sealed again, properly this time, its spiral whole. [594] The archivist never learned that his key had been borrowed, though he remarked for weeks that the vault smelled of hot noon. [616] Ilva did not correct him; she had learned that a secret, like a rune, holds only when it closes. [635] She climbed to the shutters and wrote, in a steady hand, the first honest entry the observatory had recorded since the Bending. [657] Shadows go under at noon, she wrote, and they will keep going until somebody closes every spoon. [674] In the morning she began the work she would spend her life on, and she began it the only way she knew: one loop at a time, patiently, in chalk. [704]
