The leash broke at the low tide bell, and Barrow was gone into the fog before Nell could close her fist. [21] She had been holding him too tightly for a year, ever since her brother shipped out and the house went quiet. [42] The frayed strap dangled from her hand like a question. [52] Up the cliff path, where the water's hush thinned into something like a shoreline lullaby humming through the gorse, she found the veiled ridgeway. [76] Mist lay over it in long gauze folds, and lanterns hung from posts that had no business standing on bare stone. [97] Two ferry guides waited at the first post, old women with salt in their braids and oars they clearly did not need. [119] "You want the dog," said the taller one, "but the dog is only the ticket." [134] "Barrow ran for the place where unused courage waits," said the other. [146] "Dogs smell it. People only suspect it." [153] Nell said she only wanted her dog back, and the guides nodded as though that were the same thing said badly. [174] They were gently persistent in the way tides are. [183] Each time she turned back toward the lights of town, one of them said her name softly, and the path turned with her, and she was still climbing. [211] At last she took the paper strip they offered. [220] The shorter guide produced a ticket punch with a star stamp, pressed it, and a small star of nothing opened in the middle of the card. [246] "One crossing," she said. [250] "Keep it. The punch proves you asked." [257] They did not go straight. [262] To traverse the ridgeway, the guides explained, you went through indirect routes: down into a hollow of dripping ferns, sideways along a sheep track, back beneath an arch of blackthorn that seemed to lead the wrong way entirely. [300] "The straight road is for people who already know what they are," the taller guide said. [316] "You are still deciding." [320] Nell followed, calling Barrow's name until her throat went thin, and the mist gave her back small pieces of answer: a paw print, a bent stalk, once a bark that folded into the sound of surf. [356] The lullaby was always ahead of her, never nearer. [365] They came out at twilight onto a flat shelf of rock the guides called the Landing, though there was no water in sight, only the long white lake of cloud below and the town's lamps drowned somewhere under it. [404] "Here is where you wait," said the shorter guide. [413] "A vigil, until the last light goes. Whatever you have not spent will come and find you." [430] So Nell sat. [433] The cold came up through the stone and the guides stood behind her, humming, not touching her shoulders, not leaving. [453] She thought about the leash. [458] She thought about the morning her brother left and how she had stood at the window instead of running down to the dock, how she had swallowed every brave word she owned and kept it, hoarded, unspent, believing there would be a better day to spend it. [505] The light went violet, then ash. [511] And when the last of it slid off the ridge, something moved in the cloud below and came up the rock: Barrow, wet to the belly, trailing the broken half of his collar strap, and behind him a crowd of small pale shapes like moths made of held breath. [560] They settled on her hands and arms and did not weigh anything, and each one was a thing she had not said. [582] "Take them down with you," the taller guide said. [591] "Courage keeps badly. Spend it on letters, on the dock, on your mother at breakfast." [606] Nell went down by the straight road, and it took no time at all. [620] In her pocket the punched ticket kept its little star, a hole shaped like permission, and she did not bother buying a new leash. [644] That night she wrote her brother nine pages, and the sea under the window kept humming the tune the guides had taught her, gently, persistently, like something waiting to be answered. [675]
