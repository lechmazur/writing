Ilva worked in the grotto that floated a thousand feet above the tundra, where the aurora poured green light through the ice-mouth and made the walls glow like the inside of a lantern. [33] She was the last beautician mortician in the northern reaches, and the dead came to her by cable-sled, wrapped in wool, patient as bread dough. [58] Her work had two halves: the face, and the spark. [68] The face was rouge and wax and the careful closing of eyes; anyone with steady hands could learn it. [87] The spark was harder. [91]

For eleven hours after breath stopped, a person hung in what the grotto-keepers called a threshold of stillness -- not living, not gone, the mind's last weather still drifting out as noise. [122] Ilva caught that noise with a copper cradle and an old aurora-tuned receiver, and she listened. [138] To anyone else it was static, a hiss with no floor and no ceiling. [152] Ilva had spent thirty years finding patterns in static: the three-beat stammer of a laugh, the long descending sigh of a name someone had loved saying. [178] When she found one, she would infuse it into an emerald chip -- a sliver of stone no bigger than a fingernail clipping -- and the chip would hold that single spark, warm and faintly humming, for as long as the family kept it dry. [221] It was, she told grieving daughters, not a soul. [230] It was one spark, captured before oblivion took the rest. [240]

On the last night of the aurora season, the sled brought her Ovek. [253] He had taught her the craft when she was fourteen, sitting behind her in this same grotto with his hands over her hands, saying: be lucidly watchful, never hopeful, because hope hears what it wants. [288] He was eighty-one and had died in his chair with his boots on, which he would have considered tidy. [307]

She washed him, set his jaw, brushed powder into the hollows under his eyes, and worked the wax until his mouth looked like a man about to say something dry. [337] Then she lifted the copper cradle. [343] Outside the ice-mouth, the aurora had thinned to a pale ribbon; by dawn it would be gone for eight months, and with it the current that fed the receiver. [372] Eleven hours of threshold, four hours of light. [380]

The grotto went radiant and hushed, the way it always did when she began: green light on white walls, her own breath the loudest thing in the world. [408] For three hours, Ovek gave her nothing but hiss. [417] No stammer, no sigh, no shape at all -- the flat grey sound of a man who had been thorough about leaving. [438] She caught herself leaning toward the noise, wanting, and heard his voice correct her: hope hears what it wants. [457] So she sat back, went cold and open, and simply watched. [468]

In the fourth hour, with the ribbon overhead down to a thread, the pattern came. [483] It was not a laugh or a name. [491] It was a slow four-beat knock, repeated, the exact rhythm he used to tap on the cradle rim when a student's hands went shaky -- steady, steady, steady, steady. [519] He had not left her a memory. [526] He had left her an instruction, the only thing he had ever thought worth keeping. [541]

Ilva set the emerald chip in the cradle's socket and opened the gate. [554] The last of the aurora bent down through the ice-mouth, and she let it infuse the knock into the stone, four beats folded into green, until the chip warmed against her thumb like a small animal. [590] Then the sky went dark, and the receiver died with a click, and Ovek was entirely gone. [607]

He had no family left to keep the chip dry. [617] Ilva threaded it onto the wire she wore at her wrist and went back to his face, which needed one more pass of powder along the temple. [644] Eight months of dark work ahead, with no current, no patterns, nothing but faces and wax and the long patience of the tundra. [667] Her hands, she noticed, were not shaking. [674] At her wrist, faint as a pulse under the floating grotto's floor, the emerald kept time. [690] Steady, steady, steady, steady. [694]
