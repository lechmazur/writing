In Arvent, a family was only as real as its paperwork, and I sold paperwork. [15] Grandfathers, cousins, whole orchards of ancestors, notarized by a clerk who took half in salt. [30] For clients who could not carry a forgery without flinching, I did the finer work: I manufactured false memories, small ones, a kitchen with blue tile, a grandmother humming off-key over lentils. [62] A memory is only a story told often enough that the body agrees to it. [77] I told good stories. [81]

Bexley Torr found me on the cobalt terrace at dusk, where the tiles hold the last light like water holds a coin. [103] He was sixty, soft-voiced, and he did not want a pedigree. [114] He wanted his mother's name, the real one, and he was willing to pay for the weight of a withheld truth. [135] "Someone took it," he said. [140] "I remember a house I have never lived in." [149]

I knew the house. [153] I had built it for him nineteen years ago, blue tile and lentils, when a wealthier family bought the Torr line out from under him and needed his mother's citizenship number scrubbed clean of a son. [189] She was deported that winter as a woman with no dependents. [200] I had signed the affidavit and slept badly ever since, which is the cheapest form of penance there is. [219]

I did not tell him that evening. [226] We sat under the weight of unspoken words while the terrace went from cobalt to ink, and I let the silence do what silence does in my trade: I sieved it. [257] A sieved hush is an old forger's habit - you strain a quiet until the coarse grit of what a person wants stays in the mesh, and the fine truth of what they already suspect runs through. [293] Bexley already suspected. [296] That was the grit I could not sell him past. [306]

The registries were ash; the bribe-clerks were dead or careful. [316] Only one record of the deported remained, and it was not made of paper. [330] North of the salt flats lies a forgotten shore where the old coast families kept their genealogy the way their grandmothers had: by carving each birth into driftwood and returning it to the tideline, so that no soldier could burn a whole bloodline in one afternoon. [376] I spent eleven days there, sifting through driftwood carvings, wading, turning gray planks with my boot, reading notches by thumb when my eyes gave out. [401] It was the first honest research I had done in twenty years, and it cost me a fever and two fingernails. [422]

On the eleventh day I found her: ANNEKE TORR, cut deep, and beneath it a second line, a son, unnamed, born at the hour marked by a watch hand hammered into the wood. [455] A minute hand, brass, green with verdigris, pointing at a notch near dawn. [468] She had kept him after all, in the only ledger she trusted, and the state's paper had never touched it. [488] I pried the watch hand loose with my knife and carried it home in a matchbox. [504]

We met again on the cobalt terrace, same hour, the light going down like something being decided. [521] I could have handed him a gentle fiction; I am very good at gentle fiction, and it would have quelled him for a decade. [545] Instead I put the matchbox in his palm and told him what I had done, in order, without softening the affidavit. [566] He opened it and held the little brass hand up against the last cobalt in the sky. [583] "It weighs nothing," he said. [588] "That is what I came to learn." [595]

He did not forgive me, and I had not asked him to; forgiveness was not the transaction. [612] He wanted the true weight of a thing withheld, and now he had it: two grams of brass and a mother who had counted the hour of his birth into wood before the sea took her name off the rolls. [652]

I have not forged a lineage since. [659] I go north instead, three weeks each spring, and copy the tideline carvings into a book nobody has paid for. [679] It does not quell the guilt; nothing does, and I have stopped expecting it to. [694] But each true name I set down is a small hush strained clean, and at dusk, when the terrace turns blue, I find I can sit in it. [722]
