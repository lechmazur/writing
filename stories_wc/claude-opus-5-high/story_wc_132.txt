Every storm season, Mirela Vasc closed the nursery gates and let the rain do her watering. [16] She had inherited eleven acres of seedlings, a leaking glasshouse, and a habit of tying everything down. [33] Wire, twine, bamboo splint: if a stem wandered, she corrected it. [44] Her customers called her the best grower in the valley, and they were right, and that was the problem. [63] She did not want to sell plants anymore. [71] She wanted to study plant behaviors: how they decided, how they chose, whether they chose at all. [88] The evidence sat in a folder on her desk: nine hundred linden leaves, pressed and numbered, collected from one tree over nine years. [111] Her plan was to reconstruct the tree's entire growth history from vein patterns, to prove that a linden responds to a storm the way a person responds to bad news. [141] For three years the reconstruction had failed. [148] Then the letters began arriving from Dr. Anselm Kohout, a retired phytologist in the next county, whose handwriting looked like a root system. [171] He was circuitously sincere. [175] He would spend two pages praising her penmanship, a third on the weather over Moravia, a fourth on a cousin's failed vineyard, and then, in the last line, say something that took the floor out from under her. [213] Her leaves, he wrote, were beautiful and useless. [221] She had pressed them flat, and behavior does not survive flattening. [232] If she wanted to see a plant decide, she had to let it do something she had not planned. [251] Mirela wrote back four furious drafts and mailed none of them. [262] In August the season turned mean. [268] Storms came every third evening, and on the fourth she went out to the mossy trellis after rain, where the honeysuckle she had trained for six years hung in obedient rows. [299] The moss on the cedar slats had gone emerald and swollen; water ticked off it in a rhythm like a slow clock. [321] A linden leaf, torn loose from the old tree at the fence line, had plastered itself across the trellis knots. [341] She peeled it away and held it up, wet and whole, and saw what no press had ever let her see: the veins were not symmetrical. [367] One side had grown fat and branching, the other thin, as though the leaf had leaned all its life toward something and away from something else. [393] A decision, recorded in tissue. [398] She had flattened nine hundred of these and never noticed, because she had always known in advance what she expected to find. [420] The metamorphosis she required of herself came slowly and against her will. [432] She began, by releasing control, with the honeysuckle. [440] She cut the twine. [444] Her hands shook doing it; twenty years of craft argued with her the whole time. [459] The vines sagged, ugly, half of them touching mud, and she made herself walk away and write down only what happened. [480] Through the rest of storm season she did this in widening circles: the untied trellis, then a bed of beans left unsupported, then the whole eastern slope where she had meant to plant rows and instead planted nothing and watched what arrived. [522] The nursery's income dropped by a third. [529] Her notebooks tripled. [532] She learned that the honeysuckle, unmanaged, rebuilt itself toward the trellis anyway, choosing the mossy side, the damp side, the side she would never have chosen for it. [560] She learned that beans left to fall find each other. [570] In October she sent Kohout a parcel: one fresh linden leaf in a jar of water, and a single sentence. [590] You were right, and I resented it for nine months, which I suppose is the honest length of time such things take. [612] He replied with six pages on Moravian weather and, at the bottom, a line she pinned above her desk: A grower who can be changed by a leaf will never run out of work. [646] The storms kept coming, year on year, and each spring she untied a little more. [661] It never stopped costing her something. [667] That, she decided, was what sustained it: a transformation is not a door you pass through but a trellis you keep growing on, mossy and crooked and still standing after the rain. [699]
