For forty-one years, Marit Halloran ate the same breakfast on the same chipped blue plate, and for forty-one years she remembered nothing of the summer she turned nine. [28] She was a folklorist of the hands-on sort: she did not merely transcribe charms, she tested them. [45] She had slept in barrows, drunk from nine wells before sunrise, and left milk out for things that drank it. [65] Colleagues called her chronically curious, which was a polite way of saying she could not leave a door shut. [84] Except one. [86]

The orchard had gone under in the storm of '73, when the sea took the dyke and the apple rows together. [107] What remained was the great glasshouse at its center, standing knee-deep in brackish water, ringed by the hulls of the fishing fleet that had been flung inland and left to rot at anchorless angles. [141] Six wrecks, prow to stern, like a fence nobody had meant to build. [154]

Old Hessel, the orchardman, had painted the glass roof with stars. [165] Every pane a piece of sky, so the seedlings underneath would think they were sleeping outdoors. [181] Marit remembered that much, and the smell of tar, and a boy named Pieter who had counted the painted stars with her. [203] After that, nothing: a clean white gap where August should have been, and Pieter's family moved away, and nobody in the village would say why. [228]

Decades later, she came back with a notebook and no plate. [239]

The rule she had assembled out of forty-one years of collected stories was simple and unpleasant: the folk under the hill do not take what you notice missing. [267] They feed on routine, on the smooth unexamined groove of a life, and their doors open only when habits break. [287] So she had smashed the blue plate on the doorstep, skipped breakfast, and walked out at low tide across a field that was learning to be a seabed. [315]

The glasshouse door still stuck on the third push, exactly as her hands expected. [329] Inside, salt water lay flat as poured glass, and the painted sky hung in ruins above her. [346] Storms had punched panes out by the dozen; whole constellations had lost their stars. [360]

It took her an hour, wading, to understand that the gaps were not random. [374] A folklorist learns to detect pattern the way a fisherman detects weather, and this pattern was a ledger. [392] Hessel had painted a named star for every child who worked the orchard, and by studying the broken constellations she could read which children had been robbed and of what. [422] Her own star was gone from the Plough's handle. [431] Pieter's was missing from Orion's belt, leaving two lonely points and a hole where the third had been. [449]

Beneath each absence, on the silted staging benches, grew a ring of small pale mushrooms. [464] Fairy ring mushrooms, Marasmius oreades, impossible in salt water and growing anyway. [476]

She knelt in the cold and picked one from the ring under her own missing star. [492] It smelled of cut grass and hot tar and nine years old. [504] She ate it. [507]

August came back all at once, crisp as a windfall apple: the ladder, the wasps, Pieter daring her into the ring of mushrooms under the painted Plough, the two of them laughing, and then the long grey bargaining with something that spoke in Hessel's voice and wore nobody's face. [556] It had taken the summer as payment for letting them out. [567] It had taken more from Pieter, who had gone in first. [578]

Marit stood in the water with her stolen years returned to her, shaking, forty-one and nine at the same time. [598] Then she did what a hands-on folklorist does: she wrote it down. [610] Six rings, six stolen childhoods, six names she could match to villagers who had grown up vaguely sad and never known why. [632] She picked the mushrooms carefully, one from each ring, and wrapped them in waxed paper while the tide turned against the hulls outside. [655]

The last ring was Pieter's, under the gap in Orion. [665] She had heard he was in a care home two towns over at fifty, quiet, with a mind like a house where most of the rooms were locked. [693] She would bring him his August. [698]

Walking out between the wrecks, she made herself a promise and a discipline both: buy a new plate, and break it every year. [721] The door only opens when habits break, and she intended to keep coming back until every star was accounted for. [741] Behind her, in the dark glass, the painted sky waited to be read. [754]
