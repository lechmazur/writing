Obadiah Fen called himself a whimsical tinkerer, which was a polite way of saying nobody in Lowmeadow trusted him with anything that mattered. [23] That changed the autumn the vines went gray. [31] For three generations the valley had kept the harvest moon rites: the whole village walking the terraces at dusk, singing the old names of the fields until the fruit heard itself praised. [63] Then the rail line came, and the songs went the way of hand-scythes, and within a decade nobody could remember more than a stray line or two. [90] The grapes soured, the pears cracked on the branch, and the agronomists from the capital shrugged and recommended lime. [109] Obadiah, who repaired music boxes and believed that machines were merely patient animals, thought the remedy was likelier to be found in sound than in soil. [135]

He built his listening engine out of a cider press, a phonograph horn, and forty brass spindles no larger than wrens' bones. [157] His theory, which he explained to anyone slow enough to be cornered, was simple: the oldest grapevine in the valley had heard every rite ever sung, and wood remembers what people forget. [189] That vine grew in Ilsabet Marrow's verdant arbor, and Ilsabet did not like visitors. [203] She let him in anyway, because her pears had cracked too. [214]

They worked through September, and on the last morning Obadiah arrived before light, when the arbor at dawn held that gathering hush that comes just before birds decide the day is real. [246] Ilsabet was already there in her shawl, holding a lantern she hadn't needed to light. [261] The engine required one living connection. [267] Obadiah coaxed a vine tendril, green and coiled as a watch spring, into the smallest brass spindle, and the tendril gripped it the way a baby grips a finger. [296] He had waited for this particular morning because his almanac promised the rare hour when everything aligns: the harvest moon still low and full in the west, the sun not yet up in the east, the vine held for one balanced minute between two lights. [341] Sap moves then, Ilsabet said; her mother had told her so, and her mother had sung. [357]

He turned the crank. [361] The spindles caught the tendril's tremor and drew it out as thread, and the thread came off the reel gold - not metal, but the color of late light on skin - and where it crossed the horn it made a sound. [401] It was a woman's voice, thin and unhurried, singing a line about the eastern terrace and somebody named Hollis who planted it. [423] Ilsabet sat down hard on the stone bench. [431] The machine spins golden stories, Obadiah whispered, delighted and a little frightened, because he had built it to catch a tune and it was handing him a whole dead century. [461] Names of fields, names of the dead, the blessing for grafting, the round they sang while treading - all of it braided into the shining thread, an hour's worth before the sun cleared the ridge and the vine let go. [500]

Obadiah wanted to patent it. [505] Ilsabet told him he was a fool, and that a machine could not be the remedy, only the messenger. [524] The blight, she said, was not in the roots but in the silence, and a valley cannot be healed by a crank. [546] So they went down into Lowmeadow with a spool of golden thread and taught it, house by house, through forgotten songs that came back to people's mouths faster than anyone expected, the way a stairway in a childhood house comes back to the feet. [590] Obadiah's true motive, he admitted much later, had never been the fruit at all; he wanted to restore sacred practices because a man who fixes broken things cannot bear to watch a whole people forget how they were made. [629]

They walked the terraces that October under the full moon, ninety-one voices strong, and whether it was the singing or the lime nobody could prove. [654] The pears came in whole the following year. [662] The listening engine sat rusting in the arbor, its spindles stilled, one vine tendril grown clean through the horn - which Ilsabet said was exactly right, since the vine had only ever been lending them something that was theirs. [700] Every autumn now, the village gathers at dawn in the arbor first, and holds its hush, and waits for someone brave to start. [723]
