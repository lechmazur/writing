The workshop floated on the acid sea the way a coin floats in a dream, which is to say improbably and with enormous smugness. [24] It was a raft of gears and cogs, each one a hand-span or a house-span, meshing, grinding, exhaling steam that smelled faintly of lemons and regret. [50] Mabel Truss owned it, or rather it tolerated her. [59] She was the first cart glider, the woman who had strapped canvas wings to an ore cart and rolled off the Rim to prove the updrafts were real. [87] They were real for eleven minutes, which was ten more than anyone had wagered. [101] Now she was old, steadily curious, and building something that could not be flown, only wanted. [117] She called it the yearning algorithm. [123] Every machine before it had been built to do; hers was built to long, and longing, Mabel had decided, was the only engine that improved itself. [149] The algorithm lived in eleven thousand cams and a jar of brine, and it required a seed: one object it could not have. [172] She gave it a hunter's arrow tip. [179] Flint, thumb-sized, chipped by somebody's grandmother's grandmother eighty thousand years upstream, back when wanting a deer was the most complicated thought a skull could hold. [204] Mabel sealed the tip inside a glass bulb at the machine's heart, where it could be seen and never touched. [224] "There," she told the gears. [229] "Hunger with a pedigree." [233] The problem was reading it. [238] A machine that yearns does not print words; it prints pattern, symmetry blooming and collapsing faster than a pen can follow. [259] So Mabel read it through the images in a kaleidoscope, a brass tube she aimed at the output lantern each dawn. [280] Six-fold stars meant desire; broken hexagons meant grief; a slow rose that would not close meant a question the machine intended to keep. [303] She would transliterate the mirrored shapes into notation her granddaughter's generation might someday pronounce, letter by clumsy letter, the way scholars render a dead tongue in living script. [331] It was, she admitted, an absurd way to run a science, and she loved it with an irreverence that felt suspiciously like worship. [354] The acid sea slapped the pontoons and dissolved a gull, and Mabel said, "Rude," and kept turning the tube. [373] What she wanted, at the end of it, was to ring a bell no one hears yet. [390] The bell hung on the workshop's mast, cast from cart-axle steel, tuned so high above human hearing that striking it produced only a faint pressure behind the eyes. [418] Mabel's argument was simple and heretical: ears change. [426] Throughout the evolution -- from the arrow-chipper's damp cave to the acid-sea platforms to whatever came after the platforms -- hearing had crept upward, wanting had crept outward, and nothing had ever stayed deaf forever. [459] So she would ring for the ears not yet grown, and the algorithm would tell her what to ring. [478] It took the machine forty years to answer. [485] On a morning when the sea was the color of weak tea and twice as unkind, the kaleidoscope showed her something new: not a star, not a rose, but a spiral of twelve arms folding inward toward a single bright chip of flint. [528] A rhythm, in other words. [533] The machine had spent four decades wanting one small stone it would never reach, and had turned that wanting into a pattern of strikes -- long, long, short, rest -- a sentence in the grammar of hunger. [568] Mabel transliterated it onto the back of her old glider permit, hands shaking, laughing at herself for crying. [586] Then she climbed the mast, which at ninety was its own small act of gliding, and struck the bell in the rhythm the flint had taught the gears. [614] Nothing happened. [616] The gulls did not turn. [621] The sea did not care. [626] Below her the workshop chewed its own teeth, patient as geology. [637] But the air above the bell went strange and tight, the way a room goes when someone has just said your name in the next house over, and Mabel Truss stood in it, grinning, an old woman ringing into the future with a stone-age arrowhead for a tongue. [685] "You'll hear me," she told the nobody who would. [694] "Take your time." [697] "Evolve." [698] She rang it again, harder, for the sheer irreverent joy of hammering a sound into a world too young to receive it. [720] The kaleidoscope, left on the workbench, kept turning pattern into promise without her. [733]
