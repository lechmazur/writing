The kayak launch at Sorrel Point was a concrete ramp, a rack of borrowed paddles, and a hand-lettered sign that read RENTALS - ASK AMBROSE. [24] Ambrose Vail had been a ship's navigator for twenty-two years, and for nine he had been what the harbor called a broken navigator, which was a polite way of saying he no longer trusted himself past the buoys. [62] He whistled while he coiled rope, a merrily melancholic sound, the sort of tune that grins and weeps in the same bar. [84] Tourists liked him for it. [88] On the shelf above the cash tin sat an astronomical compass, brass, heavy, its gimbals seized with salt. [106] It had belonged to his father and it had failed nobody; Ambrose had failed it, nine years ago, when he plotted a course by it while too proud to admit the sky was wrong and put the Kestrel's keel across a shoal that everyone had warned him about. [154] Nobody drowned. [156] But his daughter Neve had been aboard, twelve years old, and she had watched her father stand frozen at the chart table while the deck tilted and strangers carried her off in a launch. [190] She was twenty-one now, in Halloran Bay, eleven miles up the coast road, and she had written in August to say she might visit in October. [216] Might had been enough to repaint the shack. [224] He swept the ramp twice a day. [231] He bought coffee she probably no longer drank. [239] On the last Sunday in October, at four in the afternoon, the phone rang and it was not her but a friend of hers, explaining kindly that Neve had thought about it a long while and could not come yet. [279] There is a particular hour when expectations collapse, and it is quieter than people expect. [294] Ambrose thanked the friend, hung up, and felt the backlash of regrets come in like a wave off a seawall - not the first hit, but the return, the water that comes back at you carrying everything it tore loose. [333] Nine years of waiting politely at a concrete ramp, believing that if he stayed still enough and kind enough, forgiveness would gravitate toward him. [357] Things gravitate toward mass, he thought, and he had made himself very small. [370] He took the astronomical compass off the shelf, put it in a dry bag, and dragged the yellow touring kayak down the ramp. [393] Eleven miles was nothing to a man who had crossed oceans; eleven miles was everything to a man who had not passed the buoys in nine years. [420] Dusk came with fog, the low kind that erases stars first and horizons second. [434] He opened the dry bag anyway and held the compass up, and of course it gave him nothing: no Polaris, no altitude, no sight line, only a seized ring and his father's initials. [467] He laughed, one short wet sound, and stowed it. [476] Then he looked landward, and there they were: the utility poles of the coast road, strung along the bluff, each with a sodium lamp flickering awake in the damp. [505] Navigating by utility poles is not in any manual. [514] But he knew the spacing, one hundred and thirty feet, because he had walked that road a thousand times rehearsing apologies, and he knew that pole 291 stood at the Halloran Bay jetty. [547] So he paddled, counting lamps. [552] Some were dark. [556] Others stuttered, held, died, and caught again, and he found that he did not need every one to burn, only the next one, only faith enough to reach it. [585] At pole 180 his shoulders burned and the regrets came back again, listing every year he had spent being pleasant instead of being brave. [609] He answered them out loud, whistling between counts, because a man can be sorry and still be moving. [627] That was the whole of it, he decided somewhere past pole 240: he had spent nine years wanting to be forgiven and no hours at all trying to align heart and action. [659] Love that stays on the ramp is only weather. [668] Pole 291 came at half past nine, and the jetty lamp was out. [681] He climbed the ladder, legs shaking, and sat on the wet planks with the dry bag in his lap, and he did not knock on any doors, because arriving uninvited at midnight is not courage, it is a demand. [720] At dawn he left the astronomical compass at her door with a note: I came the whole way. The stars were no help. I counted the poles. I will come again next month, and the month after, and you never have to open the door. [765] Then he paddled home, whistling, counting down. [772] Pole 290, pole 289, and the lamps came on behind him one by one, as if something had begun to gravitate at last. [795]
