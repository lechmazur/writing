The attic above Renner's Vintage Books had one skylight, and Ilse had learned to read it like a barometer. [19] She trained courier pigeons there, and pigeons care more about air than about people. [33] Every morning she sat cross-legged on the floorboards and mapped the swirl of dust motes under that single shaft of light, sketching their eddies in a grid-ruled notebook until she knew exactly how the wind would lift off the roof at noon. [75] The motes never lied. [79] People did, and so did she. [85] To the customers downstairs she was a Dutch orphan, or a retired radio engineer, or the granddaughter of a Bosnian wedding singer, depending on which story kept them from asking about the village she actually came from. [122] She reinvented herself the way other people rearranged furniture, and for eleven years it had worked. [138]

The pigeons carried paper. [142] That was the whole point of them. [149] Ilse wrote out songs on slips of onionskin: harvest rounds, lullabies, the long mourning verses her grandmother had sung in a kitchen that no longer existed, all in a shorthand of notes and syllables, four bars to a slip. [188] Then she sent them to the scattered ones, the eighty-odd survivors whose addresses she had copied from refugee rolls, and asked each of them to send back what they remembered. [218] Digital archives could be deleted by a bored clerk; paper trails were stubborn, and a song that existed in eighty handwritten copies could not be unremembered. [244] She was preserving an echo, nothing more, but an echo was what she had. [258]

The capsules had to balance. [263] Too heavy and a bird flew low and tired; too light and the tube rattled and chafed the leg. [282] For this she used a jeweler's scale and, on the far pan, a chip of Martian meteorite the size of a fingernail, bought at an estate auction for the price of a coat. [315] It weighed 1.4 grams and always would. [322] Everything else in her life could be revised; the rock from another planet could not, and she loved it for that. [343]

Piotr Renner, who owned the bookshop and the roof over her birds, asked about the chip in her first winter. [363] She told him it was a family heirloom from an uncle at the observatory in Leiden. [379] He nodded, set down the tea he had carried up four flights, and said nothing. [394] He kept bringing tea for three years. [401]

Trust, she discovered, was not a decision but a season, and it came on her slowly across those three years, the way the wisteria on the shop's back wall took its time. [433] It bloomed and withered and bloomed again. [440] Two of her oldest birds died in the second spring. [450] Nine letters came back from the scattered ones with verses she had never heard, and four came back stamped deceased. [470] She grew and lost and grew, and one evening in the third autumn she found Piotr in the attic with her grid-ruled notebooks open on his knees. [497] He had not touched the songs. [503]

"You have written the same story four different ways," he said, "and none of them has a kitchen in it." [523]

Ilse stood in the dust-light and felt the old reflex rise, the urge to invent a fifth version. [541] Instead she sat down, took the meteorite chip from the scale, and put it in his palm. [558]

"It cost me a coat, and there was no uncle," she said. [570] "There was a kitchen, and a well, and my grandmother, and in April 1993 there was none of it." [589]

Piotr closed his hand around the 1.4 grams of another world and said, "Then let us write that one down." [609]

In the morning the motes swirled clockwise under the skylight, which meant a good northeast lift. [625] She loaded eleven capsules, weighed each against the chip, and let the birds go. [639] Then she came back to the attic, unrolled a clean sheet of onionskin, and wrote, for the first time, her own name above her grandmother's melody. [665] It was a small paper trail, and it led back to a real place. [679] Piotr carried up the tea, and she let him read it. [690]
