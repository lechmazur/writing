The greenhouse on the roof of the old Ministry had rusted to lace, and that, Odile thought, was exactly why it would work. [23] Rust let in light. [27] Rust let in rain. [31] Rust let in her, too, through a seam in the panels the guards had never bothered to weld. [49] She had been the Ministry's chef for eleven years, which meant she had tasted everything they wanted forgotten. [67] It was not a trick or a talent she had trained; it simply happened. [81] Bread carried the baker's grief, apples carried the orchard's last good summer, and the Ministry's own banquet lamb had carried the trembling hands of the boy who slaughtered it. [110] Boone, who tended the Ministry gardens before the Ministry decided gardens were sentimental, used to say that a cook is just a person who listens with her mouth. [138] He was informally wise in the way of men who never read a book past forty and never needed to. [158] It was Boone who gave her the seed, six days before he died, pressed into her palm like a coin for a ferryman. [181] "Knowledge fruit," he said. [185] "Last one off the Archive vine before they burned the Archive." [196] "Eat the fruit and you don't learn a thing, girl -- you remember what got taken." [211] She had looked it up, later, in a manual the censors had missed: the fruit's compounds worked through synaptic plasticity, coaxing pruned connections to grow back, restoring the pathways the Ministry's edits had let wither. [246] The seed needed heat and light that the dead city could not give it. [260] That was why she waited two years for the alignment. [270] Five planets and a comet, strung along the same thread of sky, close enough to pour a strange doubled brightness down on the northern hemisphere for one night only, and not again for four thousand years. [306] Boone had said the Archive vines flowered only under that light, which was how the Archive had kept them rare. [326] So she planted, and she waited, and she came up through the rusted seam every night with water in a soup pot. [348] The vine grew greedy and shapeless, and she learned to prune it, cutting back the runners that sprawled toward the shade so the strength went where the light was. [377] It struck her as the whole of her plan in miniature. [388] She was not going to burn the Ministry down. [397] She was fifty-three, her knees ached, and she had watched what burning things did to a city. [414] Hers would be a gentler rebellion. [420] She was going to cook. [425] On the night of the alignment the greenhouse filled with a pale green-silver glow, and every rust hole threw a coin of light onto the floor, and the vine put out nine fruits the color of wet slate. [463] She tasted one raw and nearly went to her knees. [473] It tasted of her mother's kitchen, of a language she had been fined for speaking, of a square full of people who had once stood in the rain for eleven hours and won something. [507] All of it had been in her the whole time, pruned back and waiting for light. [523] The autumn banquet was four days later. [530] Odile made a simple thing: a clear broth, nine fruits' worth of pulp folded into the finishing oil, one hundred and forty bowls. [553] She served the ministers, the clerks, the guards who had never welded the seam, the boy who slaughtered the lamb. [573] She did not poison anyone. [578] She simply gave them back what they had agreed to lose, and then she stood in the kitchen doorway with a towel over her shoulder and watched a room of powerful people begin, very quietly, to weep. [615] Nobody arrested her. [618] There was no one left in the room who wanted to. [628] By spring the greenhouse had new glass, donated anonymously, and Odile kept the rusted panels along the north wall because Boone would have laughed at anything too finished. [656] She had no interest in monuments. [662] She had wanted only to make one beautiful thing out of the ruins -- a soup, a vine, a room of people remembering their own names for things. [689] The vine would not flower again in her lifetime, or in anyone's. [701] But it fruited once, and once was enough to be worth the waiting, and she wrote the recipe on the wall in chalk where the rain could reach it. [730] People copied it down and made the broth without the fruit, and they swore it still tasted like that night, which Boone would have said was the whole point. [759]
