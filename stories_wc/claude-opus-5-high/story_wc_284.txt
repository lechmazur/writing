They burned her on a Tuesday, with a form letter and a taxi voucher, and for eleven months Ilse Rauch believed that was the end of it. [27] Then, at the tilt of the earth toward spring, the snow on Zephyr Bluff began speaking to her. [45] She had taken the caretaker's cottage there because the bluff was useless to everyone: too steep for building, too foggy for views, a hump of granite where the valley wind curled up and died in mist. [81] A burned spy learns to love useless places. [89] Every morning she walked the ridge in that silver hush that comes before dawn, when the fog holds the light without giving it back. [113]

On the fourteenth of March she found the first mark: three shallow trenches dragged into the accumulated snow of the north face, each a meter long, the middle one doubled. [143] It was her own alphabet. [148] She had invented it nine winters ago in Tbilisi for a boy named Kerem, who could not be trusted with paper and could not be taught radios, but who could walk through a field and leave sentences behind him. [187] The trenches said: I AM ALIVE. [193]

Kerem had died in a warehouse fire in Batumi, according to the file that had also ended her career for approving the meeting that put him there. [220] She had accepted the blame because accepting it was simpler than proving the file was a forgery, and because grief had made her tired. [244] Tired was not the same as finished. [250]

The next morning brought a second message, forty paces east: THEY WATCH THE ROAD. [264] The third, on the seventeenth: I NEED YOUR HAND. [273] She understood the shape of it then, and understood equally that it might be a trap built from her own dead vocabulary, since the alphabet had gone into her reports and her reports had gone everywhere. [309]

So she was prudently daring: she answered. [316] She trampled a single word into the snow at the cottage gate, PROVE, and then spent two nights in the rocks above with a thermos and her grandmother's field glasses, watching who came to read it. [352] A man came at four in the morning, limping badly, alone, and left behind him in the snow the name of a hotel in Trieste where she had once vouchsafed him a passport and a promise. [388] Nobody in the service knew about Trieste. [394]

She met him at the overlook in the mist, where the zephyr came up the cliff face and turned the fog into slow-moving rope. [418] Kerem was thirty now, burn-scarred along the jaw, and he had walked out of Batumi with a ledger stitched into his coat lining and spent nine years failing to find anyone who would take it. [453] Everyone he approached wanted him to be dead, because his being dead had been signed for. [469]

"You are the only one who cannot be embarrassed by me," he said. [482] "You are already broken." [486]

"Then we fix what's broken," she said, "starting with the file." [497]

She took the engraved pillbox out of her coat and set it on the wet stone between them. [515] It was silver, thumb-sized, with her initials and a date cut into the lid by a workshop in Vienna that did such work for people in her trade, and it had been issued with one pill inside. [552] She had carried it through four hostile borders and never opened it, and after they burned her she had kept it as the last proof that she had once been someone who might be asked to be brave. [590] That was what her walks had really been for: to find where unused courage waits, in what drawer of a person, under what weather. [614]

She opened the lid, tipped the pill over the cliff into the mist, and folded the first page of Kerem's ledger into the empty box. [639]

"Courier's habit," she said. [643] "Small things travel." [646]

They went down the bluff separately, an hour apart, in the silver hush. [659] Behind them the equinox sun found the north face at last, and the accumulated snow of the winter began to go, taking the trenches, the letters, the whole patient alphabet with it into the rock and the grass beneath. [698] By the time anyone thought to look for a message on Zephyr Bluff, there was nothing there to read. [717]
