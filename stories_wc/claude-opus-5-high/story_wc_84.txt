Dusk at the Sundar Yantra came the way it always did, a brilliant dimness, the sky too bright to be night and too dark to work by. [27] Meera climbed the marble stair of the great quadrant, drone case under one arm, and waited for the pilgrim the radio had promised. [50] Fourteen years a drone pilot, and sharp-eyed enough to read a serial number from two hundred meters, she still could not read the signal that mattered. [76] Every evening at moonrise the old relay on Ghat Hill broadcast a list, and the list was names. [94] Whoever appeared on it was counted, fed, and sheltered by the valley councils; whoever did not was wind. [112] Meera had spent three winters trying to decode the frequency of belonging, and every transmission came through pocked with holes, letters replaced by empty boxes. [137] The missing pieces were always the same: the glyphs of the old script, the ones her own name was written in. [158]

The pilgrim arrived as the first stars pricked through, a thin man in a dust cloak, and his face stopped her. [179] Fire had taken his eyebrows years ago and the scar had healed high, so that he looked perpetually surprised, as though the world kept handing him gifts. [206] "You are the one who asked for a matrix chip," he said. [218] He drew from his collar a wafer of ceramic the size of a thumbnail: the last matrix chip from the Deolali type foundry, holding the cut of every character the old script ever had. [252] Foundries had once cast letters in metal from such matrices; this one cast them in light. [268] "How did you find this place?" she asked, because the roads were gone and the maps lied. [285] "By following star trails," the pilgrim said. [292] "Your stone instruments are famous even in the low country." [302] "I walked only at night, and let the arcs overhead pull me west." [315]

Meera understood that method; it was hers too. [323] She flew her drone in long exposures, and the star trails burned into the frames like measuring wire, letting her fix the relay tower's bearing to within a degree. [352] What she had never had was the alphabet. [359]

He wanted something in return, of course. [366] "My village is not on the list either," he said, and the high scar made the statement look like wonder instead of grief. [389] "Whatever you hear, I want it heard by the councils too." [400]

They worked in the observatory's dimness while the sky went from violet to black and brilliant. [416] She seated the chip in her decoder's spare socket and sent the drone up to hover beside the great gnomon, antenna extended. [438] At moonrise the carrier wave arrived, and the empty boxes began to fill. [451] Names bloomed across her screen in a script three centuries old, cut long ago by a foundryman who could not have imagined this use. [475]

The list was longer than the councils ever read aloud. [485] It held her name. [489] It held his village, all eighty-one of them, and forty other settlements besides. [502] The relay had never excluded anyone; it had simply been transmitting in an alphabet the valley no longer owned, and the councils had read only the parts their machines could render. [533] Belonging had not been withheld. [538] It had been unreadable. [542]

The pilgrim laughed, a startled sound that matched his face at last. [554] Meera's hands were shaking, which made the next part harder. [564] To rebroadcast the decoded list across the valley she needed the observatory's old signal mast, and the mast's counterweight had seized in its channel forty years back. [591] They put their shoulders to the chain and pulled. [600] They pulled until the rust cracked, until the mast groaned upward through the dusk-warm air and stood, and the drone rose to meet it with the chip's light in its belly. [631] Then Meera sent the list back out, whole, every glyph in place. [643]

Below them, one window at a time, the valley began to answer with lamps. [656] The pilgrim watched the lights come on with his everlasting expression of surprise, and for once it was accurate. [675] Meera looked at the stone instruments around them, built by people who had believed that everything missing could be found if you were patient with the sky. [702] She had been patient. [706] She had simply been missing the letters. [713] "Stay," she said. [716] "There are other frequencies, and I will need someone who can find his way by starlight." [732] He did not look surprised, though of course he did. [742]
