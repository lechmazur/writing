After the oasis dried, Verdance learned to breathe its own exhale. [11] Ten thousand glasshouses sprawled beneath a sky the color of iodine, and the only honest water left was what the leaves gave back. [34] Ilsa Marr lettered names in gilded leaf for the Registry of Undelivered Things. [47] Her gold came in sheets thinner than a moth's wing, and her hand had to be steady, which was a problem, because Ilsa wilted near dishonesty. [73] It was not a metaphor. [78] Greenhouse children born in the sealed wards grew strange sympathies; hers was a vascular one. [93] A lie in the room and her fingers went gray at the tips, her shoulders curling like a cut stem. [113] A small truth, freely given, and she straightened as if watered. [124] This made her the best clerk in the Registry and the loneliest woman in Glasshouse Nine. [140]

She wanted one thing: to find where lost conversations go. [150] Her mother had died in the drought wards mid-sentence, and the rest of that sentence had been posted and never arrived. [171] Ilsa had reread the surviving half so many times the paper had gone soft as skin. [187]

In the third autumn of rationing she pried up a loose floorboard in her rented room and found the shredded map. [208] With it lay a zinc-etched postal plate fragment, a corner of an old printing block, its reversed letters still legible: ROUTE NINE, CONSERVATORY, DEAD. [232] The previous tenant had been Ovid Bell, last postmaster of the outer ring, and he had shredded his map by hand into two hundred slivers. [257] It took her a winter, working by grow-lamp, gilding by day and piecing together the shredded treasure map by night. [277] Each sliver was a false start; only the plate fragment, laid at the map's torn corner, told her which edge was true. [299] Assembled, it showed no gold and no water. [307] It showed a service tunnel under the fern terraces, and at its end, inked in a postmaster's careful hand, the Dead Letter Conservatory. [330]

She went down with a lamp and her gilding kit, because she did not know how else to be brave. [350] The tunnel smelled of wet zinc and rot. [358] At its end, in a glass vault threaded with pipes, Ovid Bell sat among empty sorting frames, feeding letters into a boiler. [380] He was very old, and he did not stop. [389] 'The seedling wards need heat,' he said, 'and paper burns.' [399] Ilsa felt her fingers gray, and knew he was lying, though not about the paper. [414] She knelt, opened her kit, and began to gild the vault's brass plate with the Registry motto, because her hands needed work. [436] 'I am not here to stop you,' she said, 'I am here because my mother's last sentence is in that stack.' [457]

Ovid's hand paused above the boiler door. [464] He said the true thing then, the small one, the one he had shredded his own map to avoid saying. [484] 'After the oasis dried, nobody could pay the outer routes,' he said, 'so I stopped delivering, and I kept taking the letters, and I have burned forty years of other people's words to keep my ferns alive.' [521]

Ilsa straightened. [523] Her spine uncurled, her fingertips flushed green-warm, and the gold leaf went down on the brass without a single tear. [543] That was how she learned what she had come to learn. [553] Lost conversations do not go anywhere grand. [560] They go into boilers and soil and the silence of people who cannot say a plain thing on a plain day. [581] They are not lost in transit; they are lost in the moment someone chooses not to say them. [599]

She found no letter from her mother in the remaining stacks; she had not really expected to. [616] She took instead the eleven thousand unburned envelopes, and Ovid, who had nothing left to hide, carried the baskets. [635] By spring there was a table under the iodine glass on Fern Terrace, where anyone could come and hear their own old mail read aloud, or say the short overdue true thing to whoever was standing there. [672] Ilsa gilded the sign herself, using the zinc-etched fragment as a straightedge. [684] She still rereads her mother's half-sentence some nights, but she no longer waits for the rest. [700] In a city that had run out of water, candor was the cheapest thing left to give, and Ilsa had never grown so tall. [724]
