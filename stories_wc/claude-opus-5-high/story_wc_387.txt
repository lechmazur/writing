Old Tameki mended nets on the whispering parapet, where rain had just stopped and the stones exhaled. [17] It was firefly season, and the lights came up out of the wet grass below like sparks deciding to be patient. [38] Girls from the village called him a philosopher, which he denied while continuing to talk like one. [55] His apprentice that summer was Sera, fourteen, quick with knots and quicker with questions. [69] She had come to learn mending, but she had stayed for the hidden patterns. [83] Every net, Tameki said, carried the signature of its maker: a habit of tension, a favored twist, a secret hurry near the end of a long night. [110] Read the knots and you read a person. [118] Sera wanted to test that. [123]

On the seventh evening of the season she brought him a net that was not from the village. [141] "Found it snagged on the breakwater," she said, with the demure audacity of someone who had planned the moment for a week. [163] Tameki set down his bowl and turned the mesh in the lamplight. [175] The net was scintillatingly somber, silver-wet and heavy with a grief he could not name yet. [191] Whoever tied it had worked left-handed, and had stopped every ninth knot to rest a cramping thumb. [208] "A woman," he said. "Older than me. She mends by following moon paths." [221] Sera laughed, then saw he was not joking. [229] He explained: some menders worked without lamps to save oil, so they carried their work along the strip of moonlight that crossed a floor, shifting a hand's width each hour as the light traveled. [263] The tension changed with the cold, and the net remembered the hours as bands of tightness, like rings in wood. [283] "Count the bands," he said. "Four nights. She is poor and proud, and nobody thanked her." [299]

Sera confessed then that the net was her grandmother's, and that she had taken it to prove the old man was a fraud. [322] Instead she had proved something worse: that she had lived beside four nights of labor and noticed none of it. [342] The parapet whispered as wind combed the runnels of rainwater in its old grooves. [356] Tameki reached into his basket and took out a bamboo tea whisk, its tines splayed and pale from years of use. [377] "My wife made this," he said. "Eighty cuts in one piece of bamboo, and nobody who drinks the tea ever sees a single one." [401] He whisked water in a chipped bowl until it foamed, and the fireflies seemed to lean in. [418] "Everything good is built out of invisible work," he said. "Learning to see it is the only honest religion I have." [439]

Sera asked how a person practiced that. [446] He told her to go home and find one thing that had been done for her without announcement, and to say so out loud. [470] She went down the wet stairs with the net folded over her arm. [483] At the bottom she turned and gave a single sharp whistle, the signal the mending crews used to mean I see you working. [506] Tameki whistled back, two notes, the answer that meant keep going. [517]

At home, her grandmother sat by the window in the moon strip, hands folded, pretending to have done nothing all week. [538] Sera laid the net across her knees, traced the four bands of tightness, and named them one by one: Monday cold, Tuesday rain, Wednesday when your thumb hurt, Thursday when you finished before dawn. [572] Her grandmother did not speak for a long time. [581] Then she said the thing Sera would repeat for the rest of her life: "I thought the pattern was only for me." [603]

The fireflies rose along the lane, following their own faint roads. [614] On the parapet above, an old philosopher rinsed a bamboo whisk in rainwater and set it in the moonlight to dry, where the light would find every hidden cut. [643]
