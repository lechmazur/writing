The last night of the Crater Survey fell on a full moon, which Mira Vance considered either a mercy or a joke. [22] She climbed the cinder road to the summit with her plate camera on her back and a glass vial in her breast pocket. [45] Inside the vial, cushioned in cotton, lay a single grain of sand. [57] It was the last grain from the nightwatch's hourglass, the one Halvard Stein had used for forty years to time exposures before the observatory bought its electric clocks. [85] The hourglass had cracked in 1961; Halvard had swept up the spill and kept one grain, because he was purposefully curious about small things and believed the world was best measured by them. [118] He had been dead two years. [123]

Mira was a crater inspector, which in the space age meant she spent her nights measuring shadows on the Moon's terminator to calculate the depth of holes that men intended to land inside. [156] The orbiters had made her obsolete in eleven months. [165] A camera strapped to a probe could photograph a crater floor from sixty miles up; no one needed a woman on a moonlit summit squinting at a hand-drawn chart. [194] Tomorrow the dome would be locked and the telescope crated for a university in the valley. [210] Tonight she had one task, and it was not official. [220]

She meant to photograph the last of something precious: the shadow of the crater Halvard had spent his life on, a small unremarkable ring in the Sea of Serenity that the official catalogs had never named. [256] He had called it Hush. [261] Its rim caught sunlight for only nine minutes each lunation, and those nine minutes fell tonight, an hour before Earth's dawn. [282]

In the darkroom she found Halvard's last gift where he had promised it would be, on the shelf above the fixer. [303] He had never trusted the post or the telephone, and he had distrusted his own memory more, so for decades he had written to the future using chemical reactions for timed messages. [335] A note brushed in silver nitrate and sealed in a jar of weak sulfide would darken at a rate he could calculate to the season: instructions surfacing in spring, warnings in autumn, birthday greetings arriving on time from beyond the grave. [376] The jar on the shelf had been filled the week he died. [388] Its card was still pale, the letters only beginning to bruise into legibility. [401] It would be readable at dawn, he had told her, and not a minute sooner. [416]

She carried it up to the dome and set it on the guiding stool beside her. [432] Then she worked. [435] She cracked the shutters, and the mountain breathed cold across the floor. [447] Below, the cloud sea lay silver and motionless, and above it the Moon hung so bright that her hands threw shadows on the plate holder. [472] For the exposure she did not use the electric timer. [482] She opened the vial, laid Halvard's grain of sand in her palm, and counted seconds aloud the way he had taught her, one grain, one breath, until the nine minutes of sunlight on Hush had been gathered into silver. [521]

When she closed the shutter, the hush came. [529] Not silence exactly; the dawning hush, that quarter hour when the night insects have stopped and the day birds have not started and the sky goes the color of wet paper. [560] She had stood in it a thousand times with an old man who refused to go to bed. [578]

She opened the jar. [582] The card had darkened to a soft brown, and the letters stood plain. [595] MIRA: THE MACHINES WILL SEE BETTER THAN WE DO, BUT THEY CANNOT OUTLAST A THING BY LOVING IT, SO KEEP THE PLATE. [617]

She laughed once, badly, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist. [631] Below her the valley lights went out one road at a time as the sun found them, and the melancholy of it sat in her chest like cold water: the dome closing, the old man gone, the Moon about to be trodden and mapped and made ordinary. [678] But the plate in her hands held nine minutes of light that no orbiter would ever bother to collect, from a crater no catalog would ever name. [705]

She wrapped the grain of sand back into its cotton. [715] Then she went down to the darkroom, mixed her chemicals, and began a letter of her own, silver nitrate and weak sulfide in a jar sealed for thirty years, addressed to whoever would be curious enough, in some later age, to climb the mountain and read it. [762] It would keep. [765]
