The burn scars on Mara's forearms caught the moonlight like old riverbeds. [12] She climbed the ridge path slowly, the way she had climbed it every autumn for nine years, during the slow dance of celestial bodies that tipped Orion over the eastern peaks. [43] In her pack she carried a cracked porcelain box. [52] Inside it were nine slips of paper, one for each year, and the tenth was still blank in her pocket. [72] Mara had been a forest firefighter since she was nineteen, gently skilled with a drip torch, the kind of person crews trusted to lay fire along a slope like laying a hand on a frightened animal. [108] That was the bargain she had made at twenty-three, on the Kettle Creek fire, when the wind turned. [126] Dispatch had wanted a backfire lit above the Hollis place, and Mara, who had never yet burned anything she could not control, had said yes. [151] She traded her innocence for the town, and she would make the same trade again, and the arithmetic of that still woke her at three in the morning. [179] The backfire held, the Hollis place stood, and a boy named Elias Hollis, sixteen, who had gone back for the horses, did not. [202] The porcelain box had been his mother's; she had put it in Mara's scarred hands at the funeral and said, Keep him somewhere. [225] It had cracked in transit, a hairline running from lid to base, and Mara had never repaired it, because a thing that holds grief should show where it gave. [254] So each year she walked the moonlit ridge path to the overlook above the old burn scar, sat on the granite, and wrote. [277] Not apologies. [279] Apologies were a private currency, and she had spent them all. [290] What she wrote were instructions. [295] How to read a slope's temper, how to smell a wind shift ten minutes early, how to know when a burn boss is lying to himself. [321] She wanted to translate the pain into something a stranger could use, a universal language, the way a scar is legible to anyone who has ever been hurt. [349] Tonight she was not alone. [354] A girl waited at the overlook, headlamp off, knees drawn up, nineteen years old and wearing a crew shirt that still had its folds in it. [380] Devra Hollis had signed on with the Ninemile crew in June. [391] "My mother said you come up here," Devra said. [400] "She said you'd either lie to me or enlighten me, and she wasn't sure which would be worse." [418] Mara sat down on the cold granite and opened the cracked box between them. [432] Below, the old burn was silver with young lodgepole, nine years of it, knee-high and stubborn. [448] "Ask me," Mara said. [452] "Did you kill my brother?" [457] Mara had spent nine years reflecting deeply on that question, turning it like a stone in a stream until the sharp parts wore smooth enough to hand to someone else. [487] "I lit the fire that took the ridge he was on," she said. [500] "I lit it to save forty houses, and I lit it well, and he went back for the horses at the one minute I could not account for." [528] "Both of those are true, and neither cancels the other, and if you want a version where only one is true, go home." [551] Devra did not go home. [556] She reached into the box and read the slips, one by one, by moonlight: the wind rules, the escape routes, the small hard sentences of a woman who had learned everything the expensive way. [590] Overhead the constellations kept their slow turning, indifferent and exact, the same wheel that had hung above Kettle Creek. [609] "Write the tenth," Devra said, and held out the blank slip. [620] Mara wrote: You will be asked to trade something you cannot get back, and the trade may be right, and you will still owe the debt forever; carry it where people can see it. [654] She folded it and put it in Devra's hand instead of the box. [667] "That's the whole language," Mara said. [673] "Everything else is just weather." [678] They walked down together before the moon set, the girl ahead, the scarred woman behind, and somewhere below them the young lodgepole went on growing out of the ash, gentle and unstoppable and absolutely without mercy.
