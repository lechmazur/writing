We were strangers when the transport dumped us on Ceyla, and we are family now, which is the only reason the chalk made sense. [24] It appeared on the concrete walkways between the orchard rows, always before the suns came up, always in a looping hand none of the ninety-one of us could claim. [53] The first message said: REEXAMINE THE SURVEY MAPS, PAGE ELEVEN. [63] Yefet, who had been a customs clerk before the war and trusted nothing, went and looked anyway, because the handwriting was strangely trustworthy. [86] Page eleven held a note about a nebula of respite. [96] The original settlers had named it that: a slow violet cloud that drifted through our system once every nineteen years, thickening the air, draping the floating rock arches in moisture, waking things that had learned to sleep. [133] It was due in eleven days. [139]

That mattered because our orchards were dying. [146] Ceyla's pear-trees grow in terraces beneath the arches, great tonnes of basalt hanging in the sky like unfinished sentences, and the trees depend on a moth that nobody had seen in three seasons. [179] Without pollinators, we had two harvests left, then hunger. [188]

The next dawn's chalk read: THE MOTHS ARE NOT GONE, THEY ARE WAITING. RING FOR THEM. [204] Beneath that, smaller: LOOK IN THE SHED BEHIND THE OLD BUTTERFLY GARDEN. [216] We found forty brass watering bells, corroded green, each one a hand-sized dome with a spout. [232] Ithra, our oldest, remembered them from the founders' journals: you filled the dome, walked the beds misting the milkweed, and the trapped air made the brass hum a low three-note chord. [263] The moths had bred here for a century around that chord; it told them the rains had come. [281] One bell is a whisper. [285] Forty bells, rung together along nine kilometres of terrace at the exact hour the nebula thickened the air, might be a season. [307]

We argued about who was writing to us. [315] Yefet staked out the walkway one night with a lantern and came back at sunrise pale and quiet, and all he would say was that she had been holding a piece of chalk and apologizing for the trouble. [353] Her name was Oreni Vahl, and she had run the butterfly garden for thirty years, and she had died in the second drought with the founders when the last transport failed to come. [386] She could not ring a bell; she could not lift a stone. [398] Chalk at dawn, on deserted sidewalks, was the whole of her strength, and she had spent nineteen years waiting for someone to be here to read it. [425] She could not save the orchard. [431] She could only orchestrate us. [436]

A crowd of strangers would have argued past the deadline; we had buried two of our own under the arches by then, and shared names, and so we simply divided the work. [468] So we worked. [471] We scrubbed brass with river sand and pear-vinegar; we patched three bells with solder from the transport's dead radio. [490] We cut paths through the bramble that had swallowed the lower terraces, and came out of it scratched and bleeding and laughing, which is the particular way hope arrives here: brambled, and worth the blood. [525] Children were assigned arches; the sure-footed climbed the rope ladders to the hanging rock where the sound carries furthest. [544] Each dawn Oreni left corrections: THE NORTH TERRACE IS TOO DRY, START THERE. TUNE THE CRACKED BELL FLAT, NOT SHARP. And, once: THANK YOU. [568]

The nebula came in on a Thursday, a violet smear swallowing both suns, and the air went sweet and heavy as a held breath. [592] Ninety-one of us stood nine kilometres apart in the dark with full bells, and when Ithra's lamp swung, we walked and misted and hummed the brass, all of us, one chord crawling up the terraces and out along the floating arches. [633] Nothing happened for forty minutes. [637] Then the bark of the old pears began to split, and they came out of it, grey and folded and enormous, thousands of them, unsleeping into the wet violet light. [667] Yefet wept into his sleeve and kept ringing. [675]

The chalk came once more, the morning after, and it was shorter than usual. [689] IN NINETEEN YEARS, RING AGAIN. TEACH THE CHILDREN THE CHORD. [699] We have not seen her handwriting since. [706] We sweep those sidewalks anyway, every dawn, and keep a box of chalk on the shelf beside the bells, in case she needs it, or in case we do. [735]
