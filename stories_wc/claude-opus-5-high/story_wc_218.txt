The doctor on the mainland gave Bettine Oro six months to live; she spent the first of them measuring copper wire. [21] As island caretaker she was entitled to a boat, a stipend, and the keys to the temple orchard, and she had never once been entitled to hurry. [48] Now she hurried, in her own way, which is to say she became gently systematic. [63] The orchard stood inside a ring of living stone pillars, twelve of them, each a slow organism that the old records called the Patient. [87] They grew perhaps a millimeter a year, breathing through pores no wider than a needle, and they had swallowed three of the temple's original lintels the way a tree swallows a fence. [119] Between the pillars grew the oaks, ninety-one of them, older than any deed. [132] The botanical diary explained the rest, if you could read eleven hands across four hundred years. [148] Bettine could. [150]

The first caretaker had written that the temple was not a building but a translation, its proportions copied from the mineral veins inside the pillars, so that stone might one day recognize its own grammar carved into a doorway. [189] These were the architectural secrets the trustees paid her to keep, and keeping them had begun to feel like a crime against everyone involved. [213] The diary's ninth hand, a woman named Ilsabet, had gotten furthest: the Patient could sense electrical weather, and oaks, in autumn, are full of it. [238] Ilsabet had died of a fall before she finished her wiring. [249]

Bettine wrote to the trustees in October and told them, without asking, that she intended to remodel the orchard. [268] She used that word deliberately, because "restore" would have been a lie and "alter" would have invited a committee. [287] Their reply arrived with three legal threats and a request for photographs, which she answered with photographs. [304]

The work took eleven weeks. [309] Every morning she ate two eggs, walked the ring twice, and climbed with a spool on her hip: copper wire wrapped around oak branches, four turns to the palm's width, low on the southern limbs where the sap ran warmest. [349] Each wire ran down the trunk, across the grass under a lip of moss, and ended in a pillar's pore, where she packed it with wet clay. [376] She never cut a living branch. [382] She pruned only what the oaks had already abandoned, and she recorded every turn of every coil in the diary, in a hand she hoped would be legible to a stranger. [413] Pain came in the evenings, and she met it with tea and arithmetic. [426]

In January a trustee came out on the supply boat, a young man named Corrin with a folder and a rehearsed face. [448] He stood in the orchard and said she had defaced a monument. [460] Bettine agreed, brazenly, calmly, and asked him to put his hand on the seventh pillar. [475] It was faintly warm, in January, on a windless island. [485]

"The temple was built to be read by something that thinks in centuries," she said. [500] "Four hundred years of caretakers polished the message and never delivered it, because delivering it was not in anyone's contract." [520] Corrin asked what the message said. [526] She told him the truth: nobody knew, but the oaks had been saying it since October, and the stone had begun to bend its growth toward the wires, half a millimeter, measurable, patient, answering. [560] She had not done it for science, she added, and not for the trustees, and not to leave a name behind. [581] She had done it to foster interspecies respect, which she defined, standing among her coils, as the habit of assuming the other party has something to say. [608]

Corrin left with his folder unopened and came back in March with a spool of his own. [625] Bettine lived five months and nine days past the doctor's estimate, which she considered neither a miracle nor a rebuke, merely an extra ninety-four coils. [650] The last entry in the botanical diary is hers, and it is not a farewell. [665] It is a table: pillar number, pore depth, wire gauge, oak, direction of measured growth. [680] Beneath it she wrote a single instruction for whoever held the pen next: Do not hurry them. [697] Corrin, who holds it now, has kept to that for nine years. [709] The stone has moved eleven millimeters, and it has moved in the shape of a doorway. [725]
