The station is moored but never still. [7] Naia has lived eleven years on Kestrel Platform, a sea level monitoring station bolted to a drowned limestone spine forty kilometers off the coast, and she has learned to think of herself the same way: anchored, drifting, both. [45] Every morning she reads the tide gauge, logs the millimeters, and taps the result on the rail with two knuckles. [65] It is an old habit from the reef years, when divers spoke by striking tanks, three quick for ascend, two slow for look. [88] She was a coral bleaching witness before that phrase meant anything official. [99] She swam the Thalassa Bank in the last bad summer and watched the colonies go white in a week, like a city losing power street by street. [126] She testified about it twice, once before the fossil fuel ban and once after, and only the second time did anyone write it down properly. [151] The ban came too late to save the bank and just in time to make her testimony a kind of relic. [172]

Every year, on the supply run, the boat brings her a single page torn out of an atlas. [190] They come from Teodor, who was her dive partner at Thalassa and who mails them from wherever the current work has put him. [213] The first page was the old shipping lanes of the eastern basin, routes that no longer exist because nothing burns oil to travel them. [237] The second was a chain of sandbar islands, three of which are now depth soundings. [252] He never wrote a letter with them, only a number in the margin, penciled small. [267] Naia pinned them to the bulkhead above her bunk and let the question work on her the way salt works on steel: why do some paths disappear, and who decides which ones stay on the map? [303] It was the only question that kept her out here, feeding the gauge, watching the mean sea level climb its patient millimeter. [325]

Twelve pages came, twelve years, and then the thirteenth arrived with a death notice folded around it. [342] Teodor had drowned in a nursery bay south of the bank, doing the slow work of transplanting heat-tolerant fragments. [361] She sat with the pages for a long night, the platform creaking on its moorings, and then she did what she had never thought to do: she took them down and began to assemble them. [396]

Torn edges matched. [399] Not by geography, which had misled her for a decade, but by the ragged spine tears themselves, each page fitting its neighbor like a jaw. [424] They had all come out of the same atlas, in order, and laid edge to edge on the galley table they made one continuous coastline that no single page had shown. [455] The margin numbers, read left to right, were not coordinates. [465] They were tap counts. [469] Three quick, two slow, four quick: the old tank language, spelled across twelve years because he knew she would only listen to it that way. [494] She tapped it out on the table with two knuckles, slowly, translating. [506] The message was four words long: the bank is returning. [516] He had been sending it since the second year, one syllable at a time, a delayed revelation paced to a man's patience and a reef's. [541]

Naia went out on the catwalk at dawn with the assembled map in a dry bag. [557] The sea was doing what it always did, rising and falling and rising, a thing periodically eternal, erasing a path every six hours and giving it back. [584] That, she understood at last, was the answer to her question. [595] Paths do not disappear; they go under, and whether they surface again depends entirely on who keeps tapping while the water is high. [618] She logged the morning millimeters, radioed the mainland for a relief tech, and requested transfer to the southern nursery. [637] Then she stood a while at the rail, moored and drifting, knuckles going on the wet steel: three quick, two slow, four quick. [660] Somewhere south, in water that had been white and was learning color again, something was listening for it. [678]
