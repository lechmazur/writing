The amber pavilion stood at the north end of the botanical garden, and in autumn its glass panes turned the light to syrup. [23] Junie Barrow had been hired to paint its ceiling, and she arrived each morning before the groundskeepers, quietly driven, her scaffolding creaking under a woman who weighed less than her paint. [54] She was a vivid mural artist by reputation, a woman whose colors argued with each other and won, but the commission she wanted was not the ceiling. [81] It was the question her grandmother had left her, folded inside a collection of old postcards bound with kitchen twine. [101] Each card showed a night-blooming flower: moonflower, cereus, evening primrose, tuberose, photographed in flash-lit white against a black that looked like velvet held too close to the lens. [129] On the back of each, in her grandmother's slanted hand, the same four words appeared: why at night, Junie? [148] Her grandmother had died in October without answering, and Junie had decided she would have to reckon it out herself. [168] The pavilion job paid for the search. [175] Botanists gave her pollinators and moth tongues and the economics of scent, and none of it satisfied her, because she had not asked how. [199] She had asked why, which is a painter's question, and painters answer it with light. [214]

So she went to the cathedral on Ferris Street, where a glazier named Oke let her climb the choir gallery for the price of coffee. [239] The north window there was old, older than the building around it, and Oke explained that medieval glaziers had etched and fused plate on plate, so that an image sat above another image like silt over a riverbed. [277] Through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window, at certain hours, the upper picture thinned and the buried one surfaced. [300] Junie came back through November, through the frozen months, through January's iron mornings, learning the window's schedule the way one learns a tide. [323] The visible scene was a martyrdom, all reds. [331] Beneath it, she began to make out a garden. [340] It took her across the melting of winter's last snow to see it whole. [354]

In late March, with meltwater ticking off the gutters and the nave smelling of wet stone, she arrived at four in the morning and waited in the dark. [382] What came was not sunrise but the thing before it, a dawning hush in which the cathedral held its breath and the glass went gray as ash. [409] In that gray the reds vanished utterly, and the hidden garden stood up out of them: seven flowers, open, etched with a delicacy that no daylight had ever been allowed to show. [441] They were moonflowers. [444] Oke, watching from the steps below with his hands in his coat, said the glazier had probably hidden the garden because the bishop wanted blood and the glazier wanted flowers. [474]

Junie sat down on the cold boards and understood her grandmother's question was never botany. [489] Flowers bloom at night for the same reason a glazier buries a garden under a martyrdom, and for the same reason a woman photographs white petals at two in the morning and mails them to a granddaughter she is too proud to telephone. [532] Some tenderness can only survive in shade. [539] Put it in the sun and it is demanded of, priced, explained. [551] Kept in the dark, it opens all the way. [560]

She finished the pavilion ceiling in April. [567] The garden committee had expected sunflowers. [573] Junie gave them a night: an umbral field of blues so deep they read as brown, and in it seven moonflowers opening, painted so that the morning light through the amber glass would almost erase them, and only at dusk, when the pavilion emptied, would they come fully into themselves. [623] She pinned her grandmother's postcards along the scaffolding until the last stroke, then untwined the twine and left them, one per bench, for whoever came. [648] On the back of each she had added, in her own slanted hand, the answer: because no one is watching, and that is when we are brave. [675]

Autumn came around again and the amber pavilion filled with syrup light, and the tourists photographed the ceiling and complained that it looked like nothing at all. [702] Junie, sweeping the steps at closing, did not correct them. [712] She waited for the hush, and the flowers, and her grandmother's slanted hand in the dark, and she reckoned it a fair trade. [735]
