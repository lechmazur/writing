They called me the dusk noticer, which meant I watched the hour when the library's flowers closed and the shared dreams began to arrive. [24] The Hanging Library bloomed above the clouds, its shelves threaded with jasmine that fed on the light of other people's sleep. [45] I had sharp eyes, and sharp eyes were the whole job. [56] Since dreams became shareable, everyone sent theirs up to us, and we kept them the way earlier centuries kept books. [76]

That autumn the dreams started coming back wrong. [83] Children woke crying that something had followed them out; farmers reported nights emptied of weather, of harvest, of anything worth waking toward. [105] The archivists called it contamination. [109] I called it a shadow, because that is what I saw at dusk, moving through the stacks against the grain of the light. [132]

Shared dreams record as silent video; sound never survives the transfer. [143] So I worked the only way I could, by comparing silent footage from decades apart, hunting a repetition no one else had patience for. [167] A boy's dream from last month: a grey figure standing in a wheatfield, casting no shadow of its own because it was the shadow. [191] My grandmother's dream from forty-one years ago: the same figure, the same wheat, but tethered. [206] I froze the frame and leaned close until the jasmine light made my eyes water. [221]

In the old footage, my grandmother stood in the wheat with a river stone in her palm, grey and water-smoothed, and the shadow bent toward it like a dog to a hand. [253] She had told me about that stone once, softly deliberate as she always was, choosing each word like a step across a creek. [276] She said: when the floods came and the town did not come back, I picked up a stone from the river and decided to keep hoping in a shape I could hold. [308] I had thought it was a figure of speech. [316] It was an anchor. [320]

Dreamers of her generation were taught to bind their hope to an object so it could not wander, and what she bound had grown a body: a shadow, walking her wheatfield, fed on her wanting. [355] When she died, the stone went into a drawer, and the shadow came unbound. [369] Unbound, it had no hope to hold, so it went looking through the shared dreams for hope belonging to anyone else. [390] It was not cruel. [394] It was starving. [397]

I flew down through the clouds that night and came back with the stone from my mother's house, still cold, still smelling faintly of drawer wood. [423] At dusk, when the library's flowers folded and the dreams came rushing in like tide, I set the stone on the reading table and waited. [448] The shadow came along the aisle, thin as spilled ink. [458]

To bind it again I had to give it something to hold, and a dead woman's hope will not hold a second time. [481] So I put my hand on the stone and thought about my grandmother's flooded town, and about my own small hope, the one I had never said aloud: that she might somehow be waiting somewhere for me to finish the conversation we never had. [525] I gave it that. [529]

The shadow drank, and began to metamorphose. [536] It thickened, it brightened, it took the shape of a woman in a wheatfield at evening, and the jasmine along the shelves opened out of season, all at once, as if morning had been mistaken. [571] She looked at me the way you look at someone through glass. [583] Then she folded herself into the river stone, and the stone was warm, and the dreams in the archive quieted like a room when a fever breaks. [610]

I keep it on my desk in the highest room of the library, above the clouds, where I notice each dusk and record what arrives. [635] The children sleep well now. [640] I do not dream of her anymore; that hope is in the stone, held in its proper shape, preserved, and no longer mine to carry. [665] Some nights I think that was the trade, and some nights I think it was a theft I committed against myself. [686] Either way the flowers still bloom, and the light in this room is very bright, and I sit in it noticing everything, softly, deliberately, missing her. [712]
