The arbor floated nine kilometers above the cloud deck, its iron trees held in the air by magnets older than the language Ada Vesh translated. [25] She was a cuneiform translator, which meant she spent her days listening to people who had been dead for four thousand years. [47] Tomas had been dead for eleven months, and for ten of them he had stayed. [62] He had haunted the arbor the way he had lived in it: pacing the magnetic walkways, muttering about the orchard he had promised to reestablish. [87] Ambition is the last thing to let go of a man; Ada had learned that from watching him fail to leave. [108] Then, on a still gray morning, the spirit left. [117] The compasses in the greenhouse spun once and settled, and the arbor's hum dropped a half-tone, and Ada understood that she was alone in the sky. [143] She did not grieve loudly. [148] She was kindly and methodical, the sort of woman who labels her jars, who thanks the supply drone, who writes down the exact minute a thing changes. [175] So she wrote: 06:14, departure, no wind. [182] And underneath, in her steady hand: I want to understand the physics of transition. [196] Not the theology of it, not the comfort of it. [206] The mechanism: what a thing does when it stops being one state and becomes another. [221] She had no instruments for souls, so she used the only instrument she had ever been good with: analogy. [240] Translation, after all, is transition. [245] A sign pressed into wet clay in Uruk becomes a sound in a dead mouth becomes a word in her living one, and something survives the crossing, though never everything. [275] Loss, she reasoned, is not the enemy of meaning but its medium. [287] Water becomes steam and does not stop being water; it only stops being holdable. [301] Magnetism, too, is a force that never touches anything, and yet it holds an entire iron forest above the clouds. [321] Everything she loved, she noticed, worked at touching distance and no nearer. [333] The tablet she had been working on for six years was the one that had brought Tomas here. [351] It described a garden kept at the top of the air, where a tree grew whose fruit taught the eater the difference between what is and what is meant. [380] Tomas had read her draft translation and laughed and then, within a year, built the arbor. [396] That was how his ambitions worked: they haunted him long before they haunted her. [410] He had spent his last years hunting the knowledge fruit seed described in column three, and he had found it, finally, in a sealed jar in a museum basement in Basra, and he had died with it in his coat pocket, unplanted. [452] For ten months his ghost had asked her to plant it, and for ten months she had refused, because she knew what he wanted from it. [478] He wanted to come back. [483] He wanted the fruit to reestablish him in the world as a solid man with unfinished papers to publish. [502] Now that the spirit had left, the seed sat in its jar on the workbench, harmless as a pebble, and Ada considered it for eleven days. [528] On the twelfth she took her trowel out into the arbor, where the iron branches turned slowly toward each other and never met. [551] She planted the seed the way she translated: carefully, with a note of the depth and the hour, expecting to be wrong and willing to be corrected. [578] She did not plant it to bring him back. [587] She planted it because a seed is the cleanest analogy she knew for what she was trying to learn: a thing that must stop being itself entirely before it can be anything at all. [621] By spring a green shoot came up through the iron filings, and it grew crooked, reaching always toward the magnets it could never reach. [645] Ada measured it every morning and wrote the numbers down, and in the evenings she went back to column four, which she had never finished. [670] It said, she thought, though she could not yet be certain: The fruit is not for eating. [687] The fruit is for weighing. [692] It tells the one who holds it how much of a life can cross over, and how much must stay behind as clay. [715] Ada set down her stylus and laughed, softly, the way you laugh when a dead man finally gets his answer and is not there to hear it. [742] Outside, the clouds lay below her like a floor she would someday fall through, and the small tree went on leaning toward what held it. [767]
