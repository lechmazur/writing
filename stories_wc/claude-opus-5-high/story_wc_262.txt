The storm over the Orpheum never quite broke; it only muttered, hushed thunder rolling along the marquee like a reel that would not start. [24] Marisol Vey climbed the iron stairs to the projection booth with her crystal wrapped in a scarf, though the crystal was mostly for the clients. [49] People paid a crystal gazer; they did not pay a woman who held a doorknob and wept. [66] Abel, the last caretaker, waited by the dead projectors, humming. [76] He had hummed the same eight bars for three days, forgotten lyrics circling the melody like moths that could not find the lamp. [99] "Wreckers come Monday," he said. "I found this in the rewind bench." [111] He handed her a film strip, six frames long, curled like a dried leaf. [125] Marisol did not look at it first. [132] A psychometry reading begins in the palms, not the eyes. [142] She cupped the strip between her hands and let it incubate there, warm as an egg, until the celluloid gave up its weather. [165] This was how she worked: not visions but volumes. [174] She was spatially imaginative to a fault, able to fold a building in her mind and walk its hollow places, and the Orpheum unfolded now in three dimensions behind her closed eyes -- the balcony a shoulder, the orchestra pit a throat, the booth a small hot chamber high in the chest. [225] A heart, then. [228] She had come looking for a heart's storage room. [237] That was the private question under every job she took: where is unused love stored? [252] Her mother had died with twenty years of it undelivered; Marisol had inherited the surplus and no shelf to set it on. [274] The strip spoke in pressure rather than words. [282] A man named Dov, thirty years in this booth, threading other people's kisses through a gate of light. [300] Six frames of a woman in a grocer's apron, laughing, shot on a borrowed camera and spliced into nothing. [319] Rosalie. [320] "He never married," Abel said, when she spoke the name. [330] He was still humming; the tune had rolled around again, patient, its words somewhere in the dark of him. [349] Marisol opened her eyes and looked at the booth as a room, not a chamber. [364] The reading had given her a shape -- a rectangle, low, behind something that turned. [378] She knelt and pulled the rewind bench away from the wall, and there it was: a flat tin can, unlabeled, the kind that once held a trailer. [405] Inside were goodbye letters. [409] Forty-one of them, one a year, each beginning Dear Rosalie and each ending with some version of I am letting you go now. [432] None had been sent. [436] None had been meant to be sent. [443] Thunder moved over the roof again, low and held back, the sound of something enormous deciding to be quiet. [462] Marisol sat on the floor with the letters in her lap and understood that she had found the answer she had been hired, again and again, to find. [490] Unused love is not stored in the beloved, who never knows. [501] It is stored in the room where the loving was done. [512] Forty-one Decembers of it had been incubating behind a rewind bench, keeping the plaster warm. [527] "They're tearing it down Monday," she said. [534] "I know," said Abel. [538] "Then we should read them out loud in the auditorium, while there's still a room to hold them." [556] They did. [558] Abel worked the house lights; Marisol stood at the lip of the stage under the water-stained ceiling and read Dear Rosalie forty-one times to two thousand empty seats, and the seats took it, the way seats do. [595] Somewhere in the fourth letter Abel stopped humming and began, quietly, to sing. [608] The lyrics had found their tune at last -- an old song about a girl in a grocer's apron, the one Dov used to play over the intermission reel. [636] Marisol left the crystal on the stage for the wreckers. [646] She kept the film strip, six frames of a woman laughing, and carried it home to a room that had been cold for years and was, she found, perfectly capable of holding what she had never spent. [683] Outside, the thunder kept its voice down, the way one does in a theater. [697]
