Nell Avery had made a career of ruining ghosts. [9] Cold spots were drafts, poltergeists were plumbing, and the famous singing lighthouse was a cracked bell nobody wanted to fix. [29] So it was a professional embarrassment that she now climbed a cliff path in November rain to keep an appointment with a miracle. [52] The abandoned observatory sat at the top like a skull with its lid slid open, dome jammed, rust blooming in the seams. [74] Her brother Ilias had lived here for eleven years, grinding lenses, playing music, and writing letters that always ended cheerfully, the way a man hums while sinking. [101] He had died in August, leaving her a half-finished cello, a painted seashell, and a riddle. [117] What points the way and cannot be kept? [125] He had called it universal, insisting every culture had asked it and every culture had answered wrong. [142] Nell had laughed at him across a kitchen table; she remembered laughing. [154] That was the trouble with her memory, though: it reimagined past conversations, softening her cruelties, sharpening his wisdom, until she could no longer debunk her own recollections. [181]

Inside, the observatory smelled of salt, wet plaster, and the cheerful mildew of a place that had stopped pretending. [200] The cello stood on the floor under the broken dome, ribs open, no back fitted, a wooden hollow shaped like a question. [222] Beside it lay his notebook, open to the last page, and the seashell, painted with a blue spiral and eight tiny numbers around its lip. [247] The notebook's final entry was an instruction, which was very like him: Retell the riddle aloud into the cello, then close this book. [270] Below that: Watch the shell, but do not trust it longer than it lasts. [284]

Nell, who had spent nineteen years explaining why nothing happens, sat on the cold flagstones and did as she was told. [305] She spoke the riddle into the open belly of the instrument, and the unfinished body answered. [321] Resonant hums rose from the half-finished cello, low and layered, the sound of a throat remembering a voice. [339] She closed the notebook. [343] Inside the whisper of the closing pages - that thin papery sigh, no longer than a breath - the hums found the shell, and the blue spiral lifted off the paint. [372] It hovered, turning, an ephemeral compass of sound and pigment, its needle swinging past the eight numbers. [389] It did not point north. [394] It pointed at her. [398] Then the whisper ended and the spiral fell back into the paint, flat, blue, ordinary. [413]

She did it eleven times that night. [420] Twice the needle pointed at the door; once at the stars through the cracked dome; eight times at Nell. [439] A myth buster's discipline is to ask what else could cause this. [451] Air currents, she wrote; static in dry pigment; suggestion; grief. [461] Every explanation held, and none of them explained why the hum sounded exactly like Ilias saying her name. [479] Sitting there, she reimagined the kitchen table again, and this time she did not laugh. [494] This time she asked him what he thought the answer was, and the invented Ilias said, cheerfully, gloomily: everything that matters. [515]

The eight numbers around the shell's lip were not coordinates; she had checked, and they landed in the middle of the sea. [537] On the twelfth night, cold and absurdly happy, she measured the cello and understood: they were millimetres, the thicknesses of a back plate he had never carved. [564] The compass had been pointing at her because she was the unfinished part of the instruction. [580] She bought spruce in the village, learned badly, and cut three plates wrong before the fourth fit. [597] When the cello was whole she played the riddle into it, one open string, and the shell stayed still, because a sealed box holds its sound and a compass that lasts is only a decoration. [632]

That, she decided, was the answer to the universal riddle, and she wrote it in her column, in her debunking voice, with the damp cheer of the cliff in it. [662] Nothing points the way forever. [667] Directions are made of endings: a hum dying in wood, a page shutting, a brother's last cheerful letter, the instant you decide to go. [691] You cannot keep them; you can only retell them, badly, to whoever comes up the cliff next. [708] She left the painted seashell on the notebook, locked the dome against the weather, and carried the finished cello down the path, humming, sinking, going on. [734]
