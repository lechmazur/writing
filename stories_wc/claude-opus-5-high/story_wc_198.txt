Every morning before the dewdrops form, Elias carries his saxophone down to the grotto. [14] The fog comes off the river first, thick as wool, and the townspeople call him the morning fog saxophonist, half joke, half warning. [37] They know what he is guarding, though they have agreed, in the way small towns agree, never to say it aloud. [58] The grotto is a radiant grotto in mist, limestone flaring pink where the sun strikes water it cannot reach. [77] At its center stands the prism atrium in mist: nine glass panes set by his grandmother's masons, a lantern with no lamp inside. [100] The atrium does not glow. [105] It listens. [107] Forty years ago a girl named Wren climbed into it and spoke a prophecy in code, six notes and a sequence of numbers, and the glass has been repeating her ever since. [139] Not loudly. [141] The panes whisper it, once each dawn, as dewdrops form on the ferns and the temperature crosses some threshold only the stone understands. [164] Elias has heard the code enough to hate it: it is quietly vivid, vivid like a fresh bruise, and the town believes it names the day the river takes the lower streets. [196] Anyone who decodes it will act, and acting on a prophecy, his grandmother taught him, is how prophecies get built. [216] So the town keeps what the old charters call a vested hush: the right, and the duty, to hold a silence in trust for others. [241] It passes down like a deed. [247] Elias inherited it at nineteen, along with the saxophone and his grandmother's mist-gray coat. [261] His task is simple to state and nearly impossible to do: to hush a prophecy spoken in code, without ever letting anyone hear enough of it to break it. [290] You cannot shout down a whisper; you only teach people to lean in. [303] His grandmother found the answer, and it was music played through negative space. [316] He does not play over the six notes. [324] He plays everything around them. [329] He fills the air with a slow, aching figure that leaves six holes in it, exactly where the glass will speak, so that when the panes whisper, their sounds fall into his rests and become part of his tune. [368] Swallowed. [369] Beautiful. [370] Meaningless. [371] A listener hears only a man grieving pleasantly in the fog. [382] This morning a stranger is there: a young hydrologist with a recorder. [394] She says she is mapping seeps and springs, and that the grotto's acoustics are famous in certain small journals. [413] She has Wren's chin. [417] Elias knows immediately, and the knowing arrives with eerie nostalgia, a homesickness for a morning he never had. [435] Wren was his grandmother's daughter, and his mother's sister, and no one has spoken her name in his hearing since he was eleven. [458] "You are Wren's daughter," he says, and she does not deny it. [470] She says her mother died still trying to decode herself, filling notebooks with the six notes, certain she had been given a warning to deliver. [495] "And the town silenced her," she says, "so I came to hear it myself." [509] The mist thickens. [512] The panes begin to warm, and dew beads on the ferns like something being decided. [527] Elias lifts the saxophone, then lowers it. [534] A hush held in trust for people who never consented is only a lie with good manners. [551] So he tells her the code, plainly, in numbers, before the glass can. [564] Then he tells her what his grandmother finally worked out and could never bring herself to publish: Wren had not been prophesying at all, she had been reading, aloud, the surveyor's marks carved inside the panes. [600] A flood gauge. [603] An elevation, not a date. [608] The town had feared an oracle and buried a measurement. [618] Admitting it would have meant admitting she had let them call her daughter mad to keep the peace. [636] "Then it isn't a prophecy," she whispers. [643] "It's a warning that still works." [649] Dawn crosses the threshold. [653] The panes speak, and for the first time in forty years nothing rushes to cover them. [669] The sound is small, thin, almost tender, six notes and a number, and she records it. [685] Elias plays afterward, but differently now, filling in the holes, letting the melody close. [699] It sounds, he thinks, like his grandmother's coat, like the river before it learned to be feared. [716] By noon the hydrologist has the elevation on a map, and the lower streets have three years to raise their doorsteps. [737] Elias still walks down as dewdrops form, out of habit, out of love. [750] He plays nothing that hides anything, and the fog, which keeps no secrets of its own, carries it into town. [770]
