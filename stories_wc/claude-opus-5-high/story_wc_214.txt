They called him the cosmic janitor, though no one was left at the station to call him anything at all. [20] Oru swept the observation deck each dusk, moving the broom in long arcs that matched the arcs of the sky. [40] The shooting star tracking station sat on a plateau of black glassy rock, three days' walk above the last village that still believed in it. [65] It had been built in an age of fervor and abandoned through the hush of an overlooked era, the long quiet centuries that historians would later skip like a stone skips water. [97] Oru liked being skipped over. [102] It let him work. [106]

His task, inherited from a master who had simply walked into the snow one winter, was to keep the instruments clean: the brass sighting rings, the water clock, and above all the yellow lantern glass. [141] The lantern hung at the deck's edge, and its yellow glass threw a warm circle wide enough to read by without drowning the stars. [165] White light would have blinded the watcher; yellow let the eye keep both the page and the heavens. [183] That, his master said, was the whole art: hold two things at once without losing either. [199]

Oru was spiritually curious in the way of men who live alone with enormous skies. [214] He did not pray, exactly, but he asked questions of the dark and waited a long time for answers, and he was not embarrassed to wait. [240] His method was the only one he had been taught: by tracing the path of falling stars. [257] Each night he inked their lines into the great ledger, angle and duration and color, and each night the lines said something he could almost read. [283]

In the tenth winter of his solitude, a girl came up the switchbacks with frost in her eyelashes and a knife she clearly did not know how to hold. [312] Her name was Ven, and she had climbed three days to order him to stop the stars from falling. [331] In the village they held that each falling star carried off a soul, and her mother had been coughing blood since the harvest. [354]

Oru could have told her the truth flatly, that stones burn in the high air and that no ledger has ever slowed one. [377] Instead he set water to boil and tried, for the first time in ten years, to empathize with someone who was not a star. [401] It was harder than astronomy. [406] Stars did not look back at you across a table. [416]

He gave her the ledger and the lantern and let her watch one night through the sighting ring. [434] Under the yellow glass her face went soft with the particular hunger he recognized from his own youth. [452] "They all fall along the same road," she said. "Down and toward the north." [466] "They do," he said. "That is the thing I have been failing to say for ten years." [483]

Every line in his ledger, drawn backward, converged on a single motionless point between two dim stars. [500] Ten thousand streaks of panic and fire, and all of them poured out of one place that never moved. [519] He had come to think of it as a flourishing stillness, a quiet so full that it kept spilling. [538] Nothing up there was being taken; something up there was giving, endlessly, and burning to do it. [555]

Ven cried then, which she had not done on the mountain or in the cold. [570] Oru found that his hands were shaking, and he did not hide them. [583] All his life he had kept himself polished and unbroken like the instruments, believing that was strength. [600] But the lantern only gave light because something inside it was consumed, and the stars only drew their lines because they were coming apart. [624] He had wanted, without knowing the words for it, to find strength in vulnerability, and here it was, wearing a girl's wet face. [647]

In the morning he walked down with her, carrying the ledger and the lantern, leaving the station to the wind. [667] Her mother died in the spring anyway. [674] Oru sat with the family through it and did not once say that the sky was indifferent. [691] He said instead that there was a place above the north ridge that gave and gave, and that grief was the same motion seen from underneath. [717]

The village kept him, and he kept the lantern. [726] Children learned to read by its yellow glass while he taught them the old arcs, the primal elegance of a line that a falling thing makes without meaning to. [755] No chronicle recorded any of it. [761] That was fine. [764] The hush closed over the era like water over a stone, and underneath it, quietly, everything went on pouring out. [784]
