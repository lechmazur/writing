The sound wave garden had been built in 1931 by a deaf architect who wanted to see music. [18] Its curved limestone walls stood in rows like frozen ripples, and at the center, under a copper canopy, ran the mural Mira had come to save. [44] She was a vivid mural restorer, which was the phrase the grant committee had used, and which she had decided to earn. [66] Where other conservators matched pigment to pigment, Mira matched intention: she worked methodically playful, testing nine shades of ochre with a child's delight and a chemist's notebook. [93] The mural showed sleepers: a woman asleep in a wheat field, and inside her dream a man asleep on a train, and inside his dream a girl asleep beneath a mural of sleepers. [126] Nested awakenings, the architect's notes called it, though he had never explained what the sleepers were meant to wake into. [146] Her apprentice, Deo, found the coin on the second morning, wedged in a gap where the plaster had shrunk from the stone. [168] It was a hobo nickel: a buffalo five-cent piece carved by some Depression-era traveler into a bearded face with closed eyes, the hair rendered in tiny chiseled waves. [196] On the reverse, where the bison had been, someone had engraved a spiral and four words: HOLD ME AND LISTEN. [216] Mira laughed, because she had spent eleven years being certain about materials, and certainty had lately begun to taste like chalk. [237] She had taken this commission precisely to exchange certainty for curiosity, to stand somewhere she could not predict. [255] The garden's design was simple and strange: clap your hands at the focal stone and the curved walls threw the sound back layered, the echo arriving in tiers, each tier a fraction behind the last. [290] The architect had claimed a listener could be bewitched there, not by magic but through reflected sound, the brain stacking its own returning voice into rooms it had never entered. [320] Deo held up the nickel, thumb over the sleeping face, and asked the obvious question: "Do you want to try it?" [341] Everything that follows happened in the pause between question and reply. [352] Mira took the coin, stepped onto the focal stone, closed her hand, and said her own name aloud. [370] The walls gave it back to her nine times, and on the third return the light changed. [387] She woke in a wheat field. [393] The stalks were the exact ochre she had mixed that morning, shade number six, the one she had rejected as too warm. [415] A woman slept beside her, and Mira, being methodical even in dreams, counted the woman's breaths and found them matched to the echo's rhythm. [439] She said her name again, playfully, to see what would happen, and woke on a train. [455] A man across the aisle was carving a nickel with a nail, his tongue between his teeth, and he did not look up. [478] "I hold it and I listen," he said, "and it tells me where the next town is. Doesn't always tell true." [499] He grinned at that, as though wrongness were the best part, and Mira understood him instantly: a man who preferred the guess to the map. [524] She said her name a third time and woke beneath the mural itself, a girl of maybe nine, staring up at painted sleepers while somewhere above her a deaf architect clapped his hands to feel the walls answer. [562] The girl in the mural was already awake; she had been awake the whole time, waiting for someone to stop insisting they knew what the painting meant. [589] Mira surfaced through the layers the way a diver rises, wheat then train then copper canopy, each awakening returning her a little less sure and a little more alive. [618] Deo was still holding out his hand; the last echo of her name was still dissolving against the farthest wall. [638] "Yes," she said, "and I want shade six back on the palette." [650] Deo frowned, because shade six was wrong, demonstrably wrong, and she had proved it herself with a spectrophotometer. [668] "It's wrong," he said. [672] "It's what he painted," Mira said, and set the hobo nickel in the mortar gap where it had waited eighty years, pressing it back with her thumb like a seed. [702] Then she clapped once, hard, and the garden answered in tiers, and she let herself not know why the answer sounded like laughter. [725]
