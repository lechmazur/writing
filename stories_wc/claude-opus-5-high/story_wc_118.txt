<story>
The silo had held barley for ninety years before it held books, and it still smelled faintly of both. [19] Orsolya Vane climbed its iron stair every morning at the hour she still called dawn, though the word had come loose from its meaning. [43] When the axis slipped -- eleven degrees in a single winter, the almanacs said, the poles wandering like furniture in a shaken house -- the seasons lost their order and the nights their length. [75] Sleep came, but shallow, tideless. [80] Somewhere in the fourth year of the new tilt a woman in Kerrow dreamed of a stairway made of bees, told her daughter at breakfast, and that was the last human dream anybody recorded. [114] After that the nights were only dark rooms people sat in with their eyes shut. [129]

Orsolya was an odd historian, everyone agreed. [136] She did not care for treaties or kings. [144] She wanted the stories walls had heard: the confessions muttered into plaster, the arguments absorbed by wainscoting, the lullabies that had soaked into beams and stayed. [170] Without dreams, she argued to the three neighbors who listened, memory was the only interior left, and interiors were kept in houses. [192] So she walked the emptied valleys with a hand lamp, laying her palms on chimney breasts, being sensitively perceptive in the way that had made her unmarriageable and useful. [221] Where others felt cold stone, she felt the grain of what had happened there. [235]

In the burned wing of Ansel House she found the fireplace still choked with paper. [250] Someone, in the last panic of the tilt, had fed forty years of diaries to a fire that went out halfway through the work. [274] She carried the half-burned bundle back to the silo in her coat, against her ribs, like a hurt animal. [293]

The silo library was her one extravagance: shelves spiraling up the curved wall, sixty feet of them, bolted where the grain chutes had been. [317] At noon the light fell through the old top hatch and the whole interior went the color of a struck bell, a bronze quiet she worked inside as though it were water. [349] There she began to probe the diaries, page by scorched page, with tweezers and a cold flat iron. [367] The writer was a steward named Hoby Deane, and he had no gift for prose and every gift for noticing. [387] He recorded what the house heard. [392] He recorded, too, an inventory of the attic, and on a page burned to a brown crescent, item nine: a fragment of medieval tapestry showing dragons. [418] Two of them, Hoby wrote, coiled nose to tail around a slanted pole, and beneath the pole a line of sleeping figures with open eyes. [443] He thought it ugly and stored it behind the cistern. [453]

Orsolya went back for it in the rain. [461] It was the size of a pillowcase, moth-eaten, the wool gone the brown of old blood, but the drawing held. [481] The pole through the dragons was tilted, and stitched along it, in a hand six hundred years dead, were degree marks. [502] Eleven of them. [505]

She sat with it in the bronze light for a long time, understanding slowly, the way she understood walls. [524] The axis had been displaced before. [530] Someone medieval had watched it happen, had seen the dreams go out of people, and had recorded it in the only archive they trusted to outlast a language: wool, and dragons, and a crooked line. [565] And the dreams had come back, because those weavers' grandchildren had dreamed enough to invent dragons. [581]

The tilt corrects, she wrote in her ledger that evening, in the careful hand she used for things she could not prove. [603] Wait. [604]

She hung the tapestry fragment where the noon light would strike it, and pinned Hoby's brown crescent of a page beside it, steward and weaver on the same nail. [633] Then she went out again, into the valleys, to lay her hands on more cold chimneys. [649]

The neighbors came to the silo that winter, more of them each month, to sit in the bronze quiet and be read to. [672] They listened to what the walls had heard -- a marriage mended in a kitchen, a boy who hid in a coal scuttle for two days, a nurse singing to a child who was already dead -- and some of them wept, which nobody had done much since the dreams stopped. [721] Orsolya watched them and thought: this is the other thing the walls do. [734] They keep the heat until someone comes back inside. [743]

Four years later a girl in Kerrow woke and said she had seen a stairway made of bees. [761] Orsolya wrote it down, dated it, and shelved it in the silo among the stories the walls had kept for her. [782]
