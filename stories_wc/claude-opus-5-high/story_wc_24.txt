<story>The station stood where the fjord narrowed to a blade of black water, and the quiet there had weight. [19] Halvard Sund had come to it eleven months earlier, after blowing the whistle. [32] He had been a hydraulic clock shepherd in the capital, tending the great water-driven regulators that fed the time signal to every harbor on the coast. [58] When the Bureau began shaving seconds off the noon signal to flatter the railway ledgers, he said so, loudly, in writing. [79] They did not dismiss him; they reassigned him to the horizon measurement station at Kvitsund, where a man could be honest at no one's expense. [104]

The work was eminently peculiar and almost entirely solitary. [113] Twice a day he climbed the iron ladder to the theodolite, blew the brass whistle so the lighthouse keeper across the water would know to hold his lamp steady, and measured the horizon. [146] Not the distance to it, which never changed, but its edge. [157] Some mornings the horizon lay soft as wool; other mornings it came up in razor edges, so sharp that the sea seemed cut from the sky with a knife. [186] The Bureau wanted the sharpness logged, because razor edges meant cold dense air, and cold dense air bent light, and bent light lied to navigators. [211] Halvard would squint until his eyes watered, grading each edge from one to nine, and enter the number in a ledger nobody in the capital read. [237]

He did not mind. [241] He had come north with a question, and the fjord quiet was the only room large enough to hold it: he wanted to understand what clocks really measure. [269] In the capital he had believed the answer was water. [279] Now he suspected it was something closer to attention. [288]

His only inheritance sat on the sill of the observation hut: a mantle clock key, brass, worn to a shine at the grip. [311] The clock it belonged to had been sold to pay for his mother's burial, but his father had kept the key and given it to him, saying a man should hold on to the winding and let the world keep the face. [353] The station had no mantle clock. [359] It had a hydraulic regulator in the cellar, a cistern and a float and a copper drip, and Halvard shepherded it as he always had, wiping the orifice, bleeding the air, coaxing it through frost. [394] But he did his real work with the key in his fist. [406]

The method came to him in February, on a day of nines, when the horizon was all razor edges. [425] After blowing the whistle he did not start counting forward to the lighthouse lamp's answering flash, as the manual instructed. [445] Instead he worked through counting backwards, from sixty down to one, holding the key and pressing its teeth into his palm. [466] Counting forward, he had learned, made time into a road that ran away from you; counting backwards made it a thing arriving. [488] A countdown has an edge at the end of it, as sharp as any horizon. [503] He counted himself down to zero and found he was still there, and that the water in the cellar was still falling, and that the two facts had nothing to do with one another. [537]

That was the answer, or its beginning. [544] Clocks do not measure time; they measure their own decay, and we agree to squint at it and call it a day. [566] Water falls, springs unwind, a key must be turned again next Sunday. [578] The Bureau had not stolen seconds; there are no seconds to steal. [590] What it had stolen was the agreement, and the agreement was all the ships had. [605]

He wrote this in the margin of the ledger, in the narrow fjord quiet of an afternoon with no wind. [625] Then he wrote a letter to the Bureau's chief inspector, and it was not an accusation this time but an instruction: keep the drip clean, keep the whistle loud, and never tell a navigator the horizon is soft when it has razor edges. [668]

In April the inspector came in person, an old man who hated boats. [681] He read the margin twice and squinted at the sea for a long while. [695] Then he asked Halvard to come back and shepherd the capital's regulators again, at his old wage. [712] Halvard said he would, on one condition: that the noon signal be given, from then on, by counting backwards. [731] Ten, nine, eight, so that every harbor in the country would hear the edge coming and agree to arrive at it together. [753] The inspector, being eminently peculiar himself, agreed. [760] Halvard left the mantle clock key on the windowsill at Kvitsund, where the light could reach it, for whoever came next. [781]</story>
