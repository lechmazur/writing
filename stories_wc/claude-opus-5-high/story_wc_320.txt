The city of Antenna Fall hung over the gorge on nine rusted towers, and every one of them still sang. [20] Mira Oyelaran, celestial signal interpreter, third grade, climbed the catwalk with a thermos of chicory tea and a toolbag, because the sky did not care how tired you were. [49] Tonight was lunar perigee, the moon swollen and close, and at perigee the old antennae pulled in things no observatory would admit existed. [72] Half the city was abandoned now, apartments bolted to lattice-work, rope bridges swinging between masts, doors left open on furniture nobody had wanted to carry down. [98] Her job was simple in the way that heavy work is simple: listen, transcribe, and tune the arrays before the tide of moonlight overloaded them. [123] Her tuning stone was a hollywood star fragment, a wedge of pink terrazzo and brass her grandmother had pried from a drowned boulevard, the letters broken to read only ELL. [153] Brass held frequencies the way a dog holds a scent, and the terrazzo, being stubborn, kept them steady. [171] She wedged it into the yoke of Tower Six, tightened two bolts, and put on the headphones. [188]

For eleven years the towers had carried the same transmission: the long dying note of a star that had gone out before Mira's great-grandmother was born. [214] The city existed to hear it out, the way you sit with the dying. [228] Most of the interpreters had left when the note began to thin; Mira stayed because her grandmother had taught her to find the beginning in every ending, and had died halfway through proving it. [262]

At 11:40, under the fat low moon, the note stopped. [272] What replaced it was not silence. [277] Mira had assumed silence was the absence of signal; what came through the brass was a presence, dense and cool, a shard of hush that sat in her ears like a stone in a palm. [312] It had edges. [315] Her meters read nothing, which was, professionally speaking, the loudest thing they had ever said. [330]

She did what the manual said to do with anomalies: logged the time, drank her tea while it was hot, and checked the guy-wires, because a tower that falls interprets nothing. [361] Then she sat down on the grating with her legs through the rail, three hundred meters of gorge air beneath her boots, and tried to tune the hush. [389] Every method failed. [392] Sweeping the band failed, because the hush was not on a band. [404] Amplifying failed, because it was not weak. [411]

Near midnight, with the moon at its closest and the towers humming in their bones, Mira did the only thing left, which her grandmother had always called the real trade: she worked by discarding assumptions. [446] She discarded the assumption that a signal must come from far away. [458] She discarded the assumption that the star had been speaking to anyone. [470] She discarded, last and hardest, the assumption that an ending needed comforting. [482]

When the last assumption went, the hush turned over like a key. [494] She understood it the way you understand a handshake. [503] The hush was not the star's death rattle; it was the pause a voice makes before it says the first word, and the dead star's long note had been the tail of a sentence begun somewhere Mira had no word for. [544] Beginnings, it turned out, were simply endings heard from the far side. [556]

The brass fragment grew warm in its yoke, serenely electric, humming with a charge that raised the hair on her arms without alarming her at all. [582] Across the gorge, one by one, the other eight towers picked it up. [595]

Mira logged it in the ledger in her grandmother's shorthand, then did the practical things: reset the yoke bolts, replaced a corroded lug, wrote a note for whoever climbed next. [625] The note said: THE SIGNAL IS NOT ENDING, IT IS CLEARING ITS THROAT. [638] DISCARD WHAT YOU ASSUME. [642] TIGHTEN THE BOLTS FIRST. [646]

By dawn the radio guild would send climbers back across the gorge bridges, and the empty flats under the towers would fill again with people who wanted to hear the first word when it came. [681] It might take another eleven years. [687] Mira found she did not mind waiting; waiting was just listening with the volume down. [702] She unclipped the hollywood star fragment, warm as bread, and put it in her coat pocket against her ribs. [721] The moon was already pulling away, and the catwalk rang under her boots all the long climb down. [739]
