The benthic city of Caldera Nine had no horizon, only walls of black water pressing against glass. [17] Mirelle Ondaatje had taught navigation there for thirty years, and in all that time she had never once seen a star. [38] Then the signal came from the trench floor, and after that nothing in her classroom meant what it used to mean. [59]

The visitors had been sleeping in the mud for eleven thousand years, and they did not speak so much as demonstrate. [80] They folded space the way Mirelle folded paper charts: along edges, by creases, making distant points touch. [97]

Edge geometry had been her life's small discipline, a dead art in a city that navigated by sonar beacons. [116] She taught it to eleven students a year: how a route is not a line but a fold, how the edge where two surfaces meet tells you everything about the space you cannot see. [150] On the first day of every term she set a rusted boat anchor chain link on the table, her grandmother's, salvaged from a trawler that had worked the surface before the Drowning. [182] A link is two curves and two edges, she told them, and if you open one edge the chain becomes a line. [204] Close it, and the line becomes a net. [212] Geometry, she said, is only the question of which edges you permit to touch. [226]

The visitors, when the councils finally let her descend to them, seemed to find this amusing. [242] They were ridged shapes the color of old ivory, sessile as coral, and they had come here by folding the void along its edges the same way, only larger, only farther. [273] What they offered was not technology but instruction, and what they asked in return was a pilot. [290] A fold requires two points, and they knew only one of ours. [302] To expand their map - to expand ours - someone had to learn to name the far point by its light. [321] Which meant stars. [324]

Mirelle was sixty-one years old and had spent her life in a city whose ceiling was six kilometers of water. [344] She volunteered, not out of courage, which she did not have much of, but because she was balancedly curious - equally eager and equally afraid, which is the only ratio in which a person can be trusted with something enormous. [383]

The ascent took nine days in a pressure hull the size of her classroom. [397] She brought two things: her charts, and the chain link, which she hung from a cord so she could watch it stop swinging as the hull slowed. [424] On the ninth night she surfaced into air for the first time in her life, and the sky unfolded above her like a chart no one had ever creased. [453] She wept, and was embarrassed, and kept working. [461]

Learning to navigate by starlight took her four months, alone on a raft of salvage with a borrowed sextant and the patience of a woman who had taught eleven students a year for thirty years. [496] The stars were edges too, she found - each one the corner where a direction met a distance. [513] She had expected joy and got something quieter and heavier: the marvel of a woman who understands, at last, how small the room she was raised in had been. [542] Her grandmother had died in the dark, without seeing this. [552] So had everyone she loved. [557]

When she gave the visitors her second point - a yellow star forty light-years out, fixed by twelve nights of measurement - they folded, and the horizon of the human species expanded by a distance no chart in Caldera Nine had words for. [598]

She did not go through. [603] Someone had to teach the next ones, and she was, after all, an instructor. [617] She went back down. [621]

The chain link hangs in the classroom still, on its cord, swinging a little with the currents. [638] Her students - there are two hundred now, and a waiting list - learn edge geometry first, and starlight second, and they learn both in a room with no windows worth looking through. [669] She tells them the same thing every term, holding up the link. [681] A horizon is only a fold. [687] You were not born inside a limit. [694] You were born inside a crease, and creases open. [703]
