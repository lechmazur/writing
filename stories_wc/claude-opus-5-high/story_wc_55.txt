Ilde had been a clock scribe for nineteen years, and in all that time she had spoken perhaps nine hundred words. [21] The Sanctuary of Aumbre did not permit more. [29] It was a soundless sanctuary, built into the blue shoulder of a glacier, where the brothers and sisters kept time by writing it down. [53] No bells. [55] No chimes, no gongs, no hour struck aloud. [63] Instead, every sixty seconds, Ilde dipped her pen and drew the minute in a ledger the length of a corridor. [83] The glacial hush of the place had gotten into her bones, and she had learned to love it the way one loves a heavy coat. [108] Still, the dream would not die quietly. [115] At twelve she had wanted to be a carillonneur. [124] She had stood in the rain of the lowland city with her palms flat against the bell tower's stone, feeling the bronze thunder travel up her arms, and she had decided that she would spend her life making the sky ring. [165] Then her hearing began to fail in the left ear, then the right, and the tower masters were kind, which was worse than cruelty. [189] They were excruciatingly gentle with her, the way people are gentle with something already broken, and she fled uphill into silence before their pity could finish the job. [217] Nineteen years of minutes later, the Sanctuary's roof cracked under a spring storm. [230] Water came down the eastern stair and pooled in the forecourt, which was paved in old quartz. [247] Ilde was sent to bear the flood buckets, since the younger scribes had steadier hands for the ledger and she had steadier shoulders for labor. [272] She worked until the storm thinned, and then she heard it. [283] Heard - with ears that had kept only the low registers, the registers of thunder and distant trains. [300] The wet crystal was whispering. [305] Each drop striking the quartz set the whole forecourt humming at a pitch so deep it was less a sound than a pressure in the sternum, and the puddles carried it slab to slab, a chord that rose and fell as the rain slackened. [349] She knelt in the standing water and put her palms flat against the stone, and she was twelve again, and the air smelled of wet dust and cold mineral - petrichor, the smell of the lowland city, the smell of the bell tower in April. [393] She told no one. [397] For eleven nights she studied the forecourt. [404] She learned that the quartz slabs had been quarried in seven thicknesses, and that each thickness answered the rain with its own note. [427] She learned that a channel of meltwater, released at the right hour, would run the slabs in sequence. [445] She had no instrument, no permission, and no method she could name for what she intended; she simply moved stones until the water went where she wanted it. [473] On the twelfth morning, in the hush of a hidden sunrise - the sun rising somewhere behind the glacier's shoulder, its light arriving secondhand and blue - Ilde opened the meltwater gate. [503] The forecourt sang. [506] It was not loud. [510] Nothing in Aumbre was ever loud. [516] But the slabs took the water one after another, and the whispering rose through the soles of every sleeping foot in the Sanctuary, and the brothers and sisters came out into the cold in their nightclothes, standing barefoot in the runoff with their mouths open and no words in them. [566] It lasted four minutes. [570] Ilde did not write them down. [576] Afterward the Abbess came to her, an old woman with ink to the elbow, and Ilde braced for the excruciating gentleness she had run from at twelve. [603] The Abbess only pressed a dry thumb to Ilde's wrist, where a pulse keeps time without being asked, and nodded once. [624] Then she went back inside, and the glacial hush closed over the Sanctuary again like water over a dropped stone. [644] Ilde returned to her ledger and drew the minute she had missed, and the one after that. [661] She would not do it again; once was the whole of it. [673] The dream had wanted a lifetime and had settled for four minutes, and to her surprise, four minutes had been enough to bear. [696] Every spring after, when the rains came and the forecourt whispered under its own weight of water, she would stop her pen mid-stroke, close her eyes, and let the smell of wet stone tell her who she had been. [735] Then she would finish the line. [741]
