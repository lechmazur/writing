They called Vesna a hopeless alchemist, and she had stopped arguing. [11] Hope, she had decided, was simply a slower method than arithmetic. [22] Every evening she climbed to the ember nexus, where six veins of old fire met beneath the mountain's cracked shoulder, and every evening she failed a little more precisely. [51] Twilight was the only hour the nexus would answer; before dusk the embers slept, and after full dark they raged past all governing. [74] In the narrow window between, frost still clung to the stone lip of the crater, and the heat drew it up into white flowers that never melted -- a frozen bloom ringing the pit like a wreath at a funeral no one had scheduled. [117]

Vesna carried her uncle's broken bishop's crozier in a sailcloth sling across her back. [131] It had snapped at the crook when the cathedral roof came down on him, and the diocese had given it to her the way one gives a leash to the next dog. [163] She was to be consecrated at midsummer, third in her family, shepherd of a valley that had already decided every word she would ever say from the pulpit. [191] That was the destiny she meant to interrupt, and she meant to do it without cruelty, which was the difficult part. [212]

Villagers thought her cold. [216] They had seen her bury her uncle with a dry face and a steady voice, and they had not seen her afterward, kneeling in the ash, holding the two halves of the crozier together as if warmth could weld them. [256] She was stoically sensitive, a combination the valley had no word for and therefore mistrusted. [271]

Her work was simple to state and impossible to do: to vivify dead matter, to make a thing that could choose. [292] If she could make one object choose, she reasoned, the valley could no longer argue that anything was fixed. [311] Her method was unglamorous: through successive approximation, four hundred and nine attempts, each one destroying its own best result. [330] A copper coin dropped into the nexus at the exact hinge of twilight would glow, twitch, and sometimes crawl three fingers across the stone before going still. [357] Each time she adjusted the alloy, the angle, the instant of release, and dropped another penny. [373] Attempt four hundred and ten crawled a handspan and turned toward her before it died. [388] She wrote the number down, because grief without records is only weather. [400]

On the four hundred and eleventh evening she noticed what she had been recording for a year without reading: the pennies that moved furthest were the ones that lost the most weight. [432] Motion was not added to them. [438] It was subtracted from them; the nexus did not animate, it consumed, and what looked like life was the shape of a thing spending itself. [463] When the penny dropped -- the four hundred and twelfth, and the understanding with it -- she sat very still while the frozen bloom around the crater glittered and did not melt. [493] Nothing could be given life cheaply; something had to be unmade to pay for it. [508]

She took the broken crozier out of its sling and held it over the pit. [523] Silver, three hundred years old, warm as a hand from being carried. [535] It was the only thing she loved and the only thing heavy enough to buy what she wanted, and those two facts, she understood at last, were the same fact. [565] She cried without changing her expression, which was how she had always done it, and let go. [582]

The crozier went into the embers and came apart in a long white breath. [596] What rose from the nexus was small, silver, and unsteady, shaped a little like a bird and a little like a crook, and it had not been in her notes. [626] It landed on the frost flowers, snapped one off, and ate it. [638] Then it looked at her and chose, plainly and stupidly, to stay. [650]

She walked down at full dark with the thing riding her shoulder and the empty sling flapping. [667] In the valley they would ask where the crozier was, and she would tell them the truth: she had spent it. [688] There would be no consecration at midsummer, because there was no staff to consecrate, and a diocese cannot ordain a woman who has burned her own inheritance into a creature that eats ice. [721] Her uncle, she thought, would have called it sacrilege, and then asked to hold it. [736] The hopeless alchemist had been right about herself all along: she had never had hope, only a ledger and a willingness to lose things in order. [762] It turned out to be enough. [768]
