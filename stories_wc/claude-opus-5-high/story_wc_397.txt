Ilse arrived at the drift-ice marina with a one-way ticket and eleven days of patience left. [16] The clinic on the hill took appointments only after a waiting period, and the period was the point: they wanted you certain. [38] The staff had a phrase for people like her, not unkindly meant: euthanasia tourists. [52] She had woken changed in a hospital in Bremen nine months earlier, after the aneurysm, and the woman who sat up in that bed heard things the old one hadn't. [82] Doctors called it auditory agnosia with confabulation; Ilse called it gripping reality loosely, the way you hold a bird you intend to release. [105] Faces slid off people, rooms rearranged themselves behind her back, but sound had become exact. [120] She could hear, in a stairwell or a kettle or a man's cough, the small preliminary click of something preparing to stop. [142] That was why she had come north: not only to end, but to learn the sound of approaching endings well enough to recognize her own. [167] She carried one inheritance: the silver tip of her grandmother's lace parasol, unscrewed from a rotted shaft, a finger-length spike worn smooth at the thumb. [192] Her grandmother had used it to test ice on the Haff before crossing, tapping until the note came back hollow. [212] On the third day, the surge came. [219] A February gale drove the drift ice into the marina like a fist into a crate, and the floating docks folded, and four hundred people in the harbor district lost their heat, their windows, their boats. [255] The relief coordinator, a tired woman with a clipboard, asked if Ilse could hold a rope. [271] She said she could hold a rope for eight days. [281] So began the trawling. [285] Each morning they took the flat-bottomed boat out between the floes to trawl the shallows for what the surge had swallowed: outboard motors, a christening cup, a dog's collar, a drowned upright piano that surfaced groaning. [321] Ilse worked the net and, at every pause, leaned over the gunwale and tapped the parasol tip against the ice. [341] It taught her a vocabulary. [345] Sound ice rang like a struck tooth; rotten ice answered with a dull, patient thud, the note of a thing already decided. [367] On the fifth day she heard that thud under the pontoon where three volunteers were stacking generators, and she shouted, and they came off it ninety seconds before the pontoon tore free and slid under. [402] Nobody asked how she knew. [407] In the shelter that night, the wind came off the ice with a long unbroken note, and Ilse found she did not mind it. [431] It was the first solace she had accepted in nine months, and it was windborne, which suited her; she could not have held anything heavier. [456] An old fisherman named Teodor sat mending net beside her and asked what the little silver spike was for. [475] "To hear what's ending," she said. [481] He nodded and told her that ice does not break at the thinnest place but at the fold, where two sheets have pressed against each other so long that the pressure has nowhere left to go. [517] She thought about that for a long time. [525] Her appointment was a fold in her patience: two certainties pressed flat against each other, the waiting period and the wanting, and she had assumed the break would come on the scheduled day. [558] But a fold can also be a place where a thing doubles back and lies alongside itself, longer than it looks. [579] On the eighth day she climbed the hill to the clinic. [590] The doctor was kind, unhurried, and entirely willing. [598] Ilse told her the truth: that she still gripped the world loosely, that faces still slid, that she wanted an ending she could hear coming rather than one she had to schedule. [630] She asked to fold the waiting period again, and again after that, and the doctor wrote a new date in pencil. [651] Downhill, the marina was still trawling, still hauling other people's lives out of the freezing water. [667] Ilse went back to the boat with her grandmother's parasol tip warm in her fist, and all that spring she tapped the ice ahead of the volunteers, listening for the dull note, buying strangers their ninety seconds. [704] She was still learning the sound of approaching endings. [713] It turned out to be the same sound as the wind: continuous, impersonal, and, if you stood in it long enough, oddly kind. [736]
