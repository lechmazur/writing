The dock at Vresholm was called golden because some merchant had once lacquered its planks in gilt, and the sea had spent a century politely taking it back. [28] Under snow, the flecks that remained glimmered like seeds spilled from a torn sack. [42] Eira noticed that, because Eira noticed seeds everywhere; it was her profession and, she would admit, her only philosophy. [61] She had walked eleven days along the coast road with a satchel of paper envelopes, each holding grains that had outlived their gardeners. [84] A seed, she liked to say, is an argument that the future is worth the wait. [100]

At the end of the dock sat Halvard, mending a rope he could no longer see. [116] His eyes had gone milky years ago, but his hands remained fluent. [128] "You are the one who writes letters about my mother's rope," he said. [141] "I am," Eira said, kneeling on the wet gold. [150] "Then look, before the fog comes and takes the light with it." [162]

The rope was longer than the boat it had served, coiled in loops like a sleeping animal, and every arm's length bore a knot of a different family. [190] Eira had chased rumors of such ropes for six years, ever since an archivist told her about the secret weavers. [210] When the northern lords burned the granary books to make the villages dependent, the rope-makers' wives copied everything into knots: sowing depths, frost dates, the salt-and-smoke treatments that woke stubborn seeds. [241] Soldiers searched houses for paper and found only fishing tackle. [251] The knowledge went to sea and came back, generation after generation, hauled up dripping with cod. [267]

"My mother taught me the knots as chores," Halvard said. [277] "I tied them a thousand times without being told what they said." [289]

Eira drew out the fulgurite glass. [295] A finger of sand fused by lightning, dug from the dunes above the harbor, hollow and rough as bone, with one end polished by her thumb into a crude lens. [325] Storms made it in an instant; she had spent two winters rubbing it clear. [339] That contrast pleased her more than she could explain to anyone. [350]

Through the glass, the knots opened. [356] Tar-black turns of hemp resolved into countable crossings: three over, one under, a bight pulled left for barley, right for rye. [377] She read with a stub of pencil in her teeth, translating rope into sentences. [391]

The fog came as promised, rolling across the harbor in a long grey sleeve, swallowing the masts, the bell buoy, the far shore. [414] Sound went with it. [417] What remained was an oceanic hush, the sea breathing under the planks, snow landing without weight. [433] In that quiet the rope seemed to be speaking at the speed it preferred. [447]

Halvard's hands never stopped. [451] "You want the last three fathoms," he said. [459] "Nobody tied those but her." [464]

There the pattern changed: knots doubled back on themselves, small and dense as burrs. [478] It took Eira until dusk to understand that it was not a calendar but an instruction on how to coax seed from a plant that refuses to give it. [507] Scar the stem at the second frost, then wait a full year and scar it again. [523] Three seasons of small injuries, and the plant, believing itself dying, would finally make seed. [538]

"Infinitely patient, those women," she murmured. [544]

"They had to be," Halvard said. [550] "A lie in a rope drowns your grandson." [558]

She asked whether she might take the rope south to the seed vault, where it would lie in dry air under glass. [580] Halvard laughed, which in the hush sounded like a rowlock turning. [591] "Ropes rot in dry air," he said. [598] "Learn to tie them. [602] That's the only vault there was." [608]

So she stayed the winter on the golden dock, snow on her shoulders, learning the old methods the only way they had ever survived -- hand to hand, in the dark, at the speed of rope. [643] In spring she left with eleven new knots in her fingers and a length of hemp she had tied herself, and she did not take his mother's rope. [671] The fulgurite she left behind, propped in the window of his hut, where it caught what light the fog allowed. [691] Lightning had made it in a heartbeat, and it would sit there for a thousand years; the weavers, she thought, would have appreciated the joke. [716]
