The first origami iris arrived the week Ines sold her workbench to pay for her mother's headstone. [17] She had hit bottom so precisely that she almost admired the accuracy: no tools, no commissions, no hair left to braid but her own. [41] For eleven years she had made mourning jewelry: lockets of woven hair, jet beads, rings that held a widow's grief in a circle small enough to wear. [68] Then the orders stopped, and grief, it turned out, was the only thing she could not make wearable for herself. [88] The iris was folded from graph paper, and inside it, in pencil, were coordinates. [102] She walked to them out of boredom, which is what she called despair that still had shoes on. [120] The coordinates ended at an observatory above the reservoir, a copper dome stitched with verdigris, its clocks all stopped at different hours and none of them wrong. [147] Inside, the air smelled of brass polish and winter, and the light did not seem to come from any particular year. [168] The keeper was waiting at the base of the great refractor, and it did not pretend to be a man. [188] Its face was porcelain over clockwork; its voice came from a bellows; it introduced itself as Vesper and said, with something like pride, that it was genuinely artificial. [216] "Not a false man," it clarified, "but a true machine." [226] Vesper had folded the irises, hundreds of them, and sent them to people who had stopped. [242] Above them, the dome was hung with what Vesper called the curtains of intention: long translucent sheets, faintly moving, each one a thing someone had meant to do and had not. [273] They hung grey, Vesper said, until someone chose to illuminate them, and then it swung the great mirror so that evening light climbed the folds and colour came up through the cloth like blood returning to a numb hand. [312] Ines recognised one of them immediately. [317] It was her own: a tattoo stencil on vellum, drawn eight years ago for a woman dying of something slow who wanted an amaranth on her forearm, so that the last thing on her skin would be a flower that refuses to wilt. [360] The woman had died in the waiting week; Ines had never inked it; she had kept the vellum in her apron for eight years like a debt. [387] "I was built to keep the hours," Vesper said, "and then the hours ended." [401] "What I do now is teach withering to bloom again, but I have no hands for flowers, only for paper." [421] It wanted her hands. [425] She thought of every locket she had made, beautiful boxes for endings, and understood that she had spent eleven years sealing things shut. [448] So Ines stayed through a winter that the observatory's stopped clocks declined to count. [462] She worked by mirror-light with a steady, banked heat she had not felt since her apprenticeship, quiet, unhurried, burning anyway. [482] She melted her mother's jet beads and set them as seed-hearts; she braided hair from her own head into stems. [502] And she learned to fold, because Vesper insisted the coordinates had to be carried by something that could be opened. [522] Together they went on leaving origami figures with coordinates inside, on windowsills, in coat pockets, in the hands of people at the bottom of their own long year. [550] Some came; most did not; the dome filled slowly anyway. [560] In spring she finally cut the amaranth stencil from its vellum and used it not as a tattoo pattern but as a template, piercing the shape into thin silver. [589] She hung the pierced flower in front of the mirror, so the light came through the petals and threw an amaranth thirty feet wide across the curtains of intention. [618] Her own grey sheet took the colour and did not give it back. [631] "That is one," Vesper said, and its bellows made a sound she chose to hear as breath. [648] Ines wiped the silver dust from her palms, took a fresh square of graph paper, and began to fold the next iris. [670]
