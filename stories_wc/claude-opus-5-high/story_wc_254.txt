The ban lifted at dusk on the first day of thaw-month, and Veren walked to the cobalt atrium by the frozen lake with nothing but a wax tablet and a stylus of burnt willow. [34] For eleven years she had been a cloud-shadow poet, which meant she wrote only what the clouds wrote first: the long gray sentences they dragged across the ice, the commas of gulls, the erasures of snow. [70] It was a lawful art, a humble art, and it had made her famous in the way a good fence is famous to the field it keeps. [97] The unlawful art stood at the atrium's far end: the humming quartz colonnade, twelve pillars of blue-white stone that sang whenever weather moved through them. [122] For two centuries the Consistory had forbidden anyone to paraphrase that hum. [134] One could listen; one could weep; one could not put the sound into other words, because the words would be a boundary the sound had not chosen. [161]

The hush in the atrium that evening was the hush of lantern-moths, that soft battering of soft things against glass, a hundred listeners breathing and not speaking. [188] They had come to see whether anyone would dare, now that daring was merely legal. [203]

Veren was aesthetically driven, and she knew it, and she had stopped apologizing for it in her thirties. [221] Justice did not move her; beauty did, and the colonnade was the most beautiful unsolved thing she had ever stood near. [242] She wanted to stride through boundaries once thought sacred, not to break them but because a boundary, to her, was simply a place where two beauties had not yet been introduced. [273]

The old Archivist Sull found her by the third pillar. [283] "You will ruin it," he said. [289] "The hum is not language." [294] "Whatever you write will be smaller than it." [302]

"Everything I write is smaller than what wrote it," she said. [313] "That is what a shadow is." [319]

Then the wind came off the lake. [326] The colonnade took it and turned it into sound, and the sound was not a chord and not a voice, but something like both remembering each other. [353] Veren felt it in her sternum: a tremor of certainty, the first of her life. [368] She had always worked in approximations, in maybes, in the honest cowardice of the almost. [383] Now she knew, with the whole weight of her body, what the hum was saying, and she knew that knowing it was not the same as owning it. [411]

So she did not paraphrase the hum alone. [419] She worked through dissolving boundaries instead, the only method that had ever felt like courtesy to her. [436] She waited until a cloud crossed the low moon and laid its shadow over the frozen lake, and then she wrote the shadow, as she always had, and into the same lines she let the hum come, untranslated at first, then bleeding into the verbs until neither could be extracted from the other. [489] The poem she read aloud was neither a description of clouds nor a paraphrase of stone; it was the place where the two had been introduced. [515]

The listeners did not applaud, which she took as the compliment it was. [528] The lantern-moth hush held; even the ice held; Sull sat down on the cold cobalt floor and put his hands over his face. [551]

Later he told her she had been right, and that he hated it, and that he would copy the poem into the Archive himself. [575] She asked him why he hated it. [582] "Because the ban was easier than the truth," he said. [592] "We said the hum was sacred." [598] "We meant we could not hear it." [605]

Veren went out onto the lake that night, where the boundary between water and floor and sky had dissolved into one blue plane, and stood listening to the colonnade hum behind her. [637] There were eleven more boundaries in the city that the Consistory still called sacred. [651] She was fifty-one years old, legal at last, and beautifully out of patience. [664]
