The lantern maker Ossian Vey had a reputation for craft in both senses: the lanterns he sold were beautiful, and the bargains he struck were not. [26] He kept a stall at the floating island tea garden, where the moss terraces drifted a mile above the salt flats and the kettles sang all night. [53] Travelers came for the jasmine; Ossian came for the hour after the shadow detaches. [67] At the third bell, every shadow in the garden unhooked itself from its owner's heels and went wandering among the lanterns, and in that unguarded interval a person's grief lay bare as a pulled tooth. [102]

Ossian sold sorrow-lanterns then. [106] Each held a gem that took dream recordings the way a cold window takes breath. [121] Sleep beside one and it drank the night out of you; wake, and the stone would be clouded with everything you had refused to say. [146] His wife Pell had made the first one, years ago, for her own insomnia. [160] She had fallen from the garden's north terrace in a windshear, and Ossian had been holding a lantern, not her hand. [181] Since then he had worn grief like a coat sewn shut, and he had one plan for it: to fold grief into origami cranes and let the wind take them. [211]

This was not metaphor. [215] In the glass basilica on a floating isle three leagues east - a hollow cathedral of lenses that the old dream-wrights had raised and abandoned - a polished dream could be drawn out of its gem as a ribbon of luminous paper. [255] Paper could be folded. [259] Folded things could be released. [264]

The work was gem polishing, and it was slow, and it hurt. [276] You could not use a wheel; the dream would shred. [286] You used your thumbs and a paste of rouge, and as the stone cleared you felt every hour inside it pass through your hands again. [311] Ossian had been polishing Pell's gem for four hundred nights. [321]

The basilica's stair was the trouble. [327] It descends while ascending: every step up carries you one step further into the isle's underside, so that a climber arrives at the dome by way of the dark, and arrives at himself by way of everything he has buried. [367] Craftiness had gotten Ossian this far - he had bartered nine sorrow-lanterns to the ferryman, each one loaded with somebody else's easy sadness, none with his own. [393] Craftiness could not carry him up the stair. [401]

He climbed anyway, on the night the gem finally came clear. [412] Down through the ascent he went, past the windshear terrace rendered in cold glass, past his own hands holding a lantern instead of her sleeve, past the four hundred nights he had spent rubbing a stone rather than saying her name aloud to anyone living. [457] The basilica was luminous torment: ten thousand lenses throwing his worst light back at him, beautifully. [473]

At the dome he set the gem in the reading cradle. [484] The dream unspooled: Pell at the kettle, laughing at something he had said and forgotten; Pell sleeping; Pell, in the last recorded night, dreaming of him standing in a garden holding a light he refused to put down. [522] It came out as paper, warm, glowing faintly, a ribbon as long as his arm. [537]

He folded. [539] He had practiced on ordinary paper for a year, and his hands knew the creases better than they knew rest: valley, mountain, the long neck, the head bent as if listening. [570] Eleven cranes, one for each month she had been gone. [580] The twelfth he could not finish, because the last of the ribbon held the part he had never let himself watch: her final morning, her ordinary hope, her plan to plant lemon balm on the south terrace in spring. [619]

Grief, it turned out, would not transform by being folded away; it transformed only by being held long enough to change shape in the hand. [644] So he stood in the glass dome until the third bell, and when his shadow detached and went wandering, he let it go, and he kept the rest. [672]

At dawn he released the eleven cranes from the basilica's high window. [684] They fell, glowing, and then they climbed. [691] He carried the twelfth home unfolded, a plain warm ribbon in his coat pocket, and in spring he planted lemon balm on the south terrace, and sold lanterns that held nothing but light. [724]
</story>
