The fog came off the canal and stood in the street like something waiting to be invited in. [18] Ilse walked through it with her grandmother's clock in a bread basket, and she could not smell the water, the yeast, or the rust. [42] Nine months ago a fever had taken her sense of smell and left her career standing on one leg. [61] An aromatherapist without a nose, her colleagues had said, is a cartographer without a country. [76] They were wrong, though it took her the whole nine months to understand why. [90] She had never really mapped scent; she had mapped emotional terrain, the ridges and marshes people carried in under their coats, and she had read it in the lines between what they told her. [124] Lavender had only ever been an excuse to sit close to a stranger and listen. [139]

The water clock repair shop had no sign, only a window so old it had gone soft and wavy, and every view through it arrived occluded, bent, generous. [167] Inside, brass drums turned under trickles of water, and the air ticked wetly, like a room full of patient animals drinking. [188] Corvin, the repairman, did not look up. [195]

"It's broken," Ilse said. [199]

"Everything here is broken," he said. "That's the arrangement." [208]

She set the clock on his bench: a squat cedar box, a glass cylinder scored with hour lines, a float, a brass rod, and a hole where a screw should be. [239] Corvin's hands stopped. [242]

"Where is the screw?" [246]

"In my pocket. I have carried it since the funeral." [256]

She laid it on the felt, a fat brass screw with a wide flat head, the thread worn shiny. [275]

"It came out of a midwife's birthing clamp," she said. "My grandmother delivered four hundred children in the villages up the valley, and when the clamp finally cracked she kept the screw, because she said a screw that had helped that many people into the world ought to keep working." [325]

"So she put it in the clock," Corvin said. [334]

"She timed labors with it. Contractions, dilation, the hour to send for the doctor. She called it her honest witness." [354]

Corvin turned the screw slowly in the fogged light, and Ilse watched him do the thing she had come for without knowing she had come for it: he handled the object as though it were a person. [391]

"You humanize them," she said. [396]

"Machines don't keep time," he said. "People do. The machine only agrees to remember." [410]

He fitted the screw, tightened it a quarter turn, and poured a cup of canal water into the cistern. [429] The float rose. [432] The rod scraped, hesitated, and then the pointer crossed the first scored line, and Ilse felt her throat close with a trembling calm, the way it used to when a client finally said the true sentence under the polite one. [472] She could not smell the cedar or the wet brass. [482] She did not need to. [486]

"My grandmother used to say the miracle isn't the birth," Ilse said. "It's that a room full of frightened people keeps breathing in order." [510]

"Ordinary miracles," Corvin said. "Nobody sells tickets." [517]

"I want to," she said, and heard how strange it sounded, and said it better. "I want to unveil them. I still have hands, and I can still hear what people leave out." [550]

Corvin wiped his fingers and looked at her through the wavy glass of the window, where the fog had turned the street into suggestion. [574]

"Bad glass makes better looking," he said. "You stop checking and start noticing." [587]

In the hush of the morning fog she carried the clock home through streets she could hear but not see, and the water ticked against her ribs. [614] By spring she had a new sign on her door: no oils, no promises, only a chair, a listening hour, and a water clock with a midwife's screw in its side, measuring out sixty honest minutes for anyone who needed to be heard into being. [659] She never told clients what she smelled, because she smelled nothing. [670] She told them what she heard between their lines, and watched their shoulders drop, and marked the hour. [688] The water kept agreeing to remember. [694]
