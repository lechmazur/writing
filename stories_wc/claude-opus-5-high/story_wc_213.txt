Mira Vess had the smallest job on the mountain, and she had never wanted a larger one. [17] Once a week she climbed to the windblown sanctuary above the clouds and tested the storm siren. [34] The test came at zero hour, noon exactly, so the shepherds in the valley would know that any wail they heard was only a drill. [59] She threw the switch, counted to thirty, and threw it back. [70] That was the whole of her office, and the Warden Council paid her in firewood and flour. [87] What the Council did not pay her for was the listening. [98]

Mira was credibly curious, which is a rarer thing than clever; she kept notebooks, and her notebooks were right. [117] For six years she had been tracking microclimate variations along the ridge: the temperature of the spring at the north stair, the direction the swallows fled, the weight of the fog on her sleeves, the hour the snowmelt quickened. [156] The mountain announced itself in small ways, always, hours before it shouted. [168]

The siren, she had come to believe, was the problem. [178] It was so loud that the valley had stopped watching the sky; people waited to be told. [195] Worse, in a true downslope gale the wind tore the sound sideways, and the sound never arrived at all. [214] The old cathedral bell in the sanctuary tower had no such trouble. [226] Its low note traveled under the wind rather than against it, and it meant something the siren could not say: not hide, but climb. [250]

In November she found the cathedral bell rope frayed to three strands above the sally, rotted by a century of fog. [271] She spliced it herself with hemp and hide glue, and wrote in her notebook: usable after the glue dries, eight days, no sooner. [294]

The eighth day was the solstice. [299] She woke that morning into a solstice hush so complete it frightened her. [312] The spring at the north stair ran warm. [320] The fog lay heavy and yellow and did not move. [330] The swallows were already gone, south and low, and the snowmelt had begun in December, which her notebooks said had happened twice before in six years. [356] Both times, the eastern slope had let go by evening. [366] Kessen Town sat directly below the eastern slope. [374]

At a quarter to noon she stood in the tower with her hand on the siren switch, exactly where she had stood three hundred times. [399] The wind had turned; it came down the ridge in long warm shoves, the kind that ate sound. [417] If she threw the switch at zero hour, the valley would hear a drill, or hear nothing, and either way would keep to their houses under the slope. [445]

She let her hand fall and crossed to the rope. [455] The splice was dry. [459] It was hard and pale and smelled faintly of the animal it had come from. [474] She had never rung the bell. [480] Nobody had rung it since her grandmother's time, when bells were how a mountain talked to the people who lived on it. [502]

Zero hour came. [505] Mira pulled. [507] The rope ran through her palms and the splice held, and above her the great bronze mouth turned over and spoke one long note that went down through the fog like a hand through water. [542] She rang it for an hour. [548] She rang it because it was quieter than the siren and therefore louder in the only way that mattered: it made people stop and listen instead of merely hear. [577]

By two o'clock the first shepherds came up the north stair, and by four the whole town stood inside the sanctuary, wet and bewildered and counting children. [604] The eastern slope let go at dusk. [611]

Afterward the Warden Council asked her how she had known, and she showed them the notebooks, and they were embarrassed by how ordinary the answer was: warm spring, low swallows, early melt, heavy fog. [645] They offered to make her Warden of the Ridge. [654] She asked instead that the siren be decommissioned and the weekly test moved to the bell, one stroke at zero hour, every Sunday, so the valley would keep the sound in its ears and keep looking up. [691] The Council agreed, since it cost nothing. [698] So Mira Vess kept the smallest job on the mountain, which was now the largest, and every Sunday she climbed through the clouds and pulled once on a rope her own hands had mended, and the mountain and the town went on talking quietly to each other, as they always should have. [750]
