The sky-bridge over Greavewater hummed with tram-cars and furnace breath, and every dusk Vesna stood at its midpoint and played. [20] Below her lay the last enchanted orchard, policed by living statues, and above her lay nothing but soot-colored sky. [39] The city called her the sky-bridge violinist and tossed her coins, but she had never played for coins. [57] She played to reawaken beauty in a mechanical world, and the orchard was the only listener that answered. [75] Its trees had been enchanted in the old century to bear fruit that sang, and now they sang only to themselves, walled in by marble wardens who catalogued every blossom for the Ministry of Yield. [110]

Vesna's plan was simple and impossible: to uproot one sapling and carry it into the city, where children had never heard a tree hum. [134] The statues could hear a whisper at forty paces. [143] They could not hear a heart. [149] That was what old Marta, the orchard's dismissed grafter, had taught her: the wardens were carved to answer sound and speech, but the orchard itself listened to pulses, root to root, the way a sleeper hears a knock inside a dream. [190]

So Vesna learned signaling through heart rhythms, pressing her chest against the bridge rail until the iron carried her heartbeat down into the soil. [214] She would play a phrase, then stop, then let her pulse repeat it. [227] For three winters nothing replied. [232] Then, on a night of frost, a young quince at the orchard's edge answered with a rhythm of its own -- three slow beats, one quick -- and Vesna wept into her strings. [263]

She carried two things down the maintenance stair that night: her violin and a locust shell she had found split open on the rail, hollow, amber, light as a held breath. [294] Marta had shown her the trick of it -- wedge the shell beneath the bridge of a violin and the instrument goes resonantly soft, a sound that travels through wood and earth but dies before it reaches stone. [331] A husk that had once let something crawl out of itself, now teaching an instrument how to whisper: Vesna thought that was the most honest tool she had ever owned. [361]

The orchard smelled of rust and cider. [368] Statues stood along the rows with lanterns grown into their hands, their carved faces worn featureless by a hundred years of weather, and the fruit above them glowed faintly, singing in a thin, tarnished way, like music remembered wrong. [407] Vesna played her soft phrase, and the quince answered: three slow, one quick. [420]

Marta's warning came back to her then, the part Vesna had never fully believed. [434] A warden hears, and then a warden comprehends, and there is a gap between the knowing and the understanding no wider than a heartbeat -- and only in that gap will the orchard let go of a root. [471] So she had to be heard. [477]

Vesna pulled the locust shell from the strings and played the loudest note of her life. [493] Marble heads turned all down the rows. [500] In the instant they knew a sound had happened but not yet what it meant, Vesna dropped to her knees, pressed both palms to the cold ground, and beat her heart's rhythm into the soil -- three slow, one quick, let go. [541] The quince came up as easily as a tooth from a child's gum. [554] Then the statues understood, and began to walk. [562]

She ran with roots slapping her shoulder and dirt in her mouth, up the maintenance stair, onto the sky-bridge, where the tram rails shone and the statues stopped at the orchard wall -- for they were carved to police the orchard, and the orchard's edge was the edge of their world. [612]

Vesna planted the quince in a cracked planter outside a foundry gate, where the soil was half slag. [630] It did not sing, not that year. [637] But the children of the foundry lane learned to put their hands against its trunk and drum their own hearts into it, three slow and one quick, the way she taught them -- breaking free being something you have to signal for, over and over, before anything answers. [684] On the sky-bridge Vesna still plays each dusk, the locust shell hanging from her scroll on a thread, and some evenings, faint under the tram-cars, the planter hums back. [713]
