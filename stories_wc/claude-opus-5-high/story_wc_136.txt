Ines mapped routes for the city transit authority, and she mapped them the way other people wrote love letters. [19] Every line was measured to the meter, every transfer timed to the second, and every margin filled with small ink drawings of the things she passed: pigeons, awnings, a man asleep under a newspaper. [53] Her supervisor called her analytically artistic, which he meant as a complaint and she took as a diagnosis. [71]

The trouble began with the cracks. [77] On the eleventh of October she noticed that the fissures in the pavement outside her apartment had arranged themselves into arrows. [98] A meticulous route mapper does not mistake randomness for intention; she photographed them, overlaid them on her grid, and found a bearing of two hundred and twelve degrees, southwest, holding steady for nine blocks. [132] The arrows pointed at the shuttered Vasari Street terminal, closed since her grandfather's day. [146]

Her grandfather had been a conductor there, and when he died he left her his ticket punch, a heavy brass thing that made no hole. [171] She had tested it on a hundred scraps of paper as a child; the jaws closed, the mechanism clicked, and the paper came away unmarked. [196] Her mother called it the phantom punch and refused to say more, and that refusal was the first of the family's many silences. [219] There were silences about why he stopped working, about the fire, about the brother nobody photographed. [235] Ines had drawn a map of those silences once, on a napkin, and labeled the blank center the fortress of unspoken truths. [257] She wanted to find it more than she wanted anything. [267]

So she made herself a dare, written in her logbook in her neat draftsman's hand: go at the hour that divides bad habits and new starts, and go alone. [296] Midnight, then. [298]

The gate at Vasari Street hung on one hinge, and the arrows in the pavement ran under it like stitches. [318] Inside, the concourse had filled with river mist, waist-deep and slow, and above it the ceiling opened into the old starlit vault: a barrel of blue plaster studded with gilt constellations, painted in 1911 so travelers would always know which way was north. [361] Ines stood in the mist under the false stars and felt the trembling certainty of someone whose measurements have all come out right. [384]

A man in a conductor's coat waited at the gate to platform four. [397] He was not her grandfather. [402] He had her grandfather's chin. [407]

"Ticket," he said. [410]

She had none, so she held out the punch instead. [420] He took it with the tenderness of a man handling his own name. [433]

"He never turned me in," the man said. [441] "Nineteen fifty-one, the strike, the fire in the signal shed." [451] "I set it, and I ran, and my brother punched my ticket with that thing, which leaves nothing, so the inspectors had no record to follow." [477] "He kept a broken punch on purpose." [484] "He lost his post for it, and then he lost me, because I never came back to say thank you." [504]

Ines understood then that the fortress of unspoken truths was not a place but a habit, passed down like the punch, and that unraveling mysteries was only ever the work of saying one plain sentence out loud. [541]

"You're my great-uncle," she said, and the mist thinned a handspan. [552] He laughed, and the laugh was old and dusty and real. [563] The station clock ticked over, the gilt stars caught some light from somewhere, and the arrows in the pavement began to close like healing skin. [588]

He gave her back the punch. [594] "Tell it," he said. [598] "All of it." [601] "Draw it, if that's how you talk." [608]

She went home in the small new hour and started a map that had no blank center: nine blocks of arrows, a signal shed, two brothers, one fire, and every silence finally labeled in her small precise hand. [646] In the margin she drew a brass punch with its jaws open, and beneath it wrote: it left no hole, and it held everything. [670]
