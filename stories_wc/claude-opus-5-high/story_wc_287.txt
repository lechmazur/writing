The last petal let go at moonrise, and Mira heard it land. [12] That was the kind of quiet her building kept after midnight: a quivering hush, thin as glass, that made small things loud. [34] She had been the fire escape gardener of 14 Harbor Row for nine years, ever since her father hauled the first crate of soil up the iron stairs and told her that things grow anywhere you insist they grow. [73] Tomato vines, mint, a stubborn fig, and the old rosebush he had carried from his mother's yard. [90] He had been the lockmaster then, the man the whole waterfront woke when a door refused them. [107] Now he was four months in the ground, and the rose had finally finished. [121] In her palm lay the one thing she had kept from his bench: a bitting gauge tooth, a sliver of hardened steel no longer than a thumbnail, filed and polished until it threw light like water. [157] He had used it to measure the depth of key cuts, and afterward, walking home, to reflect light into dark corners. [178] A streetlamp caught in that steel could find a keyhole for an old woman, a dropped coin, a cat under a stoop. [200] Before he died he had given her one instruction, in the wistfully pragmatic way he said everything. [217] "After the final petal drops from the old rosebush, row out and see what I saw," he told her, "and don't make a ceremony of it." [243]

So she went down the fire escape with the petal in her pocket and the tooth in her fist. [262] Dov, the baker's boy, was sitting on the third landing where he always sat when the stairwell bulb died, afraid of the dark between floors. [287] "Come on," she said, and he came, because she had never once told him there was nothing to be afraid of. [308]

Her father's skiff was still tied at the public dock, still bailed, still smelling of rope and iron filings. [327] She rowed with the rhythm of oars in water that he had taught her to keep - pull, glide, breathe - the rhythm he claimed could instill patience in anyone who lasted past the first blisters. [361] Dov sat in the bow with his knees up, watching the town slide backward. [375] Past the breakwater the water changed. [381] The harbor was luminous by moonlight, every swell carrying a pale seam of fire, the jellyfish beneath them glowing like lamps sunk in green glass. [406] Mira shipped the oars, and the quiver went out of the night; what was left was a hush so complete she could hear the hull ticking. [432]

This, then, was what he had seen, every month for thirty years, alone. [445] A man who spent his days inside other people's locks, rowing out to the one place that could not be locked. [466] She understood, sitting there, that he had not sent her out to grieve. [479] He had sent her to be refilled. [485]

She held up the bitting gauge tooth and turned it until it caught the moon, and the little steel face threw a coin of light across Dov's chest. [513] "It works on anything bright," she said. [520] "Streetlamp, moon, a match - you aim it where the dark is, and the dark has to move over." [538] Dov took it carefully, the way you take something that has a thorn hidden in it somewhere. [555] She thought of the rosebush, which had guarded its one bloom all summer behind a hedge of thorns, and had let it go anyway, on schedule, without complaint. [583] That was the trick of it, she decided: you armor the soft thing, and then you open your hand. [602]

They rowed back in the same rhythm, Dov's turn on the oars, badly at first and then better. [620] At the stairwell she let him go up ahead with the tooth, angling stolen light from the street door into each blind corner until he reached his family's landing. [649] He handed it back without being asked. [656]

On the fire escape, Mira pruned the spent rosebush down to hard wood, because that was what you did in autumn if you wanted anything in spring. [683] She set the dried petal in the tin where her father had kept his blanks, and laid the tooth beside it, ready. [705] Below her the harbor kept burning quietly, and the building held its breath, and she went in to sleep. [724]
