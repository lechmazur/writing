The atrium of the old customs house filled with mist at twilight, the way it had every evening since the bombardment cracked its glass roof. [25] Teuila liked the hour best, when the ward's fluorescent glare softened and the sea fog came in through the broken panes like something forgiving. [49] For eleven months she had translated for the medical unit, turning threats into flat English and flat English into apologies nobody believed. [71] Words had become cargo to her: heavy, damp, and never hers. [82]

Then came Kalo, nine years old, with a spiral fracture and a plaster cast covered in tiny smudged notes. [101] He was shrewdly innocent, the sort of child who asked doctors their first names and then used them to negotiate extra jelly. [123] He told everyone the marks were just drawings, and everyone believed him, because believing children is cheaper than asking. [142] Teuila asked. [144]

He showed her the brain coral fragment he kept in his sling, grey and grooved, its ridges winding like a maze somebody had thought about for a thousand years. [173] His grandmother had harvested fragments like it to graft onto the bleached reef off Vaitau, before the patrol boats made the lagoon a place people only crossed at night. [202] Dead coral, she used to say, is only a house waiting for a tenant. [216]

The next morning the surgeons had to break and reset the arm, and Teuila stood by to translate the consent forms into something a boy could carry. [243] After the anesthetic kicked in, his grip on the coral loosened, and his mouth kept working. [259] "Start at the mouth of the maze," he mumbled in Nau'an, "and count the turns." [274] They cut the old cast off in two pieces and dropped it in a bin with the bloodied gauze, and Teuila fished it out. [298] It took her the whole surgery, but she decrypted the smudged notes on the cast by tracing the coral's grooves with her fingernail, each valley a letter, each fork a break between words. [331]

They were not drawings. [335] They were fears. [338]

Sister Maleko is afraid her hands will start shaking during a suture. [350] The Australian anesthetist is afraid his daughter will not recognize his voice. [362] The boy in bed four is afraid of the sound a generator makes when it dies. [378] The translator is afraid she has become only a wire that other people's voices pass through. [394]

He had been collecting them for weeks, quietly, the way his grandmother collected fragments. [408] A soldier had told him once that a fear written down and hidden could not come true, and Kalo, being shrewd, had improved on the idea: he hid them in plain sight, on his own arm, where the whole ward carried them together. [451]

That evening, in the mist-clad atrium, she sat with the two halves in her lap and worked to unknot the last, hardest lines, the fog beading on the plaster. [480] Eleven months of carrying other people's words had taught her nothing except how to set them down unchanged; now she wanted, for once, to graft new meaning onto old wounds. [510]

When Kalo woke with his new white cast, she was ready. [521] She had rewritten every decoded line in his own coral cipher, but changed at the hinge of each sentence. [540] Sister Maleko's hands are steady because she is afraid; that is what steady means. [554] The anesthetist's daughter knows his voice the way the lagoon knows the rain. [567] The translator is a reef, not a wire. [575]

Kalo read it slowly, lips moving, the coral fragment turning in his good hand. [589] "You changed them," he said, not quite an accusation. [598] "I translated them," she said. [603] He considered this with the gravity of a small negotiator, then asked whether she would collect the night shift's fears too, since she was awake anyway. [629]

By the end of the week, the cast had run out of room, and the shared anxieties of the ward had begun migrating onto the atrium's cracked panes in grease pencil, where the mist blurred them into something almost tender. [669] Nobody signed them; everybody read them. [675]

Teuila kept the broken coral in her breast pocket through the last months of the war, a small grey maze pressed over an ache she had stopped trying to name. [705] When the lagoon at Vaitau opened again, she went out with Kalo at low tide and watched him wire the fragment to a dead white shelf of reef. [733] It would take years to spread, he said, shrewd and unbothered, already negotiating with time. [748] She thought of the mist, and the grease pencil, and all those borrowed fears finally living somewhere they could grow. [768]
