The last print newspaper in the world came out eleven years ago, and I tore it into strips to teach myself patience. [22] One strip a night, burned in the brazier, while the emerald lighthouse hummed above me and the stars wheeled through its green glass like fish in a bottle. [50] That was the routine, and the routine was not entirely of this world. [63] Every dusk I climbed one hundred and two steps, wound the clock, and trimmed the glass lantern wick. [81] The wick was spun glass, fine as a spider's ambition, and it did not burn; it only carried light deeper into itself, forever, through total internal reflection. [108] Nothing escaped the lamp except what the emerald lens decided to let go, and what it let go was old: light that had been trapped in the tower since before the tower, shadows that predated shorelines. [144] Ancient shadows, my predecessor called them, and she told me they could be steered. [158] I learned to steer them the way a woman learns her own heartbeat: by accident, then by devotion. [176]

The laughing storm herald came on the ninth night of every month, riding the weather he announced. [193] He was tall and wet and cheerful, a man-shaped rumor of thunder, and he laughed the whole time he spoke, which made his warnings sound like invitations. [220] "Two hulls tonight, keeper!" he would howl, delighted. [228] "Two hulls, and a widow by morning!" [235] I never asked him to stop laughing. [242] In a world with no more newspapers, a herald is a herald, even a gleeful one. [258]

My own gift was clumsier than his. [265] I was accidentally prophetic, which is the worst way to be prophetic. [277] Muttering over the wick, I would say something idle -- "the green one will run aground on her own name" -- and three weeks later a trawler called Verdigris would split on the bar. [309] I did not mean it; meaning was never required. [318] So I stopped talking, mostly, and that is when I started listening to what the routine wanted. [335]

It wanted a dawn. [339] Here is the thing nobody prints anymore: the sun has been coming up tired. [353] Thin, gray, secondhand, like light that has already been used by someone else's morning. [367] I wanted to shape a new one. [374] Not to steal the sun's job -- only to give it better scaffolding, built out of the ancient shadows the emerald lens had been hoarding since the first keeper climbed the stairs. [405] Shadows are only light that has agreed to wait. [414]

On the ninth night of the ninth month, the herald arrived laughing so hard he had to hold the rail. [434] "Keeper," he gasped, "tonight the sea comes up the stairs!" [444] I said, without thinking, "Then the stairs will learn to swim." [455] His laughter stopped for the first time in eleven years, and in the silence I understood that I had just made a promise. [478]

We went up together. [482] The storm came in green through the walls, and the stars above the lantern room swam like something being born. [502] I set my hands on the glass lantern wick and did the only thing the routine had ever taught me: I refused to let the light out. [529] I kept it folding into itself, angle after angle, deeper and brighter, until the lens could hold no more and the old shadows began to slide. [555] Then I steered. [558] Not ships -- shadows. [561] I pushed them east, laying them along the horizon like keels, like rafters, like the ribs of a morning that had not existed yesterday. [585] The sea came up the stairs and the stairs, God help us, learned to swim. [600]

At the end of it the herald was weeping and grinning at once, and so was I, and I have no better word for what we felt than shadowed elation -- joy with a drowned village in it. [637] The new dawn came up at 6:04 and it was not gray. [649] It came up green, and it held. [656]

Now the herald comes on the ninth night and does not laugh at drownings anymore; he laughs at the sunrise, which he says is in poor taste and excellent color. [686] I still trim the wick at dusk. [693] I still have four strips of the last newspaper left, and I burn one a night, and I am careful now about what I say over the flame. [721] Last night I said, "Four more mornings." [728] I meant the paper. [732] I am almost certain I meant the paper. [740]
