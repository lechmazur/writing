The fluorescent tube over the ticket window buzzed like a trapped wasp, and Mara Oyelaran waited for it to die. [20] It always did, eventually; ghosts hated competition. [27] She had come to the abandoned circus to service an elevator nobody had ridden in thirty-one years. [44] The ticket office stood at the midway's mouth, a plaster confection of scrolls and gilded lions, and its facade was webbed with cracks. [67] Behind the booth, a cage elevator dropped into the tunnels where the elephants had once been walked out of sight of the crowd. [90] The new owners wanted it certified before demolition. [98] Mara wanted something else. [102]

She had been skillfully observant since childhood, the way other children were tall: it was simply how she occupied a room. [123] In her trade, that meant reading dust. [130] Dust fell evenly in still air, so any pattern was a confession: a clean stripe where a hatch had swung, a gray drift banked against a seam, a halo of settled grit around something recently lifted. [166] Ceilings were her obsession. [170] People hid things overhead because they never looked up, and Mara had spent twenty years inside shafts, looking up, wanting to know what ceilings kept. [195]

[222] "You are the first in years who hasn't run," he said. [233] "I'm union," Mara said. [237] "We don't run, we file paperwork." [243] He laughed without sound, and she felt the familiar sensation of her work doubling: a weightless burden, heavy as a promise, light as air in her hands. [270]

He had been the circus horologist, he told her, keeper of the midway clock and of the elevator's timing gear. [290] He had died in the winter the circus folded, and something of his had never come down. [307] "Up there," he said, not pointing, because ghosts rarely need to. [318]

Mara raised her lamp to the pressed-tin ceiling and read it like a page.
