Mirren had not slept properly since the water wars ended. [10] On Saltcomb, a tidal island stitched to the mainland twice a day by a road of wet shingle, that made her useful. [32] She kept the night watch, and she painted. [40] Not for beauty; she painted to thresh meaning from experience, the way her grandmother had threshed barley, beating the husk off a thing until something edible fell out. [68] Six years of paintings, and she had not yet gotten anything edible out of the war. [84]

What she did have was rain. [90] Rain was the island's only honest wealth now, and Mirren had painted it so often she knew its habits better than her own face. [114] She knew where it pooled, where it braided, where it ran silver down the hill in trails that always, always forked left at the broken chapel and then simply stopped. [144] Water does not stop. [148]

So on the third night of the autumn storms, sleepless as usual, she went out with a lantern and mapped the island by following rain trails. [174] Every runnel on the eastern slope drained toward one seam of turf behind the chapel, and vanished into it like thread into a needle. [198] She dug until dawn with a palette knife and then with her hands. [211] Two feet down, the turf gave onto dressed stone, and the stone gave onto a vaulted dark that breathed cold at her. [233] Her lantern showed three great mouths of pipe meeting in a chamber, pre-war work, and beneath them a channel of moving water wide enough to swim. [259] An unearthed convergence, three dead aqueducts that had never actually died. [270]

Mirren sat on the wet grass in the grey light and laughed until she wept, hollow-eyed, shaking, full of a haggard awe she had no paint for. [297] Men had burned each other's cities over less than what lay under her feet. [311]

A discovery like that needed a witness with standing, or the mainland cartel would simply take it and meter it out by the cup. [335] That meant Oyo, the cistern-warden, who had surveyed Saltcomb before the wars and who trusted nobody, least of all a painter who never slept. [359] He listened to her at his door with the tide hissing in behind her. [373]

"Everyone on this rock has found a secret river," he said, wryly earnest, the way old engineers are when they have stopped expecting miracles but would still like one. [402] "Bring me something I can lose." [408]

It was an old island custom, and a cruel one: an exchange, a thing of real weight given over, so that a liar would pay for his lie. [436] Mirren had one thing of weight. [442] Her sister Del's amber brooch, dug out of the rubble at Tearn with a fragment of insect still suspended in it, the only object Mirren owned that she had never been able to paint. [476] She had tried eleven times. [481] Every canvas came out as a small orange coffin. [490] She put it in Oyo's palm and watched him understand exactly what it was. [504]

He came. [506] He went down into the chamber with a plumb line and a lamp, and stayed an hour, and came up with his face wrecked in the same way hers had been. [537]

"Eleven thousand litres an hour," he said. "It was never lost. It was only buried, and nobody thought to watch the rain." [559] He held out the amber. [564] "Custom says I keep it. Custom is an idiot." [573]

Mirren did not take it back. [579] "Melt it into the ledger stone, or bury it by the intake," she said. "Del spent four years hauling water in buckets. Let her be the one standing at the tap." [610]

That winter the island drank without rationing for the first time since the wars. [624] Mirren painted the convergence eighteen times and got it wrong eighteen times. [636] The nineteenth was only rain on a hillside, running downhill, forking left. [648] Threshed at last: the grain of it was that the world hides what it has not destroyed, and that a sleepless woman with a lantern is a kind of answer. [678] She hung it in the pump house, went home while the causeway was still open, and slept eleven hours. [697]
