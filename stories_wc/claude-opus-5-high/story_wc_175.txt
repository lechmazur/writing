Mira called herself an esoteric realist, which meant she believed the world hid meanings and also that a person had to measure them. [23] She kept a notebook, a plumb line, and one moth wing pressed under glass. [37] The grotto sat above the clouds, a green pocket in the mountain's shoulder where ferns dripped and the light came up pink through vapor each morning, a blushing stillness that made even breathing feel loud. [72] She had come for the last monarch. [79] The census people had confirmed it in spring: one female, tagged, moving north along a corridor her ancestors had used for ten thousand years. [103] Mira's job, unofficial and unpaid, was to mark the old waystations before the corridor was forgotten entirely. [120] There were nineteen of them between the delta and the high grotto, and at each she built the same thing: a lattice of green willow, four feet tall, lashed with grass. [151] They were signals, not monuments. [155] Monuments claimed permanence; her structures were transiently rooted, meant to stand one season, sprout if they could, and rot if they could not. [178] Anyone flying the corridor would see the pattern from the air and understand that someone had been counting. [196] The grotto was the nineteenth. [200] It was also the one nobody had recorded. [208] The Hual people had wintered here, watching the orange rivers pass overhead, and the survey maps called the place Unnamed Depression 14. [230] Mira had found their fire ring under two inches of moss, and beside it a child's toy: a stick doll with wings glued on, one of them a moth wing, brown and veined and intact after a century of cold, dry air. [272] That was the wing under her glass. [279] A realist would say it proved only that a child had once made a toy. [294] An esotericist would say the child had known the difference between moth and butterfly and had chosen the humbler creature on purpose. [316] Mira held both positions and let them argue. [324] She worked through the cold hour after dawn, cutting willow, bending it, driving each rib into the soft black soil. [344] Her hands kept a rhythm she had learned at the first waystation: cut, bend, set, knot. [360] Cut, bend, set, knot. [364] It was a cadence, and she had come to trust it the way sailors trust a hull. [381] On the fourth rib, the cadence broke. [388] Her fingers stopped mid-knot because something orange had entered the grotto. [399] The monarch came in low over the lip of the rock, wings ragged at the trailing edge, tag glinting like a chip of mica. [423] She did not move. [427] The unfinished lattice stood between them, half a cage, half a gesture, and the butterfly settled on the loose end of grass she had been tying. [453] For eleven minutes, by her watch, nothing in the grotto happened. [464] The vapor came pink over the rim; the ferns dripped; the last monarch opened and closed her wings as if testing a hinge. [487] Mira understood, with the flat clarity she reserved for facts, that she was the only person alive watching the end of a migration older than any written language. [515] She also understood, with the other half of herself, that she had been invited to finish the knot. [533] When the butterfly lifted and went out through the cloud gate, Mira tied off the grass and kept building. [552] She finished at noon. [556] Inside the lattice she set the fire ring's largest stone, and on the stone she laid the moth wing, unglassed, where weather would take it. [581] That felt right to both halves of her: the realist knew the wing would be dust by spring, and the esotericist knew that dust was the point. [608] In her notebook she wrote: Station 19, grotto above the clouds, Hual wintering site, monarch present 6:14 to 6:25. [627] Then, because a record of facts is not the same as a record of what mattered, she added a second line: The cadence was interrupted, and the interruption was the message. [658] She descended before dark, leaving the lattice to lean into the wind, green and doomed and visible from the air, one more small structure insisting that something had happened here, and that someone had bothered to say so.
