They call me the corn maze architect's lost child, though I was never lost in the way they mean. [19] I walked out of my father's last maze on my own feet, seven years old, after the butterfly effect had already begun rewriting the sky. [44] He stayed inside. [47] That is the whole story, and it has taken me thirty years to learn how to tell it slowly. [66]

Now I keep house on a drifting prism terrace, one of the old refraction platforms they hung above the plains when the weather stopped obeying arithmetic. [92] The terrace rides the thermals on a tether of glass and cable, and its floor throws broken rainbows across everything I own. [114] Up here the sound of the world arrives as a fractured hush, wind split into colors, thunder filed down to a whisper by distance. [138] My father would have approved. [143] He believed in necessary distances. [148] A maze, he said, is only kindness arranged at the correct intervals: too near and the walls smother, too far and there is no path at all. [175]

I work as a retrospect climatologist, which is a grand name for a woman who reads furniture. [192] After the butterfly effect, every instrument record went soft; the satellites disagreed with the buoys, the buoys disagreed with themselves. [212] Wood does not disagree. [216] So I spend my days analyzing the grain in wooden furniture - chair rails, church pews, cradle slats - counting the drought years pressed thin and the wet years spread wide, rebuilding a century of vanished summers from a dead man's sideboard. [256]

Last spring a crate came up the cable with my father's desk inside. [269] The salvage crews had found it in a silo, and someone had recognized the initials burned under the drawer. [288] In the drawer was his pilot logbook, from the years he flew crop surveys to photograph his own mazes from above. [309] Every entry ended in weather code, those clipped three-letter groups pilots used: BKN, FEW, TSRA, and at the bottom of the final page, written in the space reserved for remarks, a code that is not in any manual. [347] It read: SHE LEFT BY THE EAST GATE, CLEAR. [356] Clear, in weather code, means nothing between you and the whole sky. [368] He had not lost me. [373] He had logged me, the way he logged ceilings and visibility, as a condition of the air he flew through. [393]

I put the logbook on the terrace rail and let the prisms cut it into seven colors while I decided whether to be angry. [417] Then I went to work on the desk itself. [426] The grain told me what he never did. [434] The oak had grown through the drought decade, rings crowded like held breath, and then one impossibly fat year - the season of the maze he built for my mother, the year the rain came back. [469] Cross-referenced against the logbook's codes, his flights matched the wet years exactly; he had been chasing the return of water across four states, mapping it in corn. [496]

I could intuit the rest, the way I have always been able to intuit a maze from inside it: he sent me out the east gate because the east gate was the only one with clear air behind it. [535] The storm that ended the old climate was coming down the west rows, and he stayed to hold the wall so I would not turn back looking for him. [564]

All my life I have wanted to discover why some thresholds feel sacred - why a doorway can stop the breath, why I have never been able to step over the gap at the terrace gate without pausing. [601] Now I think a threshold is sacred when someone decided, at that exact line, how far away from you they would have to stand in order to love you correctly. [631] The gate remembers the arithmetic even after the people are gone. [642]

I have begun building again, up here where nobody expects crops. [653] Not corn - the terrace cannot carry soil - but a maze of hung glass panels, refracting, each wall set at the interval the grain and the codes together taught me. [682] Children come up the cable to walk it. [690] They say the light inside sounds like something, which is impossible, and also exactly right. [705] At the entrance I have bolted the logbook open to its last page under a pane of glass, a small sign beneath it: CLEAR. [729] My assistant, a girl of nine who is featherly determined and weighs about as much as a gull, asks me every morning what it means. [754] I tell her it is a weather report for a threshold, and that she will understand it the day she walks out of something on her own feet. [782] Then I let her go first, at the necessary distance, and watch. [794]
