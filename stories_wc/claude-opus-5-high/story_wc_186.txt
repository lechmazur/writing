Mira had been the last custodian of the beacon-tower for nineteen winters, and in all that time the light had stayed dead. [22] The cities had gone quiet long before her birth; roads had become riverbeds, and thistledown drifted over the highway where trucks once ran, catching the wind like small stubborn hopes. [52] She was retrocognitively blessed, the elders of the salt-marsh had said, though blessing was a generous word for it. [71] Press her palm to any object and its past unspooled behind her eyes: the hands that made it, the weather it stood through, the last face it saw. [99] It made her useless at farming and invaluable at ruins. [109] In the tower's archive she had found the lens schematic, and learned that the beacon's heart was a crystal prism cut in four lobes, one of which had shattered the night the grid failed. [143] Without the fourth lobe the broken crystal prism scattered lamplight into useless smears, and the coast stayed unlit, and boats kept breaking on the reef with a regularity that felt almost like argument. [176] The missing lobe had a name in the old logs: a shard of hush. [190] It was cut from a glass that swallowed sound, so that the keeper could stand inside the lamp's roar and hear the sea instead. [214] Mira wanted it for a smaller, stranger reason. [221] All her life, things had arrived exactly when she needed them, and the village called that luck, or worse, coincidence. [241] She meant to rekindle the beacon in order to prove that coincidences have meaning - that a world stripped of its people still arranged itself toward something. [268] The logs said the shard had been carried, for repair, to the glass humming pavilion twelve miles inland. [286] She reached it in autumn: a dome of angled panes set on a hill of thistles, every pane tuned so that wind drew a low chord from the structure, a sound like a held breath. [321] The floor was a mosaic of shattered glass, ten thousand fragments, and any of them might be hers. [339] Touching them one by one would take years she did not have; her hands already bled from the trying. [358] So she pressed her palm to the pavilion's central pillar instead, and let the building remember for her. [376] What came was not a face but a sky. [384] The pavilion had been an observatory as much as an instrument; its builders aligned each pane to the rising of a particular star, and the repairman, working at night by the old habit of astronomers, had set the shard down in the beam of whichever pane was lit. [432] In the vision she saw the sky he saw: Vega low, the Pleiades shouldering over the eastern rim. [450] That was the whole of her method - she would find the shard by tracking star paths, matching a dead man's night to her own. [475] She waited eleven nights for the sky to turn the same face toward her, correcting for the decades of drift with a notebook, a plumb line, and arithmetic done on the back of a seed packet. [511] On the twelfth night the Pleiades cleared the rim, a single pane took their light, and a thin ladder of starlight lay down across the broken floor. [538] Where it ended, the glass did not shine. [546] The shard drank the starlight as it drank sound, a wedge of absolute quiet in her palm, and when she closed her hand around it the pavilion's humming stopped, as though the hill itself had paused to listen. [584] She walked home through thistle-blown fields, seedheads breaking white around her knees, and she did not hurry, because certainty is patient. [605] Seating the lobe took four days of grinding and one cracked knuckle. [617] Then she filled the reservoir with rendered fish oil, struck the wick, and stood inside the lamp housing in a silence so complete she could hear the reef three miles out. [648] The mended prism gathered the small flame and threw it out whole, one white arm sweeping the water. [666] Before dawn a boat came in under it, a coastal trader who had been navigating by guesswork for a season, and her crew climbed the tower to ask who had lit the light. [699] Mira did not say luck. [704] She said that a man had set a piece of quiet down in a beam of starlight eighty years ago, and that the stars had come back around to say where. [735] Nothing in the world, she told them, is only a coincidence; some of it is simply a message that has not yet found its reader. [760] Behind her the beacon turned, and turned, and the thistledown went on going wherever the wind had meant it to go. [781]
