<story>My mother called our workshop the Vanishing Point Gallery, because every wall leaned toward one small door at the far end, and because everything made inside was meant to disappear. [30] She was the charcoal portrait ash scatterer, and for forty years the grieving came up our stairs to find her. [50] They came to crystallize a fleeting emotion: the last laugh of a husband, a daughter's unguarded face in doorway light, the strange peace that arrives an hour before a funeral. [80] My mother would listen, draw them in charcoal, and then burn the drawing in the iron dish. [97] I used to think the burning was cruelty. [104] It is the opposite. [108] She taught me to powder the cinders in a marble mortar until they turned to a black flour finer than breath, then stir that dust into glaze and fire it onto porcelain. [140] A drawing fades; a glaze holds a thousand years. [149] The emotion survives, but only as a color nobody can name. [160] Most clients took their small fired plate home and wept over it. [172] Some could not bear to, and left the porcelain behind, and those my mother broke with a brass hammer and set into the mask. [196] It hung by the door: a mask made from shattered porcelain shards, forty years of abandoned grief cemented into one calm face. [218] She wore it when she told people what their portrait had shown her. [231] She never lied. [234] She weaves truth carefully, I still say, because the present tense feels less like losing her. [250] Her method was through incomplete information: she gave a widow the part she could carry and kept the rest in her own chest, unspoken, like a stone in a coat pocket. [281] When I accused her of managing people, she called it the mirage of safety, and she was not ashamed of it. [302] A mirage, she said, is not a lie about water; it is real light bent by real heat. [320] People need something shimmering ahead of them to keep walking across the sand. [333] Then the shimmer dissolves and they find they've crossed anyway. [343] She sat in the old rocking chair at the center of the gallery all winter, where the converging walls made her look farther away than she was. [370] In February she asked me to draw her. [378] I asked her what emotion she wanted crystallized, and she said she did not know yet, which was the first thing she ever admitted not knowing. [404] It was also then that I learned the doctors had given her until autumn, and that autumn had been nine months ago, and that she had told me only the word 'tired.' [436] She had built me a mirage and let me live in it, and I had crossed a great deal of sand without knowing. [459] I wanted to be furious. [463] Instead I sharpened my charcoal. [468] I drew her for three days while she rocked, and the sound of the runners on the boards was the only clock we kept. [492] On the third day she stopped naming what she wanted and simply looked at me, and I finally saw the thing worth keeping. [515] It was not love, exactly; it was the relief of a woman who no longer had to hold anything back. [535] That lasted perhaps four seconds, which is generous, as fleeting emotions go. [547] I got it down. [551] Then, as the old rocking chair finally slows to a stop, I take the drawing to the iron dish, because she taught me that keeping the paper is the one true cruelty. [583] The charcoal catches, and for a moment her face is bright and moving before it goes gray. [600] I will powder the cinders tonight, and glaze a single plate, and fire it at dawn. [616] I know already that I will not be able to keep it. [628] So I will break it with the brass hammer, and set her shards into the mask beside all the others. [648] Tomorrow a stranger will climb our stairs with something small and bright dying in her hands. [664] I will put on the mask, and I will not tell her everything. [677] I will give her water that is only bent light, and she will walk on it as far as she needs to, and the walls of the gallery will narrow behind her toward the little door where everything goes. [716] It is the kindest thing I know how to make. [726]</story>
