The giant slept only twice a year, and the whole city of Ospria arranged its errands around the rhythm of that breathing. [22] Wren Halloway packed her brushes before the first swell. [31] She was a natural history illustrator, which in Ospria meant she drew the animals that the tides of the giant's lungs had stranded on the rooftops. [57] The rooftop canal system wove between the towers like a script nobody had finished writing, and when the giant inhaled, water climbed the aqueducts and filled every channel; when it exhaled, the canals drained and the city's boats hung in their slings. [99]

In her satchel she carried a brass cage, and in the cage sat an echo-thrush, the last one anybody had caught. [120] It had been her mother's bird. [126] Eleven years ago, her mother had been singing a lullaby in the tower observatory when her heart stopped on the fourth-to-last note, and the thrush, being what it was, had swallowed the phrase whole. [160] Since then it sang that interrupted cadence every dawn, hanging forever on the note before resolution, and Wren had grown up unable to sleep through it. [186] A song cannot end inside a cage. [193]

Everyone in Ospria knew the old claim: that echoes were born in a hollow somewhere past the ninth tower, at the headwater of the canals, and that a sound carried there would finally complete itself. [228] Nobody had ever rowed that far, because the water only ran high across a giant's nap, and a nap was nine hours. [250] The journey took eleven. [254]

Which is why, at the Lockhouse of the Second Tier, Wren knocked on the door of the woman who sculpts time itself. [276] Marisol the Hourwright answered in an apron dusted with something that glittered like powdered clock. [291] "Two hours," Wren said. [295] "I can pay." [298]

Marisol took the pendant Wren offered: a small silver infinity symbol, her mother's, worn thin at one loop. [316] "This is a lovely lie," the Hourwright said. [324] "Nothing goes on forever. [328] But it will hold two hours, if you let me hollow it." [340] She worked the metal the way a sculptor works marble, taking away rather than adding, and explained the terms: time cannot be made, only borrowed. [365] The two hours would come out of Wren's own late years, the quiet ones at the end. [382] Wren thought of eleven dawns times three hundred and sixty-five, all of them stopping short, and said yes. [400]

She launched at the first high water. [406] The canals ran silver between the towers, threading through arches, spilling down step-weirs, and she rowed with the pendant warm against her collarbone. [429] Habit made her stop to sketch: a colony of glass-shelled snails at the fourth tower, a wading heron with the wrong number of joints at the sixth. [456] Her hands recorded what her mother would have wanted recorded; that was the other inheritance. [471]

By the eighth tower the water had begun to sag, and she felt the giant shift far below, its breath thinning toward waking. [494] She broke the pendant's thin loop. [500] The two borrowed hours poured out and stood the water still, holding the canals at full flood while nothing else in the city moved. [524] Wren rowed through the frozen hush, past the ninth tower, into a hollow of wet stone where the aqueducts were born. [545] Every sound she had ever made seemed to be waiting there, patient, unfinished. [558]

She set the cage on the thwart and worked the latch to uncage the thrush. [573] It hopped to the gunwale, shook itself, and gave the lullaby back to the hollow. [588] The stone answered. [591] The phrase went out and came home with the last three notes attached, resolved, ordinary, kind, and the bird listened to itself finish and fell quiet for the first time in eleven years. [624]

Then the borrowed hours ran out, the water sank, and the boat settled into the mud of the high channel to wait for the next nap. [650] Wren did not mind. [654] She had provisions for a season, a pencil, and a hollow full of animals nobody had ever drawn. [672] The thrush stayed on the gunwale, learning new sounds to hold, and when she sang to it, she let the songs end. [694]
