The flood took the observatory's basement, the backup drives, and eleven years of timing residuals for pulsar J1748-2446. [18] Dr. Ilse Varga did not weep about it, because weeping was drama, and drama was the first thing she had trained herself to strip out. [43] A neutron star does not perform grief; it simply spins down, losing angular momentum by fractions of a nanosecond per year, and calls that decay. [68] So she drove three hours north to her grandmother's house, which the water had also visited, and stood in the storm-damaged greenhouse behind it. [92] Half the panes were gone. [97] The ones remaining wore a gray film that the rain had somehow not washed away, and the iron ribs were furred with rust the color of dried blood. [125] Her grandmother had raised swallowtails here for forty years, releasing them each August in a ceremony Ilse had always found unbearably sentimental. [147] She scraped a fingertip across the inside of one pane and looked at what came off: not soot, not pollen. [167] Dust from butterfly wing after butterfly wing, scales shed over four decades of beating against glass, settled in strata like snowfall in an ice core. [192] She recognized, with the unpleasant lurch of a theory arriving uninvited, that she was looking at a clock. [210]

Ilse's whole career ran on a pipeline that preprocesses reality before a human ever sees it: raw photon counts folded, averaged, clipped of outliers, compressed to a single tidy number per night. [242] It was an elegant habit, and it meant that when the water rose, the summaries drowned and no raw record survived to rebuild them from. [267] The greenhouse had kept no summaries at all. [275] It had only kept everything. [280]

She brought out her grandmother's dissecting scope, a lamp, a car battery, and a notebook, and she began to work the way she had not worked since graduate school: through accumulated dust patterns, layer by layer, with no algorithm standing between her and the evidence. [325] The scales sorted themselves by species and by season. [334] Black tiger-swallowtail bands lay thick in the lower strata; above them, thinner and thinner, the same black, cut with the pale grit of failing paint and the spores of a mold that liked wet wood. [369] No single year showed a catastrophe. [375] That was the finding, and it took her four days to accept it: the greenhouse had not been killed by the storm. [397] The storm had merely removed the glass from a structure that had been surrendering for twenty years, one degree of gradual change per season, each too small to alarm anyone, including the woman who swept it every Sunday. [435]

Ilse had written eleven papers on spin-down decay and had never once understood it in her hands. [452] A pulsar does not fail; it pays out, microsecond by microsecond, until the lighthouse is dark and nobody can name the night it went. [476] Her grandmother had gone the same way, two winters back, in increments the family had kept explaining away. [494] Ilse had given the eulogy and had cut every adjective out of it, proud of herself, certain that restraint was a kind of respect. [518] Kneeling on the cracked brick with a scalpel and a slide, she saw the flaw in that. [535] Stripping needless drama was good practice; stripping the record was vandalism. [546] The dust had kept both the signal and the mess, and only because it kept the mess could it tell her anything true. [569]

In September she went back to the observatory and rewrote the pipeline so that it archived the raw frames first and preprocessed them second, which cost the project a quarter of its storage budget and most of her standing with the director. [611] She argued for it badly, without rhetoric, holding up a glass slide of forty years of wing scales as though it were a graph. [635] It was, in fact, a graph. [641]

She reglazed the greenhouse herself over two autumns, badly and slowly, leaving one uncleaned pane at the east end as a control. [663] Swallowtails came back the third summer, a poor showing, six or seven, and she did not release them with a ceremony because she did not know how. [690] She simply opened the door and stood aside, feeling something small and warm kindle in her, which she identified, after some effort, as humility. [714] It was not a large feeling. [720] It would have to accumulate. [725]
