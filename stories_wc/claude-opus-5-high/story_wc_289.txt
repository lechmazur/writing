After the cloud bursts, the lily pond around the lotus temple wore a skin of silver, and Nilima came down the steps to count what the rain had knocked loose. [30] She was the city's love letter ghostwriter, and the storm had been good for business. [45] Grief and longing both rose with the water. [53] For eleven years she had written in other people's voices: the fishmonger's proposal, the widow's apology, the schoolteacher's confession that she had never loved anyone but a woman in Nagpur. [83] She was the keeper of secrets nobody else could hold, and the weight of them had begun to feel like a hundred small stones sewn into her hem. [111] What she wanted, more than payment, was to find the thread that connected all things: some proof that the fishmonger's hunger and the widow's guilt were one hunger, one guilt, differently lit. [143]

On the lowest step, half in the water, she found the thrush's nest. [156] It had come down whole from the peepal, and the rain had unwound it into a spiral of twigs, each one curling out from the center like a sentence still being spoken. [188] Woven among the twigs were threads: kite string, red tailor's cotton, a strand of somebody's hair. [204] The bird had been a keeper of secrets too, she thought, and had built a house out of them. [223]

That afternoon, in the temple's outer gallery, where light came through the alabaster petals in a prismatic quiet, Ratan the sign painter hired her for the hardest letter of her career. [254] He wanted to rejoin his wife, Ayesha, who had left him nine monsoons ago and now refused any envelope with his name on it. [278] Nilima already knew Ayesha's side; she had ghostwritten Ayesha's leaving letter, and had kept that secret like all the others. [298] A letter would not work, then, because a letter can be refused. [310] So she proposed something inevitably whimsical, which was, she admitted, the only kind of idea she ever had. [328] They would write it through a public art installation, out across the massive pond itself, where no one could refuse it and everyone would have to share it. [356]

For nine days the sign painter and the ghostwriter worked at the pond's edge. [370] They copied the thrush's nest twig spiral at enormous scale, bamboo instead of twigs, floating on rafts of sealed gourds, curling out from the temple's stair into the lilies. [399] And between the bamboo arms they strung thread. [407] This was the part Nilima could not have explained to anyone but a bird. [420] She went to every person whose secret she had carried, and asked each for one length of string and one true sentence, unsigned. [443] The fishmonger gave grey net. [448] The widow gave the cord of her husband's kurta. [457] The schoolteacher gave a ribbon the color of Nagpur dust. [467] Ratan painted the sentences on small tin plaques, in his beautiful shopfront lettering, and he never asked which sentence belonged to whom. [489] One plaque read: I am sorry I was proud in the doorway. [501] That one was his, and it hung among four hundred others, and that was the whole design. [518]

The city came the evening the next cloud burst and cleared, walking the spiral from its wide mouth inward, reading strangers' truths aloud to no one. [544] Nobody knew whose was whose, so everybody claimed several. [553] Ayesha came on the third evening, alone, and Nilima watched her from the gallery above. [568] She stopped at the doorway plaque for a long time, the way a person stops when a sentence is written in her own house's voice. [593] Then she went to the center, where the spiral ended in a single knot of all the threads together, tied by the ghostwriter's hands. [617] Ratan was waiting there, because that was the only line Nilima had written for him, and it was a line of standing still. [640] They did not embrace; they sat on the raft with their feet in the lilies and began, slowly, to speak in their own voices. [664]

Nilima went home and wrote nothing for anyone. [672] She had found her thread, and it was ordinary: everyone was carrying one sentence they could not say, and the sentences were nearly the same sentence. [698] The nest twigs she kept in a bowl on her windowsill, unwound, drying, waiting for a bird with better reasons than hers. [720] In the morning the lotus temple rose out of the pond as it always had, a white flower with four hundred small tin leaves, and the water beneath it kept everything. [751]
