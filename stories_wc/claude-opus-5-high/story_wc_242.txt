Between the hum of the streetlamps going on and the hum of them going off, Mira works the last manual switchboard in the city. [24] Her booth stands in a subway utility tunnel beneath Delancey, wedged between a sweating water main and a cable trunk that nobody has mapped since 1948. [50] The city forgot the booth; the calls did not. [59] Officially, the transit authority lists her as a night maintenance auditor; unofficially, everyone who has ever found the number calls her the underground phone booth operator. [85] Wrong numbers drift down here the way water finds a crack, and Mira answers each one. [101] She has built her whole life through wrong number friendships: the widower in Bay Ridge who dials his dead wife's office, the girl who calls a pizzeria that closed when her mother was alive, the man who asks for Ruth every Thursday and never explains. [146] They do not know her face, her floor, or her surname, and that is the point. [162] Mira calls it cultivated estrangement, and she tends it like a garden: pruning any branch that might reach her, watering only the ones that stay strangers. [188] It began with her brother's last phone call, which she let ring. [200] On the shelf above the switchboard sits a glass harmonica disc, the only piece she saved from the instrument he played in the transit museum's basement concerts. [227] Wet her finger, touch the rim, and the disc gives one long note that the tunnel takes and returns, and returns, and returns. [250] She is melodically haunted; the note lives under her sternum and hums when she is about to say something true. [270] Tonight the girl calls early, before the lamps have finished warming. [281] Her name is Ines, she is nineteen, and she has been dialing a dead pizzeria for eleven months. [299] "I'm calling to say I won't call anymore," Ines says, and the note under Mira's sternum wakes. [316] Mira knows the flatness in that voice; she has heard it in her own mouth, in a stairwell, the night she decided that no one else would ever get close enough to leave. [349] The rule is simple: never give a caller anything she cannot take back. [362] Mira breaks it. [365] She wets her finger and draws it around the glass harmonica disc, and the tunnel does what tunnels do: it loops the note back, thinner each pass, until the sound is less a note than a room remembering one. [404] "What is that?" Ines asks. [409] "My brother's," Mira says, and the word costs her the way she knew it would. [424] "He used to say a glass note never ends, it only gets quieter than the room, so he never played a last piece, only a first one, over and over." [454] She tells Ines the rest: the unanswered ring, the eleven years of tending strangers so that nothing could ever end again, because nothing was ever allowed to begin. [482] "That isn't safety," she says. [487] "It's a loop with the lights off." [494] Ines is quiet long enough for a train to pass overhead, shaking grit from the conduit. [510] "So what do I do," she says, "with an ending I already decided on?" [524] "Teach it how to begin," Mira says. [531] "Call the pizzeria number tomorrow at eight, and I'll answer, and we'll do that until it turns into something with a name." [553] It is the first appointment Mira has made in eleven years. [564] After the line clicks, she sits with the disc in her lap while the switchboard sleeps. [580] Above her, the streetlamps are between hums, that narrow hour when the city holds its breath and the filaments cool. [600] She thinks about the widower, the man who asks for Ruth, all of them kept perfectly at arm's length like specimens in a jar. [624] Tomorrow she will tell the widower her surname. [632] Tomorrow she will tell Ruth's caller that Ruth is not here, and that Mira is. [647] She touches the rim one last time before the morning shift. [658] The note rises, loops down the tunnel, and comes back changed, and she lets it go on beginning without her. [678]
