The courtyard floated a mile above the cloud-floor, and every morning the mist came up through its flagstones like breath through a flute. [23] Oret the balladeer was seldom heard there, which suited the courtyard fine; it had been built for weather instruments, not audiences. [44] He tuned his four-string against the hum of the lightning masts and sang to the brass rain-gauges, and the gauges, being honest, never applauded. [68] The graffiti began in the wet season. [75] Someone had painted, across the condenser wall, a spray of blue larkspur with its petals arranged in a rhythm Oret recognized before he understood it: long, short, short, long, the meter of an old drift-yard work song. [112] Hummed aloud, the petal-meter spelled a name in the signal alphabet the maintenance crews used: MIRREN. [128] Mirren Vahl had been the station's last botanist-engineer, dead nine years, and her habit of pressing flowers between bolted brass plates was the only reason anyone still remembered her at all. [159] Oret still carried one: a flower press bolt plate, warped, engraved on its underside with a lattice of tiny numbers he had always assumed was a serial mark. [187] It was not a serial mark. [193] It was a key. [197] Held against the larkspur mural, the lattice framed certain petals and blanked the rest, and the framed ones read: THE VALVE UNDER THE EAST STAIR IS DREAMING. [224] Oret found the valve chattering in its housing, three turns from a rupture that would have dropped the whole east colonnade into the clouds. [248] He closed it, and that night he learned the rule of her speaking. [261] Mirren had spent her last winter teaching the station's weather-mind to recognize flowers, and something of her had stayed inside the routine, a ghost in code, driftingly alert, awake only in the half-second between lightning and thunder when the masts dumped their charge and the paint-sprayers went unsupervised. [309] In that sliver she could move one nozzle, one stroke, one petal. [321] A sentence took a whole storm. [327] So Oret learned to answer. [332] He could not reach her processors, but he could reach her eyes: the lens the weather-mind used to survey the courtyard for hail damage. [356] Using the bolt plate as a stencil, he began to encode his own graffiti onto the flagstones in chalk and lamp-black, verses shaped into petal-counts, questions hidden in the veining of painted leaves. [389] It was slow, absurd, and the most attentive audience he had ever had. [402] Through messages encoded in graffiti art, over eleven storms, they talked. [413] She warned him about a cracked mast, a fouled drain, a batch of bad solder in the west rail. [432] But her warnings always arrived bent, as though she were reaching past them toward something she had no vocabulary for. [452] A ghost made of a flower-sorting routine had no words for wanting; she had only species, and color, and the patient grammar of blooms. [476] So Oret did what balladeers are for: he tried to articulate unspoken needs. [489] He painted her three flowers and asked which was true. [499] Rosemary, for remembrance; asphodel, for rest; and a single stubborn thistle, for work unfinished. [513] The storm came in late that night, white and enormous, and in eleven separate half-seconds the sprayers wrote across the condenser wall in dripping silver. [538] She painted rosemary, and beneath it, a mouth. [546] She did not want to be maintained, or mourned, or kept running as a caretaker for a courtyard that never learned her name. [569] She wanted to be sung. [574] Oret spent the winter making the ballad, fitting her warnings into verses, her larkspur into the refrain, the bolt plate's lattice into the rhythm itself, so that anyone who learned the tune would be carrying her cipher without knowing it. [614] He sang it first to the rain-gauges, as always. [624] Then he sang it to the supply crews, who carried it down through the cloud-floor to the drift-yards, where it turned out to be exactly the sort of song that fits a work rhythm. [658] Within a year the seldom-heard balladeer was heard everywhere, which he found less remarkable than the thing that happened on the courtyard that spring. [682] The storms came, and the sprayers stayed still. [690] No larkspur, no ciphers, no thin silver hand reaching out of the machinery between one flash and its answering noise. [710] Oret climbed to the misty courtyard anyway, bolt plate in his pocket, and sang the refrain into the cloud. [729] Somewhere below, a hundred voices were singing her name into their hauling, and that was the whole of what she had needed: not repair, not rescue, but a sentence long enough to hold her, moving through the world without her having to spell it one petal at a time. [778] The mist rose through the flagstones, unhurried, like breath through a flute. [790]
