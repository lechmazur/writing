The house came to her in the first yawn of evening, when the light in the sepia reading room turned to golden shadows and the dust hung like suspended commentary. [30] Ilse Varga was the last of nine generations, and she had come to end them. [45] The lawyers called her the sole heir; the village called her the dynasty ender, and she had stopped correcting them. [65] Nine generations of Vargas had tuned church bells and cast bronze chimes, and every one of them had died rich in a country that had grown poorer. [92] Ilse wanted to know why before she sold the foundry and let the name go quiet. [108] Her grandmother Margit had been eloquently silent about it for forty years. [120] Silence, in Margit's hands, had been a form of speech, a pause before the word foundry, a turned photograph, a locked drawer dusted daily. [144] In the drawer, when Ilse finally broke it open, lay a gramophone crank handle wrapped in a christening shawl. [163] The gramophone itself stood on the reading room table, handleless for as long as she could remember, a mute animal with a brass throat. [187] Beside it, one shellac disc, unlabeled, scored white with playing. [197] Ilse fitted the crank, wound it, and let the needle down. [208] What came out was not music but a single sustained tone, a low organ note held for eleven minutes. [227] She almost lifted the needle. [232] Then she noticed the room. [237] The tone filled the reading room and the reading room answered it, the way a bell-tuner's daughter knows rooms answer. [257] Through standing waves the sound divided the air into corridors of loudness and pockets of near-silence, invisible architecture she could walk. [278] She walked it. [281] At the third node, where the note thinned to almost nothing, she heard something else underneath it: a voice, recorded faintly, audible only where the louder tone cancelled itself out. [311] Margit's voice. [313] Her grandmother had hidden a confession inside a physics problem, and left the key in a drawer, and trusted that whoever came last would be Varga enough to solve a room. [344] Ilse knelt in the node with her ear to the golden air and listened. [358] In 1943 the foundry had taken a contract, Margit said. [368] Bronze was bronze, whether it rang in a steeple or a shell casing, and the bells of four villages had gone into the furnace with the family's blessing and the family's invoice. [400] That was the hidden truth Ilse had come for, and it arrived in her grandmother's voice, unhurried, without excuse. [419] Margit had not written it down because paper could be burned or found; she had built a bridge of intuition instead. [440] Only someone raised on the physics of sound, who felt a room's nodes in her teeth before she measured them, could cross it. [463] The confession was addressed, precisely, to the one descendant capable of hearing it, and Margit must have known which one that would be. [486] The tone ran out. [490] The needle hissed in the runout groove, and the invisible corridors collapsed back into ordinary evening. [506] Ilse sat among the golden shadows with the crank handle still warm in her fist and let herself muse, the way her grandmother had taught her to, slowly, without deciding anything yet. [538] She could sell the foundry quietly, as planned, and let nine generations end in a notarized signature. [555] That was still ending it, but it was the cowardly kind, an ending that kept the secret comfortable. [573] Or she could do what Margit had clearly hoped for and never dared ask aloud. [588] In the morning Ilse drove to the first of the four villages with the disc on the seat beside her. [608] She asked the priest for the use of his empty church, a room with excellent acoustics, and she brought the gramophone and the crank handle and a chair for anyone who wanted to sit. [642] Eleven minutes of one low note, and then she showed them where to stand. [656] The foundry's proceeds would recast four sets of bells; the Varga name would appear on none of them. [674] A dynasty ends best, she thought, the way it began: with bronze, and with someone listening hard enough to find the true note underneath. [698] Afterwards, standing in the cold nave, she found she had nothing to add, and understood at last how her grandmother had managed to be so eloquent. [724]
