Ilse Brandhorst had cataloged one hundred and eleven varieties of silence, and the hundred and twelfth was waiting inside a locked diary clasp. [23] Her workshop sat on the roof of the old brass foundry, a shed of glass and salvaged doors, shadowed except where the stars leaned in. [48] Below, the town of Vessel Bay was assembling itself for the centennial fireworks, folding chairs snapping open like applause. [67] She had one hour. [71]

The diary had belonged to her sister Meret, who had stopped speaking to Ilse four years before she died, and who had never explained why. [96] The clasp was a brass puzzle-lock from the foundry's last good decade, keyless, sprung by sound. [112] Ilse could have snapped it with a chisel in a second. [123] She would not. [126] She had spent her whole eclectic life refusing force: no army, no slaughterhouse meat, no swatted flies, no raised voice, not even the small violence of prying. [153] Her friends called her a crank; she called herself a pacifist with hobbies. [166]

The hobbies filled the shed: a theremin built from bicycle parts, jars of tuning forks, a wall of wax cylinders labeled in her looping hand. [191] The labels were her life's work and her one vanity, each one intuitively descriptive: Silence of a Phone Held to a Dead Ear; Silence of Snow Refusing to Fall; Silence Between the Question and the Kindness. [227] She believed, with the theatrical sincerity of someone who has been laughed at often and continued anyway, that silence was the true record of a town, and that anyone who learned to hear it would find the path to empathy laid out like a staircase. [272] Tonight she wanted only to recover one silence: her sister's. [282]

She clamped the diary in a felt vise and began tuning frequencies, sweeping the oscillator low and slow, watching the brass for the tremor that means a tumbler has recognized its name. [314] Ninety-two hertz: nothing. [317] One hundred forty: a hum in her molars. [325] Two hundred six: the first tumbler let go with a click so soft she felt it in her wrist bones rather than heard it. [349]

Down in the harbor, the crowd noise thinned. [357] The band stopped. [360] Someone with a microphone counted down, then even he went quiet, and Vessel Bay entered the final stillness before fireworks ignite - the held breath of eleven thousand people looking up. [390] Ilse had waited three years for that silence, because it was the only one quiet enough to let the last tumbler speak. [412] She turned the dial. [416] The clasp opened like a mouth. [422]

Inside, Meret's handwriting - small, hurried, honest. [428] Ilse had expected an accusation, and there was one, but it was not the one she had rehearsed. [446] "I stopped calling because Ilse listens to silence and not to me," Meret had written. [461] "She would rather catalog my quiet than ask what it costs. [472] I am not one of her cylinders." [479]

There were pages after that - hospital dates, a dog named Pastor, a man who left, a diagnosis written in the tiniest letters of all - and Ilse read them under starlight with her thumb pressed flat against the paper as if she could still reach the hand that made it. [528] The first rocket went up. [533] The shed filled with green, then red, and the glass rattled, and Ilse - who had never once struck anything - discovered that there is a violence in listening without asking, and that she had committed it for four years against the person she loved most. [577] She did not weep theatrically; she wept the way a person does when the audience is only the dead. [596]

When the last shell faded, she took down a blank cylinder and did not record the sky. [613] She labeled it anyway, in her looping hand: Silence of a Woman Who Was Asked Nothing. [629] Then she carried the diary down the foundry stairs, out among the folding chairs, and found Meret's daughter Juna smoking behind the bandstand. [652] Ilse had sent that girl birthday cards for nineteen years and never one question. [666]

"I'm your aunt," Ilse said, with the full trembling grandeur she brought to everything, "and I have been a coward with excellent equipment. [689] Tell me what your mother was like on a Tuesday." [699] Juna looked at the open clasp, the brass still warm from resonance, and started talking. [714] Ilse listened, and for the first time in her life she interrupted - to ask. [728]
