The distress signal had pulsed for sixty years before anyone came. [11] Ines followed it down through green water into the drowned valley, where a temple stood with its doors open like a held breath. [34] Nobody on the surface remembered its name. [41] Her tether spooled behind her, a thin silver line back to the boat and to ninety minutes of air. [60] The signal came from the sanctum at the temple's heart, a slow three-note knock in her headset, patient as a clock. [81]

Inside, the columns were carved with noses. [88] Hundreds of them, human and animal, tilted upward, all sniffing at something gone. [101] Ines had read the one surviving fragment about this place: the perfumers here had tried to harness the ephemeral. [120] They bottled the smell of a mother's hair, of a harvest morning, of a particular hour of a particular year. [140] They believed a scent, properly caught, touches memories the way a hand touches a face. [155]

The sanctum was a domed room full of silence. [164] Not ordinary quiet, but a gathered hush, something the builders had collected here and stored like grain. [181] Her own bubbles went soundless the moment she crossed the threshold. [192]

At the center, seated against the altar, was the sender. [202] She had been a woman in a heavy diving suit of an older century, brass and canvas, her helmet still sealed. [223] A mechanical tapper strapped to her wrist still struck the altar stone, three notes, patient, sixty years past her death. [243] Her name was stitched on the collar: M. Varda. [252]

Ines knelt, and the hush pressed around her like wool. [262] In Varda's gloved hands lay a crystal perfume vial, uncorked, empty, no bigger than a thumb. [278] Ines took it, and her whole skull filled with the smell of dry grass and woodsmoke. [294] It did not come through her nose; there was no way it could. [307] It came the way a name comes when you finally remember it. [319] She saw a hillside she had never stood on, and a girl running, and someone calling Varda in from the fields. [340] The vial had held one afternoon, and Varda had come down here to open it and had never left. [359]

The knocking, Ines understood, was not a call for rescue. [369] It was a woman refusing to let one afternoon go quiet. [380]

Her gauge read twenty-two minutes. [385] She turned to go and found her tether snarled around a carved column, three loops and a knot of coral. [405] She worked to unsnarl it, and the more she pulled, the tighter the coral bit. [420] Fifteen minutes. [422] Panic arrived, bright and useful and wrong. [429] The only way out of the loops was to unclip the tether entirely, surrender the silver line, and swim the flooded corridors on memory alone. [454] Every instinct she had said hold the line; the line was the world. [467] Varda had held on to one afternoon, and the afternoon had held her here forever. [482] So Ines did it by overriding instinct: she unclipped, and the silver line drifted away like a thread of smoke. [502]

The hush did not follow her out. [509] It stayed where it had been gathered, keeping its long, lonesome splendor, the noses on the columns still lifted toward an afternoon that had ended twice. [535] As time ran short she swam the corridors by counting turns, four left, two right, one long rising throat of stone. [556] She surfaced with nothing in her tanks and the vial still in her fist. [570]

On the boat they asked what she had found, and she could not answer for a long while. [588] Then she said: a woman who knocked for sixty years so that somebody would stand on a hillside that no longer exists. [610] She reported the wreck, filed the coordinates, and requested that the site be left alone. [625] The vial she kept on a shelf where the morning light hit it, empty, ordinary, and holding everything. [643] Some mornings, passing it, she smelled dry grass, and stopped, and let the moment go before she could try to keep it. [665]
