The nebula garden bloomed only when someone grieved in it, and Ilsevet had been grieving for three hundred years. [19] She was otherworldly even by the standards of this place, where nobody was native. [33] Her people had drifted in from a galaxy that had folded itself shut behind them, and they had brought with them seeds, sorrow, and a habit of keeping things. [62] Ilsevet kept everything, and was called a sentimentalist for it, which she took as a title rather than a diagnosis. [82] The chief of her keepings was a calculating machine gear, brass, the size of a plum, with eleven teeth worn smooth by her great-grandmother's thumb. [107] The gear had come out of an engine that computed the transit of light across dust, and its ratio, her mother said, approximates infinity: eleven against a hundred and thirteen, a fraction that never closes. [142] Ilsevet liked that. [145] It meant the family's work could not be finished, only inherited. [156]

The garden itself was a hollow in the nebula's throat, a bubble of warm gas where the bloom-trees stood rooted in nothing, their branches drinking ions. [182] It was dark there, always, and that was the trouble. [192] Her great-grandmother had solved the dark with fire: a hydrogen wick, tall as a tower, that burned for ninety years and took the eastern orchard with it. [219] Her grandmother had solved it with mirrors that cracked, her mother with a captured sun-fragment that ate her. [237] Generational patterns, Ilsevet thought, are simply grief repeating itself with better tools. [249] She intended to break the pattern, which is the most traditional intention there is. [263] Her ambition was small and absolute: to share light without using fire. [275]

So she worked by sonar pinging. [281] The garden's gas was thick enough to carry sound, and Ilsevet had built a throat of copper that sang a low note into the dark and listened for what came back. [312] The bloom-trees answered. [315] Certain notes made their petals fluoresce, cold and blue, for as long as the echo lasted. [331] She spent decades tuning, and she measured her decades across the quiet sigh of falling petals, because there were no clocks and no days, only the soft descending hush of the orchard shedding itself. [365] The gear she used as her tuning weight, hung on a wire, its eleven teeth clicking the interval she could never quite resolve. [388]

Then the transit came, as her mother's engine had predicted it would. [400] A migration ship, hull scabbed with ice, crossing the nebula on a course that would take eleven years and had no light aboard it at all. [426] They had burned it, of course. [431] Every generation burns its light going somewhere. [438] Ilsevet hailed them with sound, and they answered with sound, and in that slow exchange she understood that they were her own kind, a branch of the family that had gone the other way when the galaxy shut. [476] They asked her for fire. [481] She gave them the orchard instead. [487]

For eleven years, as the ship transited, Ilsevet sang her copper note and the bloom-trees answered in cold blue, a corridor of petals glowing as the sound rolled through them and dimming as it passed, so that the ship traveled inside a moving room of light and never once had to strike a spark. [541] It was beautiful and it cost the trees everything. [550] Fluorescing spent them; the petals fell faster, and the sigh of the orchard grew louder, then thinner, then nearly silent. [570] Ilsevet sat in the last of it with the gear in her palm, serene, and troubled by her own serenity. [590] She had not used fire. [595] She had only found a slower way to burn, which was, she admitted, the family's exact signature. [612]

When the ship cleared the nebula it sent back one last ping, and in it, faintly, a child's voice asking how the trees were made. [637] Ilsevet answered with the seeds, launched slow on the sound of her own note, eleven of them, one for each tooth. [658] They would take a hundred and thirteen years to arrive. [668] The ratio would not close; it never does; that is what makes it inheritance rather than ending. [685] She lay down in the dark orchard, among the last petals, and listened to the echo of herself come back changed. [706] Somewhere ahead of her, someone would grieve, and something would bloom, and the pattern would hold its breath and repeat. [726]
