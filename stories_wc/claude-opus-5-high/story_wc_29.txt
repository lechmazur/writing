The university radio station lived in a basement that smelled of solder and wet concrete. [15] Mira Okonjo had worked the three-a.m. shift there for two years, ever since the astronomy department stopped calling her a graduate student and started calling her their celestial signal interpreter. [45] The title was a joke that had hardened into a job. [56] On the console she spread her astronomical charts, taped edge to edge until they curled over the mixing board like a paper tide. [79] The signal came in at 3:14, as it had for eleven nights: a slow pulse riding the carrier wave, too patterned for a pulsar, too patient for a satellite. [108] Mira had plotted its source three times and gotten three different answers, each one landing in the gap between real stars. [129] That was the first clue that she was not dealing with astronomy. [141] The second clue was the town. [147] By the fourth night, the message had been decoded into plain English by an undergraduate with too much time: PICK ONE OF YOU TO SPEAK FOR ALL. [174] By the sixth, the faculty senate had split into factions, and the quad had sprouted competing tents. [191] Dean Ruhl wanted the transmitter licensed to his new Institute for Contact Studies; Professor Vance wanted it locked, then leaked to a journalist who owed him. [217] Both of them had begun sending Mira emails that were really power plays dressed as invitations. [233] She answered neither, because she had noticed the shape of the thing. [245] The pulse did not carry information so much as bait. [255] Every repetition asked the same impossible question - who speaks for you - and every night, as the community divided further, the signal grew stronger, cleaner, more delighted. [281] It fed on the argument it started. [288] Mira wrote the word in her logbook and underlined it twice: tricksters. [300] Not gods, not invaders - hustlers working a long con across light-years, harvesting the heat of a species tearing at itself. [320] You could not out-argue that, and you certainly could not appoint a representative to negotiate with it. [337] You could only interfere. [341] The old transmitter in the corner, decommissioned in 1974, still had range in the very low bands - the kind of frequencies whales used, the kind that traveled through rock and seawater and, Mira suspected, through whatever medium the pulse was really using. [383] Her charts showed the pulse arriving with a faint carrier beneath it, a hum at the bottom of hearing, the true spine of the message. [408] Break the spine and the con collapsed. [415] So she did the only thing a night-shift DJ could do. [426] She called every faction to the station at once - Ruhl, Vance, the tent kids, the campus minister, the man who phoned in weekly to complain about jazz - and told them she needed voices, all of them, talking at the same time. [467] They arrived expecting a debate, a stage, a chance to win. [478] Instead she handed out microphones and asked them to hum. [488] Forty people humming in a concrete basement makes a sound with grit in it, earthy and strange, and when Mira dropped the mix two octaves and pushed it out through subsonic frequencies on the 1974 rig, the room went quiet in a way that felt like altitude. [535] The needles on her charts' margins - she had penciled predicted amplitudes there - stayed flat, then dipped. [551] At 3:14 the pulse arrived, found its spine already occupied by forty stubborn human voices refusing to elect a spokesman, and stuttered. [573] It tried three more times, each attempt thinner than the last, like a cardsharp discovering the deck has been swapped. [593] Then nothing - not silence, exactly, but the ordinary hiss of a universe that had stopped paying attention. [610] Ruhl asked whether this counted as first contact. [618] Vance asked who would sign the paper. [625] Mira rolled up her astronomical charts and said that the point of outwitting cosmic tricksters was that nobody got to be the one who spoke. [650] She kept the hum recorded on a loop, scheduled nightly at 3:14, and told the station manager it was experimental programming. [671] Walking home under a sky she now distrusted and loved in equal measure, she felt cautiously hopeful: the con had been broken, not won, and cons always come back with a new angle. [704] But the quad tents were coming down, and somewhere below hearing, a basement full of people who could not agree on anything was still humming together, holding the line. [733]
