The last woman who could speak Ossuine had copper in her veins, thin bright seams that surfaced at her wrists like rivers seen from a great height. [27] Her name was Irro, and she was the last storyteller of the bone canyon, come back to finish what her grandmother had abandoned. [50] Ossuine was not a spoken language first; it was a felt one. [62] Her people had written it in texture, cutting grooves and burrs and glassy patches into the towers of bone that stood in the canyon of endless echoes, and they had read it with their palms. [97] Rain had been eating those towers for four hundred years. [107] Irro could still hear the language, but only barely, and only in her own mouth; there was no one left to answer her. [130] So she intended to translate texture into sound, and to give the sound to the canyon, which forgot nothing. [149]

The instrument required was a flawed diamond, and the flawed diamond was rumored to be cursed. [165] Three readers had owned it before her grandmother, and all three had died unable to speak. [181] Irro believed the curse was simpler than the stories: the stone read too well. [195] Dragged across bone, its internal fracture split every groove into a chord, and a chord carried more meaning than a throat could hold. [218] Her grandmother had called it a mercy that the diamond was flawed; a perfect one would have been silent. [237]

At the canyon's northern end lay the tunnel of echoes, a throat of polished stone a quarter mile long, where a single syllable could live for eleven minutes. [265] Irro's plan was to harness that tunnel: to feed it the diamond's chords in an order it would hold, layering them until the whole grammar of Ossuine stood inside the rock like a building. [299] The order was the problem. [303] Sound does not stack politely; two chords entering the tunnel a half-second apart will cancel each other into mud. [322] Her grandmother had spent nine years trying to calculate the intervals and had gone home with nothing but ringing ears. [342]

Irro found her answer on the third morning, when the bone-swifts came up the canyon in their thousands. [360] They flew the way they always had, and the way Ossuine was built: in turning sheets, each bird holding its distance from seven neighbors, the whole flock folding and opening without a leader. [393] Her ancestors had not invented their grammar; they had copied it from the birds, who had been solving the problem of many voices at once since before there were mouths. [423] She spent eleven days watching them, sketching the intervals of their turning, and she read her timings through the patterns in the flock. [446] Then she began. [449]

For a season she worked the towers, palm first, then diamond, quietly articulate in a language nobody would ever hear her speak. [471] She named each texture aloud before she scraped it, the way you say goodbye to a person by using their name. [492] The copper in her arms warmed and ached; the stone sang; her voice thinned. [506] Her family had always carried the metal, and the old stories said it was what let them hold so much sound without shattering. [529] By midwinter she could not say her own name. [538] That was the curse, and she had priced it in advance. [549]

On the last day she stood at the tunnel's mouth with the diamond and the final tower, a spire of vertebrae holding the hundred words for weather. [576] The swifts came up the canyon on schedule. [583] She matched her hand to their turning and drew the stone down the bone, and the tunnel took the chord and held it, and did not muddy it, and folded it into everything already standing inside. [619]

Ossuine died that afternoon, in the ordinary sense; there was no longer anyone who could speak it. [636] But in the tunnel it went on, turning and reopening, a flock of syllables with no leader and nowhere to land. [657] Irro walked home with the diamond in her pocket, silent, satisfied, the copper cooling under her skin. [674] Travelers who walk it say the noise has a texture, like a thumb run over carved bone. [691] They call the place cursed, which is how the living describe a story they can feel but cannot read. [710]
