Ines arrived at the lighthouse crestfallen, carrying brushes she no longer trusted. [12] For three years her portraits had been coming apart. [21] A client's cheek would slide half an inch left of her jaw; an eye would repeat itself in the corner of the canvas like a stutter. [47] She had blamed her hands until the mirrors began doing it too. [58] The keeper's widow had written to her about a forgotten library inside the lighthouse, three hundred books shelved in a spiral around the stairwell, and a commission: redesign the lamp-room mural before the equinox. [92] Ines took the work because she had nothing else, and because the letter ended with a line she could not stop chewing: what is a face, anyway, but light agreeing to stay still? [125] Ines answered it. [128] She always answered rhetorical questions; she considered them cowardice dressed as wisdom. [140] A face, she wrote back, is a promise that a person will still be assembled tomorrow. [156] No reply came. [159]

The library smelled of salt-swollen paper and cold lamp oil. [169] Books curved up the tower wall, and the only light came from the lens overhead, which shed a slow luminescence down the spiral, an ache of brightness that never quite reached the floor. [202] On the reading table lay a broken tarot card box, its hinge snapped, its lid inlaid with a compass rose worn down to brass gums. [227] Inside, instead of cards, were threads: hundreds of them, colored, knotted, spilling out in a mat the size of a dinner plate. [249] Each thread ended in a tiny paper tag, and each tag bore a name. [262] Ines found her own. [266]

At dusk the walls closed in. [272] This was not a figure of speech. [279] The shelves leaned, the spiral tightened by a handspan, and the stairwell narrowed until Ines had to turn her shoulders sideways to breathe. [302] A voice came from the dark under the table, dry as a moth. [315] "It does this when the threads knot," said the keeper's widow, who had been waiting in the dark under the table for someone with patient hands. [341] Her name was Maren, and she explained the lighthouse plainly, the way one explains weather. [356] The lens did not warn ships; it held the coast together. [366] Every thread was a life braided into the beam, and when the box broke the threads had tangled, and tangled threads made reality stutter: faces sliding, mirrors repeating, walls forgetting where they had agreed to stand. [402]

"Can anyone really repair the world with their fingers?" Maren asked, meaning no. [415] Ines said yes, and knelt. [420] She worked by untangling threads, one knot at a time, the only method the box allowed. [436] It was portrait work, she realized: attention held long enough to let a stranger become specific. [451] A green thread belonged to a boy in the harbor town whose laugh had been arriving before his mouth moved. [471] She freed it, and somewhere a boy laughed on time. [481] But the deepest snarl had sunk down the stairwell, past the last shelf, into the black cistern at the tower's root where the beam's threads were spun. [508] To reach it she had to descend into necessary darkness, leaving the lens's light behind, carrying the broken box like a bowl of hair. [532]

"Isn't it easier to stay where you can see?" Maren said. [543] "Easier," Ines answered, "and useless," and took the stairs. [552] In the cistern she could not see her own hands, which was, she thought, the honest condition of all portraiture. [572] She found the snarl by feel, a fist of wet knots, and among them one thread with no tag at all. [592] She worked until her nails split. [598] When the final knot gave, the tower exhaled. [606] The walls slid back to their proper distance with a sound like a held breath released. [622] Light came down the whole spiral and touched the floor for the first time. [635]

Climbing back, Ines saw the untagged thread in her fist had a tag after all, blank, waiting. [652] She wrote Maren's name on it and tied it into the beam, and the widow stepped out from under the table, visible, edges holding. [676] "What am I, now?" Maren asked, rhetorically. [683] "Sitting for me," said Ines. [688] She mended the hinge with wire, redesigned the lamp-room mural as a spiral of threads rather than saints, and painted Maren's portrait in the mornings, when the light reached the floor. [719] The face stayed where she put it. [726] Some nights the walls still tested their old habit, leaning an inch inward, and Ines would take up the box and sit with the knots until dawn, no longer crestfallen, merely employed. [758] Someone has to hold the coast together. [765]
