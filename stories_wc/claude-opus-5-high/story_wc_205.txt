Emil Vartok had been a revolutionary for nine days, and a custodian of ruins for forty years. [17] His revolution had ended not in blood but in obsolescence, the state it opposed dissolving on its own like sugar in rain. [39] So he came to Thessaly Minor, the baroque asteroid, where nothing had wanted anything for eleven centuries. [56] It was a small world entirely carved: scrolled cornices, stone drapery frozen mid-billow, cherubs whose faces the vacuum had worn to thumbprints. [78] The Vashteni had built it, then vanished, leaving behind spiral staircases that led to sealed doors and a silence with excellent acoustics. [100] Emil arrived the year after time became visible. [108] Nobody had predicted it; the physicists only shrugged and said the universe had grown old enough to show its grain. [128] Now duration hung in the air like varnish, amber and striated, and if you stood still you could watch the light of your own arrival still trembling in the doorway behind you. [160] Archaeology became a matter of reading, and every reading became a quarrel. [172] The quarrel that consumed Emil's forty years concerned a single crystal vase shard, four inches long, found in the asteroid's western gallery. [194] Because it was crystal, it held time more clearly than stone did; because it was broken, it held an argument. [214] Doctor Ilse Ganz of the Institute read in its strata a slave revolt: the vase flung, the hand that flung it calloused, the Vashteni a civilization of masters ended by the justice of its own kitchens. [250] Emil read a funeral. [254] He saw a mourner breaking a vessel on purpose, gently, the way you break bread. [269] He was poetically inclined, which the Institute considered a diagnosis rather than a credential. [283] Ganz wrote that he wished to reimagine an entire civilization out of tenderness, and she was right. [300] He had spent nine days trying to make his own species gentler and had failed; he had the rest of his life to try it on a species that could not resist him. [333] The trouble was that the shard would not settle. [342] Thessaly Minor passed each year through the Kolmar debris stream, and the impacts set the whole carved world ringing, and in those storms the visible time shook loose. [370] The Vashteni had left instructions for this, painted in six languages above every gilded doorframe: cover the mirrors during storms. [390] Emil had obeyed for four decades without understanding, hanging felt over the great pier glasses while the floor hummed. [409] In his forty-first year, with Ganz's final paper already typeset and his own dismissal already signed, he decided to devise a proper experiment instead of another poem. [436] He set the shard on a velvet stand between two uncovered mirrors and waited for the Kolmar stream. [454] The storm came at the ninth hour and the asteroid began to sing. [467] Loosened time slid off the walls, pooled, and the mirrors caught it and threw it back, and the reflections multiplied the pooling until the gallery filled with a thousand years of standing water. [500] Emil saw, at last, the whole of it, clarified and unarguable. [511] Ganz was right about the revolt. [517] The hand was calloused; the master died on the parquet with the vase in pieces around his head. [535] And Emil was right about the funeral, because forty years later - their years, not his - a woman knelt on that same parquet and broke a second vase, gently, for the man who had thrown the first, who had died old and unforgiven and much loved. [580] One shard from each, mingled, indistinguishable, catalogued as one. [589] The Vashteni had not vanished so much as emptied, generation by generation, out of a world too beautiful to argue in. [610] Emil understood the warning above the doorframes then. [618] Mirrors do not lie during storms; they make the truth complete, and a complete truth leaves nothing for the living to devise. [640] He wrote it all down in his cramped hand, every fact, and sent the account to Ganz, who deserved it. [660] Then he hung the felt back over the pier glasses, one by one, forty pounds of it, the last honest labor of a defunct revolutionary. [685] The shard he kept in his coat pocket, where it warmed to the temperature of a living thing. [703] There was no poem in the whole business, he thought, and was surprised to find that the thought had the shape of one, burnished, sorrowful, and nine days long. [732]
