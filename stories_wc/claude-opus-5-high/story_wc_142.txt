Nine days before privatization, Wren Salt stood on the Gangway of Welcomes with her hands full of strangers. [18] Her title was culture shock absorber, and the Consortium paid her to soften the blow of arrival. [35] Newcomers stepped off the ferry into a perpetual carnival afloat on a raft city, and something in them always broke a little: the noise, the stilt-dancers, the eleven bridges that moved while you crossed them. [70] Wren caught the pieces. [74] She had been doing it nine years, and she was good at it because she was an orderly intuitive: she felt patterns the way other people felt drafts, then filed them in tidy rows. [108] That was the trouble with dual allegiance. [114] The Consortium's badge sat on her collar; in her pocket sat a chip of the foundation stone, gray and salt-pitted, broken from the harbor wall the raft city had first been moored to. [147] Every absorber before her had carried it, and every one had passed it on when they chose a side. [166]

In nine days the water would be sold. [174] Surveyors had already drawn the grid: parcels numbered by coordinate, heritage rafts exempt, everything else purchasable by whoever wanted a carnival for a summer. [198] Wren had read the schedule twice and understood the flaw immediately, the way she understood which newcomer would cry first. [218] The grid fixed positions. [222] The city floated. [225] Nothing on the raft city was nailed to the sea; every shanty, oyster bar, and mirror-maze rode a pontoon lashed to its neighbors with rope that could be cut and retied in a night. [259] If the old families' homes drifted into the heritage coordinates before the surveyors returned, the surveyors would find them protected, and the Consortium would buy nine acres of empty carnival. [289]

To reorganize a floating city, though, you must tell four thousand people where to move without writing anything a Consortium clerk could read. [312] So Wren went to the painters. [318] The hulls of the raft city had always been graffiti, layer on layer, and the clerks called it decoration. [337] The raft-born called it the register. [343] A yellow gull meant a birth; a gull with a red eye meant a debt forgiven; three waves meant a mooring change at slack tide. [368] Wren spent six nights painting instructions into the carnival's skin: a ferris wheel with seven spokes, a fish swimming north, a lantern with a cracked pane. [394] Each mural told one street where to go and when, and each was signed the old way, by pressing the foundation chip into wet paint so it left a pitted gray thumbprint no forger had the stone to copy. [433]

She did not stop doing her day job. [440] She still met the ferries, still taught newcomers which bridges bit and which barkers lied, still walked a nervous accountant from Hull to a room above the sword-swallower and stayed until he laughed. [473] That was the part the raft-born could not forgive, and the part she refused to give up. [490] Gentrification was not a wave you could outrun on a raft; it was weather. [504] You navigated it, and you did that by keeping the newcomers close enough to become neighbors before they became landlords. [524]

On the eighth night the accountant found her painting a cracked lantern on the flank of the mirror-maze. [542] "That's a mooring order," he said. [548] "I've been reading your register for a week." [556] Wren's hands stayed steady, because panic was disorder and she had no shelf for it. [571] "Then you know what happens if you file it," she said. [582] He looked at the gray thumbprint drying under the lantern, and at the carnival grinding its music out over black water, and he said he had come here because nowhere else had ever let him in on the first night. [622] He held out his hand for a brush. [630]

They cut and retied until dawn, four thousand people hauling rope in the dark, the mirror-maze swapping places with the chandler's row, the oyster bars sliding east into heritage water. [660] The carnival never stopped; the wheel turned all night above the work, which is how it looked like nothing at all from the mainland. [684] The surveyors came at ten and stood a long time with their charts. [697] Wren met them with the badge on her collar and coffee in her hands, absorbing their shock, explaining gently that the city had always drifted, that the ropes were old, that nobody could be blamed for the sea. [735]

It would buy a year, maybe two. [742] They would redraw the grid, and she would have to paint again, and one day the paint would not be enough. [763] She pressed the foundation chip into the accountant's palm anyway, because an absorber holds it only until she knows who comes next. [785] Behind them, the wheel came around again. [792]
