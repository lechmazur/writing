The sea gave her ninety minutes, and not one breath more. [11] Between tidal changes the throat of Coldmouth Cave drained to a wet gravel floor, and Mireille could crawl the two hundred meters to the door that no one remembered building. [41] She hated crawling. [44] Twenty years of caving had not cured the thing in her chest that clenched when stone came close; it had only taught her to breathe around it, four counts in, six out, while the rock pressed its patient weight against her shoulder blades. [87] The cave had its own rhythm, a crashing hush: the boom of a swell far behind her, then silence so complete she could hear the salt drying on her sleeves. [117]

The door was steel, stamped STATION ELEVEN, CABLE INTEGRITY, and it opened because she had oiled its hinges on her last visit. [139] This was her return, her fourth. [145] Inside, the forgotten research facility held its breath under low ceilings: a corridor of dead fluorescent tubes, a galley with cups still ringed brown, and the listening room where forty years ago six technicians had tracked invisible things. [183] Not submarines. [185] Strain. [186] The undersea cables came ashore beneath this hill, six armored arteries carrying every voice of the northern trade, and Station Eleven had existed to feel them flex. [213] A cable cannot be seen once it leaves the beach; it can only be inferred, listened for, mourned. [231] The technicians had learned to read breaks through phase transitions, the moment brine turned to slush in the deep chill, the moment stressed polymer went glassy, the moment ice let go and a whole ocean of signal dropped into silence. [271] Then the funding moved to satellites, and the six were never reassigned, because officially they had never been posted. [290]

Mireille knew this because of the codex. [297] It sat on the desk where she had left it, a ledger bound in oilcloth, and its keeper had been a bored, gifted man named Absalon Reyes who had illuminated the margins like a medieval monk. [333] Vines of kelp, fish with clerical faces, and beside each entry a single fleck of gold leaf, pressed from a sheet he must have carried in his kit for exactly this. [364] One gold fleck per fault located. [370] Two hundred and eleven flecks, and under the last, in a hand gone unsteady: We are the only ones who know we are here. [394]

She had come back to make that untrue. [402] Her plan was unglamorous: photograph every page, carry the codex out in a dry bag, and hand it to the maritime archive in Tromso with the six names spelled correctly. [432] Three visits had gone to drying the pages in a bin of rice she had hauled in kilo by kilo, because wet gold leaf lifts and flies away like everything else nobody witnessed. [465]

At minute sixty the facility groaned. [471] Somewhere aft, a bulkhead she had never opened let go of forty years of held water, and the corridor filled ankle-deep in eleven seconds. [495] Mireille stood in the listening room with the codex against her sternum and felt the walls arrive, close, closer, the old clench climbing her throat. [520] Four counts in. [524] Six out. [526] She thought about Reyes pressing gold into a margin nobody would read, and decided that panic was simply another phase she could pass through. [550]

Then she crawled. [553] The throat of Coldmouth was filling from the far end, water sliding up to meet her chin, and she went the last forty meters on her back with the dry bag on her chest, reading the ceiling by headlamp, counting the booms. [595] Boom, hush. [597] Boom, hush. [599] She surfaced in the grey light of the outer cove while the cove was still deciding to be sea. [618]

In Tromso, a woman read the names aloud into a microphone, and the archive gave the codex a case with a small angled light. [642] Visitors bend close to the margin of the final page, where one fleck of gold catches the beam and throws it back, a tiny undrowned brightness insisting on itself. [671] Mireille visits sometimes, on her way to other holes in the ground. [683] She still hates crawling. [687] But she has learned what the six knew: that some things are real only because someone stayed to feel them, and that standing by the unremembered is a kind of listening you do with your whole body, between one tide and the next.
