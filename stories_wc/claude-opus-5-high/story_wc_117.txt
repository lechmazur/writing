The aspens on Sage Ridge were one tree wearing ten thousand masks, all of them linked underground, and Ilse had never stopped finding that ordinary and astonishing at once. [29] She climbed slowly, matching her steps to the girl behind her. [40] Wren was nine, and she had not spoken aloud since February, when her mother died in the room with the blue curtains. [62] Ilse was a music therapist, mellow by temperament and by training, and she had learned that pushing a child toward sound was like pushing a bird toward flight. [90] You waited, and you made the waiting hospitable. [98] In her coat pocket she carried a small tin, and inside the tin, wrapped in tissue, was a curl of pale hair - Wren's own, cut at eleven months, saved by a mother who had labeled it in pencil: our first miracle. [139] The mother had given it to Ilse in the hospice hallway with instructions that were half plea: not at the funeral, not while it is all still knives - later, when she can be amazed again. [174]

Today the moon would cover the sun for two minutes and eleven seconds. [187] Ilse had planned nothing elaborate, because elaborate plans break children. [197] She had brought a shruti box, the kind that breathes a single soft chord for as long as you keep pumping its bellows, and a second one for Wren. [226]

They reached the flat stone at the top where the ridge's sage grew low and silver and smelled like dust after rain. [248] Ilse liked to tell her young clients that they were sitting beneath the hush of dreaming roots, the aspen network sleeping and talking under their shoes, and that everything above ground borrowed its courage from down there. [285] Wren had rolled her eyes at that once, which Ilse counted as a triumph. [299]

They put on their cardboard glasses and watched the sun become a thick crescent, then a thin one. [317] Ilse started the drone, low, and did what she always did: she listened for Wren's breathing and bent the chord toward it, softly adaptive, the way water takes the shape of whatever holds it. [351]

The light went strange - not dim, exactly, but thinned, as though someone were rationing it. [366] The temperature dropped. [369] Crescents of light freckled the stone, shivering through the aspen leaves. [380] Birds on the ridge quit arguing all at once, and the sage went the color of old nickel. [398] Then the last sliver closed, and Ilse heard herself make a sound she had not authorized - a small, cautious whoop, the exuberance of a woman who knew better than to frighten a quiet child. [432]

Wren did not flinch. [436] She was staring up with her glasses pushed onto her forehead at the black coin and its ring of white fire. [457]

Ilse opened the tin. [461] She did not explain; she only held it out on her palm, the curl catching what light there was, the pencil label facing up. [485] Wren touched it with one finger. [491] Then she took the second shruti box into her lap and pumped the bellows, and a second chord came up out of the dark to link with Ilse's, a whole tone off, wobbling, then settling. [526]

And under the two chords, thin as a stem, Wren hummed. [537] It was not a word. [542] It was not a song anyone would name. [550] It was a nine-year-old's voice coming back the way sap comes back, and Ilse kept her own face calm and her hands steady, because this was not a thing to applaud. [581] It was a thing to accompany. [587]

The ring broke; a blade of sun came back over the ridge, and the birds resumed their arguments as if nothing had happened. [610] Wren put her glasses back on and said, hoarsely, "It does that every year somewhere." [625]

"Somewhere," Ilse agreed. [628]

"We could go see the next one." [635]

Ilse breathed out slowly, aware that she was being handed a future and that futures are easy to crush. [654] "Spain," she said, "in two summers; you would be eleven." [664] Wren nodded, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough. [676]

Walking down, Ilse thought about what she was really for - not repairing children, which was beyond her, but keeping a small door propped open so that wonder could get in again and keep coming in for sixty more years. [715] The curl went back into the tin, and the tin into Wren's fist. [728] Beneath them the roots went on with their slow, linked dreaming, unimpressed, doing the ordinary magic they did on every other afternoon of the world. [753]
