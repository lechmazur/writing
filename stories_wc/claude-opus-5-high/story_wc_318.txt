They called her the conductor, though she had never held a baton that wasn't a pull cord handle. [18] Marguerite Vance came to the Ardell house after the final grain slipped through the hourglass on their mantel, the one their mother had turned over the hour she died. [47] A year of sand, and not one laugh in the rooms above. [59] The father, Colm, met her at the culvert mouth behind the garden, holding a lantern he did not need. [78] "You understand I can't pay much," he said. [86] "I'm not paid in money," Marguerite said, mournfully eager, the way a doctor is eager for the one disease she has trained her whole life to cure. [113] Beneath the Ardell property ran an echo chamber tunnel, a brick drainage throat from the old mill days, forty feet long and shaped like a cupped palm. [140] Sound went in thin and came out doubled, tripled, wearing the voices of everyone who had ever spoken there. [159] Marguerite set her players along the curve: nine leaf blowers, each tuned by throttle and torn intake, each with a bucket at its feet. [183] In the buckets she would macerate what the family gave her. [194] Not flowers. [196] Colm brought down a bruised paper bag of his wife's quince peel, dried a year in the pantry, and Marguerite crushed and soaked it until the water went gold and sour-sweet. [227] The blowers would lift that smell and carry it up through the floor grates, and the house would remember a kitchen. [248] The two children came down at dusk, Wren and Tobias, thin as coat hangers, careful as thieves. [265] "What do we do?" Wren asked. [271] "Nothing you can say out loud," Marguerite told her. [280] "I tend to the unspoken." [285] "Think the thing you have never told your father, hold it behind your teeth, and let the machines do the rest." [306] She started them one by one, and the tunnel filled. [316] Her method was old and ridiculous and it worked: she conducted via half-sung lullabies carried on an off-key wind, humming into the intake of each blower so the motor chewed her melody and spat it out enormous. [353] The echo chamber took the nine voices and made ninety. [363] It was not music anyone would applaud. [370] It was a true haunting, and it sounded like a woman singing to a baby in a house two streets over, at three in the morning, in a language you almost knew. [402] Tobias wept without knowing why. [407] Wren held a thought so hard her jaw shook: that she had been the one to let the dog out, the day of the accident, and that her mother had gone into the road after it. [443] Marguerite did not hear it. [448] She only felt the air thicken, the way a room thickens when someone is about to confess and then does not. [469] That thickness was the thing she had come to move. [479] The ninth blower had never started for her. [487] It was Colm's, and its pull cord handle was worn to a shine by his wife's hands; she had done the leaves every autumn while he did the gutters. [516] Marguerite put the handle in Colm's palm and closed his fingers over it. [529] "Your turn," she said, "and don't say a word." [538] He pulled. [540] The engine caught on the third try, coughing, and the sound it made going into the tunnel was not a sound Marguerite had ever conducted before. [566] It came back off the bricks as laughter. [574] Her laughter, the one they had all been rationing in their heads for a year, afraid that letting it out would mean letting her go. [599] Wren laughed first, horribly, like something tearing. [606] Then Tobias, then Colm, all three of them bent double in the wet brick dark with quince steam rising around their knees. [628] Marguerite conducted until her arms ached and the tanks ran dry. [639] She did not laugh. [643] Conductors never play. [646] She had a house of her own once, quiet in the same way, and no one had come down into the dark for it. [670] In the morning she coiled her cords, left the ninth blower by the garden door, and walked out through the culvert mouth. [692] Behind her the house was noisy in the ordinary way, and Wren was telling her father about the dog. [711] Marguerite turned her own hourglass, small, kept in her coat, and went looking for the next silent house. [729]
