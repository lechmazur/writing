The pianist at Cloudspur Station played for passengers who never looked up. [11] His name was Orrin, and the station's freight of longing had long ago hardened in him into something steely: a quiet refusal to stop wanting. [36] On the piano's lid sat a broken anniversary clock, its hands frozen at 6:14, the minute the Arbor Express departed with Maren aboard and him still tuning strings on the platform. [67] They had promised each other an anniversary duet; she had kept every promise she could reach. [83] The Express ran to the tidal arbor above the clouds, a grove that rose and sank with the cloud-tide, and it ran only when the compasses agreed. [110] They rarely did. [113] All along the station wall hung navigators' compasses, flickering, needles stuttering between north and nowhere, because the arbor kept moving the way a tide keeps moving. [139] Fourteen years of flicker, and no departure board had shown the Arbor Express since. [153]

Orrin was not a patient man; he was a strategically creative one, which looks like patience from a distance. [172] He had spent those years studying the station cinema's only surviving film, a black-and-white reel of the arbor's last harvest. [192] The projectionist, half blind, let him handle it because he was gentle with fragile things. [207] Frame by frame he found what the cartographers had missed: hidden sections spliced into the reel, three dark frames every ninety feet, each holding a sliver of the arbor's tide table. [238] Someone long dead had smuggled the route through a movie, knowing film outlives paper. [252] Orrin copied the frames, laid them in order, and taught himself to read a tide in grain and shadow. [271]

On a Tuesday in autumn the compasses steadied for eleven seconds, and the board blinked: ARBOR EXPRESS, PLATFORM NINE. [290] He took the broken clock, left the piano lid open, and boarded. [302]

The arbor was exactly as the film had promised and nothing like it: pale trees rooted in cloud, their branches rising and falling with a tide that breathed. [330] Refuge, the old guidebooks called it, and the word was accurate; people came here when the world below had nothing left to offer them. [354] Among the roots stood a piano, weather-silvered, and beside it a woman with grey at her temples, tuning it with a key she had carried for fourteen years. [382] Maren looked up and did not cry out; the arbor taught stillness. [394]

"You read the dark frames," she said. [401] "I spliced them myself, the night before I left, in case the tide kept me." [416]

He set the broken anniversary clock among the roots, and its hands did not move, and he found that he no longer needed them to. [441] "Fate had us down for an ending," he said. [450] "Then we surprise it with an unplanned encore." [458]

They played the duet they had promised, badly at first, the arbor piano half a tone flat and the cloud-tide swallowing the low notes. [482] Then better. [484] Other refugees of the arbor drifted from the pale trees to listen, people who had stopped believing in second departures. [504] Orrin played until his knuckles ached, until the tide came high enough to wet the pedals, and he kept playing anyway. [525] They played until the horizon invited night, which in the arbor arrives as a slow violet undertow. [542]

When the last note went out, the compasses hanging from the branches steadied, all of them, pointing down toward Cloudspur. [562] A train would come; there would be a way back, and a choice. [575] Maren asked whether he wanted to go home. [582] Orrin looked at the stopped clock, at the smuggled frames folded in his coat pocket, at the listeners waiting for another song. [604] "Home is a station where nobody looks up," he said. [614] "Here they do." [617]

So they stayed, two tuners of fragile things, and every evening the arbor rose and the flickering compasses brought someone new, lost and wanting, and Orrin played for them with that old steely longing turned finally to use. [655] Fate, he decided, had never once asked for an encore; that was precisely why he gave one. [672]
