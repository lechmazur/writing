The coachman of Gloom Hill drove down to the river only when the rain had finished with the world. [19] His rule was older than his horses: wait until rain gutters stop ticking, then take the road. [36] Passengers thought it superstition. [40] It was only arithmetic. [44] The ferryman at the fog-bound river crossing would not push off while the gutters still counted, because the current ran crooked then, and because he wanted his fare quiet. [73] The ferryman took no coin. [78] He took a sentence. [82] One sentence, true, from each traveler, small enough to hold in the mouth like a pebble. [98] That was why people hired Aldo. [104] He was closely attentive in the way of men who have spent decades listening to weather through the roof of a coach, and his trade was to distill complexity into essence. [135] Give him your tangle on the ride down, and by the landing you would have your pebble. [152]

That October the last passenger was a woman named Marit, who carried no luggage except a yarn needle pushed through the hem of her sleeve. [177] It was blunt, bone-colored, meant for wool. [184] "My mother's," she said. [188] "She darned with it until her hands quit." [196]

Aldo let the coach roll and said nothing, which was most of his method. [210] Answers do not arrive by interrogation; they percolate, and they percolate best through ambient awareness -- the drip off the hawthorns, the gutters of the last farmhouse letting go of their count, the horses' breath, the far hiss of spindrift where the river widened and the fog made everything gentle. [259]

Marit talked for four miles. [264] There had been a promise, she said, written out and signed when she was nineteen: she would stay on the hill and keep her mother until the end. [292] She had kept it for eleven years. [299] Then her mother died in March, and Marit had taken the same sheet of paper -- the only good paper in the house -- scraped the old ink thin with a knife, and written a new vow over the ghost of the first. [340] To cross the river and find her brother Ansel, who had gone over nine years ago and never written. [359]

"A palimpsest promise," Aldo said. [364] "You can still read the old one underneath." [372]

"That is the trouble," she said. [378] "The ferryman will want one sentence, and I have two vows in one page, and I cannot tell which is the true one." [401] "If I say I am going to find Ansel, it sounds like I am running from her grave." [419]

The gutters of the toll-house were still ticking when they reached the landing, so they waited on the bench under the eaves. [441] Fog stood on the water like something patient. [449] Aldo did not think hard. [454] He listened instead: the slowing drip, the rope chafing its post, Marit turning the yarn needle over and over in her fingers the way her mother must have. [482] A yarn needle is blunt on purpose. [489] It does not pierce cloth; it goes through the holes that are already there, and it pulls the thread after it until the gap closes. [514]

"Your second vow is not written over the first," he said. [525] "It is written through it." [530] "You promised to keep your family until the end; your mother has hers, and Ansel has not." [547]

Marit sat with that. [551] The ticking thinned to single drops, and then the eaves went quiet, and across the water a bell knocked twice. [571] The ferry came out of the fog with its lamp fuzzed into a soft yellow ball, and the ferryman held out his hand for the fare. [597]

"I am still keeping the same promise," Marit said. [606] The ferryman turned the sentence over, found nothing loose in it, and stepped aside. [620]

Before she boarded, Marit scratched the words into the wet post of the landing with the yarn needle, because ink would not hold in such air. [646] Then she gave the needle to Aldo. [653] "For the next one who cannot decide," she said. [662]

He drove back up Gloom Hill in a spindrift serenity he could not have explained, the river's breath salting his coat, the fog closing the road behind him like a mended seam. [694] The needle rode in his hatband for nine more years. [704] He used it often, holding it up to passengers who had too many reasons, telling them what it was for: not to make a new hole, but to find the one already in you and draw the thread through. [743] Twice he found Marit's scratches still legible on the post, faint under newer names, and each time he waited on the bench with his hat in his hands until the gutters stopped ticking. [776] She never came back across, which he took, in the end, as good news. [790]
