After tomorrow disappeared, the tide still came into the atrium at its usual hour, and Mirenne found that comforting. [19] She was the titan-gear librarian, which meant she wore the library rather than worked in it: a hulking brass exoskeleton of cogs and counterweights, built in an age when books weighed as much as boats. [54] Every dawn the sea climbed the atrium's marble steps, read the lowest shelves with its cold grey fingers, and withdrew. [74] Winter sun came through the high glass and lay on the wet stone like a blessing nobody had asked for. [94]

The library did not hold books, not really; it held identities, and identities migrate. [108] They came in on the tide each new moon, small and silver and frantic, the selves that people had outgrown or abandoned or lost in grief. [134] Mirenne's work was to catch them, name them, and shelve them until they were needed again by someone else. [153] For that she used a fisherman's bamboo creel, older than the titan-gear, given to her by her predecessor with the single instruction: keep the lid loose. [179] The creel's weave fluctuates with moon phases - tight as a fist at full moon, wide and generous at the new - and so her whole calendar was a lunar one. [208]

Which was the trouble, now that tomorrow had disappeared. [217] It had gone quietly, the way a word goes out of a language. [230] No one in the harbor town could say what day followed this one; the almanacs ended mid-sentence, and the moon hung in the window at a phase that refused to be waxing or waning. [264] Without a next phase, the creel could not decide its weave, and the identities on the tide had nowhere to be caught. [286] Mirenne watched them wash against the stacks and dissolve like frost, and for the first time in forty years her routine had nothing in it to wonder at. [314]

That was what she could not bear. [320] Forty years of the same steps, the same ledger, the same salt on her gauntlets, and every single morning something silver and impossible in the basket. [346] Routine without wonder was only machinery, and she of all people knew what machinery cost to maintain. [363]

So she went down to the atrium's forge, which the tide filled and emptied twice a day, and decided to smelt a new gear. [387] A moon-gear, to turn inside the titan-frame and give the creel a phase to follow, a tomorrow of her own making. [408]

The trouble with smelting is that metal wants purity, and there was nothing pure left to pour. [425] She tried her old brass keys and they cracked. [434] She tried the drowned coins from the bottom step and they sulked in the crucible, refusing to join. [452]

On what should have been the third day, sitting in the wintersun with the creel on her knees, she understood. [472] An identity is not pure. [477] Every self she had ever shelved was a knot of contradictions - the coward who rowed out anyway, the mother who left and stayed - and they held together not despite that but because of it. [511] A thing could only be smelted whole by accepting contradictions into the melt. [524]

So she poured in the salt water and the fire's dry heat, the full moon's tightness and the new moon's mercy, the forty years she had loved and the forty years she had resented. [558] She poured in her own two selves, the woman and the titan-gear, and did not try to decide which was the true one. [581]

The metal took. [584] It came out of the mold pale and ringing, a small wheel with no fixed number of teeth, and when she set it in her chest-housing it began to turn at a pace she could not predict. [621] The creel on her knees breathed - loosened, tightened, loosened - and the tide came up the marble steps carrying a hundred silver migrants home. [644]

Mirenne caught them one by one, as always, and named them in the ledger, as always, and did not know what phase the moon would choose tomorrow. [671] That, she thought, was the whole of the work: the same steps forever, and never once knowing what would be in the basket. [694] The winter sun moved across the flooded floor, unhurried, and she let it. [707]
