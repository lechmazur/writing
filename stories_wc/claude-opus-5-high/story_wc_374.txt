Elections came to the forests in the last week of autumn, and the whole valley tasted like a ballot. [19] I read them the way my grandmother taught me, with the flat of my tongue against the wind: copper for the pines voting drought-first, sugar-rot for the birches arguing to flood the low meadows. [53] Sensing through taste changes is an old craft, older than the satellites that used to do it for us, and better, because satellites never had to swallow. [80] I had climbed the lavender bluff to taste the tally, and found someone already there, glowing. [96] She sat with her boots in the heather, skin lit faintly blue along the old medical seams at her wrists and collarbones, the way the long-lived ones from the orbital wards are lit. [129] Above us the aurora hung in green ropes, close enough to feel proprietary about. [143]

"You're the taster," she said. [148] "I came to disprove you." [153]

Her name was Sena, and she carried weather. [161] I mean that literally: a low cell of cloud had followed her up the switchbacks, and where she walked the lavender bent toward her as if being decided about. [190] That was why I had come north in the first place - to learn why some people carry weather with them, and whether they choose it. [216] Sena thought the question was superstition dressed in instruments. [225] She had spent forty years in the weather bureaus and had never once found a person in the equations. [244] "Show me a needle that points at a human being," she said, "and I'll believe you." [260]

So I showed her the ring. [266] It was tin, stamped in a factory that closed before I was born, a toy compass ring from a cereal box my mother had kept in a drawer of dead batteries and ticket stubs. [300] Its needle had rusted to the housing decades ago and pointed at nothing at all. [315] "That's your instrument?" Sena laughed, and her cloud brightened with it. [326] "It's a reminder," I said. [331] "The compass was never in the ring." [338]

Then the others came. [342] Tasters travel during the elections because that is when the land is most talkative, and by midnight nineteen of us crouched on the bluff, sipping air, rinsing, spitting, comparing. [371] Here is the part Sena could not argue with: not one of us can find a storm alone. [389] My tongue finds salt and guesses east. [396] Tomas finds iron and guesses south. [402] Old Ilke tastes nothing but tastes it urgently. [410] Alone we are useless; when we converge and chalk our readings onto the slate, an arrow assembles itself out of nineteen partial wrongnesses. [433] That is the emergent compass, and no hand holds it. [443]

That night the arrow pointed at Sena. [450] Nineteen tongues, nineteen different flavors of the dark, and every vector bent inward to a woman sitting in the heather with a rain cell on her shoulders. [477] She stopped laughing. [480]

I let her taste the slate air herself, and told her what I thought it meant. [496] She does not make the weather. [502] The old orbital wards rebuilt her from the inside out with ionizing seams, and now the atmosphere finds her easier to speak through than it finds the rest of us - a low place in the pressure, a person-shaped valley. [542] The taste around her wrists was terrestrially cosmic: mineral and ozone at once, the bluff's own lavender salt braided with the metal tang of the aurora burning overhead. [570] Her glow and that green sky were the same light doing the same work in two sizes. [587]

"Forty years of equations," she said, "and nobody thought to lick the wind." [600] She said it like a woman mourning a future she had been promised and had helped build wrong. [618]

I put the tin ring in her palm. [626] Her fingers were cold, and the rusted needle did not move, and the birches lost their vote at dawn by a margin of three degrees of soil temperature. [654] "Then I'll be the needle," Sena said, and pushed the ring onto her finger. [668]

She walks the ridges with us now, our luminous skeptic, demanding proof of herself twice a day and tasting the air for it. [691] Her cloud still follows, sulking above her hat. [699] When the forests convene again next autumn we will read the ballots faster than any bureau ever did, nineteen mouths and one direction, and a dead tin needle riding the hand that the sky keeps pointing at.
