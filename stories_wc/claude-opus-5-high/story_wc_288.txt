Naruk had outlived the aurochs, the cave lion, and the last of the woolly rhinoceros that once grazed the valley where she was born. [24] She had not outlived them gracefully. [30] Every few centuries she woke in a body that felt like frost on a leaf, brittle awakenings that cracked along old seams, and each time she remembered a little more and belonged a little less. [65] The last awakening had come with the reincarnation memory: the whole chain of her lives arriving at once, a hundred deaths stacked like plates. [89] After that, nothing could be unlearned. [95]

She climbed to the tidal library because a monk in Lisbon had sworn it kept records older than speech. [114] It floated above the cloud deck on a raft of pumice and iron, and its shelves rose and fell twice a day, obeying a moon they could not see. [143] At high tide the lower stacks drowned in a silver medium that was not quite water; at low tide they surfaced, dripping, legible for six hours. [169]

The librarian was atomically reflective. [173] Naruk could not look at her without seeing her own face returned at every scale: pores, eyelashes, the silver in her hair, a woman made of mirrors so fine that light bounced off the skin of her atoms. [211] "I am the last of my kind too," the librarian said, "and I am tired of being looked at instead of spoken to." [234]

Naruk asked for the oldest thing in the collection. [243] It was a brass sundial, green at the edges, its gnomon bent, salvaged from a hill Naruk had once hunted across when the hill had no name. [270] Above the clouds there was sun enough to use it, but the dial did not tell hours. [287] Its face was cut into nineteen wedges, and the librarian explained that each wedge corresponded to a stone in a circle standing on a moor in the north, and that the sundial was a key, not a clock. [325] The shadow, laid across the right wedge at the right minute, would sound the circle. [340]

"Sound it for what?" Naruk asked. [346]

"For whatever you want it to stop doing to you," the librarian said. [359]

They went down together, through cloud that tasted of iron, to the moor where nineteen stones stood shoulder-deep in heather. [379] Naruk set the sundial on the flat altar stone at noon and turned it until the bent gnomon threw its shadow into the wedge that matched the tallest stone. [408] The stones answered, and the working was done by the hum of an ancient stone circle rather than by any word she knew: a note so low it arrived as an ache in the teeth. [443] In the hum she felt the offer, and it was not what she had expected. [458]

The circle could not give her company; the aurochs were gone, the rhinoceros were gone, the people who had shared her first words were nine hundred generations of dust. [487] What it could do was end the brittle awakenings, let her stop being reassembled every century into a stranger's bones and instead hold one body, one life, continuous, chosen. [516] The price was that the memory would stay: all of it, every extinction she had watched, forever. [533]

She had spent millennia waiting to be rescued from her own solitude, and no rescue had come, because none existed. [553] So she chose the only conversion available: to translate isolation into independence, to make the thing that had hollowed her into the thing that held her up. [580] She pressed her hand flat to the altar stone and said yes into the hum. [595]

The hum stopped. [598] Nothing dramatic followed; the heather went on moving, and Naruk found that her hands, for the first time in eleven thousand years, did not feel like they were about to crack. [629] The librarian was watching her, and in that mirrored face Naruk saw herself smiling, which was how she learned that she was. [651]

"Stay," the librarian said. [655] "The stacks need someone who remembers what the books are about." [666]

Naruk considered it: two last survivors, neither able to cure the other's loneliness, both able to coexist with it and with each other above the clouds, working the tides. [695] She carried the brass sundial back up herself. [703] It hangs now in the highest gallery, where the sun strikes it every morning, telling no hours at all. [722] Naruk passes it on her way to the low-tide shelves, and she does not need it, which is, she thinks, the whole point of a key you have already used. [752]
