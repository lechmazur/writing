Voss sold lanterns to people who could not sleep. [9] He was a broker, not a maker, and every lamp that passed through his hands had belonged to someone else first: a widow's reading lamp, a sailor's storm lamp, a child's paper moon. [42] He kept them clean, priced them low, and never asked where the light had gone. [57] The one lamp he refused to sell was the ultraviolet. [67] It threw no warmth, only a bruise-colored glow that made secrets legible: forged signatures, scorpions in the woodpile, the chalk marks smugglers left on doors. [92] He carried it the night he finally walked up the hill to the glass house. [107] The greenhouse for extinct plants stood at the edge of the city like a lantern itself, lit from within, full of things that existed nowhere else. [133] Inside, the air was heavy and layered, and Voss breathed through his sleeve. [146] He had been aromatically cautious for eleven years, ever since a stranger's soap in a crowded market dropped him to his knees weeping in front of forty people who did not know his name. [180] Since then he avoided bakeries, rain on hot stone, wet wool, orange peel. [193] He wanted, more than he wanted sleep, to understand why certain smells opened a door in him that no photograph or song could reach. [217] The curator, Ilsabet, was waiting among the ghost orchids with a crystal perfume vial in her palm. [234] "You brought the lamp," she said. [240] He had agreed to it in a letter: one ultraviolet lantern, loaned for one night, in exchange for one answer. [260] Ilsabet's last living specimen was a moonvine called sorrowbell, extinct in the wild since his grandmother's girlhood, and it would not open. [282] It needed the exact light of the desert nights it came from: short, invisible, cruel. [297] Voss set the lantern beneath the vine and opened its shutter. [308] The greenhouse changed. [311] Glass turned to crystal shadows, every pane doubling, every leaf edged in a light that was not quite a color, and the whole room seemed to hold its breath under water. [342] The sorrowbell began to move. [347] It unwound like a fist forgetting itself, petal after petal, white going to bone going to faintly blue. [365] "The ultraviolet does not make it bloom," Ilsabet said quietly. [375] "It only reveals what the flower was already willing to do." [386] She held out the vial. [390] Inside was a single amber drop, the last distillation of sorrowbell scent, made sixty years ago by a perfumer who sold it by the milligram. [415] "Here is your answer," she said. [421] "Every other sense is filed, labeled, argued with. [429] Scent takes the short road, straight past the guards and into the room where you keep the people you have lost. [450] That is why you weep before you remember. [458] The memory arrives first; you arrive second." [465] Voss thought of the market, the soap, the forty strangers. [475] He had never let himself finish the thought that followed, which was his mother's hands, which was the room where she had died while he was three streets away brokering a lamp he did not need. [511] Eleven years of holding his breath, so as never to be ambushed. [523] The flower was still opening. [527] "Sorrowbell was a funeral flower," Ilsabet said. [534] "They planted it over graves in the dry country, because it only showed itself at night, and only to people willing to sit in the dark and wait." [562] She unstoppered the crystal vial and set it on the gravel, a small clear tooth glowing in the lamp's strange light. [583] Voss lowered his sleeve. [587] As the transformation completed, the last petal folding back like a hand opening, the scent arrived: green, cold, faintly of almonds and wet stone and the inside of his mother's coat. [618] He did not resist it. [623] He sat down on the gravel path among the extinct and impossible things and grieved, properly, for the first time, eleven years late. [646] Ilsabet did not comfort him; she only stood among the crystal shadows and let the flower do its work. [665] In the morning the sorrowbell closed and would not open again for a year. [679] Voss left the ultraviolet lantern hanging from its hook above the vine, unsold, unbrokered, the only thing he ever gave away. [700] He took the empty vial instead, and kept it in his coat, and on bad days he held it to his nose and smelled nothing at all, which was its own kind of mercy. [734] After that he stopped avoiding bakeries. [740] He walked through the orange sellers' street slowly, on purpose, wistful and unhurried, letting the dead come and go as they pleased. [762]
