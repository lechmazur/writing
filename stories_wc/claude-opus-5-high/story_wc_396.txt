The orchard grew on the lighthouse's shoulders, forty apple trees in salted barrels, and below them the ice floes rearranged themselves like a sentence nobody could finish. [27] Ilsa Varn had kept the peace here for eleven winters, and the peace had taken its fee in shadow. [46] Her own lay behind her in strands, frayed and doubled, laced with the darker outlines of everyone she had turned away at the water stair. [71] The girl standing in the orchard door was an anomaly, and the ledger of the Concord had no line for her. [92] Born on the floes, unregistered, she had crossed nine miles of breaking ice alone, carrying a wet canvas bag. [111] She was perhaps nine years old and she was not shaking. [122] "They sent me to ask," she said, "if the lighthouse will take forty of us before the channel closes." [141] Then she breathed in and waited, and that weighted breath before reply became the only room Ilsa had to live in. [162]

In Ilsa's coat pocket was the fragment: one curved shard of the orchard's crystal ball, broken the night the treaty was signed. [184] It had never shown futures. [189] It had only ever shown feeling: whose hands were cold, whose hope was thinning, and the Concord had smashed it for being useless to policy. [214] Ilsa had spent eleven winters trying to reconstitute it. [223] The rest of the sphere had not scattered into snow; it had scattered into reflections, and reflections must be gathered where they gather. [246] So she had learned the harbor town's vintage stores, the crowded back rooms where old mirrors leaned in stacks, foxed and silvering, each one holding a glimpse the crystal had lost. [277]

Now, inside the girl's held breath, Ilsa turned the shard to the lamp and let the glimpses come. [295] In a cracked wardrobe mirror behind a shop on Cooper Lane: a woman on the floes boiling seal bones for a broth she would not drink herself. [322] In a hand mirror with a bone handle: an old man rationing lamp oil so the children could see each other's faces at night. [346] In a tall pier glass, tilted, dust on its feet: forty people choosing the smallest and calmest among them to walk the ice, because a child asking is harder to shoot at than a man demanding. [382] The glimpses locked together with a sound like ice knitting, and the fragment in her palm was no longer a fragment. [403] It was whole, cold, and heavy, and it showed her nothing new. [415] That was the point, she understood at last: the sphere had never been an oracle but an instrument, and its only purpose was to map empathy onto action. [443] Feeling without a decision was just weather. [450]

The Concord's standing order was three words long, and she had recited it at the water stair for eleven winters. [470] Hold the line. [473] She looked at the girl, who had not yet finished breathing in, and who was calmly intuitive in the way of children who have had to read adults to survive: she had already found the apple barrels with her eyes, already counted the beds by the number of windows. [522] She had also, Ilsa saw, looked once at Ilsa's shadow and not flinched. [535]

"The trees need pruning," Ilsa said. [541] "Forty pairs of hands would do it faster than one." [551] The breath went out of the girl in a long white plume, and the anomaly in the ledger became, in that instant, a courageous anomaly with a lighthouse behind her. [581] The Concord would send a cutter in the spring, and she would answer for it with her name and her post. [602] Ilsa lit the lamp early that night so the floes could find the stair. [616] Behind her, in the doorway's long light, her shadow lay single and unlaced, thin as a pruning knife. [634] She set the reconstituted sphere in the crotch of the oldest apple tree, where it caught the beam and threw it outward, and did not look into it again. [663] She knew what it would show her, and she was already doing it. [676]
