The flood had risen to the third step of the orchestra pit, and Mira waded in anyway, her cello case held above her head like an offering. [27] Lanterns hung from the balcony rail, flickering, and the black water threw their light back in slow, trembling rings. [46] Nobody had played here since the canal wall broke, but Mira came every night, because every night the theatre answered her. [67]

She had been a pit musician for nineteen years, a woman who lived in the in-between space: below the singers, above the mice, visible to no one, essential to everything. [97] Lately she had been inspired, and it frightened her, because the inspiration was not hers. [112]

It began with the program. [117] She had found it floating in the aisle on the first night, an antique opera program from 1893, the ink still legible under a skin of silt: Orfeo, with Giulia Varesi as the shade. [151] Mira had dried it on the harpsichord lid, and the water had done something to it. [167] Where the damp had bled the ink, shapes had formed - not smears, but images: a staff of music, five lines with the ghost of a clef, and notes rising out of the stains like bubbles from a drowned mouth. [206] She had played the first phrase aloud, there in the pit, and the building had gone quiet in a way that buildings do not. [230] Then, upstairs, a door had knocked twice. [237] So began their wordless conversation. [242]

Tonight Mira set her stool on the conductor's podium, the only dry island left, and unrolled the program under a lantern. [263] New stains had risen overnight; the flood kept writing. [272] Giulia Varesi had died in this house, the archives said, on a night when a stagehand's mistake dropped a sandbag into the pit and disrupted the premiere forever. [300] She had been singing the shade's aria when it happened, and she had never finished it. [316]

The stains were a code, and Mira's whole purpose now was to unravel it. [330] Not the notes themselves - those were only the alphabet - but the intervals between them. [344] A ghost who had lived her life on a stage could not speak; she could only point at the gaps. [364] Mira bowed the sequence slowly, and in the hush of unstruck chords - the silence where the orchestra should have breathed with her - she counted. [388] Third, sixth, second, seventh. [392] Letters, if you numbered the alphabet the way a repetiteur numbers scale degrees. [405] C-F-B-G, then a rest, then more. [411] By the ninth lantern's guttering she had it: BOX FIVE FLOOR. [422]

Mira waded up the flooded aisle, water at her thighs, cold enough to make her teeth chatter in rhythm. [441] Box five's carpet had rotted to threads; beneath it a floorboard had swollen loose. [455] Under the board lay a tin case, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth, the last four pages of the shade's aria in Giulia's own hand - never copied, never performed, hidden by a jealous composer's widow or by Giulia herself; the paper did not say. [498]

Mira carried the pages down to the podium and propped them where a conductor's score would go. [515] She understood then what was being asked of her, and it was not archival. [529] A pit musician spends her life supporting a voice she cannot see. [540] Tonight there was no voice, no soprano, no house, only the water and the lanterns and a woman in the in-between place with an instrument. [565]

So Mira played the aria's ending on the cello, taking the singer's line herself, disrupting a hundred and thirty years of a rule she had never once broken. [593] The water shivered around the podium legs. [600] The lanterns steadied, all of them at once, burning clean and gold as gaslight. [614] And in the flooded stalls, in every trembling ring of light on the black surface, an image rose: a woman in a stage shade's grey, bowing, again and again, to an empty house that was finally, finally listening. [652]

Mira held the last note until her arm ached. [661] When it died, the program on her stand was blank - clean paper, no stains, no code, nothing left to say. [681] She sat a long while in the dark water, listening to the hush where an orchestra used to be, and found she did not mind it anymore. [708] In the morning she would send the pages to the conservatory, signed with two names. [723]
