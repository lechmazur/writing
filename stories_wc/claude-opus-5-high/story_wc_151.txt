The Bureau of Surface Mail opened at dawn, and Ilsa Vrey was already inside the golden arcade, waiting for the light to come down the glass like slow honey. [29] She was the Bureau's last pseudonym creator. [36] Her work was simple and nearly obsolete: citizens who wished to send paper letters without their legal names came to her window, and she invented names for them. [64] Not lies, she insisted, but instruments. [70] A name was a lens; the right one let a person say a true thing at last. [87] She kept her ledgers on an ebony clipboard, a relic from her grandmother's shipping office, its clip still strong enough to hold a hundred pages against the wind. [115]

Above her, the arcade's ancient advertising panels cycled through cities that no longer existed, and below her feet the tiles were covered in children's sidewalk chalk. [141] The chalk was why she came early. [148] For eleven weeks the courier children of the arcade had been writing codes at dawn: hopscotch grids with too many squares, spirals of pink numerals, arrows that pointed into doorways where nothing waited. [181] They were carrying messages for people who distrusted the networks, and they were paid in tangerines and batteries. [199] Ilsa was carefully inventive by trade, and she had begun to copy the chalk into her margins. [216]

That was her private vice: the margins. [223] Every letter that passed her window had them, and people wrote strange things in the white space around their own words. [244] Grocery sums. [246] A dead sister's birthday. [250] Half a hymn. [253] She had spent nine years trying to discover meaning in the margins, believing that the truth of an era lived beside the message, not inside it. [279]

At six-forty a girl came up the arcade steps with chalk dust to her elbows and a sealed envelope held flat against her chest. [303] She said her name was Nettie and that she needed another one. [315] The letter had to go by surface mail, she explained, because surface mail could not be read from orbit, only stolen by hand. [338] Ilsa asked who the letter was for. [345]

"My father," Nettie said. [349] "He's in the Pell Street facility. [355] They open anything with our family name on it." [364]

Ilsa set the envelope on the ebony clipboard and turned it over, and there, on the back flap, was a chalk-smudged line of numerals she had copied three days earlier from the tiles outside the fountain. [400]

"You wrote your letter on the ground first," Ilsa said. [410]

Nettie shrugged. [412] "If it gets taken, somebody still read it." [420]

And Ilsa understood, all at once, what she had been collecting. [431] The chalk was not a cipher for the letters; it was the margin of the whole city, written large enough that the rain would have to work to erase it. [461] She had the minute she needed, the minute before a letter is mailed, when a name can still be changed. [481]

She worked as she always did: she would juxtapose two things that did not belong together and wait for a third to appear. [504] She juxtaposed the chalk numerals with the advertisement flickering overhead, GOLDEN ARCADE, SUNRISE FARES, and wrote a sender on the envelope: Aurelia Sunrise. [527]

"He won't know that name," Nettie said. [534]

"He will know the chalk," Ilsa said, and copied Nettie's pink numerals along the envelope's margin, small, like a decoration a censor would skip. [558]

Nettie looked at it for a long time, then licked the stamp, a real one, gummed with something that tasted of the last century, and pressed it down. [586] The chute took it with a sound Ilsa loved, a soft wooden knock from a machine that had been built when knocking still meant arrival. [611]

Outside, the dawn finished climbing the glass, and the arcade turned the color of an old photograph of itself. [630] Ilsa clipped her ledger shut, went down to the tiles, and knelt in the wet chalk with her grandmother's clipboard on her knees. [653] There were new marks by the fountain already, a blue grid, a name she did not recognize, an arrow aimed east. [674] She began to copy them into the margin of her ledger, where she kept everything the Bureau had no column for. [695] Somebody, eventually, would come looking for the meaning of a century, and it would not be in the letters. [714] It would be here, in the chalk and the white space, waiting to be read by hand.
