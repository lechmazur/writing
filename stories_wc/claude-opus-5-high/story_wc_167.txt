Imre Vass had cut glass for the harbor at Pearl Bay for forty years, and he had learned that glass, like people, tells the truth only when you look through it rather than at it. [35] He was the harbor's glazier, and a reflective man, which the fishermen mistook for slowness. [50] They came to him for window panes and left with the odd feeling that he had read between the lines of their orders -- that he knew the cracked skylight was about a marriage, and the frosted pane about a daughter who no longer visited. [94]

Every twenty years or so, when the high summer clouds kept burning silver long after the sun had gone, Pearl Bay held its noctilucent reckonings. [119] The custom was old and practical: under that cold borrowed light the town counted what it owned and what it was worth, and whatever went unnamed in the tally was let go -- sold, dredged, paved, forgotten. [155] This year the surveyors from the deepwater consortium had come early, with their own ledgers, and everyone understood that the reckoning would be the last one before everything shifted. [184] Their columns had room for tonnage and berth fees and nothing else. [196]

Imre's answer was the prism atrium. [202] He built it on the chandlery point out of eleven hundred wedges of his own casting, a small round room of angled glass that stood most days inside the harbor mist like a lantern nobody had lit. [239] Mist was the point. [243] Dry air let light pass through unremarked; vapor caught the split beams and made them visible, so that anything carried into the atrium threw colors on the walls. [271] A gutting knife, a coil of tarred line, a tin cup worn thin at the rim: each one, set on the plinth, unfolded into bands of light complex enough to hold a stranger still. [305] That was all he wanted, to illuminate value in the mundane, since the mundane was what the consortium's arithmetic could not see. [327]

But an atrium can only show what you bring it, and Imre did not know which of ten thousand small things the town would need argued for. [354] For that he went up to the freshwater pond behind the chapel, as his mother had, and did the thing the family called communing. [378] It was nothing mystical, or nothing he would call mystical: he threw a stone and read the rings. [396] Water remembers every shore it touches, and the ripples came back off the reeds and the sunk skiff and the stone steps, and where they crossed they raised lines and hollows, a pattern as legible as a page to a man who had spent his life watching light interfere with itself. [446] He read the spaces between the rings, which was where the answers lived. [459] The pond told him three things and then a fourth: the bell buoy, the oyster-shell crosswalk that appeared only at low tide, Nils Aaro's ice house, and, last, his dead wife's bread pans. [492] He argued with the pond about the pans and lost. [502]

The reckoning was called for the hour the silver clouds faded, which at Pearl Bay meant dawn, when the water goes the color of the inside of a shell. [531] Half the town walked out to the point through the mist, carrying what the pond had named. [548] The consortium's assessor came too, a courteous woman with a folder, and Imre held the door for her. [566] Inside, the first sun came across the bay flat and low and struck the eleven hundred wedges at once. [585] The mist inside the atrium lit up in bars and fans of color, and the bread pans on the plinth threw a shadow with seven edges. [611] Nobody spoke for a while. [616]

"I can't enter this," the assessor said at last, tapping her folder, and she did not sound triumphant; she sounded like someone who had been handed a problem. [644] "Then write the number you can," Imre said, "and under it write what you saw." [659] "Somebody will read between them." [664]

The pier came anyway, the next spring; things shift, and glass does not stop them. [679] But the plans were redrawn around the oyster-shell crosswalk, and the bell buoy still speaks in fog, and the atrium stands on the point where the mist gathers thickest. [708] Children bring in bottle caps and gull feathers to watch them become complicated. [721] Imre, who is old now, tells them the same thing every time: nothing is plain, it is only unlit. [740]
