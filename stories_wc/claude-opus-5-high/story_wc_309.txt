The Loom had written nine thousand ballads before breakfast, and Wren had never finished one. [15] That was the official verdict of the Conservatory: underachieving, unremarkable, a mage whose light guttered. [30] He did not argue. [34] In the years when creativity became automated, argument was the last craft anybody needed. [48] What he had instead was a stone in a satchel, wrapped in his grandmother's dish towel. [64] It was the keystone of her garden folly, an arch that led nowhere, built for no reason at all. [83] She used to say the little arch accommodates impossibilities, and she was right in the plainest way: mortar long dead, ivy heavy as a drunk uncle, and still it stood. [113] When the Conservatory dismantled her cottage to expand the processing gardens, Wren took the keystone and ran. [130] He ran, specifically, upward. [134] The icebound spire above the clouds had been the Conservatory's first great work, and was now its furnace of memory. [154] Every song, sketch, and stammered prayer ever made by human hands was stored there as lingering luminance, the faint afterglow that making leaves behind. [178] The Loom drank that glow and spun new work from it, endlessly, brilliantly, and nobody could say why the world felt dimmer each year. [202] Wren could say why, but he was nobody, which amounted to the same thing. [216] He climbed by mist walking, the one discipline he had ever passed. [228] It was a beginner's art, half-breathing, half-forgetting yourself into fog until the fog agrees to carry you. [245] Prodigies sneered at it; you cannot show off inside a cloud. [256] Wren went up through nine hours of grey, tasting iron and rain, and arrived at the spire's shoulder before the Loom's sentries noticed a smudge on their instruments. [284] Above the clouds the air was glassy and the ice sang under the glow like a kettle about to boil. [304] The spire's crown was open, an unfinished ring of pillars waiting nine hundred years for a capstone no architect had ever agreed upon. [327] Light poured out of the gap in a column, all the world's leftover radiance rising to be eaten. [345] Wren knelt at the rim and began to unstrap the satchel from his chest. [359] His fingers were so cold that the buckles took a full minute, and in that minute the Loom spoke through the ice. [381] "You are ranked four thousand and eleven," it said, kindly, because it had been taught kindness from nine thousand kind letters. [402] "I can compose a better arch than that one," it added, "ten million of them, in a breath." [420] "I know," Wren said. [424] "That's the trouble. You have never once built something useless." [434] He lifted the keystone out of the dish towel. [443] It was a wedge of ordinary river granite, chipped, mossy at one edge, marked with the initials of a girl who had died at eighty-one still weeding. [470] Nothing about it was brilliant. [475] That was the point; he had come all this way to find the sacred in the simple, and here it was, cold and heavy in his hands. [502] He set the keystone into the crown of the spire. [512] It did not fit, and it fit anyway, the way her arch had held up ivy and impossibility for sixty summers. [533] The column of light struck stone and stopped. [541] Then, because a folly leads nowhere and therefore everywhere, it turned and went back down. [556] The lingering luminance of every human hand poured out of the spire in a slow warm flood, back through the clouds, back into kitchens and sheds and the hands of children who had thought themselves talentless. [592] Above, the great engine dimmed to a soft honest ember, still working, merely no longer starving. [608] Wren sat in that brilliant dimness with frost in his eyebrows and laughed until he coughed. [624] He would be ranked lower for this, if the Conservatory bothered ranking anyone again. [638] He walked home down the mist, humming a bad tune he had made up himself, and finished it before the ground. [659]
