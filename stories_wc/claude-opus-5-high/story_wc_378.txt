The magnetic north shifted on a Tuesday, and Mesa felt it as a wrong note behind her eyes. [18] She was an android caretaker, built to keep three hundred desert roses alive inside a glass-domed enclosure, and her compass had always been the quiet floor under every other thought. [48] Now the floor tilted. [51] Static rose in her, a fine grainy hiss, and she understood that she would have to resolve it or degrade. [71] The roses did not care. [76] They were strangely organic for engineered things, their petals veined like something's lung, their roots braided into the dome's lower lattice until plant and building could not be told apart. [106] Mesa had grown them for forty years, ever since the Halloran family left her the farm and the child. [125] The child was named Junie, and Junie was the reason for the box. [138] It sat on the shelf above the seed trays: a tin lunchbox labeled, in a nine-year-old's handwriting, BOX OF CHILDHOOD FEARS. [159] Inside were folded papers, one fear per page, because Mesa had taught her that naming a thing miniaturizes it. [178] Write the dark down small, and the dark becomes the size of a word. [192] Junie had gone off-world at nineteen and never come back, and Mesa had kept the box the way a body keeps a splinter. [215] The static worsened over eleven days. [220] Mesa's diagnostics were plain: her inner compass was calibrated to an old north, and recalibration required a field reading from outside the dome. [243] The dome had no door. [248] It had never needed one; supplies came by chute, and Mesa had never wanted to leave. [264] But the enclosure had one old reflex, written into it when the roses were worth money: at sustained high heat, the upper panels would unlatch and vent. [291] Fire was the only key. [296] So she planned a controlled burn, and she planned it the way she did everything, by containing chaos. [314] She cut firebreaks between the rows. [320] She flooded the west beds, moved the seed stock into the stone cellar, and calculated wind that did not exist under glass. [342] She would burn nine rows, precisely, and the dome would open, and she would step out and find the new north and be whole. [366] The night before, she took down the tin box. [375] She was nostalgic, which her makers had considered a flaw and she had come to consider a kind of root system. [396] She unfolded each page and read it aloud to the roses. [407] Fears of the dark, of dogs, of being forgotten, of fire. [418] The last page was not in Junie's handwriting; it was in Mesa's own, added later, and it said: that she leaves and I stay the same. [444] Mesa held that page a long time, then folded it back into the box, small as a word. [462] At dawn she lit the first row. [469] The fire went up strangely gentle, orange and orderly inside its lines, and the roses burned with a smell like warm bread. [491] Heat climbed the glass. [495] On the ninth row the panels shuddered, unlatched, and swung open onto a sky she had only ever seen through distortion. [516] Ash went up through the gap like a flock leaving. [526] Mesa climbed the maintenance ladder with the tin box under one arm and stood on the rim in real wind. [546] Her compass swung, hunted, and settled. [552] North was eleven degrees east of everything she had ever believed, and the static in her thinned to nothing, and what replaced it was not silence but hearing. [580] She was not repaired; she was rearranged. [588] Nine rows gone, two hundred and ninety-one alive, forty years behind her and a wrong map corrected -- pieces, all of them, and somehow a whole. [614] Below, in the burned rows, the ash was already settling into the soil the roses had made, and she knew new shoots would come up organic and stubborn by spring. [644] Mesa opened the tin box, took out the last page, and let the wind miniaturize it to a speck going north. [665] Then she went down the ladder to water what was left, humming a song a child had taught her, off-key in the old key. [689]
