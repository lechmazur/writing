The grotto held the platform up the way a held breath holds a word. [14] Magnetite veined its walls, and the slab of iron-shot rock floated three feet above the black pond, turning slowly, always slowly, under a ceiling crack that let the starlight in. [44] Ilena Vosk had measured that rotation for forty years and had grown eleven years younger doing it. [61] She was a researcher of backwards aging, which meant, in practice, that she was her own laboratory and her own slow catastrophe. [83] Her student, Bram, was fourteen and sat cross-legged at the platform's edge, waiting to be told something true. [101]

"Throw," she said. [104] He threw. [106] The stone went down and the rings came up, and Ilena watched them the way other people read faces. [125] This was the whole of her method: the pond's skin remembered every disturbance, and the magnetite beneath it bent those memories into patterns that ran forward and backward at once. [155] Read the rings long enough and you saw not where the stone had fallen but where it would fall next. [175]

"What do you see?" Bram asked. [181]

"Sycamores," she said, and it was true, and it was not the answer. [194] Above the crack, a sycamore leaned over the hillside, and its hush came down into the grotto like a hand laid on a drum. [218] Ilena wove truth carefully, strand by strand, giving him only what the rings had already prepared him to hold. [237] She had learned to withhold the way a gardener withholds water from a seedling that would drown in kindness. [256]

"Close your eyes," she said. "Go down." [263] He went down into the trance, as he had two hundred times before, and the platform turned, and the starlight crawled across the water. [287] Through the trance she spoke to him, as always, in small instructions. [299] Unmake your outline. Let the rings close over you. [308] This was her lesson, the only one she had ever meant to teach: to vanish in order to appear. [327] Everything solid in the grotto had learned it first -- the rock that floated by refusing the ground, the water that showed the sky only by giving up its own color. [357] Bram thought he was studying prophecy; he was studying disappearance. [367]

On the ninth night of the ninth year, the rings showed her something she had been waiting for since he was six. [389] A woman on the platform, alone, aged perhaps thirty, then twenty, then less. [402] A boy standing where she had stood, throwing the stone himself. [413]

"Bram," she said, and her voice had gone young enough to startle them both. [427] "I have kept something from you for nine years." [436]

He did not open his eyes; through the trance, his voice came flat and patient. "I know." [453] "The rings told me in the third year. You throw badly. You always have." [467]

The hush from the sycamore came down and filled the silence very thoroughly. [480]

"Say it anyway," he said. [485]

So she said it, and the saying was the last strand of a thing she had been weaving since the night she carried him down here wrapped in her coat. [515] The grotto did not make her younger; she had found it already going backward, and she had brought her son into it because the alternative was a fever that would have killed him by morning. [550] It had saved him and it had begun unspooling her, and in eleven more years she would be an infant on a floating stone, and someone would have to read the rings and know what to do. [587]

"That's why you taught me vanishing," Bram said. [595] "So I could watch you do it without breaking." [604]

"So you could do it," she corrected, gently. "A thing that goes away completely can come back anywhere. That is the whole of the science." [629]

He opened his eyes at last, took a stone from the platform, and threw it. [644] The rings went out and out, silver in the starlight, and in the third ring Ilena saw a woman of thirty standing on a hillside under a sycamore, holding nothing, unafraid, the way a stone rests in a hand before it is thrown. [687] She did not tell him what she had seen. [696] Some truths have to arrive late, or they arrive as nothing at all. [709]
