The town gave Ilse eleven weeks, the length of the butterfly migration, to carve one stone for forty-one names. [19] She worked in the ancient greenhouse at the edge of the cemetery, because the light there at dusk fell soft enough to show her where the granite wanted to open. [49] Ilse was a thoughtful carver, slow with her chisel, and the mourners mistook her slowness for indifference. [66] It was not indifference. [70] She did not know the shape of collective grief, and she refused to guess. [84] Her own griefs had shapes she could name: her mother's was a narrow vertical thing, her brother's a flat gray plate. [105] But forty-one at once, taken by one flood in one night, had a shape she could not see from inside her own life. [128]

On the ninth night, with monarchs clotting the broken panes above her like orange scales, Bartholomew Reece came back. [147] He had taught her lettering for six years and then died in his chair, and now he stood among the dead lemon trees with his sleeves rolled, faintly, the way frost is faint. [180] "You are carving a list," he said. [187] "A list is not a monument." [193]

"Then tell me what is," she said, and he laughed, because in six years he had never once told her anything outright. [215] He only ever set her a task and went quiet, which is a kind of teaching that outlives the teacher. [235]

"Go eat with them," he said. [241] "All forty-one houses. [244] Do the poem thing your people do." [251]

It was a family custom, older than Ilse: at dinner, everyone traded a favorite poem, and no one was allowed to explain why. [274] So she went, one house per evening, across the butterfly migration, and she brought bread and asked each family to trade. [295] The Okonjos gave her Psalm 130 and a fishing song. [305] The Larkins gave her four lines about a bell that keeps ringing after the rope is dropped. [322] A widower recited nothing and hummed instead, and she wrote that down too. [335] Each family gave her a ribbon as she left, since ribbons tied to the drowned fence posts had become the town's custom, and she wound them onto one spool until she carried a woven ribbon roll as thick as her forearm. [376]

In the greenhouse she unrolled it along the granite and saw that the ribbons did not lie straight; woven together, they curved. [398] Forty-one strands, each one pulling slightly toward its own house, made an arc. [411]

"That is not the shape," Reece said, from somewhere behind the seed trays. [424] "That is the measurement. [428] Now retrace it." [431]

So she retraced the arc with charcoal, then with her thumb, then with the chisel's blunt end, forty times, until her wrist knew it without her. [457] She became fiercely meditative in those weeks, eating standing up, sleeping under the potting bench, going hours without a thought she could have said aloud. [482] The greenhouse at dusk filled with thundering calm, the roar of eighty thousand wings overhead and, beneath it, a stillness so complete she could hear the granite giving way grain by grain. [514] She carved the arc first and the names after, set along it at the depths their poems asked for: the bell lines deep, the hummed nothing shallow as a breath on glass. [546]

On the last night of the migration, when the monarchs lifted off the panes all at once and left the glass clear, Reece looked at the stone for a long time. [577] "A curve," he said at last. [583] "Not a list, not a wall. [589] Why?" [590]

"Because no one grieves in a straight line," Ilse said. [600] "They lean toward each other. [605] The stone should show them leaning." [611]

He nodded, and went out of the world the way he had come into her work: without instruction, leaving her the task. [633]

In the morning the town came to the greenhouse, and they did not say much. [648] They found their own names' neighbors on the curve and put their hands on the letters, and one by one they tied their ribbons back on, this time to the stone. [679] Ilse stood at the back with her chisel in her apron, thinking that grief has no shape of its own; it takes the shape of whatever the living build to hold it. [711] That winter she began teaching a boy from the Larkin house to letter, and when he asked her what to carve, she set him a task and said nothing at all. [742]
