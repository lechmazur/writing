Mira called herself a probability busker, though the chalk sign said only: MIRACLES, SMALL, BY REQUEST. [16] She worked the tidal arcade, that long colonnade of salt-eaten arches where the sea came in at midnight and took the floor back from the tourists. [42] Between acts, when the tide was low and the stars hung close enough to seem interested, she set out her table and her cup and the single bishop she never sold. [73] Her act was simple to describe and difficult to believe. [83] She asked a stranger to think of something small and lost, and then, within nine minutes, she made it happen. [103] The trick, if it was a trick, was not magic but bookkeeping. [115] Mira had spent four years cataloguing the arcade: which busker played at which hour, which gull owned which arch, when the ferry horn startled the pigeons off the ironwork, how the puddle under the third lamp reflected anyone who paused there. [156] She did not control the crowd; she made luck by ordering randomness, the way a gardener orders weather by knowing it. [177] Orchestrated serendipity, she called it, and she was proud of the phrase the way people are proud of a scar. [197] The bishop was the seed of all of it. [206] At nineteen she had been a competent, joyless chess player in a town where nothing ever changed, and the bishop was the piece she resigned with, flicking it over on a board in a hall that smelled of radiators. [245] She kept it to remind herself that the diagonals were still there, everywhere, under everything, and that a life could be played across them instead of along the dull straight file of days. [278] She had come to the coast to serenade monotony with creativity, which sounds like a slogan until you watch someone do it nightly for tips. [303]

Tonight's stranger was a man in a ferry company jacket, lightly attentive, the way exhausted people are: present, unhurried, willing to be surprised but not expecting it. [330] "Something small and lost," Mira said. [336] He thought about it. [340] "My daughter's hair clip," he said, "blue enamel, dropped somewhere along here in June; she has stopped asking about it." [360]

Mira did not go looking. [365] She set the bishop on the wet stone, faced it down the arcade, and began to work. [382] She sent a boy chasing a thrown coin toward the drain grate at arch eleven, where the tide deposits everything light and bright that falls between the boards. [410] She asked the accordionist to change key, which reliably moved the drinkers three arches south and cleared the sightline. [429] She timed her juggling to the ferry horn so the pigeons rose exactly when the man's eyes were high, and when they settled he was looking down, at the grate, at a fleck of blue enamel in the silt. [468] Eight minutes, forty seconds. [472] He laughed the startled laugh she lived on, and knelt in the water to dig it out. [489] "How," he said. [492] "The sea sorts things," she said. [498] "It puts the heavy things deep and the light things at grate eleven, every tide, without fail." [515] "All I did was arrange for you to look." [524]

That was the whole of her art: a calculated wilderness, chaos kept as a garden, wildness weeded into shape. [543] He offered money and she refused, then pointed at the bishop. [554] "Take that instead," she said. [559] "Teach her the diagonals; tell her nothing in a straight line is ever the only route." [575]

The tide came up over the lowest step, the stars did their slow indifferent turn, and the arcade began to empty as it did every night at this hour, precisely, predictably. [606] Mira folded her table between acts and felt, for the first time in four years, the small vertigo of an empty pocket where the bishop had been. [633] She would have to trust, now, that the diagonals were still there without a piece to prove it. [651] Out past the last arch the water was doing what it always did, and she stood a while and let it teach her the next act. [677]
