Nobody in Vesper Cove called Tomas a poet to his face. [11] He was simply the quiet one who walked the shoreline at low tide, notebook under his arm, a drake's tail feather tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil. [40] The feather was green-black and curled, molted by a mallard he had nursed through a broken winter, and he used it to write because ink slid along its spine without blotting. [71] That was the whole of his reputation until the summer the lagoon went wrong. [85]

Vesper Cove kept a cobalt lagoon behind its dunes, and every year, on the seven mornings after the first big rain, the water threw back dawns that had not happened yet. [116] The old women called them woven dawns: light braided out of every sunrise the coast had ever held, laid over the surface like cloth on a table. [143] Fishermen read their year in those colors, and mothers brought babies to be blessed by them. [159]

That summer the weaving frayed. [164] The rain came, the lagoon turned its impossible blue, and the dawn arrived in pieces - a band of copper, a gap of gray nothing, a stutter of rose that quit halfway across the water. [198] The village panicked in its polite way and sent for a surveyor, then a priest, then Tomas, who had once written four lines about the lagoon that people still repeated at funerals. [230]

He came at four in the morning and did what he always did, which was listen. [246] The lagoon was loud. [250] A new jetty at the north end sent waves back against the incoming swell; the pump house hummed a flat note; gulls quarreled over the tideline; the reeds hissed where someone had cut them for thatch. [286] Tomas wrote all of it down with the drake's feather and understood, by the third morning, that the dawns were not broken but drowned out. [311] Light here was woven on sound; the loom needed a steady beat, and the cove had given it a dozen arguing ones. [333]

So he set out to tend it, the way he had tended the mallard, by harmonizing discord rather than silencing it. [354] He was warmly attentive to every complaint the water made. [364] He asked the pump keeper to drop the valve pressure a half turn, which lowered the flat hum into the reeds' key. [386] He paid two boys to weight the jetty's last stones with net bags of shell so the backwash came a beat late instead of a beat early. [413] He replanted reeds in two rows instead of one, and he fed the gulls at the far spit at dawn so their quarrel became a chorus at the edge of things rather than a brawl at the center. [451]

On the sixth morning the villagers came down to watch, and the lagoon lay in a burnished hush, copper and rose running nearly edge to edge. [477] Nearly. [478] A gap remained, a hand's width of plain gray water near the south bank, and Tomas could not close it. [498]

He had been awake three nights over that gap when a child ran past him along the bank with a paper kite, and as the kite string snapped, the small dry crack of it cut across the whole soft morning. [538] The kite went out over the lagoon, free and ruined, and the gray gap caught its shadow and held it. [558] The child did not cry; she laughed at the sight of it going. [570]

Tomas took the drake's tail feather from behind his ear and wrote the only thing he had learned in forty years on that shore: that a finished dawn would have nothing left to want, and that he had come here not to repair the light but to discover beauty in the incomplete. [622] He read the lines aloud to the villagers, four of them, badly, in his quiet voice. [638]

On the seventh morning the gap was still there, and no one asked him to mend it. [655] They brought chairs instead, and the woven dawns came in over the cobalt water with one thread missing, and the cove sat in that burnished hush and looked at the place where the light let the gray show through. [694]

Tomas tended the lagoon for another nineteen years, and every year he left the gap alone. [710] When he died they buried the feather with him, and the children of Vesper Cove still fly kites over the south bank on purpose, cutting the strings, to give the unfinished part of the morning something to hold. [748]
