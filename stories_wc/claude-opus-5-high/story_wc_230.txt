Mira Solvei had always distrusted miracles, which was inconvenient, because she had bought one from a smuggler at the ravine's rim. [21] The relic came wrapped in oilcloth: an antique glass eye, hazel iris, pupil flecked with gold leaf, cold as a river stone. [43] She paid in truffles, three winters' worth, because after the empathy virus money had grown thin and mushrooms had grown strange. [64] Mira was a forager by trade and a rationalist by temperament, and she had a forager's faith: dig where the evidence smells right. [87] The evidence, in this case, smelled faintly of mildew, since it had come to her through rumored cures in discredited textbooks. [108] Volume four of Hessler's Sympathetic Pathologies, condemned by every board that still met, claimed that leaded glass could occlude the bleed. [129] The bleed was what they called it now, the way one person's grief walked into another's chest uninvited, the way nobody could hold a private thought behind their teeth. [158] Everyone was involuntarily truthful, and everyone was exhausted. [166]

Mira's orchard hung in the ravine on braided cables her grandmother had strung, sixty pear trees in barrels suspended above the white noise of the river. [192] Storms came up the gorge twice a week and battered the whole contraption until the trees swung like censers and the fruit came down in a green hail. [220] It was an absurd place to farm and a perfect place to hide, which was why the frightened kept arriving. [240] Nine of them lived in the rock cut below the cables: a surgeon who could no longer operate, twin boys, an old broadcaster who wept whenever anyone said his name. [270] Mira had not invited them, exactly; she had simply failed to turn them away, and then found she wanted them fed. [291] Her aim, stated plainly because she could state nothing otherwise, was to cultivate psychological safety: a place where honesty cost you nothing, since honesty was no longer optional. [319]

The glass eye was meant to be the last piece of that. [331] Hessler's discredited chapter described a nineteenth-century mourner who wore such an eye and felt nothing of her neighbors' sorrow, shuttered, sovereign, alone. [353] Mira tested it the way she tested a new truffle ground: patiently, in the dark, expecting nothing. [370] She held the eye against her own left socket during the Tuesday storm, while the surgeon below was drowning in a memory of a boy he had lost on the table. [401] The bleed kept coming; the glass changed nothing. [409]

She sat with that result for a long while, rain sluicing off the cables, and admitted aloud, to no one, that she had wasted three winters of truffles on a superstition. [440] The broadcaster heard her, because everyone heard everything, and he came up the ladder in the wet to look at the thing. [462] He turned the eye in his palm and said it was beautiful, and that he had once smuggled worse for men who deserved less. [486] Then he asked whether it mattered that the eye did nothing, if people believed it did. [502] Mira, being what she was, said yes, it mattered enormously, and also that she was already considering it. [520]

So they made a rite instead of a cure. [529] Each evening, before the storms, the eye was set in a niche in the rock and whoever needed to hide sat before it and said the worst true thing they carried. [560] The glass could not occlude anything, but it gave the eyes somewhere to go, and the others learned to receive without flinching, which was a skill and not a miracle. [590] The surgeon spoke the boy's name into the gold-flecked pupil and did not dissolve. [604] Within a season he was setting bones again, hands steady, weeping openly while he worked, which the twins found hilarious and no one found shameful. [629]

Mira kept foraging, dogless, reading the ground by smell and slope, and she kept her ledger honest about what the relic was: a nineteenth-century prosthesis, pretty, inert, smuggled across a border by frightened people for the wrong reason. [667] When newcomers asked if it healed them, she told the truth, because she had no choice: no, it does not, and yes, you will still be safe here. [695] That answer, she noticed, worked better than the lie would have. [706] The orchard swung on in the storms, fruit dropping, cables groaning, the ravine full of people who could not hide and had stopped needing to. [731] It was not grace as the old textbooks promised it, whole and smooth and purchasable. [746] It was grace in pieces, broken and reassembled every evening in front of a glass eye that saw nothing at all. [767]
