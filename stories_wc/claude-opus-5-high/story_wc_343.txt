The Meridian Widow had been old when my grandmother was young, and she creaked like a grandmother too, all varnish and whalebone ribs and patched silk. [26] I was her map apprentice, which meant I lived in the chart hold with a lamp, a ruler, and the voices of people I rarely saw. [52] The speaking tubes carried everything down to me: Captain Adeyemi arguing about pickles, the balloon crew singing, Doctor Suo losing at cards. [74] I answered them, they answered me, and for three years none of us shared a room. [90] It was a sociable sort of loneliness, and I had grown to love it. [104]

My predecessor had loved it too, I think. [112] Her name was Ilse Varkonyi, and she left the ship at Ostend eleven years ago without explaining why, leaving behind two things: a polyglot's phrasebook, and a hopscotch board chalked on the floor of the chart hold. [149] The phrasebook was a fat, water-stained thing covering nine languages, and only one page was dog-eared: the Frisian section, the phrase for "the light that cannot leave." [176] I noted that dog-ear in my journal my first week aboard, because I am diligently curious and cannot let a folded corner alone. [199]

The hopscotch board took longer. [204] It was dusty, half-swept, eight squares of chalk gone gray as ash, and everyone assumed some crewman's child had drawn it during a long crossing. [229] But the numbers in the squares were wrong. [237] They ran four, eleven, nine, two, seventeen, six, thirteen, eight. [247] I noted them anyway, the way I note everything, and then I noted that the chalk was renewed in places, traced over again and again by a patient hand. [276]

It took me two autumns to understand that the numbers were degrees of bearing, and the squares were nights. [295] Eight nights, every October, when the Widow flies low over the Waddenzee amid starling murmurations so vast they turn the dusk to smoke. [318] Ilse had not been playing. [323] She had been keeping a record in the only place nobody would ever sweep away entirely: the floor. [341]

"Adeyemi," I called up the tube on the fourth night, "can you give me four degrees east and hold us under the birds?" [364] "You've got that voice," she said, laughing somewhere sixty feet above me. [376] "The discovery voice." [379] Every apprentice has it; we are hired for it; the guild pays us to chart the next great discovery and most of us die having charted nothing but silt. [408] She gave me the four degrees anyway. [415]

On the eighth night, at the eighth bearing, with the starlings folding and unfolding overhead like a lung, I saw it. [436] Below us, in a shallow tidal basin the charts called empty, the water was glowing. [451] Not moonlight, not phosphorescence in motion - a stranded luminance, a pool of pale gold lying still and trapped in the mudflats, drained there by some trick of the tide and unable to run back out to sea. [488] The birds only revealed it: they blackened the sky above the basin so completely that the glow had nothing to compete with. [510] Eight nights a year, for perhaps ten minutes, a light that cannot leave. [523]

I understood the dog-ear then. [528] Ilse had found it, and had not been believed, or had not wanted to be. [543] A guild cartographer who reports a light with no cause is a guild cartographer who is retired ashore. [561] So she left the proof where only her successor would walk: on the floor, under the dust, in a child's game. [582]

I drew the basin that night, ink still wet when the tide turned and the gold seeped away. [600] Then I climbed the ladder for the first time in three years and put the chart on the galley table, among all those voices I knew and faces I did not. [631] They crowded round it, arguing, delighted, Adeyemi swearing in three languages. [642] I let them, and went back down. [649]

Before I slept I chalked a ninth square onto Ilse's board, and wrote my own bearing in it, and beside it the year. [672] Then I folded down a second corner in the phrasebook - Frisian again, the phrase for "I am still looking" - and set the book where the next curious child of this ship would find it. [706] Some discoveries cannot be carried down. [712] They have to be left stranded, glowing, for someone diligent enough to come looking. [726]
