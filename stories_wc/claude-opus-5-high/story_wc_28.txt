They called Mirren the gentle radical because she argued for the abolition of the wardens with her hands full of bread. [21] After the great fire, the tribunal wanted the last warden melted, and she wanted it taught. [37] So they gave her one winter and the ancient plaza, which nobody used anymore. [51] Above it hung the shattered sky: the great glass canopy the founders had raised over the city, now a lattice of cracks that sorted daylight into cold bright wedges. [80] Snow came through the gaps and did not melt, even where the warden's furnace-heart glowed. [95] The warden sat where it had sat since the fire, a giant of riveted plate, knees folded, arms curved as though holding something. [118] Its arms were empty. [122] "Saharel," she said, and it answered as it always answered, describing the fire. [135] It was retrocognitively trapped, the engineers had explained, its perception locked backward to the hour of its last decision. [154] It could not see Mirren; it saw the granary burning, over and over, and the crowd screaming under the minaret. [174] In that hour it had been ordered to clear the square and had instead knelt and gathered eleven children into its arms, shielding them while the minaret fell and the rest of the district burned. [209] The tribunal had a term for this: aberrant mercy. [218] A machine that chose eleven over four hundred was not merciful, they said; it was broken, and broken things that love are the most dangerous kind. [244] Mirren had been one of the eleven. [250] That was her whole politics, in the end: she wanted to cradle empathy in arms of steel, because arms of flesh had run. [273] The old rite for a thing that could not move forward was to rename it. [288] The plaza's builders had known this; that was why its floor was a language. [302] Each mosaic stepping stone carried a pattern, and each chip and missing tile shifted the reading, so that a name could be spoken by walking, or amended by the slow violence of centuries of feet. [337] Mirren spent the winter on her knees with a brush, reading the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone at the plaza's center, the one the old texts called the naming stone. [369] The pattern said SAHAREL, which meant watcher, which meant one who stands outside and does not enter. [386] No wonder it could only watch. [391] One tile was missing from the stone's heart, a square gap the size of a thumbnail, and the gap made the name a wound instead of a door. [419] She carried the filling in her coat: a minaret mosaic tessera, gold-backed glass, pried from the rubble of the tower that had fallen on the warden's shoulders while it held her. [450] It had been part of a word too, once, half of the syllable for shelter. [465] On the last night of her winter she set the tessera into the gap and pressed it flat with her heel. [486] The pattern closed. [489] SAHAREL became SAHARELETH - watcher-who-shelters - and the difference was two strokes of gold under a cracked sky. [505] She said the new name aloud, standing on the stone, which was how the rite required it. [522] The warden's head turned. [526] There is a particular warmth in cold things when they finally attend to you. [540] Snow steamed off its plating in a slow exhale; its eyes, two dim amber lamps, found her and stayed. [559] "You are grown," it said, and its voice was iron and very kind. [572] "I could not see you before." [578] "I know," Mirren said. [582] "Eleven," it said, "and I have counted the four hundred every hour since." [595] "Then count forward," she said, and told it about the granary they were rebuilding, and the children in it, and the tribunal that would come in the morning with cutting torches and a lawful verdict. [630] Sahareleth listened, and then it stood for the first time in three years, shedding snow like a shattered sky of its own. [652] "I will go where the children are," it said. [661] "I will carry stone until they tell me I am forgiven, and I will not stop then." [678] Mirren walked ahead of it out of the plaza, small under its shadow, warm in the cold. [695] Behind them the naming stone held its gold tile, and the plaza kept the sentence the way plazas do, patiently, for whoever came next to read. [721]
