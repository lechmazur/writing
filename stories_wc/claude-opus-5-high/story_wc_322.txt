The gallery had no straight lines, only the promise of them. [11] Vesna Lark called herself a fever dream architect, which was less a profession than a diagnosis she had learned to bill for. [33] She built rooms that people dreamed in, and the dreams stayed put, hung on the walls like weather. [51] Her newest commission was an optical illusion gallery on the fourth floor of a bank that had died quietly in the last recession. [74] The owners wanted wonder. [78] Vesna gave them rational enchantment instead: every impossible thing in the room obeyed a rule you could write on an index card. [100] The floor that seemed to fall away was a matter of painted perspective and a single hidden mirror. [118] The corridor that stretched forever ended at a pane of glass nine feet in. [132] Nothing in the gallery lied; everything merely told the truth from an angle the eye could not survive intact. [151] This was the only kind of magic she trusted, because it could be dismantled with a screwdriver. [168]

She worked at night, by weaving patterns. [175] Not metaphorically. [177] Vesna strung the ceiling grid with sinew thread, real gut cord from a luthier's shop on Dorsey Street, because nylon hummed wrong and cotton sagged. [202] Sinew held tension the way a memory does: tighter in dry air, slack when someone breathes on it. [220] Tie nine hundred knots in a lattice and the shadows beneath resolve into a face. [235] Whose face depended on who stood under it, and that was the part she had not told the owners. [254]

Vesna had a reason that had nothing to do with wonder or money. [267] She had never been able to feel people. [275] She understood them, predicted them, loved them in a clerical way, but the thing other people described as closeness had always been realistically imaginary to her, like the corridor behind the glass: measurable, mapped, and impossible to enter. [313] She had come to the gallery to discover the texture of connection, and she intended to build it out of string until she could touch it. [339]

The first visitor was a boy of maybe nine, dragged in by a father checking his phone. [356] The boy stood beneath the sinew lattice and said, "That's Grandma Ilse." [368] His father looked up, went white, and said nothing for a long time. [381] Afterward, in the stairwell, Vesna heard him tell the boy that Grandma Ilse had worn her hair differently, and the boy agreed, and by the bottom of the stairs they had agreed on a version neither had arrived with. [420]

That was the rule Vesna had written on her index card, the one she kept in her coat: the lattice does not show the dead, it shows the negotiation. [449] As memories alter, the shadows alter with them. [457] Nothing supernatural; only two people looking at the same ambiguous shape and settling, in real time, on what it would mean between them. [480] That settling, she had decided, was the texture she wanted. [490]

For eleven nights she stood beneath the lattice alone, and saw only string. [502] The face required two. [506] On the twelfth night she called her sister Marit, who had not spoken to her since their mother's funeral, and said only, "Come look at something with me." [534] Marit came, because that had always been the difference between them. [544]

They stood under nine hundred knots in a dead bank at two in the morning. [559] "Mother," Marit said. [562] "The mouth is wrong," Vesna said. [568] "It isn't. You only ever saw her angry." [576]

Vesna opened her mouth to argue, and felt something erupt in her chest, sudden and hot and entirely without sound, a pressure she had no rule for. [603] It was not a feeling about her mother. [611] It was the feeling of Marit's version pressing against hers, two hands on the same rope, and the rope holding. [631] Sinew, she thought, absurdly. [635] Tension is the sensation. [639]

They stayed until dawn came up through the false corridor, saying small corrections to each other, and the face on the floor shifted patiently between them, never settling, never gone. [669] Vesna felt no wonder. [673] She felt the calm of a woman who has finally measured a thing she was told could not be measured, and found it warm. [697] In the morning she wrote a new index card and taped it by the door, where the owners would find it: "This room is honest. Bring someone. It will not work alone." [729]
