The telephone rang at the top of the world, and Ilse Vane let it ring twice, because she was devotedly observant and the sky was doing something. [27] Below the observatory, the clouds lay flat and silver, a second ocean. [39] Above it, Ilse's father's money had bought forty feet of dome and a very good mirror. [55] Newsprint had paid for starlight; the heiress of it had always found that funny in a solemn way. [73] On the third ring she answered. [79] "Miss Vane," said the journalist, "we're running the Kestrel Mine story Sunday." [91] "Your father buried eleven dead men under a weather report in 1979." [103] "I'm calling because I believe in giving the accused a night." [114] "He's been dead nine years," Ilse said. "Give him two." [124] The dome echoed her, gently, the way it echoed everything: two, two, two. [137] She hung up and went to the cabinet where she kept her orbits of regret. [152] It was a real chart, drawn on real paper, and she was only half joking about it. [169] Every regret she owned had a period, she had decided, like a comet. [182] Some came back yearly; some took decades and arrived enormous. [192] The Kestrel Mine was on the chart, plotted at seventeen years and now returning, right on schedule, with a tail of eleven names. [215] She had been nineteen that summer, shelving proofs in her uncle Aurel's composing room. [229] She remembered a page that existed at four in the morning and did not exist at six. [246] She had said nothing, for years, and then for more years. [257] The kill order was in the retraction ledger, initialed in her father's hand: E.V. [271] That was what the journalist had, and it was damning, and it was wrong. [285] Ilse knew it was wrong because of the pen. [293] Her father had written with one instrument his whole life, a rosewood pen nib he had split on a drunken oath in 1952 and refused to replace. [320] A split nib doubles every downstroke; it writes each letter with a faint twin, like a star seen through bad air. [341] She had spent her childhood reading that doubling on birthday cards and dismissal notices. [355] She took the ledger page from the cabinet and put it under the eyepiece lamp. [370] The initials were single-stroked, clean, no twin anywhere. [378] So: suppose her father wrote it. [384] Then the ink would double, because his nib doubled everything he had signed since before she was born. [402] The ink did not double. [407] Therefore he did not write it - proof by contradiction, the only kind of proof she had ever trusted, since it asks the world to lie and then catches it. [436] Someone had forged a dead man's initials, and someone had done it while he was still alive to be blamed later. [457] Uncle Aurel wrote with a fountain pen, single-stroked, and still ran the paper from the ninth floor. [474] Ilse sat with that for a while, in the cold, listening to the dome breathe. [489] She had built her life on watching: stars, ledgers, the small tells of relatives at Christmas. [505] Watching was honorable and it was also a way of never having to intervene. [519] An astronomer does not touch the sky; she only records what it already decided to do. [535] That had been her excuse at nineteen, and it had a seventeen-year period, and here it came again. [553] The nib was in the drawer, wrapped in flannel, rosewood gone dark as dried blood. [568] She had kept it for nine years to have something of him that told the truth twice. [585] At five in the morning she called the journalist back. [595] "Hold the story three days," Ilse said. "I'll give you a better one, with a living man in it." [614] "Bring a handwriting examiner, and bring a photographer, because I want the nib photographed before I hand it over." [633] "Why now?" the journalist asked. [638] Ilse looked at the east, where the cloud sea had begun to go pink and ridiculous. [654] "Because I have spent seventeen years proving I was only a witness," she said, "and the proof keeps contradicting itself." [674] She hung up, and the dome gave the last word back to her, softer each time, until it was only the sound of a room agreeing. [700] Then she went up the ladder, because there were eleven minutes of dark left, and she intended to spend them observing. [721] Eleven names, eleven minutes; she had always suspected the hidden truths were merely the ones nobody had been willing to look at long enough. [745]
