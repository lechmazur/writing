The road down from Gloom Hill is one lane wide, and it holds the whole sky. [16] Corvin had driven it for thirty years, first with passengers, then with coffins, and now with a satchel of dried seed pods rattling on the seat beside him. [44] He was the last coachman of the hill, and the only man alive who could read the Concord. [62] The Submerged Concord had been signed underwater, three fathoms down, by two admirals who had agreed that no treaty made in air could be trusted. [87] They carved its terms into the living reef in a language already dying then, and left the coral to keep the peace for them. [111] Corvin had learned that language from a drowned grammar in his father's attic, along with two others nobody spoke. [130] Now the reef was dead. [135] It had bleached white in the summer the war ended, and the carved words went soft and began to crumble into sand. [157] With the stone tongue gone, both fleets had quietly begun to remember their grievances. [171] The shallows where they had fought were a graveyard of ribs and iron, and the jetty above them was the narrowest place Corvin knew, and the widest. [198] He arrived at twilight, as he always did, because that was when the birds came in. [214] Terns, curlews, one hoarse old oystercatcher who had nested on the second piling for six seasons. [230] Corvin could not swim, could not dive, could not re-carve a reef. [242] But he had noticed, years ago, that birds argue in a grammar of their own, and that their arguments are always about food. [265] A tern's third cry meant shallow water thick with fry; the curlew's rising note meant soft mud, turned and rich; the oystercatcher's rattle meant hard bottom, dead bottom, nothing here. [295] By listening, he had mapped every inch of the drowned battlefield without wetting his boots. [310] The seed pods came from the marsh above Gloom Hill: eelgrass, samphire, sea-heath, saltwort, gathered dry and kept in a tin box labeled in a dead hand. [337] His plan was neither grand nor quick. [344] He would let a garden reclaim the battlefield. [352] Eelgrass would hold the silt; the silt would fertilize itself with rot and shell; in a decade, perhaps, the meadow would soften the iron and give the small fish somewhere to hide. [384] And Corvin, meanwhile, was carving the Concord again. [392] Not into coral, which could die, but into the jetty's own pilings, one clause per post, in three dead languages so that no future scholar could claim a mistranslation. [421] The pilings were narrow. [425] The sea was not. [429] Tonight a woman in an admiral's coat walked out along the boards and watched him press seed pods into a ball of mud and clay. [454] "The treaty is void," she said. [460] "The stone that held it is gone." [467] Corvin threw the ball where the curlew had told him the mud was soft. [481] "The stone is gone," he agreed, and recited the ninth clause in the admirals' own tongue, the rolling one, the one that sounded like water over gravel. [508] Her face changed; her grandmother had sung her that sound as a child, without knowing the words. [525] "What does it say?" [529] "That peace must be planted, not signed," Corvin said. [538] "They meant it literally." [542] "They were farmers before they were admirals." [549] He handed her a pod. [554] She held it a long time while the light went out of the west and the oystercatcher rattled twice, meaning nothing here, nothing yet. [578] Then she knelt at the jetty's edge, in her good coat, and threw it as far as she could. [597] By midsummer the jetty was blooming: samphire in the seams of the boards, sea-heath crowning the pilings over the carved clauses, green blades combing the current below. [624] Corvin came every twilight with the tin box, one lane of boards under him and everything else above. [642] The terns cried three times, and he understood them, and he went where they said the water was young again. [662] Nobody rode the coach anymore, but he kept the horses fed, because a garden needs a road, and a road needs someone patient enough to be its last driver. [691]
