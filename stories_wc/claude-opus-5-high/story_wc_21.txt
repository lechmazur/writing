The letters had been out of the wall for eleven days, and Ida Cobb had not slept in nine. [19] She read them in the belly of the Parallax, a library that floated because the counties below had burned three of its predecessors. [42] Zeppelin canvas breathed above her like a lung, and below, the Carolina coast unspooled in salt haze. [59] As the archive's only civil-rights archivist, she was solemn by trade; grief, catalogued, becomes posture. [74] The letters were ordinary -- a voter drive, a church supper, a boy named Wilmer Tate asking his sister for socks. [94] The diary sewn into the packet's oilcloth lining was not. [104] Every page was encrypted: columns of numbers, each triplet keyed to something Wilmer had trusted more than paper. [122] It took Ida six nights to understand that the key was the beach itself. [136] In 1961, Wilmer had walked the tideline arranging beach debris into messages -- crab traps, bottle glass, bleached rope -- knowing the survey airships photographed that shoreline every Tuesday for the phosphate companies. [167] His cipher lived in the archive's own aerial plates, filed under Coastal Erosion, 1959-1963. [181] Decoded, the first entry named a place: the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes, a sinkhole of black water the locals called the Eye, where the phosphate men dumped what they did not want counted. [215] The second entry named three men who had gone into the dunes with Wilmer's friend Cleatus Poole and come out lighter. [236] Ida took the winch-basket down at dawn. [243] Truth, she had learned in thirty years of depositions, only surfaces under tension -- two accounts pulling against each other until something tears and shows its grain. [269] She wanted Wilmer's account and the water's, laid side by side. [280] The Eye was exactly as written: a lens of volcanic-looking stillness ringed by sea oats, reflecting nothing, not even the zeppelin's shadow crossing it. [304] She knelt, and the year fell out from under her. [314] It was August 1961, and it stayed August 1961. [323] She was retrocognitively trapped -- not a ghost, not a witness, simply a woman folded into the crease of an afternoon that refused to finish. [347] Heat rang off the sand like a struck bell. [356] Cleatus Poole ran the dune line with three men behind him, and the black water took him, and then the sand smoothed, and Cleatus ran again. [382] Forty times, sixty, she could not count; it was a searing reverie, beautiful in the way a furnace is beautiful, and she could not look away because looking away was the crime she had spent her life documenting. [420] On some iteration, Wilmer Tate sat down beside her in the sea oats, seventeen and sunburnt, holding a coil of bleached rope. [442] "You're the one reading," he said. [448] "I'm the one who never stopped watching." [455] So they held a parley there in the loop's hot margin: what each would give, what each would keep. [474] He would give her the fourth name, the one he had never dared encode, the deputy who drove the truck. [494] She would give him release from his own watching -- she would carry Cleatus out of the Eye in words, since nobody had carried him out in anything else. [522] "Then you have to tell them where you are," Wilmer said, "the only way this beach talks." [539] So Ida rose in the unending afternoon and did what a boy had taught her across sixty years of filed photographs. [560] She dragged driftwood, hauled a rusted crab trap, laid bottle glass in glittering rows, and wrote across the tideline in letters thirty feet tall: IDA AT THE EYE. LOWER THE BASKET. [591] The loop erased it; she wrote it again. [599] She wrote it until the debris held its shape, until the survey airship's Tuesday shutter clicked above her in 1961. [619] Aboard the Parallax, in present time, a clerk pulling plate 1961-08-22 for the Poole file saw a living woman's name spelled in driftwood and screamed for the winch. [647] They found her at the lagoon's rim at dusk, sunburnt, forty years of heat in her face, whispering a deputy's name. [668] The deputy was eighty-one and still alive in Georgetown, and that was the tension Ida needed: the diary's word against his. [689] Divers went into the Eye that November and brought up Cleatus Poole's bones, and a length of chain, and the truth pulled tight between them. [714] Ida catalogued it all in her steady hand, solemn as ever, though sometimes at altitude she still smelled hot sand. [734] She kept one plate framed above her desk: a beach, a boy's rope, and a message she would not remember writing until the day she wrote it. [761]
