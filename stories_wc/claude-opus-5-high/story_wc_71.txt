Marisol Vane had a small, unfashionable talent: she noticed the things that changed so slowly nobody else admitted they had changed. [21] The bay had lost nine inches of beach in eleven springs, and the neighbors still called it the same beach. [41] Ferries came twenty minutes later each decade; the herring shrank; the Dorsett house went from breathing to merely standing, and nobody could say which Tuesday it happened. [68] That last one was the question that had eaten her forties. [79] Why do some houses feel alive? [85] She worked as an estate clearer, which meant she was paid to be in rooms after the people left them, and she had begun keeping notes. [111] Her theory, which she never said aloud because it sounded like religion, was that a house is sanctified by displaced objects. [132] Not tidy houses. [135] Houses where a thimble had migrated to the windowsill and stayed twenty years, holding a drawer's worth of somebody's attention. [155] A teaspoon in the bathroom. [160] A child's shoe on the mantel above a coal that never burned. [172] Objects out of place were evidence that someone had loved the room enough to ruin its logic. [189] Her own displaced object lived in her coat pocket: a shard of orris root, snapped from her grandmother's perfumery stock in Grasse, gray as a knucklebone and smelling faintly of violets and cellar dust. [223] Orris is a fixative; it does not want to be smelled, it wants to make other smells stay. [241] Her grandmother had said that once, wiping her hands, inadvertently profound: 'Some things exist only to hold other things still.' [261] Marisol had carried the shard for thirty-one years and had come to think of it as a shard of hush, a piece of held quiet, portable, the way other people carry a saint's finger. [295] On the first warm evening of April she let herself into the Dorsett house with the agency key. [313] The bay outside was flat and burning, that spring trick where the water holds the sunset without making any noise about it. [335] A hushed blaze. [338] It was the hour when the streetlights daydream, coming on pink and uncertain while there is still light to waste, glowing at nothing. [361] Inside, the house was perfect and dead. [368] Ada Dorsett's son had come up from the city in March and put everything where it belonged. [385] Cups with cups. [388] Books by height. [391] He had put the brass compass back in the desk drawer, and the desk drawer had closed over it like water. [412] Marisol remembered the compass on the kitchen sill, where Ada had kept it for sixty years so she could check, every morning, that the bay was still east. [440] That was the Tuesday, then. [445] Not a death: a tidying. [450] The house had not stopped breathing when Ada stopped; it stopped when the last thing out of place was put back. [471] Marisol opened the drawer and set the compass on the sill, east side, dead center. [486] Then, because a borrowed gesture is only half a gesture, she took the orris shard out of her pocket and laid it beside the compass. [511] It had no business in a Maine kitchen. [519] That was the entire point. [524] Fixative. [525] Something to hold the room's remaining smells still: linoleum, cold ash, fifty summers of salt in the curtains. [543] She stood there a long time while the light went from blaze to ember and the streetlights stopped pretending. [562] The house did not come alive. [568] She was not a fool. [573] But the room had acquired a wrongness now, a small unexplainable object that the next family would have to decide about, and wondering is a kind of attention, and attention is what she had been calling life all along. [612] Walking home along the quiet bay, she found her pocket strange and light, thirty-one years of held quiet gone out of it. [634] Creeping normality works both directions, she thought; you can smuggle things in an inch at a time too. [652] She began, the next week, with the Halloran place: a teaspoon in the bathroom, and a violet-smelling stone she chipped for herself from her grandmother's last block. [679] Nobody on the bay ever noticed. [685] That, she had learned, was how anything sacred gets built. [695]
