The alchemist's market hung two hundred feet above the rooftops, a lattice of rope-bridges and lantern-poles swaying whenever the harbor wind remembered it had somewhere to be. [27] Ilse crossed it twice a day, because she was the only between-worlds translator the guild still trusted. [44] Down in the sunken quarter, where the old harbor had drowned, the dead spoke in pressures and pauses rather than words. [65] Ilse turned that grammar into something a merchant could sign. [74] She had learned it between childhood and now, in the long unmeasured stretch after the Lamp Commission tore down the Grey Head lighthouse and scrubbed its keepers from the registry. [104] Her mother had been one of those keepers. [111] Officially, no one had ever kept that light. [119] What Ilse carried instead of proof was a lighthouse lens fragment, a wedge of prism the size of a plum stone, strung on her mother's brass chain. [146] It was elusively tangible: warm when she stopped thinking about it, thin as rumor when she looked straight on. [165] The alchemists on the high bridges had a phrase for such things. [176] They called them anchors of intuition, objects that hold a knowing in place, the way a stone holds a boat against the tide. [199] Without an anchor, they said, a knowing drifts off and becomes a mood. [211] Ilse's knowing was this: the overlooked are not gone, only unindexed. [222] She found her proof through hidden details in paintings. [231] Master Oreb sold canvases at the market's western span, harbor scenes so conventional that inspectors never paused at his stall. [251] Held up to the lens fragment, though, his grey water broke into rows of tiny painted faces, and every face wore a name in brushstrokes finer than a hair. [280] Dredgers, lamp-keepers, bridge-women who fell and were recorded as weather. [291] Oreb had been hiding the deleted city in plain sight for forty years, waiting for someone with the eyes to unfasten it. [313] "Reading is not enough," he told her, the morning the Commission posted notice that the sunken quarter would be filled with slag. [335] "The dead below cannot testify in a language the council accepts. You can." [348] So Ilse did what translators do, which is refuse to let meaning stay in one room. [363] At the center of the market hung the Assay Lamp, a great alchemical lantern that projected proofs onto the low clouds so the whole city could read them. [391] Its focusing socket was empty, had been empty since the Grey Head was demolished and its glass carted off. [410] Ilse climbed the mast at dusk with Oreb's largest canvas lashed to her back. [423] Her hands shook at the clasp. [429] To unfasten the chain was to give up her anchor, and she understood, with the clarity of someone paying a real price, that afterward she might no longer be certain of anything. [461] Her mother's face might thin out into rumor, like the glass itself. [472] She unfastened it anyway. [476] The fragment seated into the socket with a sound like a held breath released. [489] Light went through it, through Oreb's harbor, and up. [498] What struck the clouds was not a painting of water. [508] It was ten thousand faces, each with its name legible from the alleys, from the counting houses, from the Commission's own balconies. [530] The market stopped swaying, or seemed to. [537] Later, people argued whether the display lasted four minutes or all night, and Ilse could never settle it, because for her it was neither. [561] It was an eternal instant: her mother at the Grey Head lamp, herself at nine on the gallery rail, the drowned quarter murmuring its pressures below, all of it happening at once and still happening. [596] The filling of the sunken quarter was suspended pending inquiry, which is a small, bureaucratic sentence for a thing that felt like a tide turning. [621] Ilse kept translating, though her certainty came slower now, unanchored, arriving only after long listening. [636] She did not mind. [640] The knowing had simply moved to a larger anchor: a lamp above the rooftops, holding ten thousand overlooked histories steady against the current, honoring them in the only language a city truly reads, which is light.
