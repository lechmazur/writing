After the severance, the tides of remembrance came only twice a year, and I had missed the first one arguing with my sister. [23] So I took the ferry to Marrow Islet, which is breezy after rain and smells like salt and cut cedar. [43] The bonsai tutor was waiting at the end of the pier with a tray of shears, speaking so slowly that the gulls had time to finish their sentences first. [72] 'You have come,' he said, 'for the compass.' [80] Everyone did. [82] The ghost ship's compass had washed up on the islet three winters ago, still bolted to a fragment of deck, and it did not point north. [108] It pointed toward whatever a person had left unsaid. [117] My sister and I had been severed for eleven months, not by anger exactly, but by a silence that had hardened like sap into amber. [142] I wanted to know the color of the apology I had never given her. [156] The tutor did not hand the compass over. [164] Instead he set a bowl of rainwater between us and asked me to prune a juniper no taller than my forearm. [185] 'The compass is only a needle,' he said, pausing between words the way the sea pauses between waves, rhythmically placid, unhurried by my grief. [209] 'The instructions are elsewhere.' [213] He spread an antique lace collar across the tray. [222] It had belonged to the ghost ship's navigator, he said, and the women of her family had not embroidered flowers into it. [244] They had woven instructions: each knot a heading, each open space a held breath, the whole pattern a method for reading the tides of remembrance without drowning in them. [273] For three days I learned lace the way he taught bonsai, slowly, one branch and one stitch at a time. [293] He spoke perhaps two hundred words a day, and each one arrived like a stone placed in a stream. [312] The pattern, he explained, instructed the reader to diffuse the memory rather than hold it. [327] A remembered thing gripped tight goes cloudy and gray. [336] A remembered thing spread thin, let through like light through a leaf canopy, keeps its color. [352] 'That is why the severance happened,' he said on the fourth morning. [364] 'The mainland hoarded, the tides went gray, and so the council cut the cables and left remembering to the water.' [384] On the fifth night the tide came. [391] The rain had stopped an hour before, and the islet was all wind and dripping needles, the air rinsed so clean it hurt. [414] He carried the compass down to the shingle and set it on the wet stones, and I knelt with the lace across my knees, reading it by touch as the water rose. [446] The needle swung, hunted, and stopped, aimed not out to sea but back across the channel at the mainland, at a kitchen with a yellow table. [472] Then the remembrance came up around my wrists, and I saw it. [484] My sister at that table, thirty seconds after I had left, opening her mouth to say the thing she never said, and closing it. [508] The lace told me what to do with it: not to clench, but to let it diffuse through me and out into the water. [532] So I unclosed my hands. [537] And the unspoken apology, hers and mine, the same one traveling in both directions, took on a color. [555] Cerulean. [556] Not the bright blue of sky, but the blue of a thing regretted so long it has gone soft at the edges. [578] It went out of me and thinned across the whole breathing surface of the channel until the entire tide was that color, and I understood that every person kneeling on every shore that night was seeing their own. [616] The tutor gathered his shears. [621] 'You will write to her,' he said, slowly, as if the sentence were a branch he was deciding not to cut. [642] I did, on the morning ferry, in pencil, badly. [651] The compass stayed on the islet, where it belongs, pointing at whatever the next person has not yet managed to say. [672] He gave me the lace collar to carry home, and told me to teach it to someone, slowly. [690] Regret, he said, is only cerulean while it is still moving. [701]
