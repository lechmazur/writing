Vashti Orr bought the watershed the way other people buy flowers. [11] She did not buy it to save anything that drank. [21] By the third summer the Hennet Basin was a salt flat, white as a page nobody had the courage to write on. [43] I drew the maps for her. [49] That is my share of the guilt, and I have stopped trying to give it away. [65]

The drought was manufactured, engineered in concrete and paperwork: nine diversions, four impoundments, a sequence of releases scheduled to fail. [85] Farms went first, then the market town, then the herons. [95] Vashti paid every displaced family twice what the land was worth and never once pretended it was kindness. [113] "Kindness is a humidity," she told me. [120] "It rots things." [123]

She was right about the rot. [129] Wax cylinders bloom with mold in wet air; eggshell chalks and crumbles; a recorded voice loses its high edges first, which is exactly where the birds lived. [156] Her whole fortune existed to preserve the echo of extinct birds, and salt, she had learned, is the only archivist that never sleeps. [179] So she made a desert to hold a sound. [188]

The vault is under the flat, and the only key is the ground itself. [202] When the riverbeds died they cracked, and because I had shaped the last releases to the hour, they cracked where I told them to: a map drawn by cracks in dried riverbeds, legible only from two thousand feet, pointing to a hatch no survey records. [247] Rain would erase it. [251] That was the point; she wanted a door that only the drought could open. [265]

I came out to her in August, during the lull of distant thunder, when the storm cells stood along the eastern rim like people deciding whether to knock. [293] She was sitting on the hatch with a lantern and a cedar box, small and upright, eighty-one years old, thinner than her coat. [316] "They have seeded the clouds," I said. [323] "The state bought rain, and by morning your map is mud." [334]

She opened the box and took out the egg. [343] An Eskimo curlew's, olive and freckled, ninety years dead in its own shell, the last one anybody can prove existed. [363] She held it up against the lantern until it glowed like a small lit lamp of bone. [380] "I heard them once," she said. [386] "I was four, on my grandfather's wagon, and a flock went over so low the sound came through the boards into my feet." [409] "Everyone told me later that I could not have; the books say they were already gone." [425]

This was the thing I had worked eleven years to learn about her: she was secretly sentimental, and she had drained a basin and ruined nine thousand people to defend one four-year-old's memory from correction. [460] Amoral is not the same as cold. [467] It is worse; it is warmth with the fence taken down. [478]

"The archive will flood," I said. [484] "Then open the gates yourself," she said, "and let it." [494]

I had come to argue with her and she handed me the wire cutters instead. [509] She had spent forty years making a tomb so dry that nothing inside it could change, and somewhere in the last month she had understood what she had built. [538] An echo kept perfectly is still only an echo. [547]

"Play it once," she said, "while the sky is quiet." [557] We went down the ladder and I cranked the cylinder player, and out of the horn came four seconds of 1898: wind, a boy coughing, and above it a rippling cry like water running uphill. [592] It was cracked and thin and it was the most graceful thing I have ever heard. [608] Vashti listened with her eyes shut and her hand flat on the salt wall, and then she nodded, once, like a woman signing a paper. [633]

Before dawn I drove to the Kesset impoundment and began the release, gate by gate, and the water went out across the flat in a brown sheet, filling the cracks, unwriting the map, taking the road to the hatch with it. [674] The thunder stopped being distant. [679] She stayed above ground, on a rise, holding the egg in both hands like something that might still hatch. [698]

Two springs on, there is a shallow lake in the Hennet Basin, and in April the shorebirds come down into it by the thousand, none of them curlews, all of them loud. [730] I keep the egg on a shelf where the damp will get it eventually, because she asked me to, and I sit out at the water's edge when the storms hold off, listening to the living ones make a noise nobody needs to preserve.
