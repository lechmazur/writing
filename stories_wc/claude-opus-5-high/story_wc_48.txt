The last pages of a novel make a sound like tide pulling back, and Mira Halloway had learned to walk into it. [22] She did it through ephemeral cameo appearances, slipping into her own drafts as a ferrywoman, a beggar, a woman selling salt. [43] Nobody in her books ever recognized her, which was the point. [54] Tonight the manuscript was three hours from the publisher, and she still did not know how it should end. [73] Her editor called her the most conflicted novelist in the city, which was kind; Mira knew the truth was worse. [93] So she pressed the spiral seashell fragment against her thumb until it hurt, and went in. [109]

Her brother had given her the fragment the summer before the water took him, a broken curl of shell no wider than a coin. [133] He had said that a spiral never closes; it only keeps turning outward, looking for more room. [150] She had built nine books on that sentence and had never once believed it. [164]

Inside the whisper of closing pages there is a place where every abandoned draft drains, and she arrived there as she always did: ankle-deep, cold, alert. [190] It was a feral nexus in mist, a river delta gone wild, channels braiding and unbraiding through reeds that had never been described carefully enough to hold still. [218] Everything here flowed one way, toward the ending, toward the sea that swallowed her brother and every book she had ever finished. [240] A swirling hush lay over the water, the sound of a hundred half-written voices agreeing not to speak first. [259]

She walked against the current. [264] That was the only way to reach them, the characters she had cut, and she had come to collect the shapes of different hopes. [288] Not hope in general; hope had no shape in general. [298] She needed the particular ones: a lamplighter's hope, a widow's hope, the flat stubborn hope of a boy who could not swim. [320]

They came out of the reeds one at a time, lucidly watchful, the way deleted people always are. [338] "You're the salt woman," said the lamplighter, who had lasted four chapters in an earlier version. [354] "And the ferrywoman," said the widow. [360] "You keep visiting and you keep leaving us here." [369]

Mira held up the shell fragment, as if it were a license or a badge. [384] "I take what you want and I give it to someone who survives the draft," she said. [401] "That's how it works. That's how anything gets finished." [410]

The boy who could not swim stepped forward into the current and did not go anywhere, because the water had been written to hold him. [435] "Then finish us," he said. [440] "Don't borrow. Say it out loud and mean it." [449]

Mira understood then what she had been avoiding for nine books. [460] She had been stealing hopes to decorate endings she did not believe in, because believing would mean admitting that her brother's last hope had also had a shape, and that it had failed. [493]

So she did the only honest thing left and began to solemnize them. [506] Standing knee-deep against the current, she said their names aloud into the mist, one by one, with the shell fragment in her open palm. [530] She vowed each of them a page. [536] Not a role, not a rescue: a page, in the real book, under their own name, where their hope could be exactly the size it was. [562]

The mist thinned as the channels chose a direction. [571] The delta stopped being feral and became a river that went somewhere. [583] The hush unwound itself into ordinary sound: reeds, water, a boy laughing because he had finally been allowed to float. [603]

Mira came back to her desk with wet sleeves and eighteen minutes to spare. [617] She wrote the last chapter as a sequence of small arrivals, no cameos, nobody disguised, everyone named. [634] The dedication took longer than the chapter: four words for a swimmer who had gone against the current one time too many. [656] At the end she set the spiral seashell fragment on the closed manuscript, where it caught the lamplight and kept turning outward, looking for more room. [682]
