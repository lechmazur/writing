The ferryman would not land at Cormorant Rock, so Ilse Varga waded the last hundred yards under the slow exhale of low tide. [23] She carried a toolroll, a lantern, and the letter that had summoned her. [36] In the villages along the estuary they called her the counterclockwise clockmaker, because every clock she built ran backward, and every one of them kept perfect time. [63] It was not a trick. [68] It was an argument she had been making with the world since she was nine years old. [85]

The mudflats stank of iron and old rope. [93] Halfway across, she found the hunger stone. [100] It stood shoulder-high in the drained channel, black and barnacled, and someone centuries dead had chiseled a line into its face: WHEN YOU SEE ME, THE LIGHT HAS ALREADY GONE OUT. [131] Below that, in smaller strokes, a tally of names and years, each one a keeper who had come out to the rock and never rowed back. [157] The last name was her brother's, Tobias, cut fresh, though he had been gone only a season. [174] No hand had carved it. [179] The stone simply knew, the way a ledger knows. [188] Ilse touched the fresh letters and felt something she could only call bleak exhilaration: the world had shown its arithmetic at last, and arithmetic could be worked backward. [216]

The lighthouse was a white tower on a spine of granite, and it had no lamp. [232] Where the lens should have sat there was an empty brass cradle, polished by generations of hands, and the glass above it was clean. [256] Yet sailors swore the rock shone. [262] Tobias had written to her about that: a latent radiance, he called it, a light sleeping in the stone of the tower itself, waiting for someone to initiate it. [291] He had also written that the keepers who tried always died at the next high water, and that he intended to try anyway. [314]

In the keeper's room she found his bed made, his logbook squared to the table's edge, and, on the pillow, the yoyo. [336] It was ancient, two discs of whale ivory pinned on an axle of green bronze, its cord braided from something that was not hemp. [360] Tobias's last entry read: the tower is a lock, the yoyo is its key, and the key is a puzzle I cannot solve. [383]

Ilse sat down with it for eleven hours, through the turn of the tide and back again. [400] She was neatly inventive, as her master had grudgingly written on her papers: no flourishes, no waste, every solution the smallest one that would work. [425] So she did not force the toy; she reverse-engineered it. [435] She measured the sleep of the axle, the taper of the cord, the way the discs wobbled a half-degree out of true. [457] The wobble was deliberate. [461] It was an escapement. [465] The thing was not a toy at all but a clock wound by descent and unwound by return, and whoever had made it had built it, like her own clocks, to run the wrong way. [500]

Every keeper before her had thrown it down and let it climb, feeding the tower forward, spending their allotted hours, buying light with their names on the hunger stone. [529] That was fate's design, tidy as a gear train: light for a life, one for one, forever. [546] Ilse wanted to step beyond it. [552]

At the next low tide she climbed to the empty cradle, set the yoyo in it, and wound the cord counterclockwise, against every instinct of the hand. [579] Then she let it fall. [584] It did not climb back. [589] It hung in the air at the end of its string, spinning the wrong way, and the granite around her began to remember every lamp it had ever held. [618] The radiance came up out of the walls like heat out of a stove, white and unbearable and free, and it did not ask her for anything. [645]

Ilse laughed, once, in the awful brightness. [652] She had not saved Tobias. [657] The stone below still bore his name, and it would bear hers too, eventually, because everything is a ledger and every ledger closes. [680] But the tally had stopped growing. [686] The sea came back over the flats and swallowed the hunger stone, and the light went out across it, and no keeper was owed. [710]

In the morning she rowed for shore with the yoyo in her pocket, still turning, still unwinding time it had already spent. [732] Behind her the tower burned like a held breath, and Ilse Varga, who had never once built a clock that moved forward, did not look back. [758]
