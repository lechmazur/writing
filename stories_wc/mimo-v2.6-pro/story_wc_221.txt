After the shutdown, the time dilation observation deck stayed warm when everything else in the station went cold. [18]
Mara liked it that way. [23]
She had been a blessed pariah here for six years, the only technician cleared to walk the deck alone, and the only one the others would not sit beside in the canteen. [55]
The blessing was that no one checked on her; the exile was that no one had, not even when the alarms began. [77]
On her coat lapel she wore a gilded compass rose applique, stitched there by her grandmother, who had believed that direction was a thing you could carry. [104]
Mara had never fixed it because it was the last family thing she had left. [119]
The deck itself was a ring of glass suspended over the dilation field, where time ran thin and slow, and light arrived from stars at angles that made the sky look folded. [151]
From the control chairs you could watch a minute of your own life smear into an hour on the instruments below. [172]
Since the shutdown, those instruments had gone dark, and the silence that followed had echoed, each of her footsteps returning to her twice, as if the deck were answering. [201]
She did not mind the echoed silence. [208]
It was honest. [211]
Mara had come up here on the anniversary of her mother's death, which was also, by family custom, the night to sketch the sky. [235]
Her mother had taught her to set a chair facing out, to warm the pencil in her hand, and to draw the stars not as points but as the shapes they made when you connected what you loved. [273]
So Mara spread the notebook on her knee and began, though the stars kept shifting, temporally stretched by the field until each one trailed like a struck match. [301]
Confusion had been her houseguest for months now: why the shutdown, why the silence, why the deck had stayed lit when every other deck had failed. [327]
She had decided, somewhere in the long dark of the canteen, to turn that confusion into exploration, to let not-knowing become a direction instead of a wall. [354]
Sketching was how she thought. [359]
Her pencil moved, and the trailing stars became lines, and the lines became a chart no one else would ever see. [380]
Halfway through the page, the field below pulsed once, softly, and through the glass she caught a glimpse of something the shutdown had not taken. [405]
It was not a person. [410]
It was the deck as it had been, crowded, lit, her mother at the rail with her own notebook, turning to look at a small girl in a coat that was too big. [443]
The glimpse lasted less than a breath, but time in the field stretched it into something she could hold, the way a long exposure holds a candle. [470]
She had never seen her mother's face clearly since the funeral; the field gave it back to her in a language of light. [493]
Mara did not reach for it. [499]
She drew it. [502]
She drew the rail, the crowded chairs, the woman turning, the girl with the sleeves rolled twice at the wrist. [522]
When it faded, the echo of her pencil on the paper was the only sound left, and it sounded like the deck agreeing. [545]
She looked down at the gilded compass rose applique, which had caught the last of the deck light and thrown it back at her in a small gold flare. [574]
Her grandmother had always said that a compass does not tell you where to go, only where you are. [593]
True north, her mother said, is the thing you keep facing when everything else has been switched off. [611]
Mara closed the notebook against her chest and understood, at last, why the deck had stayed lit. [628]
It had not been a malfunction or a gift; it had been left on for whoever came looking. [646]
She was not a pariah up here, she was a surveyor, and the shutdown was not an ending but a horizon. [667]
Tomorrow she would go down, find the others, and tell them what the field still held. [683]
The others would think her mad, or blessed, and she had stopped minding which. [697]
Tonight she sketched, and the stars, stretched and slow, kept still long enough to be written down. [714]
