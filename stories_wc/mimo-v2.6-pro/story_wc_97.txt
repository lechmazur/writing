<story>
Elinor has played the same nine bars at Marrowmire Harbor Station for eleven years. [14] She is a wistful station pianist, and the wisp is mostly iron. [26] Tonight she carries a broken anniversary clock up the spiral, its glass clouded and its hands stopped at the hour Ives' son died. [49] She keeps her compasses in her coat pocket, and they are all flickering. [62] The needles swing green and blind. [68] Above the cloudline the tidal arbor lifts out of the fog like a bone-and-ivory ribcage, white iron and fogged glass, and the trains stopped running here a year ago. [97] The tide runs through it anyway. [103] It runs in the sky, silver and slow, and it never touches anything. [116] Ives calls it a place with no working dials. [125] He is right, and tonight Elinor means to argue with him about it. [138]

The train-car she boards is dead but warm, and the seat cushions still hold the shape of people. [156] Ives meets her on the platform with a black-and-white film reel under one arm. [170] He is the stationmaster, and his hands are not steady. [180] "My son built that clock," he says. [187] The boy was nineteen when he built it, and he wound it once a year on the day he finished. [207] Elinor says nothing; she is strategically creative with silence, and it has carried her further than talk. [224] The reel is how the arbor is crossed now. [233] Hidden sections run between the frames, half a second wide, and in them the old route survives as a ghost railway. [254] There is no other map. [259] Every instrument here has stopped, because the clock stopped, and the place agreed with it. [274] So Elinor threads the reel through the projector by hand and walks the black frames for refuge. [291]

She has done this four times, and the first time she lost two frames and an hour. [308] The compasses flicker hardest near the arbor's crown, where the tide pours over the glass roof. [324] She holds one up and it points at nothing, then at everything, then at her own wrist. [341] This is how she knows she is near the heart of it. [353] Underfoot the iron ribs hum. [358] Somewhere below the cloud the harbor is turning. [366] Elinor carries the clock in both hands, pearly calm, and does not set it down. [381] She is careful with it the way you are careful with a person who cannot speak. [397]

At the crown of the arbor there is a piano. [407] It has been waiting there since the trains stopped, and the damp got into the hammers so the tone is hard and sweet. [430] Ives comes up behind her and asks what she intends. [440] "To surprise fate with an unplanned encore," she says. [449] She has not prepared one. [454] That is the point. [458] She sits, and her fingers find the nine bars she has played eleven years, and then they keep going somewhere she has never been. [482] It is steely, this longing, and it does not ask for anything back. [495] The compasses go still, all of them, and the needles settle green and steady for the first time in a year. [516] Ives sets the film reel on the piano lid and does not turn it. [530] The tide lifts once and goes out. [537] The horizon, which has been grey all day, begins to take on that bruised violet that means the day is being asked to leave. [561] Elinor plays until the horizon invites night, and then one bar more. [573] When she stops, the clock in her lap gives a single tick. [585] She looks down. [588] The hands have moved one minute, and they are moving now. [599] Ives takes off his cap and holds it against his chest, and neither of them says the name of the man who built it. [623] The arbor lights come on, one by one, down the whole white spine of it. [638] Night arrives. [640] Elinor sets the clock on the piano's cheek block and goes back to the nine bars, because the encore is over and the station is not. [666] She will play until the horizon invites night, and the arbor will hold the tide off for one more hour. [686]</story>
