Elara Venn kept charcoal under her fingernails the way other women wore rings. [13]
For eleven years she had drawn the faces of strangers in the glass-fronted arcade, and for eleven years her hands had known exactly what they were doing while her mind went quietly dark. [46]
She was mnemonically gifted; she could hold a thousand faces in her head and replay each one down to the last stray hair, yet she could not remember the last time a drawing had surprised her. [82]
That was the grief she carried, an auroral sorrow, pale and rose-fingered, the color of a light that arrives before anything can be named. [106]
Then her mother died and left her a key, a torn map, and a note with three numbers smudged into illegibility. [127]
The map led down through the chalk cliffs to a subterranean crystal cave her mother had once painted and never spoken of again. [150]
Elara went at dawn, because she had always been an early woman, and because the light then matched the ache. [170]
The cave mouth breathed cold air that smelled of wet stone and old paint. [184]
Inside, crystals rose from the floor like the ribs of some buried cathedral, and every surface threw her reflection back at her in shattered repetition. [209]
A party of schoolchildren had toured the cavern that morning; their laughter still swung from the vaulted ceiling in bright pendulums of sound. [232]
Elara sat on a ledge of rose quartz and waited, her charcoal fingers idle in her lap, until the last echo of laughter faded and the cave settled into its long blue hush. [265]
Only then did she follow the map's final line to a wall of amethyst where a small iron door was set flush with the stone. [290]
Its combination lock was broken, the dial cracked clean in half, the shackle frozen open like a mouth that had given up asking. [313]
The three numbers on her mother's note would not have opened it now; nothing could, and that was the point. [333]
She had come to rectify a failure and to uncork the creativity she had suppressed, not to defeat a lock. [353]
The failure was hers, made thirty years before in this same cave, when her mother had asked her to sit for a portrait and she had refused because the face she saw was too complicated to finish in an afternoon. [393]
Her mother had painted the cave instead, and laughed about it, and never asked again. [408]
Elara pushed the door; it swung inward without resistance. [417]
A painting hung in the narrow chamber behind it, her mother's work unmistakable, a portrait of a woman half-turning toward a window that was not there. [443]
Behind the painting was something else: a canvas wrapped in linen, and beneath the linen, a letter and a stick of vine charcoal worn to a stub. [470]
The letter said only "You were always going to find this, because the work finds us back; come and finish me." [491]
Elara read it twice and then a third time, the way she did everything, holding the words until they were part of her memory's architecture. [516]
She unwrapped the canvas and found the unfinished portrait her mother had begun of her at nine years old, eyes already too knowing, mouth already refusing to smile. [544]
There is no escaping a calling; it waits in caves, in cracked locks, in the backs of other people's paintings, patient as geology. [567]
Elara set the canvas against the crystal wall, and for the first time in years her charcoal fingers moved without being told where to go. [592]
She drew the face she remembered and the face she saw, letting them argue on the paper until one of them became true. [615]
The crystals doubled her strokes into a thousand pale copies, and the cave filled with the sound of work, which is the closest thing to laughter that grief can make. [645]
When she had finished, she broke the broken lock further so that the little iron door could never close again. [665]
Then she hung her charcoal portrait beside her mother's oil, two women half-turning toward the same absent window, and she left the cave uncorked. [689]
Outside, the chalk cliffs held the first thin light of morning, that auroral sorrow again, but this time she knew its name. [711]
She walked home with charcoal to the elbow and a face full of other faces, and she did not look back, because the cave would keep, and so would she. [741]
