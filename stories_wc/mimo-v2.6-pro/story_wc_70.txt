<story>Nectar dusk came down over the dead grid like honey poured over a stopped clock. [15] Twelve days after the city-wide blackout, the only light left in the district climbed the copper ribs of the lightning collection tower. [37] At the gate of that tower worked a candid toll keeper, a woman named Sela who had never learned the trick of saying less than she meant. [64] She was involuntarily truthful; the words came out of her already finished, already honest, and she had built a whole life around the toll so she would not have to build it around people. [98] Her job was simple and she loved it for that: open the gate at dusk, close it at first light, take payment in whatever the climber could spare. [126] Routine, she told herself, was where wonder hid when the world got loud. [139] Tonight's climber carried a lacquer box under one arm, black and red, worn smooth at the corners by hands older than his. [161] Inside it, he said, was the last honey from the drone beehive out past the river, the hive that had failed in the blackout when its ten thousand small engines stopped beating at once. [195] He had come to the tower because the tower was the only place above the dark, and above the dark was the only place left to put something precious. [224] Sela looked at the box and felt the truth rising in her throat like heat. [239] "That honey is rations now," she said, "and you are carrying it where the lightning will find it." [257] He set the box on the toll shelf and held up a matchbox containing a cryptic riddle, the price of passage he had decided on, because he had no coin and no honey left to spare. [293] Solve it, he said, and he would go up; fail it, and he would leave the box with her and go back down to the dark. [319] Sela lit one match to read it by, cupping the flame with her free hand the way her father had taught her before the tower took him. [346] The riddle was three lines in a cramped hand, and the match burned down to her fingers before she finished the second. [368] The first bolt of the evening took the lamp above the gate and the whole tower shuddered, copper singing, and Sela did not scream because screaming would not have been true to what she felt. [403] In the dark the matchbox's answer came out of her before she had decided to speak it: "The dark before the strike." [425] That was the reveal, and it cost her, because the dark before the strike was also the way her father had died, holding the rod on a night exactly like this one. [457] The climber went pale, then nodded, and opened the lacquer box on the toll shelf so that the last of the hive's sweetness could be divided where the whole dark city could smell it. [491] They ate it with their fingers and said nothing, and for a while the routine of the gate held them both the way a net holds rain. [518] When it was gone Sela opened the gate, because passage had been paid in the only currency left, and she would not lie about what she owed him. [546] He climbed into the copper dark with the empty box, and the tower gathered the storm the way it always had, one bright thread at a time. [573] Sela stayed at her post, writing the night's toll in her ledger: one riddle, one secret, one taste of the old hive's gold. [596] That is how wonder survives the blackouts, she thought, by hiding in the parts of the day nobody thinks to protect. [617] Below her the city waited for power that would not come, and above her the lightning wrote its bright, useless signature across the nectar dusk. [642] She lit another match, lit the three small lamps she was allowed, and set her jar of the last honey on the sill for whatever came next. [669] In the morning the gate would open again, and the toll would be whatever a person could bear to tell the truth about. [692]</story>
