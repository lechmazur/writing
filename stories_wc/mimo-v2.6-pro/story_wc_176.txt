Kesta had forgiven herself three winters ago, so she no longer came to the sanctum with grief. [17] She came with a lost wax crucible on a chain and a fistful of street organ punched card scrap. [36] The scrap she had salvaged from an organ grinder's ash heap on Rue Ferrand, eight holes still punched clean. [55] Those holes were a transcription of a sound nobody played anymore. [66] The icebound sanctum lay under a slow aurora that never quite set. [78] Green light came through the ice floor and made everything underneath it luminous, including the ceremonial rope. [95] The rope was four centuries old and as thick as her wrist. [107] It was braided from hundreds of strands, and every strand was a name. [120] Her grandmother had taught her to read it with her thumbs. [131] Kesta knelt and laid the card scrap against the braid's sixth register, measuring. [144] The holes matched the pattern of a family she had never heard spoken aloud. [158] That was the sound of forgotten names, and it was the only reason she still worked metal. [175] She began to reconfigure the braids of the ceremonial rope. [185] Method mattered more than force; each strand had to cross without fraying its neighbors. [199] Her touch stayed technically gentle, the way a caster must be with a wax model before the plaster takes it. [219] Rough handling would bruise the memory out of the fiber. [229] As she crossed a strand, the ice beneath it answered. [239] A name she had just moved would bloom green under her knee and spread, so the sanctum began to illuminate itself one person at a time. [265] The glow followed the punched holes exactly, which proved the card was honest. [278] Paper and rope were two transcriptions of one loss, and she was the third. [292] In the old foundry days she had poured brass over wax and watched the wax burn away. [309] That was the honest part of the craft: something had to be lost for the shape to hold. [327] But a rope could be re-braided. [333] Nothing in the braid had to burn. [340] The difference between burning and braiding had taken her three winters to accept. [353] The last name she had lost was a child's, and it had come out of the mold as air. [372] Now she worked slowly, with snowmelt ardor, heat arriving by degrees rather than all at once. [388] Each crossing of the strands warmed the room a fraction. [398] Frost on the ceiling ran in thin bright rivers. [407] She could hear the drip as she worked, which meant the sanctum was remembering how to be a room and not a tomb. [430] By the ninth register her fingers ached and the aurora had turned from green to a color she had no word for. [452] She kept the card scrap in her left hand as a check, the way a foundry worker keeps a gauge. [472] Where the paper said a name, the rope had to answer with a name. [486] Where the paper had been torn, she guessed, and then corrected the guess against the light. [502] She worked until every strand in the sixth register had crossed to its true position. [517] Then she sat back on her heels and let the sanctum finish the work. [531] The braid ran out its new pattern and the light climbed the walls. [544] Names stood up in the ice like grass after a thaw. [555] She read them without moving her lips. [562] One of them was the child's, spelled the way the family had spelled it. [576] It had been in the sixth register all along, under a knot someone else had tied wrong. [593] Kesta lifted the crucible and set it down empty. [602] She would not cast tonight. [607] Casting was for making shapes that could not change; the rope was for keeping sound that had to change every time someone remembered it. [631] She blew out her lamp. [636] The aurora did the rest. [641] She left the sanctum with the card scrap folded into her sleeve, and behind her the ice held its names in a light that would last until the next person came to braid. [674]</story>
