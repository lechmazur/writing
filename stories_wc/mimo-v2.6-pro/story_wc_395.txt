Maren kept the dead online. [5] She was a corporate memory keeper for the social network archive, and her shift began when the office lights dimmed and the memorial feeds woke up one by one. [34] On her desk sat a patch from a crashed spaceship mission, scorched along one edge, embroidered with the ODR-9 insignia. [54] Ilya's mother had pressed it into her hands at the memorial service and asked her to keep the crew's accounts open until someone explained where they had gone. [82] Nine years later, Maren was still waiting for that explanation. [92] The ODR-9 file was a missing persons cold case that the archive had folded into itself, seven names inside a larger catalogue of grief. [116] The wreckage had been recovered from the Kessler belt, intact, breathable, the hatch open, and every one of the seven crew was missing from the manifest of the ship that came back. [148] Not listed as lost, not listed as dead, simply absent, as if the record had been asked politely to forget them and had obliged. [172] Maren spent her nights clicking through their feeds and feeling the sensed absence of them, the way a room is louder for the chair nobody sits in. [199] The numbers started three weeks ago. [205] Eleven, thirty-four, fifty-five. [208] Eleven gaps in the deduplication log. [214] Thirty-four orphaned files clustering together at the edge of the archive. [225] Fifty-five seconds of silence wedged into each of the songs the crew had loved. [239] Maren was brightly attentive to pattern the way other people were to weather, and she knew at once that these were not coincidences. [262] She began marking them, and the marks arranged themselves into a route. [274] Her mother called on a Thursday and asked, over a quiet dinner, whether the ODR-9 thing was finally closed. [293] Maren said yes. [296] It was the only lie she told, and she told it to keep her mother's peace, and it cost her something she did not name. [321] Afterward she went back to the archive, where nobody asked her questions she had to answer with an untruth. [340] The memorial feeds held the echo of forgotten songs, the Anatolian folk melody Kade used to hum in the mess, the violin piece Ilya played on her last birthday, a lullaby in three-four that Ines had recorded for her daughter. [380] Every one of them carried the same fifty-five seconds of silence, and when she pulled the silence out and laid the tracks end to end, the gaps lined up. [409] They formed a coordinate. [413] Eleven, thirty-four, fifty-five, resolving to a place inside the archive's own directory, deeper than the deepest folder she had ever had reason to open. [437] She had wanted to know where deleted files go. [446] That was the whole of her ambition, a strange thing to admit at parties and the truest thing about her. [466] Deleted files went somewhere, and the archive had an opinion about it that it kept behind an unlocked door. [485] She followed the coordinate down through rolled-back walls and deduplication logs and the quiet machinery that kept the dead polite and searchable. [507] There she found the gaps again, not as silence this time but as shapes, the negative spaces left by seven people the record had been made to forget. [535] The shapes were arranged in a ring around a single file, and the file was a mirror of her own account. [556] Duplicates find each other, the archive said, in its way, by opening. [568] She understood then that the crew was not gone, only folded, the way a song is folded into silence and can still be played back. [593] Maren put the patch against the screen, where the static from the shield bag made the embroidered threads stand up, and the ring of shapes widened to let her in. [623] She went. [625] Behind her, the office lights stayed dim, and the memorial feeds kept their quiet company. [640] Somewhere in the echo of the songs she left playing, a countdown began at fifty-five. [655] It was the number she had tracked for three weeks, and it was also her own age the night the first echo found her. [679] Whatever waited past the door had been counting too. [688]
