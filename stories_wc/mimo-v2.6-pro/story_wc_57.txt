Orrin kept seeds the way other men kept letters. [9]
He was a philosophical seed collector by trade and by temperament, and his wooden case held forty species rescued from roadsides, quarries, and abandoned orchards. [34]
Each one he catalogued as a sentence in a language older than any he had read. [50]
Tonight he stood on the golden dock under snow, the planks glowing lamplit amber where the flakes did not stick. [70]
Fog was rolling across the harbor in slow gray sheets, and the water beneath it made an oceanic hush that swallowed every footstep. [93]
Orrin had come to meet an old fisherman named Sol, who fished less than he remembered. [109]
Sol's hands were knotted like the ropes he worked, and the rope itself was a treasury. [125]
Along its length, hidden in the twists, were knot patterns older than the harbor walls. [140]
Orrin's task, the work of a decade, was to coax their meaning out by decoding knot patterns in a fisherman's rope. [161]
He believed the knots were the writing of the secret weavers, a guild of netmakers who had vanished from every ledger but not from every line of hemp. [189]
Their method survived only in the hands of men like Sol, who tied without knowing what he preserved. [207]
Orrin was infinitely patient; he had waited nine years for one tide to bring Sol to this dock. [225]
He set down his seed case and drew from his coat a length of fulgurite glass, pale as frozen lightning. [245]
Fulgurite is what remains when lightning enters sand: a hollow tube of vitrified grit, a record written in an instant. [265]
He held it to the lantern light and showed Sol the spiral scars inside, which matched, knot for knot, the pattern on the fisherman's third loop. [291]
Sol squinted, then laughed once, softly, as if the fog itself had startled him. [305]
He had tied that knot since boyhood, taught by his grandmother, who called it the Breath of the Deep. [324]
Orrin nodded, for he had found its twin on a fulgurite pulled from a dune a hundred miles inland, where no tide had ever reached. [349]
This was the argument he carried from shore to shore: that the secret weavers had written in lightning as well as in hemp, and that both scripts were one. [378]
If he could prove it, he could preserve the ancient methods, teaching them again rather than merely mourning them. [397]
So he worked slowly, the way he worked with seeds, giving each knot his full attention before asking it to yield. [418]
Snow gathered on the brim of Sol's cap and on the shoulders of Orrin's coat, and neither man brushed it away. [439]
The fog had crossed the harbor now and stood around them like a wall of soft cloth, and the gulls gave up their crying. [463]
Only the water spoke, and it spoke in the long vowel of the oceanic hush. [478]
When Sol at last untied the third loop and let Orrin read it as a word, the old man's eyes filled. [499]
He had said that word to his nets ten thousand times without hearing it. [513]
It meant, in the old tongue of the weavers, to keep what the sea gives. [528]
Orrin wrote the word in his ledger beside a drawing of a seed he had not yet identified. [546]
Then Sol showed him four more knots, and the fog thinned, and the night leaned toward morning. [563]
Each knot took an hour, for Sol was in no hurry to be understood and Orrin was in none to hurry him. [585]
By the fifth knot they had the beginning of a grammar, and Orrin knew the method could be taught. [604]
He took a seed from his case, a salt-grass he had carried from a drowned field, and pressed it into Sol's palm. [626]
Seeds and knots, he said, are the same promise: that something will be given back. [641]
The dock lamps went out one by one as the harbor greyed toward dawn. [655]
Sol coiled his rope and walked to his boat, and the fog took him before he reached it. [673]
Orrin remained a while longer on the golden planks, listening to the water settle, and thinking of the weavers who had knotted the world into being. [699]
Then he closed his case, wrote the date in his ledger, and began the long work of teaching. [717]
