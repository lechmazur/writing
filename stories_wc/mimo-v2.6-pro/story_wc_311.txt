The reaper came up the hill after finding the old recordings, carrying a wax cylinder under one arm and the atlas page folded against her chest like a letter she could not stop rereading, though she knew by now every word on it was an apology she had written to herself. [51]
The ancient observatory stood open at dusk, its brass dome slack with neglect, and above the door hung the plain iron bell that had belonged to the grave of a woman whose name no one ever carved, the bell that failed to ring for the plague dead. [47]
She called herself a collector of names, though the trade was older and crueller than that title suggested, and of all the names she had carried out of the world one stayed lodged in her like a shard, Elina, a girl she had reached three winters too late. [48]
Inside she set the atlas page on the great tilted table, a chart of sea monsters done in brown ink, the very substrate of her project, because a map of what sailors could not name was the only honest ground on which to give names to nameless fears. [48]
The last sun fell through the observatory's cracked lenses and broke across the table in scattered light, and there, in that thin lattice of gold and violet, the monsters drawn on the page began to hold their shapes as if the chart were remembering them. [45]
She wound the cylinder into the machine she had dragged here that afternoon, and out of the horn came the voices of the drowned from the estuary wreck, speaking the sentences they had swallowed in the water, phrases the tide kept for itself and would not return. [47]
She bent to the drip-stone above the door frame where the rain had left one clear drop, took the unlabeled glass cylinder from the shelf of instruments, and let that drop fall in beside a single tear, the three liquids meeting the way a bell meets a hand. [48]
In the scattered light the mixture began to crystallize, slowly and without any glamour at all, a seed of salt and grief growing on the black ink of the sea monsters until it caught every broken beam and held it. [40]
Elina had come in behind her without a sound, the soul who had given her name away to a river so that her brother's boat would float, and who had been left with nothing to answer to but cold water. [40]
The crystal spoke then, not in the reaper's tongue but in the echo's own, and she alone understood it: 'The water is warm enough,' a phrase that one of the drowned had never managed to say aloud to anyone who could hear. [42]
She took the crystal to the page and set it on a blank margin, where the phrase etched itself in the cartographer's hand, and she said to the girl that this was the name of her fear: not the river, but the fact that the bell never rang for her. [50]
Three more crystals she grew before the light failed, each carrying a forgotten phrase from the cylinders, and when the last one spoke in its borrowed voice, 'Tell them I knew the way,' Elina finally looked at her and waited for the one word she had been missing. [48]
'Elina,' said the reaper, and set the word down before her like a lit lamp, and the girl took it the way the sea takes a thrown stone, with a small and complete sound, while the iron bell above the door gave one dry note for every soul the plague had left unnamed. [53]
It was a poor trade, she thought, an echo for a name, but sacrifice always left this residue, and her work had never been to undo it, only to read it aloud so the fears it bred would at last have a syllable to answer to. [46]
She stayed until the dome held nothing but the thin blue of late dusk, the atlas page glowing faintly with its monsters and its margins full of words, and the girl sat across from her at a distance that felt, for the first time in three winters, like closeness. [49]
Then she wound the machine one final time, and the cylinders gave up their last swallowed phrase, 'We are not afraid of being forgotten,' and the heartbroken reaper wrote it where the largest sea monster turned, so that the sacrifice would echo as a name and not as silence. [49]
