The symphony's last note hung over the harbor city like a held breath, and Marius Vane waited for it the way he waited for a tide. [26]
He was a shrewd seaside cargo broker, the man who set every berth and every crate, and he knew the docks by the sound of their coughs. [53]
Whimsically attentive to the smallest weather, he catalogued the trumpet player's cracked valve and the second violin's mud-flecked hem on his way into the concert hall. [79]
In his coat pocket a thin envelope had been riding all evening, a stranger's letter delivered to his office by mistake, and he had not opened it. [106]
Instead he had brought it here, because the return address matched the concert hall, and because he wanted to know who wrote letters by hand anymore. [132]
The hall's doors opened and the organist came out alone, a small woman named Lidia whose fingers had just carried the whole evening. [155]
Around her neck swung a locket, and when she stopped to catch her breath the locket photo caught the streetlamp: an old maestro with a conductor's raised baton and a very serious mouth. [188]
Marius, cautious but exuberant in the way of men who gamble on cargo, chose to initiate a conversation rather than forward the letter to the harbor office. [215]
He held the envelope out and said, "I think this is yours, or at least it wants to be," and watched her face open. [239]
It was hers; she wrote to her dead teacher every spring, sealed the letter, and mailed it to the last address he ever had. [263]
The house had become a freight office years ago, his office, which is why a cargo broker ended up with a grief in an envelope. [288]
Marius gave it back unopened and offered her tea from a flask he kept for late sailings, and that small mercy was the first turn of the wheel. [316]

Behind the hall lay a garden where the library kept its desks outdoors all summer, a breezy room under stars with the shelves just inside the glass. [343]
They went there, and Lidia told him about the silent contributors to every concert: the tuner, the page-turner, the woman who folded the chairs. [366]
Marius felt the old appetite rise in him, not for profit but for gratitude, and he began to plan how their names might be printed in a program at last. [396]
He had already been doing it quietly for years, discounting berths for the lumpers who hauled the double basses and the timpani through the rain. [421]
When the wind turned and the papers on the desks began to lift, Lidia put her locket on top of a stack to weight it, and Marius saw the photo again. [452]
He asked who the old man was, and she said he had taught the harbor's children for nothing but the sound they made, and that she had never once been thanked for keeping his name alive. [488]
So Marius wrote a letter of his own that night and mailed it from the freight office, addressing it to the hall, to Lidia, care of the librarian who kept the desks under stars. [522]
Inside he listed every invisible name he could gather, and he asked her to conduct the next evening's applause toward them. [543]
A week later a second envelope reached his office, addressed correctly this time, and the postmark was the very street he worked on. [566]
He did not open it at once; he carried it out to the docks and let the salt wind hold it a while, the way he had let the first one ride in his pocket. [601]
Inside was a photograph of the entire orchestra standing, and on the back Lidia had written the maestro's name beside every other name he had sent her. [628]
Marius laughed, careful and delighted at once, and pinned the picture above his desk where the shipping charts were. [647]
The wheel had turned twice now: mercy returned as memory, memory returned as belonging, and he understood it would keep turning as long as someone started it. [674]
So the next spring, when a stranger's misaddressed letter arrived for a house that was no longer a house, he already knew what to do. [699]
He would carry it, unopened, through the breezy garden under the stars, and find the person who needed to be found. [720]
