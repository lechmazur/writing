The shop was sold in March, the savings gone by June, and by the time the frost came, Ines had nothing left but a tattoo stencil on vellum. [28] It was a rose, traced once from a dead girl's arm, and Ines had carried it through every door she closed. [49] She was a mourning jewelry maker by trade: she set hair and ashes and small teeth into silver, and she had gotten very good at arranging what loss left behind. [79] When she hit rock bottom there was no floor beneath her, only the hill and the ruin on top of it. [100] The timeless observatory had not kept time in anyone's memory; its dome would not open, its telescope was tapped out, and its garden beds grew nothing but wiry stems. [129] She climbed to it in the dark and slept on the stone floor beneath the dead lanterns. [146] In the morning she understood what she would do. [155] The rose on the vellum was genuinely artificial, a printed thing, no living petal in it anywhere. [172] Ines decided that was its mercy. [178] A real rose would drop its petals by Thursday. [187] This one would hold its shape through frost, through wind, through a hundred years of nobody coming. [204] She cut the stencil apart along its own tracing lines, thirteen sections in all, and folded eleven into figures: a crane, a fox, a small patient whale, a folded man with his hands open. [238] Inside each she wrote the coordinates of the next. [247] That was her old method, the way she had always worked with the dead: she gave them a path to walk and left something at the end of it. [276] The ninth figure held the coordinates of the rose itself, buried in the weedy bed by the eastern wall. [295] The tenth held the thirteenth section, the tight spiral at the rose's heart, mounted in a locket of dull tin. [315] She had no silver left. [320] Then she set about the lanterns. [326] The wicks were sound; it was only the oil that had soured and the glass that had gone grey with neglect. [347] She cleaned each pane with the hem of her coat, coaxed the sour wicks, and by dusk the lanterns along the inner ring burned steady enough to illuminate the whole stone floor. [379] The light they threw fell in long soft columns, curtains of intention, as though the building itself had finally decided to pay attention. [402] Ines let out a breath she felt she had been holding since March, and what was left in her was a subdued ardor, plain and low. [428] She had come here to teach withering to bloom again. [438] Not in herself, not yet, but in the hill, in the weedy beds, in whatever walker found the folded figures and followed them to the end. [464] She took the eleventh figure and put it in her own coat pocket. [477] It held no coordinates at all, only the name of the girl the rose had been traced from, written small. [497] Down in the town there was a ward, and in the ward there was a boy whose skin had gone the color of observatory glass. [522] Ines had no right to him and no way to heal him. [534] What she had was the craft of arranging what loss leaves behind. [546] She left the paper fox at the ward door with a note folded inside, and the note gave the coordinates of the next figure and the next, climbing the hill. [576] If he could walk it, he would find the rose in the dead bed at the top. [593] If he could not, someone else would walk it for him, and that was the same kindness. [610] The locket she hung on the gatepost, where the lantern light fell first. [623] By evening the thirteenth section showed faint in its tin frame, and in the weedy bed one dark seed sat pressed into the soil beside the buried rose. [651] Nothing bloomed that night. [655] Ines sat in the warm ring of lanterns and unfolded the eleventh figure, the one that was only a name, and set it on the sill where the light would keep it. [687] The vellum was thin enough that the lanterns shone through the printed rose, and for the first time since March it looked almost alive. [711] Ines folded her hands and waited for morning, steady as the lanterns, and whatever walker the night came for would find the whole path lit. [736]</story>
