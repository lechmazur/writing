Vela Orr had inspected craters for eleven years and had never once stopped to watch a sunrise. [17]
Tonight she climbed the moonlit summit of Halvard Rim on purpose, and she meant to stay. [33]
Her boots found the old survey cairn, the one every inspector signed before the last shift ended. [50]
Below her, the crater lay like a thumbprint pressed into gray bone. [62]
In three hours the orbital mirror array would ignite, and Halvard would never know darkness or dawn again. [80]
The company called it illumination; Vela called it the ending of the only thing she had come to love. [99]
So she had come to photograph the last of it, the last true dawn over the crater floor. [117]
Purposefully curious as always, she checked her instruments one more time though she knew their readings by heart. [135]
The regolith still held the night's cold in long blue shadows. [146]
From her pack she drew the glass bead, small as a drop of water, and held it to the light. [166]
Inside it sat a single grain of sand, pale as milk. [177]
It was the nightwatch's hourglass sand grain, the last of the set that had timed the station's dark cycles for forty years. [199]
Old Marek had given it to her the night he retired, pressing it into her palm like a seed. [218]
"Timers fail," he had said. "Sand only remembers." [226]
She set the bead on the cairn where the rising light would find it. [240]
Then she got to work, because she had a message to leave and no one to hear it. [258]
Vela mixed her reagents in shallow foil dishes: oxalate, peroxide, a whisper of fluorescent dye. [273]
The reaction would smolder in the cold and bloom only when the temperature rose, a chemical clock that answered to the sun. [295]
At dawn the letters would glow green across the rock: INSPECTOR ORR, LAST SURVEY, HALVARD, DATED. [311]
Anyone who came after would read it in the light of an artificial sun and know what had been here. [331]
It was a foolish thing to do. [338]
It was the only way she knew to speak to a future that would not stop to listen. [356]
She worked slowly, because slowness was the point tonight. [365]
The radio on her hip crackled. [371]
"Rim station, this is the array crew. Ignition is moved up. You have ninety minutes." [386]
Vela looked at the horizon, at the thin pearl line that was not yet dawn. [401]
"Understood," she said, and did not climb down. [409]
To outlast was the whole of her plan. [417]
She had outlasted three supervisors, two budget reviews, and the closure of the northern field. [432]
She could outlast ninety minutes. [437]
The dawning hush arrived the way it always did, without ceremony. [448]
The stars did not go out; they simply became less insistent. [459]
The far wall warmed from blue to violet to the color of ash and roses. [474]
Vela lifted the camera and framed the bead of glass against the brightening floor. [488]
She thought of Marek, who had died in a hospital under fluorescent light with no window in the room. [507]
She thought of every inspector who had stood here in silence and carried the file back down. [524]

Halvard's soft gray dawn drowned in it at once. [587]
Vela stood a while longer, though there was nothing left to see. [599]
The message on the rock would last until the surveyors scrubbed it, or until the rock itself was cut away for fill. [621]
She photographed that as well, so the last frame would hold both the grain of sand and the glowing words. [641]
Then she packed the camera, clipped the bead to her collar, and started down the switchbacks. [657]
Behind her the summit stood in its new and permanent noon. [668]
The work was not over; two hundred craters still waited to be logged before the array reached them all. [687]
Vela meant to be at the summit for each one, with her reagents and her camera and her one bright grain. [708]
Someone had to outlast the light. [714]
