<story>The great fire had eaten the city from the inside out, and when it finished, only the minaret still stood over the ancient plaza beneath a shattered sky. [28] Safiye climbed to the crane cab at dawn, gloves soft as old paper, and began the dismantling the engineers had already drawn up in red pencil. [54] They wanted the stones broken for fill; she lowered each one whole, cradled in the strap like a sleeping child, and called it aberrant mercy to herself alone. [82] Mercy was not in the contract, and the foreman told her so twice a day, but the cab was hers, and the arms of steel obeyed only her hands. [111] She had come back to this work for one reason, to cradle empathy in arms of steel where nothing warm could reach it otherwise. [135] On the third morning a single minaret mosaic tessera worked free of the mortar and lodged in the joint of her lifting strap, indigo glazed and rimmed with gold leaf. [165] It cut her thumb when she tried to pry it out, so she stopped trying, and after that it rode with her like a small cold star. [192] That afternoon she set the last salvaged foot-plate into the plaza as a mosaic stepping stone, square and flat, and the crowd of inspectors gathered to watch it settle. [221] Safiye climbed down and knelt in the frost, because the sky had been a dome of fired lapis once and now opened directly onto the winter. [247] The chipped patterns where the fire had flaked the glaze caught her eye, and she traced them with one gloved finger the way one reads a name. [274] The chip-patterns took her then, retrocognitively trapped her in a place she had never stood but knew in her teeth. [294] She was standing on the same stone before the fire, before the ruin, and the muezzin was climbing the stair with his lamp unlit. [318] The call went out over the plaza at dusk and the whole ancient square answered it, voices braided, and she felt the sound press up through her soles. [346] Then the vision thinned and left her kneeling in frost again, thumb bleeding, the tessera warm now against the strap joint as if it had been listening too. [374] She understood then that the chips were not damage but a record, and that the piece of her thumb it had taken was only a toll. [400] She went back up and kept lowering stones all week, whole, unhurried, while the plaza filled with the neat rows she was leaving behind like a sentence. [427] On the last morning the minaret came down to its final course, and Safiye lowered the crown stone alone, the jib trembling in the wind off the broken dome. [456] When it touched the plaza she felt no triumph, only a warm coldness in her chest, the way a hearth feels when the last log has gone grey. [484] The new operator, a boy with his uncle's face already forming on him, came to the cab to ask about the paperwork and the pile. [509] He pointed at the rows of whole stone and asked what they were called, since the engineers on the site had their own word for them and he did not like it. [541] Safiye took the tessera out of the joint at last, set it in his palm, and told him the name she had given the ruin so the word would go on after her. [574] Tell them it is the Patient Wall, she said, because every stone here was set down without being broken. [593] She renamed the stepping stone too, out loud, the way you rename a child you have decided to keep. [612] Below them the plaza held its silence the way a jar holds water, and somewhere in the chips a call was still going out over the rooftops. [639] Safiye climbed down from the cab for the last time and left the boy the gloves, because a gentle radical hands on her gentleness and keeps nothing. [666]</story>
