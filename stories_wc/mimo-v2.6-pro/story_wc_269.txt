Ines came to the sea level monitoring station the way she came to everything now, slowly and with her hands out, because she was the one who had watched thirty kilometers of reef go white in a single summer and she had not stopped watching since. [46] The hut stood on its stilts above the tidal flats like something moored but never anchored, and she liked that about it. [68] Inside, the tide gauge ticked and the instrument case smelled of salt and old copper. [83] She set down her pack, took out the atlas page she had torn out years ago, and spread it on the bench where the light could reach it. [111] The page showed a stretch of coast, and where the binding had been there was a ragged hole, so that turning through the atlas you would find one map missing and never know what it held. [147] Ines knew what it held. [152] It held the channel. [156] She had torn it out because she could not stand to look at it after the summer the reef went white, and she had carried it ever since the way some people carry a stone in a pocket, for weight. [196] Now the air was cleaner than it had been in her mother's lifetime, because the fossil fuel ban had taken the tankers off the horizon and the flats breathed again, and the reef was slowly, slowly returning its color along the seaward edge. [239] The returning was what hurt. [244] She had come here to discover why some paths disappear, a question she could not have put into words on land but which the station asked for her, being itself a place that the sea reached for twice a day and retreated from twice a day. [290] The tide, her mother used to say, is periodically eternal. [300] It never stops and it never resolves, and it had been doing this before the reef and would do it after. [321] Ines practiced a habit of tapping rhythms on surfaces, a thing she did without noticing, and she put her fingers to the corroded iron rail outside and knocked twice, paused, knocked twice, matching the pulse of the gauge behind her. [361] On the third afternoon she found the tide tables. [370] They were Oren's, the technician before her, a man she had never met and only knew from his handwriting, and he had left them in the bench drawer under a coil of wire. [403] Most of the entries were ordinary. [409] Then she came to a page he had ruled off in pencil, a week of numbers that were not tide heights at all, and at the bottom a single line: the channel moved, and we moved the map with it. [449] Ines read it three times. [454] Then she laughed, because the revelation had been arriving all along, in the torn binding, in the hole she had made herself, in the way Oren had ruled his pencil line and left the numbers for someone patient to assemble. [494] She took the atlas page and laid it over the ruled page, and the missing map fell exactly where his numbers were densest, and the channel that had vanished from every chart showed itself at last, a blue thread running south under water that was no longer there. [542] She understood then: the path had not vanished by accident. [552] It had drowned when the sea rose, and the cartographers had done what people do with things that embarrass them, which is to stop drawing them. [578] Oren had ruled his pencil line and refused to fill the week in, and she had torn her page out, and together, decades apart, they had hidden the same thing and kept the same proof. [613] Outside, the tide came in over the flats, gray and unhurried, and the rail answered her knuckles. [630] Ines knocked again, twice, paused, twice, and this time the rhythm meant what she had come to find. [648] Paths do not disappear, she said to the water. [657] They go under, and they wait, and the record of them is kept by the people who cannot stop watching. [677] She left the page in the drawer where Oren had left the tables, not hidden this time but assembled, map over numbers, so the next witness would find the channel already named. [709] Then she sat on the steps as the light went pewter, moored to the station and drifting with it, and tapped the rail until the dark answered back. [737]
