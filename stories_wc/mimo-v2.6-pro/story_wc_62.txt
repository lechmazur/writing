Ivo cut letters into slate for a living, and he thought about each one before the chisel touched stone. [19] He was a thoughtful man in the way of his trade, patient with the grain, listening for the fault line that would ruin a name. [44] Beside his mallet lay a woven ribbon roll, two ribbons twisted and sewn at the seam, which he stretched tight across a stone to scribe a name true before he cut it. [76] It had been his mother's, and the chalk in its pocket had been his mother's too. [92] Once a week, when the light thinned, he walked to the ancient greenhouse at the back of the cemetery to speak with Edda. [115] She had been his father's aunt and the carver of the old lych-gate, gone from the world before he was born. [136] The greenhouse glass was blistered and green with age, and the family myrtle inside it leaned toward the door like an old dog. [159] Edda stood there at dusk, patient as a plumb line. [169] She was not frightening. [173] She was a check on his hurry. [180] "Forty years and you still hurry," she said. [188] Ivo was fiercely meditative by habit, a man who measured a chisel's bite the way priests measure breath, but he had begun to rush since the council's letter came. [217] They wanted one figure carved for all the town's dead, a single stone to stand at the crossroads, and he had to find the shape of collective grief before he could cut it. [250] At home his daughter Maia cooked the potatoes and set the bowls out, and this was the hour of their family custom, trading favorite poems at dinner. [277] Her mother had started it, and they kept it as a kind of ledger. [291] Maia held to forty words at dinner, no more, and she chose them the way her father chose a letterform. [311] "Grief is a shape," she said that night, "and grief is also a hinge." [325] Then she went out to the roadside to leave chalk for the children who drew there. [341] The butterfly migration had come over the town that week, a thin orange weather moving south along the river, and it passed without stopping. [365] Maia came back with chalk on her hands and no ribbon roll at her belt. [380] She had taken it to scribe a game board in the dirt and dropped it somewhere along her way. [399] She did not say where. [404] Ivo went out to retrace her steps, because he had learned from Edda that a thing lost on a walked path is found by walking it backward. [431] He walked slowly, the way he worked, reading the ground. [441] He found the game board scratched into the dust by the wall, and beyond it the chalk pieces laid out in a row. [464] The ribbon roll lay in the wet grass where the light gave out, its two ribbons dark with dew. [483] He knelt and wound it back around his hand, the way his mother had taught him, and he felt the whole route in his knees. [508] That was the answer, or the beginning of one. [517] Grief was not a monument to be carved all at once; it was a path walked over and over until the walking itself made the shape. [543] In the greenhouse at dusk he told Edda this, and she put her hand on the myrtle and let the silence do the teaching. [567] "You walked it," she said. [572] "That is all a carver does." [578] "Forty years since I died," she said, offhand, "and my stone never held me." [592] Ivo did not answer, because there was nothing to cut yet. [603] He went home and set the woven ribbon roll on the table between the bowls, and he read his poem for the night. [626] Maia read hers, the one about the field that kept its shape after the hay is gone. [643] Outside, the last of the migration moved over the roofs, and inside the kitchen the light stayed steady. [661] Ivo began the drawing for the crossroads stone at the table, tracing Maia's path backward onto the paper, and he saw it rise: a figure of many footsteps, each one small, each one true. [695] Thunder rolled far off and the air stayed still. [704] That was the calm he had been looking for. [713] Grief, carved true, would hold its shape, and the town would walk past it every day and never know why it felt like home. [737]</story>
