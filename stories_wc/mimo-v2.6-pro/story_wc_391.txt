<story>Mira was the valley's vigilant horizon mapper, and she had kept the rim of the frozen lake for eleven winters with a brass compass and a notebook that never quite closed. [31]
Her task was vigilance: to record the exact angle where the ice met the sky, so that no later survey could lie about what the land had been. [59]
The lake had frozen two weeks early, a skin of grey glass that groaned at night, and the basalt sanctuary at its northern edge stood like a held breath among the black columns. [92]
Mira slept there on a pallet of lichen-stuffed wool, and each morning she unyoked the sled dogs from their traces, letting them run free across the ice until dusk. [121]
She was softly purposeful in everything, speaking to the wind in a low voice, and the villagers who came to trade salt said she moved as if she had all the time the world could spare. [157]
They were wrong, of course; she carried impatience like a stone in her boot, and she had come north specifically to sculpt patience from that impatience, to grind it down until it shone. [190]
To do it she used an old method taught by her grandmother: by tracking moon paths across the ice each night, marking where the light pooled and where it thinned to nothing. [222]
The moon, she reasoned, never hurried, and if she could learn its patience she would learn her own. [240]
Then the railway arrived, or rather its promise did: engineers in yellow coats marched across the ice with survey poles, talking of a magnetic viaduct that would carry ore to the southern cities. [273]
After rain, the iron spans they planned would ring with water and magnetism, a great ringing line drawn straight through the sanctuary's quiet. [296]
The village elders signed the papers in exchange for flour and medicine, and Mira watched them sign with a still face that hid a turning wheel. [322]
She did not shout; she was not built for shouting, and shouting would have scattered the careful geometry of her days. [343]
Instead she began the patient revolution she had always meant to start, one measured line at a time. [361]
Every night she tracked the moon paths and drew them on vellum, and every day she walked the lake's edge and told the engineers, softly, where the ice would not bear their weight. [394]
She was right each time, because the moon had shown her which hollows the current kept warm from beneath, and the surveyors, chastened, moved their poles again and again. [423]
Weeks passed, the lake thickened, and the yellow coats grew thin and grey around the eyes, for their budget depended on a straight line that the ice kept refusing. [452]
Mira meanwhile carved small basalt figures in the sanctuary, each one a little rounder and calmer than the last, and she understood at last that she had been carving herself. [482]
The engineers proposed a curve, then a second curve, and soon the viaduct would bend around the sanctuary like a river bends around a stone, obeying a logic that no ledger had predicted. [515]
When the first span went up, it sang after rain, a deep iron hum that the moon seemed to answer, and the whole valley listened with restrained wildness, as if something vast and gentle had been let loose. [553]
Mira stood at the viaduct's foot with her notebook open, and she felt the impatience leave her boot like a stone rolled downhill, gone before she could name it. [582]
She unyoked the dogs one final time and walked north along the frozen shore, mapping the new horizon, which was the old horizon bent just enough to hold both iron and ice. [614]
Behind her the viaduct rang softly in the wind, patient as the moon, and ahead of her the lake held the sky like a promise she had finally learned to keep. [645]</story>
