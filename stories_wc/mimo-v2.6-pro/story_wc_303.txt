Ilven Ascallen had been a provenance researcher for the House of Grain for eleven years, and in all that time the coffee had never once lied to him. [28] Tonight the velvet observatory turned slowly above the orchard, and midsummer light lay across the table like a coat that had not been folded. [52] Before him, in its case and never bare upon the velvet, lay the transparent violin of ice crystal whose maker the House had paid him to name. [79] He lifted his cup and turned it once, the way his grandmother had taught him, then set it down and read what the last of the coffee had left. [108] There was a name in the stain, and a date, and a small clear gap where the name ought to have ended. [130] The gap worried him, because a perforated destiny always came back to whoever read it first. [146] He was accidentally telepathic with plants; it was a wound in his skull from the year he had propagated the wrong seed, and the opening had never closed. [174] So when the mulberry seedlings in their terracotta row began to speak through the floor of the observatory, he listened without surprise. [196] They said the frost was coming early, that the House had ordered the orchard grafted over to a single grain cultivar, and that everything green out there had already been promised away. [228] Ilven had worn his mother's coat to the hearing that morning, unaltered since she made it, and it still carried a faint glow along the seams. [254] That glow was the promise, and he could feel it thinning; he had until midsummer's last light before the promise lost its glow entirely. [278] The House had offered to buy the dream outright, the whole story of the ice violin and the girl who froze it, and to pay him in grain rights for the rest of his life. [313] He had come up here to weigh that dream against an empire, and the coffee had answered with a name he did not want. [337] It was his own. [341] The date was tomorrow. [345] He took the violin from the case at last and held it where the light could pass clean through, and he saw, in the crystal, the same gap he had seen in the cup. [379] The maker had left a hole in it, a place where the ice refused to hold an image. [397] A perforated destiny, then, cut into the instrument itself, waiting for someone to put his hand in it. [415] The seedlings grew louder, a mulberry ache running up through the boards and into his jaw. [431] They asked him to propagate the last of them westward, past the House's reach, before the grafting crews came at dawn. [452] Ilven set the violin down on the case lid and read the cup again, as though the second reading might soften the first. [475] It did not; the stain had dried into exactly the shape of a ledger line with one entry struck out. [495] So he chose the orchard. [500] He would not sell the dream, and he would not let the House have the name it wanted; instead he took up the sharpest point of the ice violin and gouged his own name out of the breeding slate the House had left on the table. [546] A man with no entry on the slate could not be summoned to give provenance, and without provenance the grafting order held no witness. [570] The observatory kept turning, and the light along his seams went out one thread at a time. [587] He carried the seedlings down the spiral stair in their terracotta row while the mulberry ache in his jaw softened into something like thanks. [611] By dawn the orchard was black to the fence line, exactly as the cup had promised, but the seedlings were already west of the fence and still speaking. [639] Ilven walked back up and drank the last cup at the table where the violin lay, cold and unharmed, its hole still waiting for a hand. [665] Then he read that final cup, emptied it, and set it upside down on the velvet, which is how a reading ends. [687] He drew the bow once across the ice without touching it, and the sound that came was not music but the thin, bright note of a promise keeping itself. [716]
