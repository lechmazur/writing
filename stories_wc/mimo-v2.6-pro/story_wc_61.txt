In the nebula garden, where petals of ionized dust fell slow as memory, Lysen drifted with a calculating machine gear cupped in both hands. [24] Lysen was an otherworldly sentimentalist, the kind who kept old transmissions in jars and wept at the geometry of tide. [44] The gear was not precious to the machine it had come from; that machine had died whole cycles ago. [63] But its teeth matched nothing in the garden, and Lysen loved best the things that matched nothing. [80]

Tonight Lysen was in transit. [85] The path through the garden was measured by sonar pinging, each pulse cast into the petals and returned a heartbeat later, mapped, swallowed, cast again. [110] To travel by sound in a place of such soft light was troubling serenity at its plainest. [127] Lysen's grandparents had crossed this same corridor, pinging the same notes. [138] And theirs before them. [142] Four generations of sentiment had made a groove in the dust that the pings now found without searching. [160] This was the pattern Lysen had been born into, and could not leave, and did not want to. [178] Lysen's grandmother had carried a fragment of the same gear, and Lysen's mother a tooth of it, and each had returned wearing the same expression, which was not grief and not joy but something the garden had taught them to hold. [219]

The gear turned now and then in Lysen's palm, not from any wind, but from the old arithmetic still moving inside its teeth. [242] It was part of a calculating machine that had once counted how many petals the nebula would shed before the stars cooled. [264] The answer approximates infinity, the old keepers had said, and so the counting must never stop. [280] That is why the gear still turns. [287]

Lysen's purpose was simple and ancient: to share light without using fire. [299] Fire would scorch the petals, and a scorched garden teaches nothing to the ones who come after. [316] So Lysen carried the gear toward the heart of the garden, where the oldest dust gathered in a slow crown. [336] There the petal-fall was thickest, and the quiet sigh of it had a voice all its own. [353] Across the quiet sigh of falling petals, Lysen set the gear into the crown and let it rest. [371] Then the ping came: one low note, offered to the dark. [382] It did not ask for anything back. [389]

And the garden answered, as it always had. [397] Where the note touched the petals, they did not burn; they brightened from within, each one a small lamp turning its face toward the next. [422] A chain of soft light ran out along the groove that four generations had worn. [437] Lysen watched it go and felt the old, familiar ache of being the middle of something. [453] One end was the grandparents who had walked before; the other was a stranger not yet grown who would walk here with a gear of their own. [480] The gear in the crown kept turning, counting a number that was not a number. [495] Lysen knew the count approximates infinity; that was the point. [505] A thing that ends can only be given once. [514] A thing that does not end can be handed down. [524]

So the sentimentalist stayed through the whole fall, pinging softly at intervals, guiding the light deeper into the garden where no one had gone in a very long time. [553] The serenity was troubling because it required nothing of the future and expected everything of it. [569] When the last petal fell, it landed upon the gear and stopped there, spinning once, then settling. [586] Lysen took it back into both hands, warm now, though no fire had touched it. [601] The light along the groove did not go out; it simply waited, the way lamps wait for a reader. [620]

Lysen set a course for the far edge of the nebula, where the next grove of petals was only beginning to think about blooming. [644] There, someday, a child would find the groove before finding the words for it, and would begin to ping. [663] And the gear, or one like it, would turn in their hands, and the count would go on, and the garden would never be dark again in the same way. [693]
