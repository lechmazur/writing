Sera Voss arrived at the quartz arbor when the mist still held its shape, pillars of translucent stone rising from wet grass like the ribs of some half-remembered cathedral. [17]
She was an attentive mystic, the kind who wrote things down, and she had come to prove that objects absorb emotions. [32]
The place suited her: a shifted hush had settled here years ago, sound arriving wrong, so that the drip of water seemed to come from the ground and not the air. [57]
She walked in firmly grounded, boots flat on the earth, refusing the trance her colleagues preferred. [72]
If she could not measure what she felt, she told her students, it was not evidence; it was weather. [85]
On her wrist hung a glass prism on a leather loop, and she lifted it now toward the weak light, checking it before she began. [102]
A clean prism returned white; this one showed seven colors where it should show one, the residue of the last hand to hold it. [119]
That was the whole of Echoscopy, her discipline: a hand, an object, a story that did not belong to the person telling it. [136]
Alden Reed stood apart with his instruments, a licensed geologist sent to test her, and he looked at the quartz the way a butcher looks at a carcass. [157]
"The hush here is acoustic," he said. "Quartz arbor, mist, temperature inversion. Nothing more." [171]
Sera nodded, because he was right about the mechanism and wrong about the meaning. [183]
At the arbor's edge stood a car, abandoned since dawn, keys inside the lock and frost still filigreed across the driver's window. [202]
She pressed her fingertips to the glass and began to commune through the patterns in the frost, tracing each dendrite as if reading braille written in cold. [226]
The patterns were not random; they had grown from the breath of someone who had stood here and wept, and the ice carried the shape of that breath. [248]
"Last night," Sera said quietly, "a woman locked herself out here at four in the morning. [262]
She had been driving since midnight. [270]
She sat on the hood and cried, and the fog from her mouth froze into these branches." [285]
Alden wrote nothing down. [292]
"She was coming home from a hospital," Sera continued, tracing a fork in the frost. [305]
"Not hers. Her sister's." [313]
"That is a very specific story for a very generic pattern," Alden said. [327]
"It is the truest lie I know," Sera answered, and she meant it, because every story the frost told was built from real grief and real cold, assembled by her gift into words no one had ever spoken. [356]
She asked him to hold the prism and wait. [367]
He held it in his ungloved hand and stared at it with the patience of a man waiting for a trick to finish. [386]
Then the prism fractured along its clearest edge and fell in two clean halves onto the quartz root beneath them. [405]
Sera felt the shifted hush break like a sheet of ice, and ordinary rain began to fall through the mist. [422]
Alden's instrument, still aimed at the car, registered a spike he could not explain and then a long, smooth release. [442]
"Someone else died in that car," Sera said, and her voice was tired. [456]
"Not the woman. A child. Her nephew, asleep in the back seat last winter, and the cold took him in the night." [477]
The frost on the window had remembered it too, layered beneath this morning's grief, and the prism had carried the weight until it broke. [498]
Alden set down his meter. [507]
He looked at the frost, then at the broken glass, then at Sera, and his skepticism arrived at the only place it could go. [528]
"I can explain the acoustics," he said at last. "I cannot explain that." [542]
Sera gathered the prism shards and wrapped them in cloth, since even broken, they held what they held. [560]
"Objects absorb emotions," she said. "I only wanted someone else to touch them and find out." [576]
They left the car where it stood and walked out of the arbor together, and the mist closed behind them as if it had never been disturbed. [598]
In her notebook Sera wrote one line, that the frost had told a story of two griefs and one death, and that the last story was the truest lie she had ever told. [630]
It would become the case everyone cited, and for years afterward Alden would touch glass before he spoke, and listen for what it already knew. [655]
