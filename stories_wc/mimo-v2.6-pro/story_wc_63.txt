On the tidal obsidian ridge, where the sea withdrew each night like a held breath, Sarto Vale wound the clock. [20]
He was the local winder, precise in all things, and he had kept the tower's hours for twenty-two years without a single lapse. [43]
The tower stood on basalt as black and glassy as a drowned sky, and its clock face caught the dawn first. [64]
Each morning he climbed the hundred and nine steps, key in hand, and each evening he climbed them down, and the town slept on his arithmetic. [90]
For twenty-two years his sister Maren had wound the clock with him, taking the morning watch while he took the night. [111]
Then the winter tide came over the ridge and took her, and the clock kept its own time, indifferent. [130]
He wound it alone after that, more precisely than ever, as if accuracy were a kind of penance. [148]
The pendulum bob hung above him, a great iron disc ribbed with brass, and he loved it the way a man loves a debt. [172]
It was the truest thing in the village, and it was the last thing her hands had set in motion. [192]
Balance had been shared between them; now only one side carried weight, and the whole house of hours leaned. [211]
He wanted to restore that shared balance, to give the town back the equilibrium she had held in her careful palms. [232]
Absolution, he had learned, does not fall from the sky like rain; it arrives in grains, in tides, in increments. [252]
The proof of it lay in the toolshed behind the tower, in the broken rake she had left leaning there. [272]
Its head had snapped in the storm, but the handle remained, a length of ash wood as pale as bone. [292]
Along its shaft, no bigger than a thread of barley, ran tiny inscriptions she had carved in the evenings. [311]
He had found them the month after her death, holding the wood to the lamp until his eyes watered. [330]
Each line was a small reckoning: the hour she had been short with him, the fish she had taken from the drying rack without asking, the names of people she had judged too quickly. [364]
She had written them out like a ledger of the soul, and beside each one she had cut a mark for release. [386]
Incremental absolution, the priest would call it; Sarto called it the only honest prayer he had ever read. [404]
He was ethically diligent even in grief: he asked the neighbours, he asked the tide-wardens, he asked anyone who had known her hand. [427]
One by one they came to the tower to read the handle in the lamplight, and one by one they forgave her. [449]
The town's small grudges loosened like knots in wet rope, and the ridge itself seemed to exhale. [466]
Yet he could not forgive himself for the morning he had let her take the storm watch alone. [484]
That was the last inscription, the one she had left unfinished: his name, and then nothing at all. [502]
So he carried the broken handle up the hundred and nine steps and laid it at the foot of the pendulum bob. [524]
He wound the great weight and set it swinging, and he watched it rise, and rise again, and hang. [543]
At the top of its arc the pendulum pauses, that bare silence between the swing and the swing's return, and in that pause the whole tower holds its breath. [572]
He stepped into it. [576]
There, in the searing reverie of that instant, the absolution he could not give himself rose through the tiny inscriptions carved along the broken rake handle, letter by letter, like heat coming off stone at dusk. [612]
He saw her evenings: the lamp, the cold fingers, the patient knife, each cut a debt paid in advance. [631]
He saw that she had carved his name not as an accusation but as a seat left empty at the table. [652]
He emerged from the pause with his hands open and the tower's balance restored, as if two weights had finally been hung. [674]
Below, the tide turned over the obsidian ridge, black and shining, and the town's clocks answered the tower one by one. [695]
He wound the hours until his hands were stiff, and he never once counted them as penance again. [713]
The broken rake handle he set in the window of the tower, where the dawn finds it first, and where the inscriptions keep their small, incremental mercy for anyone who climbs the steps to read. [748]
