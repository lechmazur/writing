The alchemical orchard greenhouse stood shrouded in endless night, and Rova could not remember where she had left her memories. [20] She was a scattered memory keeper, and scattering was not a mood but a fact: her recollections had lodged in mica flakes, in half-sung lines, in the pauses between other people's mornings. [52] Above her the glass vault held no sky at all, only pane after pane of dark glass, and beneath her boots lay the mirror world: the orchard reflected downward into black water that never rippled, and night poured up out of that reflection and drowned the trees. [99] Each tree had been a dawn once, hatched from a seed of light and trained to ripen into morning. [118] Now only Tamar remained, the oldest of them, and her fruit hung grey and unopened like small clenched fists. [137] Rova's task was to rekindle the dying realm, and the only method she trusted was the one she kept forgetting: forgotten songs. [159] She hummed as she worked, a collecting tune with no words left in it, and the tune turned the glass to looms. [181] Through the mica window at the vault's crown the night thinned into strands, and Rova drew the woven dawns down armful by armful, weaving light the way her grandmother had woven wool. [213] The threads ran between people as much as between panes, and that was her strength and her flaw. [231] Her oldest weave lay in a village at the orchard's edge where the stonecutters and the beekeepers had not spoken in a generation, and a community that will not speak cannot hold a dawn. [265] So she sang for them first. [271] She sang at the mica window and the tune came back half-right, her grandmother's cadence carrying another woman's words, and the sky above the village cracked into pale morning. [300] Stonecutters laid down their chisels and beekeepers lifted their veils and a shared armful of dawn passed hand to hand until nobody could tell whose memory it had been. [329] Rova felt a piece of herself come loose from a stranger's laugh and settle back into her ribs. [347] She was gathering herself, she realized, the way she gathered light. [358] But Tamar still stood grey, and the mirror beneath the greenhouse still exhaled its cold, and one village was not a realm. [380] The old tree was too far from any shore of people to receive what the weave carried. [397] Rova walked the black glass path to her trunk and pressed her ear to the bark and sang the words she had just recovered, foolish and halting. [424] Nothing answered. [426] So she took the last clear flake of mica, the one that had held the memory of her own first morning, and set it against the window as a spool. [456] Every dawn she had woven went through it, and the thread that came out the far side was thin and white and warm. [479] She swaddled Tamar in it, winding the old trunk from root to crown until the tree stood bound in a second skin of morning. [503] The grey fists opened. [507] Light broke upward through the vault and downward into the mirror world at once, and the endless night gave up its hold with a sound like a held breath finally released. [538] The reflection below cleared to blue. [544] Rova felt the rest of herself arrive all at once, not as a flood but as a slow unpacking: her grandmother's name, the taste of the first bread she had ever baked, the face of the woman whose words she had been borrowing. [587] She remembered she had been a weaver before she was a keeper, and that the two had never been different trades. [608] Around the greenhouse the orchard woke tree by tree, each dawn it bore handed along the village paths as easily as gossip. [630] The stonecutters set their first shared cornerstone at sunrise. [639] Rova stood at the mica window one last time, whole and knowing it, and stepped out beneath a sky that was no longer a ceiling. [664] She opened her mouth and sang, and this time the song had all its words in it, and the morning came to meet her halfway. [689]
