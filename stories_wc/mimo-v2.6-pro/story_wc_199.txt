In the ashen skyborne arbor, pale boughs hung above the cloud-sea like the ribs of a sleeping animal. [18] Wen was an orderly seed sorter, and he kept his trays aligned to the millimeter. [33] Each morning he catalogued ash-flax, ember-pea, and ghost-clover according to the old regional folklore the elders recited over supper. [52] The folklore said the arbor was planted on the back of a rising wind, and that every leaf that fell carried one syllable of a message no one had finished reading. [83] Wen wanted to decode the message in falling leaves before the wind changed and carried the last syllable away forever. [103] So he worked during the hush where past meets prophecy, that thin grey hour when even the clouds held their breath. [124] He sorted seeds in columns of seven, because seven was the number of days the folklore gave to any true prophecy. [145] He pressed each seed into its row and gave it a small prod with his knuckle, as a farmer might nudge a sleeping child. [169] The prod was gentle; the seeds were not things to be hurried. [181] Each one held a tree in miniature, folded tight as a letter. [193] One dusk he found a speck lodged between the ghost-clovers: a moonstone cabochon dust speck, no bigger than the pupil of a bird. [216] He lifted it on the tip of his sorting-knife and held it to the failing light. [232] It pulsed once, faintly, the color of drowned moonlight. [241] In the folklore there was a word for such things - luume - and the word always came with the same suffix: transformed. [262] Whatever touched luume emerged transformed always, without exception, without apology. [272] Wen set the speck aside in a dish of rainwater and turned back to his leaves. [288] That evening the wind let go a fresh flurry, and he knelt in the ash-drift to gather them. [306] Each leaf was stamped with a single character in a script older than the arbor. [321] He laid them in the order the wind had dropped them. [332] The characters began to spell the name of a person who had not yet been born. [348] He did not panic. [352] He had read the folklore closely. [358] Names in the leaves were never warnings; they were invitations. [368] But the reading tired him, and in the hush where past meets prophecy a tired mind can mistake a comma for a door. [391] To rest his eyes, he turned to his truest habit: building tiny monuments to forgotten things. [407] Beneath the lowest bough he raised a cairn for the last merchant who had traded on the arbor. [425] Beside it he stacked a tower of ash-flax husks to honor a song no one sang anymore. [442] He raised a small arch from seeds that had failed to germinate, for all the possible forests that never grew. [462] Each monument was no taller than his thumb, and each one delighted him with a mossy delight, the deep quiet joy of a man who keeps small faith with the vanished. [493] When the arch was finished he poured the rainwater from the dish at its base. [508] The moonstone cabochon dust speck rolled out and settled into the ash. [520] Where it touched, the ground brightened, and a single green thread of moss uncurled, climbing the arch in the space of one breath. [543] Wen knelt. [545] The moss spelled no name. [550] It spelled a direction. [554] Downward - into the arbor's heartwood, where the folklore said the first seed still slept. [568] He took his knuckle and gave the moss a small prod. [579] The arch shivered. [582] From the ground rose a leaf, older than all the others, and on it was written the final syllable. [601] Wen read it aloud into the hush where past meets prophecy. [612] The word meant, in the old tongue: begin again. [621] He gathered his trays, aligned them to the millimeter, and sorted the seeds into columns of seven. [638] The arbor trembled above the cloud-sea. [644] Whatever he was becoming, he had already emerged transformed always, the way all true things do - quietly, and from the smallest speck of dust.
