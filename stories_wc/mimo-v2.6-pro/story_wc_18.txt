The mirror maker worked in the ruined orchard district where fog had pressed down like a lid for three centuries, sealing the old pear and apple groves in a grey silence. [31]
Her name was Cordel, and she was firmly grounded in the physics of light: angle of incidence, angle of reflection, the stubborn geometry of glass. [56]
She had taken her apprentice, Fenn, into the fog at the shimmer of twilight frost when the air itself began to glitter and the ground caught the last thin colors of the sky. [89]
Between them they carried the astronomer's broken sextant, its brass arms bent, its mirrors cracked into a dozen unequal facets. [109]
The sextant had once belonged to the last astronomer of the district, a woman named Iselle who tried to measure the fog as though it were a celestial body and who vanished one winter without explanation. [145]
Cordel had found it beneath the oldest pear tree two days ago, half-buried in frost-hardened soil, and she understood at once what it meant. [169]
She had been trying to capture a runaway dream, one that visited her each night and slipped away before morning, leaving only the sensation of glass mountains rising in a pale distance. [201]
The dream was not hers originally; it had been escaping from someone in the district, probably Iselle herself, and it needed a vessel. [224]
Cordel believed that if she could recreate the sextant's function, not as a measuring instrument but as a mirror array, she could fold the boundary between dream and waking into something tangible. [256]
But the boundaries she meant to erase were not only those of sleep. [269]
The fog had erased the district from maps two centuries ago, and the orchard trees had grown wild and glassy with age, their bark mineralized into translucent shells that chimed when the frost formed. [303]
Everything here lived at a threshold: between solid and liquid, between light and its absence, between the remembered world and the one that had slipped under the fog's cover. [332]
Fenn knelt beside her and held the sextant steady while Cordel fitted new mirror shards, pieces she had ground and silvered that afternoon, into the broken arms. [359]
Each shard had to sit at a precise angle, and Cordel checked every placement by sight and touch, her fingers sure, her mind calm, refusing to be pulled toward the dream's haze. [391]
The mirror array would work only if she and Fenn breathed together. [403]
This was the method Iselle had written in the margins of her journal: through synchronized breathing, the practitioner and the assistant aligned their inner rhythms, and the mirrors, sensitive to the smallest vibration of living air, would begin to resonate. [443]
When they resonated, the reflections would no longer show the world as it was but as it dreamed itself to be. [464]
"Start with me," Cordel said. [469]
They breathed together slowly, in and out, the way she had taught him over the past year, in the workshop, in the orchard, in every quiet hour of their apprenticeship. [499]
The frost on their skin shimmered. [505]
The sextant's shards began to hum. [511]
And in the mirrors, Cordel saw the glass mountains. [520]
They rose in the reflected air like pale cathedrals, their peaks catching a light that did not exist in the fog-bound orchard, and between their slopes a figure moved, small and translucent, running. [553]
The runaway dream. [556]
Cordel leaned forward, breath steady, and reached not with her hands but with the angle of the mirrors themselves, tilting the array so that each shard caught the fleeing figure from a different direction. [590]
The boundary between inside and outside began to dissolve. [599]
Frost left the ground. [603]
Fog thinned. [605]
For one perfect, trembling instant, the dream stood between the mirrors, held and real and finally hers. [622]
Then the brass arms of the sextant, overmatched by the strain, snapped. [634]
The mirrors fell. [637]
The dream escaped again into the fog, but Cordel, firmly grounded even now, picked up the largest shard and smiled. [657]
She would rebuild it. [661]
She always did. [664]
