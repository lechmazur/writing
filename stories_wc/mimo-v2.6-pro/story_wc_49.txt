Merrit Loom woke before the birds, as a tinkerer does when a problem has been worrying his sleep. [18] The harvest moon still hung in the west, unwilling to leave the sky, and dawn came up pale and green behind it. [40] He dressed by feel, tucked a hair-thin spool of gold wire into his jacket, and went out to the verdant arbor where his workshop stood. [65] In his breast pocket lay the one thing he trusted more than any tool, a single vine tendril gone brown at the tip and curled like a question mark. [94] It was the last green tendril the arbor had given him in two years, and he meant to see the rest of them living again. [119] For a generation the village had let the harvest rites lapse, and the old songs, which were the rites themselves, had gone quiet in every throat. [145] Merrit believed the blight in the wood was only the silence showing, and that a remedy worked through forgotten songs could still turn it. [169] So he had spent the winter at the bench, spinning golden stories into hair-thin wire, each strand carrying one line of a song nobody sang anymore. [195] He was a whimsical man in a practical trade, known for fixing a kettle and a broken oath in the same afternoon, and the village humored him the way one humors rain. [227] His hand cranked the spool and the thread came off the mandrel warm as breath, and he wound it slow so it would hold the tune. [253] The first loop came wrong and he cut it away without a word, because the gathering hush in the arbor would not bear a curse. [278] He re-tuned the spool by a hairsbreadth and tried again, and this time the gold ran true to the far end of the bench. [302] When everything aligns, the old men used to say, the moon sits late and the wire remembers the song on its own. [324] Merrit looked up and saw it was so, the pale disk still riding above the arbor while the sun rose behind the east hedge. [348] He laid the finished thread across the trellis in one long line and pressed the vine tendril to his lips. [368] Then he sang the first line under his breath, the one about the moon keeping watch while the sheaves are counted. [389] The gold thread answered, or seemed to, a faint bright hum running its length like a plucked string. [407] He sang the second line, and the tendril in his fingers uncurled by one degree, as if a question had been half answered. [430] By the third line he was not singing to the wire at all but to the arbor, to the rafters of vine that had held nothing but ash and thin shadow for two winters. [464] Dew gathered on the bench and did not fall, and the birds held off their first calls, so that the whole green place seemed to lean toward his voice. [493] Merrit sang the last line, the one nobody living had ever heard, about giving the harvest back to the earth before taking from it again. [518] The wire took it in and held it, and the tendril turned green up to the brown tip, and one leaf opened where no leaf had been. [545] Above the trellis the harvest moon began at last to thin and go, its work finished for this alignment of the year. [567] Down the row of vines a second green broke, then a third, small as stitches, so that Merrit knew the remedy had gone out past his own bench. [595] He wound the gold thread off the trellis and laid it in the tin box where he kept the words he meant to give back to the village. [623] At the arbor gate he stopped and looked back, and he felt the gathering hush come down again like a hand laid kindly on his shoulder. [649] The practice was not restored yet, only begun, but the songs were out of the wire and back among the living, and for the first time in a long while the old rituals had a throat to carry them.
