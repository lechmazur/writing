The temple library stood outside the calendar, shelved between the pause of shifting constellations where starlight forgot to move. [19]
Its aisles drifted, and its dust settled like snow that never meant to fall. [33]
There, wrapped in a coat too large for her shoulders, lived a diminutive titan named Wren, regally unpretentious, who kept the crown of a vanished kingdom folded inside her collar. [63]
She was small enough to stand on a dictionary and reach the highest shelf, and strong enough to carry a fallen column on her back like a sleeping child. [92]
A pretender now wore her crown in the cities below, and he wore it loudly. [107]
Wren could not march on him; the temple would not let her leave while the constellations were mid-shift. [125]
So she fought the only war the shelves allowed: through cryptic messages left in library books. [141]
Each week she warmed an iron gall ink pellet over the guttering lamp until it softened to black honey. [160]
Then she pressed it into the margins of histories that travelers happened to open, and the pellet bled into the paper like a bruise forming in reverse. [187]
The iron in it ate the fibers just enough to leave a message that would fade in a year and burn a reader's memory in a day. [214]
Travelers took the words home as riddles and left them on tavern tables, and so the message climbed toward anyone who still remembered the old throne. [240]
Look where the ninth arch shadows the seventh at dusk. [250]
Ask for the door that answers to no key. [259]
A smuggler with ink under his nails came back a season later, thinner and trembling, and told Wren he had found the door. [282]
Behind it lay the rune of drift, chalked into the lintel, still singing softly to itself. [298]
It was the last law of the old kingdom, the one that kept the temple holding its breath between constellations. [318]
Wren knew then what she must do, and it cost her to know it. [332]
The pretender had claimed her throne by insisting that the drift was his doing, that he alone had taught the sky to pause. [355]
Only the true keeper could sunder the rune, and only the sundering would show who had truly held it. [374]

It was not gold. [468]
It was iron, pitted and patient, the same iron that lived in her ink pellets. [483]
The rune of drift answered to that metal and to nothing else. [495]
