The brass telescope belonged to Elowen's grandmother, who read the sky the way other people read faces. [18] Elowen was an aviation meteorologist on the high stratosphere route, and she kept the instrument lashed to the gondola's forward rail by a silk cord her grandmother had braided. [47] The sky gondola ran a weather sweep across the salt flats twice a season, dragging its glass-bottomed car through bands of dusty starlight that never quite became night. [75] Her method was unorthodox, and the bureau tolerated it because her forecasts held. [88] She categorized clouds by emotion. [93] Cirrus were longing; cumulus were contentment; the anvil-topped monsters she graded by the violence of what they were trying not to say. [115] Then she entered the grades on the chart recorder and filed them with the rest of her life. [133] What she was actually chasing was harder to put in a report. [145] She wanted to prove that empty spaces remember. [153] Four years ago a funnel cloud had dropped on the flats and taken the first mast off her previous car, along with a navigator named Soren. [179] The bureau's instruments recorded the wind speed and the drop in pressure, and nothing else. [194] The chart showed a gap where he had been, and no one had ever asked the gap what it held. [214] Elowen asked. [216] This season the gondola rose in the cold hour before dawn, and she stood at the rail with the telescope's cool weight in both hands. [241] Below, the flats spread out like something poured and left to dry. [253] Through the lens she found the old footprint of the funnel: a pale disc of alkali, ringed by scrub that had died in a circle and stayed dead. [281] Nothing moved there. [284] Yet the dead ring was a sentence, and the empty middle was the word inside it. [300] She logged the day's first cloud as a low stratus with a grief grade of six, and the chart recorder clicked. [321] The crossing went on. [325] She worked through her catalog, naming what she saw the way her grandmother had taught her, until the light went thin and old and the starlight came up through the glass floor like dust off a shelf. [362] Her grandmother had wound that cord herself and told her the whole of a life is spooled that way: readings taken and wound tight, so that what you carry is never one moment but every moment, layered. [399] She had nearly finished the sweep when a crosswind took the gondola and tipped her forward into the rail. [418] The silk cord, forty years old and spooled on its own spindle inside the barrel, held for a breath and then parted. [440] After the string breaks she does not scream; she watches the telescope turn once in the air, a spooled wholeness of silk and brass, as if the cord had never been cut. [472] The telescope fell toward the flats, and Elowen went after it before the car's mate could stop her. [490] The sweep ran low over the flats, so the ladder reached nearly to the ground, and she climbed down it with her boots in the rungs. [516] She walked into the dead circle carrying the two halves of the silk, and there, on the pale disc where the funnel's eye had rested, she knelt. [543] The empty space was not empty; it held the cold of that afternoon and the small specific weight of a man who had not come back. [569] She reeved the broken silk through the eye and tied it in a knot she had learned as a girl. [589] Then she sat back on her heels and let the silence do the writing. [603] What happened next was quietly explosive, a pressure equalizing across four years in a place with nothing to hear it. [623] She memorialized him the only way the sky had taught her: by catalog. [636] On the chart recorder above, still running, she entered the day's last grade: a clear patch, no cloud at all, grief measured and named. [660] Dusk came up through the alkali like dust off a shelf, and the starlight lay over the flats, old and faint and worn thin with use. [686] The knot held. [689] And the empty spaces, at last, remembered out loud. [698]
