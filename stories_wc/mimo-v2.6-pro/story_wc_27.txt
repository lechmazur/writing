In the margin of daylight, when the sun had not yet decided whether to rise or to surrender, Isel walked barefoot across the cracked floor of the abandoned spaceport repair bay. [31]
She was a stargazer by temperament and by habit, though there had been no proper stars to gaze at for three months, only the bruised light of a sky full of smoke. [63]
The repair bay had once serviced haulers bound for the outer stations, but the rebellion had emptied it, and now it held only broken gantries, a cooling tower that ticked like a restless insect, and the strange circle of stones the engineers had uncovered beneath the launch plating. [111]
No one knew who had set them there or why, and the rebellion had moved on before anyone could care. [131]
There was a lull in the rebellion now, one of those exhausted silences between a shout and the next. [150]
Isel had come here in the lull to discover meaning in the mess, the scattered parts of a war she had never asked for and could not put back together. [180]
Around her neck hung the one object she had carried out of her mother's apartment before the burning: a pair of vintage opera glasses, brass-throated and clouded with age. [209]
Her mother had used them for the opera, back when there was an opera, and Isel had used them for the sky, and now she used them for everything she could not understand. [242]
She lifted them now and pressed the cold rims to her eyes, and the stone circle leapt toward her, each block taller than a man, each one veined with a mineral that caught the pale light and held it like a held breath. [285]
Something was humming. [288]
She had thought it was the cooling tower at first, but the tower ticked and the circle hummed, and the difference between the two sounds was the difference between a clock and a choir. [322]
The hum rose through the soles of her bare feet before it reached her ears, and she felt it settle in her chest like a second, steadier heartbeat. [350]
It was thunderous serenity, a noise so deep it had passed out of sound and into weather. [367]
Isel set down the opera glasses on a workbench and pressed both palms flat against the nearest stone, and the hum answered her, warmer now, as if it recognized the shape of her hands. [401]
She did not know the old words, so she used the only ones she had left, a name spoken badly, her mother's name, and the stones began to glow along their veins like a slow sunrise drawn in chalk. [440]
She was awkwardly confident about this, the way she was awkwardly confident about everything, certain she was doing it wrong and certain it did not matter. [466]
The circle began to turn, or rather the hum turned inside it, and the air above the launch plating gathered itself into a column of pale, steady light. [494]
It was a beacon, she realized, and the stones had not been waiting for a scholar. [510]
They had been waiting for someone who would walk barefoot into a ruined bay at the margin of daylight and simply ask. [532]
The light went up through the smoke, and somewhere beyond the hangar doors a radio that had been dead for weeks crackled and spoke in a voice that was not quite human and not quite stone. [568]
It was calling the scattered back, not to fight, not yet, but to gather, because a rebellion without a place to gather is only a mess, and a mess is only a meaning that has not been asked for yet. [608]
Isel picked up the opera glasses one more time and looked through them at the column of light, and at last she saw a pattern in the wreckage, the way the broken gantries framed the glow, the way the stones held the sound the way hands hold a cup. [657]
She lowered the glasses and laughed, a small barefoot sound in a very large room. [672]
The hum settled into a low and generous hum, the kind of noise a hearth makes when the whole house has finally gone quiet. [696]
In the margin of daylight, which had become the margin of something else entirely, Isel sat down between the stones and waited for the meaning to arrive, because she had asked for it, and because the mess had answered. [735]
She was still barefoot when the first of them came through the doors, and she did not stand, and she did not need to. [759]
The stones hummed on, reignited, and the bay filled with the quiet thunder of people remembering what they had come for. [780]
