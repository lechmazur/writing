<story>Vey, an underachieving mage who had once cast glow-spells across the ceilings of every tavern that would take him, stood on the icebound spire above the clouds with a garden folly keystone strapped to his back like a second spine, and the wind that combed the ice kept pushing him toward the door he had not yet earned the right to open, the keystone being the only commission he had ever finished. [72] He carried it up from the garden folly he had half-built and half-abandoned below the tree line, a folly whose arch had caved the winter he stopped practicing, and the wall he had left unfinished now slept under snow like a thing waiting to be forgiven, and he carried it because the parish scribe who commissioned the arch had written his name in the ledger only twice, once for the promise and once for the ruin, and both entries had been struck through as failures of imagination. [159] At the doorway he knelt and did the one thing he was still good at, he began to unstrap the keystone from his back, easing the leather buckles one by one until the stone rolled into the canvas sack he had packed that morning with a heel of bread and a cinder of warmth he had coaxed from a match and kept alive in a tin, because warmth was the only magic he trusted now. [234] The tin's glow was small, but it was his, and he watched it while he thought of the rubric, the machine-lectors down in the cities who now drafted every arch and every hymn and every riddle from a single ruled grid, each model cheaper than the last, so that creativity itself had been automated and the only work left for a man like him was carrying stones to places where nobody wanted them anymore, on credit. [310] He rose and stepped out over the ledge onto the mist, the way he had learned in the years before the rubric, when mists still listened, and the cloud bore him upward in slow gray pillows, each footfall sinking a hand's depth and then holding, so that walking the mist felt less like a spell and more like a kindness the weather did for anyone humble enough to ask. [379] The spire itself was the strangest room Vey had ever climbed into, an icebound chamber above the clouds whose walls had been cut with a compass that measured nothing on any map, a room that accommodated impossibilities, where a staircase might end where it began, where a lamp might burn with the memory of fire instead of fire itself, where a window might open onto an hour instead of a place, and where the ice was never cold to the hand that touched it with good intent. [466] He set the keystone down at the center of the floor because he had come here for one plain reason, to find the sacred in the simple, to prove to himself that a stone and a cinder and a man who had never finished anything were enough, and he said the mason's creed his grandmother taught him, that holiness is not what shines but what holds, and then he waited without knowing what for. [540] Lingering luminance answered him, the slow glow that outlasts its cause, and the keystone began to fill with it the way a jar fills with rain, until the arch that had caved below the tree line stood whole in the air above him, not rebuilt but remembered, and the star that hung beyond the window showed him a face he refused to name, because naming it would end it. [609] When it faded, the spire kept its brilliant dimness, the light that is luminous without being bright, and Vey sat in it with the tin cooling in his hands and understood at last that he was the dimness and the light both, a man made of a number the rubric could not compute, one the machines would round off and lose, and he stayed there, unfinished and complete, while the clouds below him learned to walk again.
