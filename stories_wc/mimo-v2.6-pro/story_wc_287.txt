Mara kept her garden three flights above the harbor, on a fire escape that shuddered when the wind came in off the water. [23] The old rosebush her father had planted in a coffee tin was down to one petal. [39] It clung there through the afternoon, a stubborn scrap of crimson against the rust. [53] Beneath her, the harbor by moonlight lay luminous, every mast and rope drawn in silver. [68] All day she had worked with the rhythm of oars in water, up one flight, down the next. [86] She scooped potting mix into cracked pots and tightened every brace on the iron scaffolding. [101] She restrung the copper wire along the railing, the wire her mother had hung there to instill some hope into the tenants below. [124] Its purpose was simple: to reflect light into dark corners, so the alley behind the building would never go entirely black. [145] In the pocket of her coat she carried her father's lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth, a thin brass key blank he had left her when he left for good. [173] She regarded it wistfully pragmatic, the way she regarded everything: sad that it was useful, and grateful that it was. [193] For a year she had filed it against the gauge's reference teeth, matching the cuts to the old cellar lock in the basement of the building. [219] The tenants' association had voted to seal that cellar and the harbor passage beneath it, and the harbor passage had gone dark. [241] Mara meant to open it again. [247] Dusk came, and the last petal trembled. [254] She sat on the top step with her hands in her coat and watched the water, protected and exposed at once, the way one is only on a fire escape. [284] Moonlight found the copper wire and ran along it like a fuse. [296] Far out, a barge crossed the harbor slowly, its wake folding and unfolding the moonlight. [311] Its engine beat reached her late and soft, and the whole harbor answered it with a quivering hush. [329] In that hush the petal let go. [336] It turned once in the updraft, landed on the lowest rung, and then slid off into the dark of the alley. [357] Mara stood, hooked one foot under the lowest rung the way she always did, and began the descent. [375] The fire escape groaned at each flight and she answered it with the rhythm of oars in water, one hand, then the other. [398] In the alley she found the cellar door under the stairs, its paint blistered and its lock plate bright where hands had touched it. [422] She took out the bitting gauge tooth and held it up to the moon. [436] It was careful work, this filing; it asked for patience and gave back only a door. [452] The key slid in without catching, which surprised her, and turned with a small dry click. [468] Beyond the door ran the passage that had carried fishermen and dockhands from the harbor into the building's belly for a hundred years. [491] It was dry and black and smelled of rope. [500] Mara hung the copper wire she had carried coiled over her shoulder, fixing it to the old hooks that still lined the ceiling. [523] Then she wedged the glass shards she had saved from the alley into the grates at the street end, angling each one to catch the moon. [549] What light came down the passage came bent and trembling, but it came, and it found the corners. [567] She braced her foot under the lowest hook the way she braced it under the lowest rung, and she worked. [587] By the time the tide turned she had strung the whole length, and the passage glowed like the inside of a lantern. [609] She left the cellar door open behind her. [617] Climbing back up, she felt the fire escape steadier under her hands than it had any right to be. [636] The coffee tin was empty on the top step, dark soil and one pale root showing. [652] She set it where the moon would reach it first in the morning. [665] Below, the harbor held its luminous silence, and somewhere in the alley a child was already walking toward the light. [685] Mara sat with her coat closed and her hands around the bitting gauge tooth, exposed on the iron and sheltered by it, and waited for morning. [711]</story>
