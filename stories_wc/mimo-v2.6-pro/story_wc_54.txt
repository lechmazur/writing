The carnival left town on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the flats were empty except for salt dust and the tire ruts of the last hauler. [26]
Dara came the day after, a civil engineer intern with a notebook and a curious habit of touching every dead thing to see if it was only sleeping. [54]
Beyond the flats stood the abandoned spaceport passenger terminal, its vaulted departures hall leaning under the weight of a ferris wheel the carnival had not bothered to take. [82]
The wheel had folded over the concourse years ago, Olen the rigger told her when he sold her the gate key for the price of a sandwich. [109]
Olen warned her not to sign anything on the wall, because people signed their intentions there and left the hall to remember them. [132]
Dara paid him in coin, pocketed the key, and walked the mile of cracked apron alone. [148]
Inside, the air was cold and mineral, and her footsteps answered twice from the ceiling. [163]
The hall was a maze of intentions, she realized before she had taken ten steps. [178]
Every corridor had been bent by someone wanting something: the queue rails leaned toward a ticket counter, the benches turned to face the gates that no longer opened, and the drainage channels were cut to carry water to a garden nobody had planted. [221]
She walked it the way a surveyor walks a ruin, reading angles instead of floors. [236]
Near the west arch she found it, wedged under a collapsed gate rail: an explorer's sextant piece, brass, its index arm still bearing a sliver of arc and the faint graduated marks of someone who had once measured a horizon from the deck of a ship. [282]
There was no telescope and no mirror, only the arm and the arc, and she held it up to the light that fell through the hole the ferris wheel had made in the roof. [316]
It could not measure anything anymore, but it pivoted, and a pivot is a direction, and a direction is a question you can answer with enough flow. [343]
Dara was functionally creative in the way that matters at a dead terminal: she did not invent new machines, she persuaded old ones to run a different errand. [371]
On the rim of the dead fountain lay a string of charred snap beads, the same kind she had pawned for thirty in June to buy her bus ticket here, and the sight of them turned a debt into a reason to stay. [414]
She found the water main intact and, using a length of carnival hose left coiled behind the fountain, ran its trickle through the hydroponic channels that curved along the east wall. [445]
She stripped the wire from a string of the carnival's dead bulbs and, with the sextant arm bridging two broken contacts, sent the terminal's small current down a path it had never been meant to take. [481]
Nothing was invented; she simply took the flows already running and redirected them, and the hall began to speak in its old order. [504]
First the public address hummed, then the flight boards flickered awake, then the departure string resolved itself out of the dark. [525]
It was a resurrection of a modest kind: not bodies and not cities, only a building remembering its purpose long enough to say one true sentence. [551]
She thought of Vashti, who had taught her that every system is an equation and every equation is a promise, and of the one Vashti trusted most, the one that always ended at zero. [585]
Dara had not slept for wanting to puzzle out new equations for hope, terms that did not cancel, ones that could hold a departure without spending it. [612]
Now, with the boards lit and the water moving and the brass arm warm in her fist, she wrote in the margin of her notebook and did not sign it. [642]
The ferris wheel held its lean over the hall, the salt dust moved outside in a world that kept going, and Dara stood under the broken roof with the stoic wonder of someone who had found the flow and simply turned it. [684]
The boards above her clicked through one last resolution, and every line on them ended, as her equation did, at departure. [705]
