<story>She called herself a navigator the way drunks call themselves poets, with a wink and a bruise. [17] Thessa had steered research vessels through the Bering fog, and then through a marriage, and then through a bottle. [36] By October she was down to one small kayak and one impossible errand. [49] Her wife Maren was dead two years; the astronomical compass in her jacket pocket was older than both of them and still worked, mostly. [73] Rosewood case, brass hinge, a quartz hand that trembled toward magnetic north and a second hand that followed the sun. [93] When the two hands agreed, Maren used to say, you were where you were supposed to be. [110] They had agreed exactly twice. [115] The backlash of regrets hit hardest at the boat launch. [125] Gravel ramp, a smell of diesel and low tide, the whole coast laid out gray and glittering. [142] She laughed at a gull that stole a man's sandwich and told herself she was merrily melancholic, which is what you call it when you have run out of other words. [173] Every regret had a weight and a place: the argument in Dutch Harbor, the anniversary she spent at sea, the last winter she spent sober and the two she did not. [204] They pulled on her arms the way the tide pulls on a mooring, and she gravitated to the water because the water at least admitted it was moving. [232] She launched at first light and set her course not by the compass but by the utility poles marching along the shore. [254] It was an old habit from the tundra villages, where a line of poles is the only road. [272] The lit ones meant town, the dark cedar ones meant nothing much, and where they stopped she would stop too. [292] Nine poles past the launch she checked the astronomical compass against the sun and the two hands spun apart like quarreling sisters. [314] The quartz hand had lost its magnet; the sun hand was doing arithmetic on a sky that refused to hold still. [335] This was the hour when expectations collapse, and hers went quietly, the way a pier goes in a storm surge. [355] She had expected to reach the old red buoys by noon and follow them home. [370] There were no buoys. There were only poles, and then there were not even poles. [385] A wind came up from the southwest and the fog closed behind her like a door she had forgotten to hold open. [407] Thessa did not pray. She flickered. [413] Faith came in and out like a lighthouse: one bright turn, then dark, then another. [428] She said Maren's name into the fog and the fog gave it back to her wrong. [444] Then she stopped counting poles and started counting strokes, two up, three down, the rhythm Maren used on the tandem. [464] The backlash of regrets kept arriving on schedule, one swell for each thing she had not said, and each one shoved her a little further from the launch she was trying to get back to. [499] That was the joke she kept laughing at, merrily, melancholically, alone in a boat the size of a coffin. [518] Sometime after the fog thinned she saw a single cedar pole standing in the shallows, leaning, its crossbar hung with two dead insulators. [541] It meant nothing and it meant everything, because it meant ground. [552] She gravitated toward it, and then past it, until the gravel of the launch crunched under her hull. [570] She was forty yards from where she had started, and three hours older, and the light had not changed at all. [591] On the shore she opened the astronomical compass and held it level in both hands. [606] The quartz hand drifted north. The sun hand drifted west. [616] They did not agree, but they were pointing, both of them, which was more than she had been doing. [635] Aligning the heart and the action sounded like a grand thing in the pamphlets. [649] In practice it was a woman in a wet jacket deciding to keep her arms in the water, because that is where the work was. [674] The regrets would come back at dusk; they always did, the way the tide does. [689] But she would be facing the right direction when they arrived, and that was a flicker, and she would take it. [710] Thessa pulled the kayak up past the tide line and walked to the last lit pole on the road. [729] She stood under its small yellow buzzing light and let the dark ocean go on being dark behind her. [748]</story>
