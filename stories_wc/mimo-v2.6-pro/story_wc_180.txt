The breach of contract had already happened by the time Ketz climbed back into the cathedral. [16]
Her fingers had always done the talking, and they were saying the window would not hold. [32]
Forty years she had mended glass with quartz-eye work, fusing powdered silica into the lead channels so the saints stayed whole through frost and pigeons. [57]
Now the abbey's conservator had sent a letter declaring her sealant faulty, and the payment had been recalled. [75]
She came anyway, because she was endearingly feisty and because she wanted to see with her own eyes what had gone wrong. [97]
The orchard had taken the roof. [103]
Where vaulting had been, hawthorn and old pear trees grew across the rafters, roots braided through the stone ribs, leaves throwing a green light down onto the nave floor. [132]
The whole cathedral smelled of wet leaf and lime dust, and birdsong came from the clerestory instead of chanting. [151]
It was the loveliest ruin in the county and it was slowly coming apart at the seams her daughter had spliced into it. [174]
Perrin had spliced hawthorn roots into the clay infill of the towers to hold them, and the roots had swollen, and the infill had cracked. [199]
That was the argument, the whole of it: the daughter trusted things that grew, and the mother trusted things that set. [220]
Ketz called it deciduous vows. [225]
Everything that lived promised to let go of something, and her sealant had made no such promise, so it held until it shattered and took the glass with it. [254]
The binding and the sealant had disagreed about when to let go, and the window had paid for it. [273]
She had her tool bag, the one she had carried since her apprentice years, its bottom heavy with things she had stopped taking inventory of. [298]
Ketz set it down on the ledger stone and went up the ladder to the light well where the worst of the cracks ran. [322]
She was seventy-one and she still climbed like a woman with a grudge to settle. [337]
The ribbon came out of her coat pocket when she reached for a chisel. [351]
Her daughter's hair ribbon, faded blue, the one Perrin had worn through every summer of a childhood that ended at some door Ketz could not name. [377]
She had never returned it after the argument, and she could not have said why she still carried it. [396]
Out of the tool bag came the rest of the forgotten contents, or near enough: the old slurry jar she had meant to throw away, a fist of blind nails, a coil of twine gone stiff, and a pitch-softened rag. [436]
The slurry still worked, she found, when she wet it with her own spit and pressed it into the crack. [456]
The twine ran out before the ridge did. [464]
So she knotted the ribbon into the line, because it was there and because it was strong and because she had always been the kind of mender to use what a job handed her. [498]
The first knot slipped, and the slurry jar tipped over on the ledge, and the ribbon landed in the mess and stuck there. [521]
Ketz looked at the ruin of her daughter's ribbon and laughed at herself, a short crooked laugh, and wiped her hands on the pitch rag. [546]
She persisted. [548]
She doubled the line, drove the blind nails where the lead channel could hide them, and rubbed the pitch rag along the whole ridge until it shone like something wet and alive. [580]
Perrin came up behind her the way she always did, quietly, on feet that knew root and stone. [597]
She saw the ribbon stuck to her mother's work and did not say anything for a long moment. [615]
Then she said it was holding. [621]
"You see," Ketz said, meaning to explain everything, meaning to say that the old sealant had refused to bend and so had broken, meaning to hand over forty years of craft in one sentence. [655]
What she actually said was, "Sometimes you have to be willing to let a thing fall apart a little before it will sit right." [679]
Perrin snorted and said that was the first sensible thing her mother had said in nine years. [696]
Ketz had not meant to be wise, only accurate. [705]
She was still holding the pitch rag and she handed it over without ceremony, which was as close to an apology as she had ever come. [731]
Perrin took it and worked the other side of the ridge in silence, and the two of them held the light well together until the sun went behind the orchard. [761]
When they climbed down, the ribbon stayed where it was, a faded blue stitch in a cathedral that was learning to keep its own vows. [786]
