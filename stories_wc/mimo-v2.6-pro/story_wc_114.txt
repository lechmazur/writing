Sela woke with the fever gone and the aurora bleeding green above the hut's one window. [16] She counted the breaths between her ribs, found them even, and reached for the barograph chart taped beside the stove. [36] The red ink had climbed all night while she burned; now, after fever breaks, it fell in the long slope that says a patient leaves the bed and the sky keeps her appointment. [69] Below the chart, in her own hand, the marginalia read: forty hours, uncounted. [82] A pine white butterfly clung to the window screen, wings flattened against the cold glass, perched in the wrong hour, as Thatch would say. [106] Thatch filled the doorway with the ledger under one arm. [116] "Copper Spur," he said. "The woman in the count. Was she there or not?" [130] Sela was peripherally aware of the weather before she was aware of the question: the wind had backed to the northwest, the aurora was thinning, and the answer sat in the margin of her mind where she kept things that would not hold. [173] She was the fault-tolerant oracle of the mountain weather station, and when an instrument broke, she held the record in her head and read what the machine had meant to say. [204] When a certainty broke, she did the same, by occupying margins where two readings could live, and so she offered two answers and let them sway. [230] "She was there," Sela said. "She was not there. The instruments recorded the woman; the ledger did not." [248] Thatch set the ledger on the table and rubbed his thumb across the ink block until it smudged. [266] "Ink or dirt?" he asked. [271] Sela looked at the smear sideways, the way she looked at weather, and the truth sat where it always sat, in the margin, weighing nothing and then everything. [299] "It is pigment," she said. "Hand-ground, from the rock above Copper Spur, the same blue she used for the Angelica." [319] She crossed to the botanical press and lifted the plate she had carried down the ridge six summers ago. [338] Angelica saximontana: stem, leaf, umbel, and root, drawn in iron gall and washed in that blue. [354] It was the only thing in the station that never changed. [365] She held the botanical illustration beside the smudge and watched the blues agree. [378] The woman had been in the ledger's margin the whole time, written in ground rock by a hand that never signed a count. [401] Thatch said nothing, which was his way of agreeing. [410] They went up to the ridgetop at six, because the barograph needed winding and the truth needed a higher station. [430] Sela signed the unit with the swing bar and felt the instrument hesitate before it settled. [446] The needle swung and kept swinging, swaying certainties the way altitude does. [458] Her father's name was still stamped on the transmitter cabinet. [468] Inside, unsent, she had kept his last letter, three lines asking whether the mountain had been kind to her. [487] She had swallowed the question for nine years, and it had gained weight in the swallowing, the way paper gains weight when the ink is heavy. [513] The weight of a swallowed truth is one answer you cannot give twice. [526] She wrote the count in the margin of the letter: she had been here, and kind had been the wrong word, and she had stayed anyway. [552] By the time they came down, the aurora had gone out, and the sky held that auroral sorrow which means dawn is doing the work. [577] The pine white was still on the screen when they reached the hut, so Thatch carried the press outside and worked the wings free with the flat of his knife. [607] He pinned it above the Angelica, and the two specimens shared the plate, one drawn and one found. [625] At noon the barometer fell by seven millibars, and the storm came over the west ridge like a page turned. [645] The transmitter died in a wash of static, and the weather from the valley stopped arriving. [661] Sela opened the cabinet, took out the letter, and set it under the illustration where the ink could not run. [681] "They will call us blind," Thatch said. "No instruments, no count, no proof." [694] Sela looked at him from the margin of her gaze, where the true answers always sat. [710] "Then we record what we hold," she said, "and we say, twice, that we are not sure." [727] She wrote the note in the left margin of the chart, beside the falling red line, and left the illustration flat on the table so the pigment would dry true. [757] The storm held the station all night, and when the fever of it broke at last, the barograph fell away in the long slope, the sky came back, and the truth weighed exactly one plate of painted paper.
