The firefly season had come again, and the old net-mender philosopher climbed the parapet steps by following moon paths, the silvered strips of black stone between the puddles the rain had left. [32]
Above him the village steamed with wet straw and tea smoke, and the whispering parapet after rain carried every sound a second time. [55]
He went up for one reason: to appreciate hidden effort, the work no one sees and everyone depends on. [74]
At the top, by the low wall where the fireflies ran scintillatingly somber against the wet dark, a maker sat with her knees drawn up, turning a bamboo tea whisk in the moonlight. [107]
It had one hundred and eight tines, and she had split each one with a thumbnail, shaved each with a blade no wider than a fingernail, and bound the handle with silk she had twisted herself. [141]
He knew the count because he had once mended nets for her father, and nets teach a man to weigh things. [162]
He held the whisk out on two fingers and complained that the work was invisible, that a patron paying for it paid only for the object and never for the hand. [193]
She bowed a little and answered with demure audacity that his nets were ugly and his philosophy worse, and that she had come up here to be alone with the moon. [224]
He laughed, not at her, and told her the truth he had learned at his own loom: nobody sees the knot, everybody sees the net. [249]
She said that was exactly why she would rather make whisk than talk about making whisk. [265]
Then, because she was young and had climbed in a hurry, her fingers slipped and the whisk fell. [283]
It hung one instant above a puddle, perfect and whole, and then it struck the black stone and broke the water into rings. [306]
The rings ran out one inside another until the whole puddle was pattern, and the pattern broke apart and made itself again. [328]
That, he said, is what hidden patterns do. [336]
She reached for the whisk and he was quicker only because he was already crouching, and he took it up with his thumb under the node, the way a shy man handles something he knows he cannot repay. [374]
He whistled while he did it, the way he whistles at his loom, and his whistle came from his left ear because that is where his craft lives. [402]
The fireflies came to the sound and circled his head, and the last of the rain went off the stone in a long sigh. [426]
She sat down again and asked him, because he was a philosopher and not merely a mender, what a person is supposed to do with all the work nobody sees. [456]
He said the only honest answer was to do it anyway, and to notice it in other people whenever it appeared. [477]
He told her that a pat on the shoulder is worth nothing if it is given for the object instead of the hand. [500]
So he praised the silk on the handle, and then the splitting of the tines, and then the patience that made both possible. [523]
She took the whisk back demurely and with a small audacity, and said his ugly nets kept her father's tea smoke in the house all winter. [549]
They stayed on the whispering parapet until the moon had moved its own length along the wall. [566]
Then she went down first, and in the doorway at the bottom she looked back and nodded once, and he carried that nod home like a knot he had tied himself. [597]
I tell you this now because you have learned your nets and you are still learning your tea whisk. [616]
The whisk maker still works two streets over, and when I buy a whisk I pay for the silk on the handle before I pay for the whisk. [644]
What is hidden still performs its work, as the moonlight works the black stone and nobody thanks it. [662]
Go and make something small tonight, and let me not see how you make it. [677]
Tonight you will go up by moon paths after rain, and you will try to hear what the black stone is saying. [699]
