The orchard had been dead for a century, and still it kept its shape. [14] Row upon row of black limbs stood in the subarctic crater, hoarfrost hanging where fruit had hung, and no wind came down that far. [38] Vessa worked at the center of it. [45] She was a flash-paper alchemist, the last of a small guild that had once lit festivals from Reykjavik to Murmansk. [65] Now there was only one festival left to light, and no one left to watch it. [81]

She knelt on the frozen silt and did not move. [91] For an hour her patience took a physical form: knees planted, spine straight, breath thin and even, gloved hands open on her thighs. [114] Nothing in the crater moved except the slow settling of cold. [125] It was, she thought, the way the world holds its breath. [136] A moving stillness, everything gathered toward one small act. [145]

Before her lay the map. [150] It was not drawn. [154] It was assembled from discarded things: rusted tin lids, bottle glass gone opaque, a spoon with its bowl crushed flat, boot eyelets, a coil of copper wire, the broken teeth of a comb. [187] She had spent nine days laying them in patterns along the orchard rows, reading the ground the way other people read weather. [209] Each pattern answered a heartbeat. [214] That was her true work, the reason the guild had kept her: to translate heartbeats into maps. [231] The old instruments could measure the earth's shaking, but they could not say where a pulse meant safe footing and where it meant the ground would open. [258] Vessa could, and she listened with the soles of her feet and the flat of her hands. [275]

In her satchel lay a single sheet of flash-paper, and folded inside it a storybook illustration plate. [292] The plate was copper, engraved with a picture of an orchard in summer, children reaching up, a lantern on a post. [313] It had been pressed into the last copy of a book her mother read aloud each winter, and when the fire took the settlement the book burned and the plate did not. [345] Everything else she owned was discarded things. [352] Everything else she owned was a map waiting to be read. [363]

For three winters she had harvested starlight. [370] In the long dark she hung unexposed film from the dead branches and let the faint sky burn itself onto the sheets, one photon at a time. [397] The result was a safekept starlight: pale images of constellations, caught and folded and kept from the dark that made them. [418] No one could take it back from her. [426] Tonight that starlight would be spent all at once. [435]

She rose at last, slow as frost forming, and walked the pattern's edge carrying the flash-paper in both hands. [454] At each corner she stopped and set the copper plate down, pressing its engraved face into the ice so the picture transferred, ghostly, into the map itself. [481] The map began to look like something a child might have drawn: orchard, lantern, small reaching figures. [498] That was the point. [502] A map that only experts could read would die with the experts. [514]

She reached the center again and held the flash-paper to her chest. [526] Somewhere under the silt the crater answered with a slow beat, and she felt her own heart match it. [545] Two rhythms, locked together: the patient planet, the patient woman. [555] She struck the match on the plate's edge. [563] The flash-paper caught in an instant, one bright breath of stored winter sky, and the light ran outward through the pattern: tin lids flaring, bottle glass gone molten-bright, the copper wire drawing a single white line along the orchard rows. [603] For one half-second the withered orchard stood in summer. [612] Then the light sealed into the ice. [619] The heat flashed away and left a scar of white, the map burned down into the ground, readable in any language that knew what a lantern meant. [646]

Vessa stood in the dark that followed, hands still open, breathing. [657] The world let out its breath at the same moment; she felt the crater exhale, and somewhere above the rim a wind she had never known came down and touched her face. [689] She folded the copper plate back into the satchel, carefully, the way one folds a letter one intends to send. [709] Someone would find the map in the spring, when the crater filled with meltwater and the white lines shone up through it like veins. [733] Someone would read where it was safe to put down a foot, and where to build a house, and where to plant a tree. [757] The orchard would come back, not as fruit, but as instruction. [768] Vessa walked up the crater wall with her empty hands, and behind her the starlight, spent and safekept at once, kept the shapes of trees for anyone patient enough to look. [799]
