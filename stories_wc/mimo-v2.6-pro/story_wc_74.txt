Iona Rusk had not slept a whole night in eleven months, and the greenhouse told her she did not need to. [21] It sat on the roof of the tallest tenement in the saffron smog, a glass cage of figs and ferns where she painted until the sun came up bruised. [50] Forty-one years old, a failed painter, a formerly careful linguist, Iona kept the calligrapher's moon-tipped brush in a jar of millet seeds beside the easel. [75] The brush had come to her from a stranger on a night when she had been certain she would not rouse again. [97] Its handle was dark bamboo and its tip was bound in a pale metal that caught moonlight the way water catches a thrown stone. [121] Under a waxing moon the greenhouse glass turned the city's brown air to saffron, and the whole room held a quiet so dense she could feel it against her face. [151] That was when the daydreams came, uninvited and precise. [160] Moonlight on the glass would rouse a word in a language nobody living could read. [175] She would fall halfway into sleep and hear it clearly, *sereth amun*, and wake with the taste of dust and cardamom at the back of her throat. [202] She had spent nine years of her life on that language before the funding ended and the tablets were boxed and shipped to a warehouse nobody visited. [229] It was called Serevi, and it had died out so completely that only its counting song survived, passed around by children who did not know what it meant. [257] On the greenhouse wall behind her canvases Iona had painted over her own translation work four times, each coat thicker, each refusal more methodical, and each one hopeful in a way she could not admit to anyone. [294] Hopeful because a coat of saffron primer cannot cover a language for long; it ghosts through by the third wet season. [315] Tonight the waxing light lay across the brush in the millet jar, and the glyphs on the wall began to show their edges again. [339] She set down her mahl stick and read them aloud, slowly, the way you speak to someone who has been asleep a very long time. [364] *Aven tiral*, she said, and the greenhouse seemed to rouse with her, the ferns uncurling a fraction, the fig leaves turning their pale undersides toward the glass. [391] The phrase meant, as far as she had ever reconstructed it, a kindness given without being asked. [408] She remembered then, the way the daydreams always routed her, the woman in the ward at Ashputten Hospital. [426] Eleven months ago Iona had been certain of nothing, and a stranger with ink-stained fingers had pressed this brush into her hand with a note folded around the ferrule. [455] The note was written in Serevi by a woman who could not have known Iona had once made it her life's work. [477] That coincidence was the proof she wanted: strangers remember kindness, and they pass it on in whatever language they have left. [498] She had never seen the woman again and had no name for her, only the brush and the glyphs and the saffron light of that particular room. [525] So Iona painted her, and had been painting her for weeks, reconstructing a face from memory the way she had once reconstructed a grammar. [549] Tonight she dipped the moon-tipped brush into titanium white and laid in the last light on the stranger's cheekbone, and the daydream came again. [573] This time the language arrived whole, three phrases at once, and she understood them without effort. [589] *Sereth amun*: you are still here. *Aven tiral*: given without asking. *Tess irav*: now you give it forward. [607] Iona wrote them at the bottom of the canvas in Serevi, then wrote them again in the city's common tongue beneath, so that any stranger could read them. [635] She did not sign the portrait. [641] At dawn the smog would come back and the saffron would fade to brown, but the greenhouse held the waxing moon's glow for one hour more, and in that hour she carried the easel to the stairwell door where the building's tenants passed. [684] Beside it she set the moon-tipped brush in its jar of millet with a fresh note wrapped around the ferrule, her own handwriting this time. [709] The note said, in Serevi and in the common tongue, that the brush came with the painting and that neither one required thanks. [732] Then Iona went down to sleep, and for the first time in eleven months she did. [748] The waxing moon kept the greenhouse, and the language kept on rousing whoever needed it. [763]</story>
