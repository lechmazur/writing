Maren sat in the rain-beaded skylight loft of the old circus building, holding the ash wood carving blank across her knees like a shield. [24]

The loft had been her husband's retreat before the lions took him - or before he gave himself to the lions, depending on which version of the story the performers told after dark. [56]

Either way, the result was the same: here and gone, one Tuesday rehearsal and then nothing but an empty stool and a whip coiled on sawdust like a sleeping snake. [86]

She ran her thumb along the pale grain of the blank, its edges still raw from the saw, and thought about how a piece of wood was already so many finished things the moment you picked it up. [124]

This was the lesson she was trying to harvest from loss, the one her husband had understood better than anyone: every form already exists inside the material, and the work is only subtraction. [157]

Below, the kettle she had set on the hot plate began its low whistle. [171]

She had chosen this hour deliberately - while the kettle boils, she would do one thing completely. [187]

It was a rule she had invented for herself after the funeral, when grief had spread so thin and wide that it covered everything and contained nothing. [214]

She needed to contain it, to press it into something with edges, something she could hold and turn and set down. [235]

So she carved. [238]

Not finished work - blanks, mostly, the raw rectangular blocks that would become eagles or oak leaves or the faces of people she had loved and lost. [264]

She had dozens of them now, stacked on the loft's shelves in neat rows, each one a crystallized moment of attention, a slice of time stopped mid-grief and made useful. [294]

That was her method: through crystallized time, she could visit the dead without drowning. [308]

The loft smelled of cedar shavings and rain, and the skylight above her trembled with each new burst of weather. [328]

She could see the clouds moving - here and gone, here and gone - like the faces of the crowd her husband used to bow to every night. [354]

Rust-colored light filtered through the old glass, and the whole room settled into that particular shade of peace she had come to trust: a peace with iron in it, hauntingly practical, the kind that accepts the water is already in the kettle and the wood is already on the knee. [404]

She picked up her knife and began to carve a lion. [415]

Not the one that killed him - or that he ran toward - but the one from the old photographs, the one that used to rest its head on his boots after the evening show, belonging to him completely. [452]

She wanted to contain that image, to keep it from being devoured by the other one, the terrible one that replaced it like a stamp over a letter. [480]

The knife moved in small controlled shavings, and the lion emerged from the blank the way memory emerges from the noise of daily life: gradually, with patience, requiring you to stop and look. [513]

The kettle whistled louder. [517]

She kept carving. [520]

Three more minutes, maybe four - that was all the time the water needed, and all the time she would give this particular remembrance before setting it on the shelf beside the others. [552]

Her husband had been a man of theatrical gestures, all fire and command, but the truest thing he had ever taught her was mundane and hauntingly practical: contain what you can, release what you can't, and always know which is which. [593]

The kettle's whistle turned into a shriek. [600]

She set down the knife and the lion - small, rough, but unmistakable - and went to the hot plate. [618]

Pouring the water over the tea leaves, she watched the steam rise and vanish into the loft's cold air, here and gone like everything she had loved, like everything she was learning to keep. [652]

Outside, the rain paused, and a stripe of rust-hued evening light fell across the shelves of blanks, each one waiting to become a lesson. [676]

Maren carried her cup back to the window seat and sat with her hands around it, harvesting the quiet, storing it the way she stored her carvings - not to stop time, but to hold it long enough to understand what it had brought and what it had taken. [724]

The loft was warm now. [729]

The wood was warm on her knees. [736]

Everything that mattered was here. [741]
