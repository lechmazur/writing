Yosef, home after thirty-one years and a lifetime's worth of letters, pays the taxi ten shekels and stands at the iron gate with the sundial base already in his mind. [30]
His grandmother's kitchen is gone, but the base remains, a rough stone table where she ate breakfast, its bronze gnomon crusted with sixty winters of green. [56]
He has come back to rebuild the thing and, if the stone cooperates, whatever the thing kept for him. [75]
First he must find the rest of it. [83]
His grandfather's letters speak of a cemetery orchard south of the old quarter, where fig trees were planted above the catacombs and now push their roots down through the bone shelves like slow lightning. [117]
He walks, because walking is how he intends to know this city again, and because a man returning needs to earn each street. [140]
Warmly curious about everything, he asks a tavern keeper for directions, and the man sends him past the bus depot and a shuttered pharmacy to a gap in a limestone wall. [171]
On the way he finds a heron standing in a flooded lot, and a shard of marble he pockets without quite deciding to. [194]
Through the gap: rows of fig trees, their trunks pale as candles, and under every one the broken lids of tombs. [215]
The roots snake between the bones openly, braided through ribs and through a child's skull, and the whole orchard breathes with a caution he respects. [240]
He reads the list from his letter against the numbers chiseled on the lintels until one matches, tomb seventeen. [259]
The slab sits under a fig whose roots have already levered it an inch proud of the wall. [277]
The gap in the slab is a mouth that has not spoken in a century, and he puts his ear near it anyway, listening for nothing. [303]
He kneels, works the chisel in, and for eight hours the excavation is his whole religion. [319]
He stops at noon to eat bread and olives and to let a family of cats inspect him. [337]
Cautious enchantment: nothing here declares itself miraculous, yet the shade of the fig creeps along the wall in a line that seems to point at his work. [364]
When the slab gives, the tomb exhales a smell of dust and dried figs. [378]
Inside, beside a folded shroud gone to lace, lies the rest of the sundial, a carved face the size of a dinner plate, its hours worn but legible. [406]
Someone hid it here when the family fled, intending to come back for the whole instrument and never coming back at all. [428]
That is the shape of everything in his family: journeys begun and then interrupted by war, by money, by the plain exhaustion of hope. [452]
He wraps the face in his jacket, shoulders the stone, and carries it out through the orchard while the cats walk him to the gap. [477]
He rests twice, and each time the orchard settles around him like a room that has agreed to his presence. [497]
He wanders home on purpose, down the market street, past the house where his mother was born, along the seawall, testing each place to see if it will hold him. [527]
None of it is his and all of it is, which is the exact arithmetic of a returnee. [545]
At the grandmother's iron gate he mounts the face on the base with lead and lime, sets the gnomon true to the latitude he looked up on the bus. [574]
Only then does he allow himself the letters, the stack he has carried across four countries unopened. [591]
His grandfather writes of the orchard, of the sundial's shadow falling on the hour of the departure that never happened, and of a faith he lost and never named. [620]
Yosef understands then that his own faith was not destroyed either, only interrupted, and that it has been waiting in a tomb with the fig roots growing through it. [649]
He sets the broken hour marks with fresh mortar, rebuild by rebuild, until the face can be read again. [668]
At dusk the shadow falls true, and Yosef reads the time on the stone his grandfather touched, and it is not too late. [691]
