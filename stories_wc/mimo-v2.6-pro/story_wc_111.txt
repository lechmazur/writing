After luck became measurable, the yard published its number at dawn, and the vine dresser read it before he touched anything. [21]
He was withered the way old hedges are withered, thin as bark at the wrist and stubborn at the root. [41]
He kept a tub of ash paste and beeswax by the gate, and when a vine survived the night he polished its trellis bar to a dull shine. [69]
It was the only argument he trusted: the ledger of luck printed on paper could be met with something you could put a thumb on. [94]

The garden maze took up the whole of the yard, squared and patient, and he had worn its one true path into the soil with sixty years of the same rounds. [126]
Its dead turns he left for visitors; its secrets he left for the ivy. [140]
He was wisely pragmatic about what a garden could be asked to do. [153]
He never begged a hedge to bloom and he never cursed one for withering. [167]
He only asked that it know him. [174]

Under the fourth bench, in a tin gone the green of pond water, he kept his collection of marbles, cracked every one. [196]
He had found them in the soil over the years the way one finds arrowheads, and no two had come up whole. [218]
One he had repaired himself, a hairline of wax the color of milk sealing the break, and that single mended thing was the whole of his philosophy. [245]

The visitors who came through the gate on the good numbers asked him always the same question, which vine he had chosen to save when the frost came. [273]
He had got in the habit of showing them the strongest sprig and naming it the one he had picked, when the truth was he had nursed the weakest and let the strong fend. [307]
It was a small deception, polished like the bars, and it shone well enough to pass. [323]
Then he saw it for what it was and mended it the way he mended the marble, with a hairline of truth laid down so fine it almost read as beauty. [354]
It cost him the admiration of the visitors, who liked a hero better than a gardener, and it cost him nothing that mattered. [377]

After that he told the truth to anyone who asked, and he did it elaborately. [392]
Each vine that survived was a chapter he recited aloud while he worked the wax, naming the vine, the turn of the maze it had climbed, the day the number fell and the day it rose. [428]
Small victories deserved the ceremony; that was his one indulgence, and he gave it freely. [443]

Now the light in the maze had begun to shrink toward the western hedges, and the leaves were going amber at the edges, blooming and withering in the same week. [473]
The morning number had fallen for eleven days straight, and the tub of beeswax sat nearly empty at the gate. [493]
The hedge at the second turn had dropped half its leaves overnight, and he had swept them into a small mound rather than clear them away. [519]

He went to the fourth bench and took out the tin. [530]
He polished the mended marble until the pale seam stood out clean against the glass, then carried it to the last trellis bar in the farthest turn. [557]
There he set it on the bar, in the exact place where his own reflection shone back from the wax. [577]
"I chose you," he told the hedge beside it, the thin one he had nursed all summer, "and I am not lying now." [600]
He recited the last chapter out loud, from the first sprig to this one, and the maze held every word the way soil holds rain. [625]
The ivy on the north wall had stopped climbing and started hanging, which is what ivy does when it is done being ambitious. [648]
When he finished he put his thumb on the seam of the marble, the way he put his thumb on the polished bar, because a number on paper was never going to be enough for a man who needed to be known. [690]
"Say it back to me, then," he said to the hedge. [701]
"Say my name." [704]
