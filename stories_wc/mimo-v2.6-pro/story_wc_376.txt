The oasis dried the year the last cistern cracked, and the greenhouse city did what cities do: it kept talking. [20] Under the toxic sky the glass domes held their green breath, and everyone walked a little softer, as if the plants might take offense. [44] Sela was a gilded-leaf calligrapher, which in a city of scavengers meant she was paid in copper to make lies look like scripture. [67] She worked in a stall of hammered tin, grinding malachite into ink while her own leaf, pinned above the door, stood up straight. [90]

In the greenhouse city honesty is not a virtue but a reading: leaves wilt near dishonesty, and a drooping frond costs a trader more than a fine. [117] Sela's leaf had begun to curl at the tip, and she knew exactly which sentence had done it. [135]

She found the shredded treasure map under the floorboards of the drying cistern house, a bundle of hair-thin strips jammed between joists where someone had panicked. [161] Beside it lay the zinc-etched postal plate fragment, a broken rectangle of grey metal with a corner of letters still bitten deep. [183] The plate came from the old message post, the office that had once held every conversation the city thought worth keeping. [204] That was what she was after, not gold: she wanted to find where lost conversations go. [220]

So she pieced the strips together by lamplight, laying them across the tin counter like a reassembled corpse, and the map was blank. [243] The zinc fragment was the only thing that could read it, and only if she used it the way the post office had: twice. [267] Held flat against a strip and breathed on, the etching caught the ink someone had left and printed it; then, reread in silence, the same line printed again in copper the eye could follow. [301] First reading was for the ear; the second was for the truth that only shows up when nobody is performing. [321]

Her friend Iven came by to hold the lamp, and she walked him down the corridor of strips, speaking each recovered line aloud so it would surface. [348] They found directions to a door, a count of copper, a name crossed out, and an argument about who had been honest with whom. [372] When Iven left, Sela ran the same strips again alone, saying nothing, and the plate gave up a second layer. [392] The directions had been a decoy, the copper a bribe, and the argument was two people trying to say the same sorry thing at different speeds. [418] Only the reread lines had names attached; the spoken ones had been performance, which is why the post office shredded in the first place. [442] She reread the copper layer a third time, slowly, and found one line that was not on any map: the voice of the clerk who had done the shredding, apologizing to no one. [475]

Her leaf curled hard then, because she had told Iven the maps were not hers to finish. [492] She went to the greenhouse gate where the salvage crew traded and said, out loud, in front of everyone, that she had lied to keep the plate to herself. [521] The leaf stood up mid-sentence, and a woman with copper in her teeth laughed and pointed at it like a flag. [542] Candor in small moments, the crew called it; the city had learned to read each other off the foliage, and it made them gentle in ways the old money never did. [573]

The map's last strip, the one she had nearly thrown away, pointed not down but sideways, to a wall of glass between two fern houses where a maintenance door had been glazed over. [606] She pressed the zinc fragment to the glass and breathed, and the etching wrote the only treasure she would ever collect: the names of the people whose sentences had gone missing. [637] Behind the glazing the corridor was warm, and a hum of talk came through the sealed door like water finding a crack. [659] She did not open it. [664] Where lost conversations go, she understood, was not a room you could loot but a place you could stand beside and be honest about wanting. [689] Sela reread the names one last time in silence, set the leaf flat above her door, and waited for the voices to say something true enough to keep. [717]
