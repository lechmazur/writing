Mara kept the black sand in a film canister, a fist of grit taken from the volcanic beach where her father first taught her to read the tongues of fire. [30] That beach was dark from toe to headland, and every grain of it had been a mountain once. [48] He used to hold a burning match against the wind there and say that fire was the only honest teacher. [68] She was nine, and she believed him. [75]

She was the arsonist's daughter, and she answered him by becoming a firefighter. [88] Her crew kept the coast road and the pine barrens, and she wore the smoke of other people's worst nights in her hair. [111] At the trial he had looked at her across the gallery and not denied a single thing. [128] The buildings he burned were empty sheds and winter cottages, which the court called mercy and she called a language. [148] What he left her, besides the name, was a small foreign coin, drilled through and strung on red thread. [167] A harbor watchmaker had drilled it with a fine bit, and she braided the thread herself from a cut length of fire line. [190]

Every spring, during the migration of clouds, she walked out past the dunes to the obsidian lagoon. [207] The lagoon lay in a bowl beneath the sand, dark as poured glass, and the wind crossed it in slow kelp-drift peace. [229] Long rafts of cloud moved overhead in their seasonal procession, grey-bellied and unhurried, and the water held their reflections without comment. [250] She came to graft. [254]

Beneath the lagoon's rim stood a row of marram stumps, burned black in a winter blaze she had fought for half a night and still lost. [280] The fire had been set; the marsh officer found a fuse of oil-soaked rags, the signature of a hand she knew in her bones. [304] Nobody asked her about it. [309] She had testified against him once already, and that was ash enough for one life. [324]

Kneeling, gracefully alert to every shift of light on the water, she unwound the coin from her wrist. [342] Her hands were scarred, a pale continent mapped across her left wrist where a burning rafter had fallen. [360] She used the coin's hard edge to score a clean crescent into the green cambium of a living willow shoot. [380] That was the graft, the wound that would hold, and she made it in one sure pass. [397] She split the stump, set the shoot into the union, bound it with the red thread, and hung the coin over the seam like a small bright seal. [425] Then she unstopped the canister and let the black sand run through her fingers around the base, a dark mulch to keep the new root cool and damp. [453]

She had hidden the scar for years, wearing long sleeves even in August. [466] Now she rolled her sleeve back to the sun so that the skin could learn the light again. [484] That was the whole reason she came here year after year, to prove that scars can sing. [501] Her father had said fire only takes; she answered him with the slow grammar of sap. [517] Where the coin caught the afternoon, it rang once, thin as a bird's note over water. [533]

She did not come to forgive him. [540] She came because she had stood in doorways while houses breathed out their last heat, and she had learned that anything broken can be taught to carry life again. [569] Compassion, for her, was not a feeling but a discipline, unyielding the way the marram is, bending with every wind and never leaving the sand. [594] A wind off the lagoon smelled of salt and cooling stone. [605] She stayed until the shadows of the dunes leaned over the water like a hand held above a candle. [624] Then she walked back through the marram with the empty canister knocking at her hip, one more graft done, one more small bright seal left singing in the dune wind. [654]
