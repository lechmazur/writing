Marlo Finch kept the lighthouse at Carrow Point the way other people kept secrets -- softly persistent, patient beyond reason, and convinced that the truth lived in what others failed to say. [31]
At nautical twilight, when the sky split its seam between blue and black, he would descend the spiral stairs and walk the opal dock, its stone pavers iridescent with salt and mist. [63]

So he began to investigate. [227]
Each evening he studied the boutonniere under the lighthouse lamp, rotating it by its bent stem, examining the papery petals for hidden ink, folded notes, scent. [253]
He found nothing, and yet he kept going, because something about the object insisted on being understood. [270]

From that evening on, he investigated the flower by observing gaps -- not the petals themselves, but the silence they carried between each fold, the emptiness inside the hollow stem, the shadow the bloom refused to cast. [357]
That night he placed the boutonniere on the opal dock and sat beside it as the mist rolled in, and he observed the gaps around it: the shadow it cast where the light failed to reach, the silence between wave strikes against the pylons, the distance between where the flower had been and where it now rested.
