Ines had been called a luminous skeptic since girlhood, and she wore the title the way miners wore dust. [19]
Her work was weather-walking: interviewing the people who carried storms inside their coats, who left fog on windowsills and thunder in their wake. [42]
She did not believe in ghosts, in prophecies, in second moons, but she believed in measurement, and measurement kept betraying her. [63]
On her right hand she wore a tin compass ring, a cheap relic her grandmother had worn through two migrations. [83]
The needle had rusted into place years ago; it pointed, stubbornly, at nothing she could name. [99]
Still she kept it, because nostalgia was the only futurism her family trusted. [112]
The interviews brought her to the lavender bluff, where the county had planted medicinal terraces and the aurora arrived like a scheduled visitor. [135]
It was election week for the forests, which meant the pines were casting their long-distance votes through root-signal, and the whole ridge hummed with arithmetic. [160]
Each tree chose which direction to lean for a season, and the leaning decided shade, firebreaks, migration corridors, everything. [179]
Ines distrusted the ceremony, yet she could not look away from ten thousand trees choosing at once. [196]
Her subject that night was a woman called Pell, who brought thunder the way other people brought scarves. [214]
Pell settled on the bluff with her shoes off, and the lavender underfoot began to taste of iron in Ines's mouth. [235]
This was how Ines navigated now: not by needle, not by satellite, but by the changing flavors the body supplied when something true approached. [259]
Iron meant north and warning; salt meant memory; green meant a decision being made near her. [275]
She had catalogued four hundred flavors and named them like constellations, which her colleagues found embarrassing and she found useful. [295]
Pell said she had carried weather since the old flood, and that she had stopped apologizing for the rain indoors. [315]
Ines asked the standard question and hated herself for it: could she prove any of this? [331]
Pell laughed, and the aurora above them answered in a green arc that seemed to bend toward the bluff. [350]
At the same moment the pines concluded their vote and their signals converged, and the entire ridge leaned, visibly, south. [370]
Something then happened to the ring. [376]
The rusted needle trembled, lifted, and turned on its old axis toward the place where Pell sat. [393]
Ines felt her tongue flood with a taste she had never catalogued: cold water, struck stone, and something like forgiveness. [413]
She understood, with a skeptic's fury and a scientist's relief, that the compass had not been broken. [430]
It had been waiting for enough signals to converge before it would declare a direction at all. [447]
An emergent compass: useless alone, inevitable in company. [455]
That was the answer to her question, though not the one she had come for. [470]
People carried weather because weather is what a crowd becomes when it finally agrees on something. [486]
Pell's storms were simply her childhood neighborhood, still voting, still leaning toward her. [499]
The aurora brightened until the lavender terraces glowed violet-white, and the two women sat inside a light that felt borrowed. [519]
Ines wrote nothing down for the first time in her career. [530]

The ring pointed at Pell, then at the pines, then at the sky, and finally at Ines's own chest. [575]
She laughed at that, a real laugh, not the careful instrument she used in interviews. [590]
Somewhere below, the election results were being sung into the soil, and the valleys would remember the count for a century. [611]
Ines stayed until the aurora thinned, then walked down through the leaning pines toward a town that still kept its lamps on for weather-bearers. [635]
Her skepticism had not vanished; it had become luminous in the older sense, which is to say it finally gave off light instead of heat. [660]
She wore the tin compass ring home, and it pointed ahead of her the whole way. [676]
