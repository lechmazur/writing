The ash on the moonlit ridge path came up in puffs under Edda's boots, and she walked it slowly because her left knee had stopped forgiving her three fires ago. [31] It was late in the season and the sky was doing what the old crews called the slow dance of celestial bodies, the moon sliding behind the black branches and the two low planets holding their places over the far ridge like watchfires nobody had put out. [64] Edda's fire-forged skin pulled at her jaw where the skin graft ended, and she kept her gloved hands loose at her sides, because closing a fist tonight would hurt and there was no one left to hide that from. [105] She had come off the line after nineteen days, and the crew had gone down to the settlement to drink, but she had taken the ridge instead, telling herself it was the shorter way, knowing it was not. [142] Then she heard the small boots behind her and turned to find the girl from the burn camp, the one whose mother had gone up on the third night with the orchard crew, and the girl was carrying a cracked porcelain box in both hands like a bowl of water. [186] "You walked past me at the hospital," the girl said, "and I didn't say anything." [212] Edda knelt, which cost her, and looked into the box, and inside was fine gray ash and the meadow flower that had been pressed into it, and the crazed glaze of the box broke her own face into seven pieces and pulled it back together when she shifted. [262] "It was hers," the girl said. "It cracked in the fire and I carried it down anyway." [291] She held out her palm, and Edda took it gently, seeing the weeping burn across the child's fingers, and she did something with her hands that was quietly and terribly precise: she folded the corner of her scarf over the burn without pulling a single thread, and she drew the girl's hair ribbon free and tied a slip knot in it and set it back, working the way she worked at a line-break, gently skilled, as if the child were the last good thing on the mountain. [374] Then Edda looked into the box again, and reflecting deeply on the face the glaze kept breaking, she understood what she had to give that was not comfort. [406] "Your mother," Edda said, "traded you down to the valley to your aunt for the summer, so you would be out of the fire's way before it ever came." [438] The girl nodded, hard, ashamed of it. [450] "That is what happened," Edda said. "She bargained your innocence away to keep you breathing, and you have been carrying that debt like it was yours to pay." [487] She put a thumbnail to the ash and let it fall. "There is no debt. Ash goes on the wind and lands on every continent, every soil, every body of water. Your grief and mine have the same grammar. Nobody has to translate it." [538] The girl cried the way children do, without guarding her face, and Edda let her see the one stitch of moonlight in the corner of his own eye before she turned her head away. [573] "Then what do I do with it?" the girl whispered. [593] "You put it back in the world," Edda said. "You light something with it that does not burn." [622] She took a spent match from her pocket and struck it, and the flame stood up in the cold air, and together they tipped the ash from the box and let the girl drop the meadow flower onto the ember, where it blackened and gave up its small heat to the night. [679] Edda ran her thumb along the porcelain and felt the crack seal, warm and whole, which was impossible, and she did not say so out loud. [711] She handed the box back to the girl uncracked. "Your mother kept the bargain," she said. "So you don't have to." [742] The moon came out from behind the branches, the planets went on turning at their slow and patient rate over the ridge, and the girl walked down the switchbacks ahead of her, holding the box level as a lantern, while Edda followed slowly, her knee forgiving her at last, her hands soft and finished in the cold.
