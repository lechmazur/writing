The oasis held out against the waste the way a fist holds water, and Sura had come to learn why. [20] Everywhere else the dunes had burned down to bone, but here the clay stayed damp and a ring of dead hives stood like vessels waiting to be glazed. [48] She was a potter, and a potter is patient with what will not move. [62]

Her hands found the faded lantern glass in the wrecked apiary shed, gone to the color of weak honey. [81] It had once fitted a rusted iron lantern that hung by the door; she emptied the lantern and set the glass in her satchel beside her sister Ilka's field notebook. [111] The notebook held Ilka's last sun angles, penciled season by season, and Sura read them the way another woman would read a prayer. [134] She had learned long ago that a still hand moves fastest, that a pot is never argued into shape. [153]

The glass was phototropic to kindness, a fact Sura discovered when it warmed her palm on the walk out and went cold the moment she snapped at the wind. [182] It drew whatever warmth remained in the air toward the gentlest thing in reach. [196] At noon it pulled heat from the dead grass to her fingertips; at dusk, when she was tired and sharp, it turned its faint glow toward the mounds of the hives. [227]

Time in the oasis was fractured, and she read it by following sun patterns across the ground. [244] Where the light fell, spring stood; a shadow's edge meant autumn; and a wet stain of shade held winter so hard that hoarfrost lay on the shed roof under a summer sun. [276] She dragged clay from the spring-side bank and rebuilt the hives one by one, vessel after vessel, letting the sun do her firing. [299] Her sister had vanished in one of those stacked seasons, and the notebook's angles were all Sura had left of her. [320]

As chimney smoke flattened to an iron seam across the western sky, she fed the shed's hearth a bound bundle of dried wormwood and set the glass on the sill. [351] The smoke hung level and unmoving, and she read the stillness as a question. [365] This was what she had come for: to understand why some places resist change, why the oasis kept its seasons stacked like unfired pots on a shelf. [392]

Then the glass warmed, and it was not warming her. [402] Out past the last hive a figure in a beekeeper's veil came walking, small and deliberate, a child who had wandered the waste alone. [426] The light reached for the child the way it had reached for her palm, and the glass shone so kindly that the child followed it in. [452] Sura said nothing sharp, nothing at all; she knelt and offered water and wet clay, and her gentleness was a blade with its edge turned away. [478]

The oasis watched, and then it moved. [485] Clay is only patience plus heat, and something in the place had been waiting for both. [501] Frost pulled back off the shed roof into its own shadow; spring advanced a whole stride past the light's edge; and the first bee of a dead season crawled out of a rebuilt hive with its wings glazed and whole. [541] It changed because she had not asked it to. [550]

Sura understood at last: places resist change only when they are handled like problems, and the glass had been her teacher, aiming its warmth at whatever was softest. [578] She set it back inside the iron lantern, where it belonged, and the light filled the whole shed without ever burning. [599] The child slept by the hearth as the smoke leveled into dusk. [611] By morning the oasis had given up its seasons one at a time, like pots drawn from a kiln, and the whole ring of hives was busy. [638] She did not stop to name the sound the hives made; she only laid her hand on the warm clay and listened to it thrum. [663] The wasteland went on burning at the edges, but the edges no longer mattered. [677]
