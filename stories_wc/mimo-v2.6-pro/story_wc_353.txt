Wren called herself a tree-song sculptor, though no one had heard a song in forty years. [16]
She came to the siskin alder grove at first light, after the hedgehog uncurled on the path and went hunting. [36]
That was her clock. [40]
When the hedgehog uncurls, the grove is fully awake, and fully silent. [52]
The silence was not an absence. [58]
It was a held breath, the whole wood waiting on the downbeat. [70]
Wren was symphonically minded and always had been. [78]
Where other people heard nothing, she heard a score: bass in the roots, mid-register in the trunks, a thin bright top line in the alder leaves. [104]
Her work, her whole life, bent toward one aim: to create music in a silent world. [120]
The sound went out of the world before she was born, and no one left alive could say how. [139]
Half-swallowed by the largest alder stood a long-forgotten sculpture. [148]
Her grandmother had carved it the year the silence began, then walked away and never finished it. [165]
It showed a woman with her arms raised and her mouth open, carved one breath before singing. [182]
Wren had avoided it for years. [188]
Finishing someone else's work felt like a kind of theft. [198]
Now she knelt and read it. [204]
By analyzing the stress cracks in the long-forgotten sculpture, she could find where the grain had been loaded for four decades. [225]
The cracks were not damage; they were notation. [233]
A fine dark line along the raised arm meant tension, the wood pulling apart. [247]
A star of small fractures at the elbow meant compression, the wood crushed inward. [261]
Read together the two lines spelled out a chord, and the chord told her where to strum. [278]
Above her, dead telegraph wire still ran between two trunks. [288]
A porcelain insulator hung from it, white as a tooth, the only thing in the grove that could not bend. [308]
She climbed and worked it free, then tied it back on a shorter span so it sat level with the sculpture's open mouth. [331]
It would be the sharp glassy note against all that low wood. [343]
She took a coil of wire in one hand and a flat alder leaf in the other. [360]
Then she knelt again and laid her palm flat on the sculpture's chest. [373]
This was the part she could never explain to anyone living. [384]
She did not strike the wood. [390]
She let the pressure in her own hand match the pressure she had read in the cracks. [407]
Empathy in motion, she called it. [413]
Not feeling for the wood. [418]
Feeling with it. [421]
Then she drew her fingers down the wire in one slow strum. [433]
Nothing came to the ear. [438]
Everything came to the hands. [443]
The grain let go along the raised arm. [451]
Under her palm the stored load moved out of the wood and into her shoulders. [466]
Her own arms lifted a finger's width without her willing it. [477]
The porcelain insulator ticked once against the wire. [485]
That was the top note. [490]
Then the alder took the chord back, low and enormous. [500]
The sculpture's mouth opened a little wider as the compression star at the elbow emptied. [515]
Wren felt her own jaw loosen. [521]
She strummed again, slower. [525]
The score came apart into its sections the way she had always heard it. [539]
Bass first, in the root plate under the sculpture's feet. [549]

The arms were lower now, resting. [616]
The mouth was closed. [620]
It looked less like a woman about to sing than a woman who had finished. [635]
Wren understood then that the piece had never been abandoned. [645]
It had been left under load, on purpose, so that someone would come along and play it. [662]
