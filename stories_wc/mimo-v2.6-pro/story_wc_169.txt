Mara Venn called herself a logical dreamer, though she admitted the phrase was a paradox she enjoyed fitting into her calculations. [21] Each morning she descended the salt-slick stair into the tide-polished grotto beneath the cliff, where the sea had carved the rock smooth as a proof. [46] The grotto served as her laboratory, her observatory, her chapel. [56] On the sand she had arranged a hundred trial balloons, each one small and hopeful, tethered to the stone by threads of copper. [79] The tide rose and fell twice a day, and so did her moods, though she kept her expression lucidly watchful, the way a mathematician watches a limit approach but never arrives. [110] At her belt hung a balloonist's altitude gauge, brass and beveled, its needle trembling at every breath of pressure. [129] She had inherited it from her grandmother, who had crossed the Great Interior by gas balloon and had recorded, in a leather journal, equations for lift, for drift, for the weight of disappointment. [162] Mara believed that hope, too, had an equation. [170] She was determined to puzzle out new equations for hope. [180] She began each session by creating patterns with decorations. [189] She strung shells, seed pods, and shards of colored glass between the balloons, so that when the light entered the grotto it broke into lattices of gold and green. [218] The decorations were not ornament; they were data. [226] Each arrangement tested a hypothesis about balance and buoyancy, and each failure taught her where hope thinned. [243] Today she released the first trial balloon, a pale violet sphere filled with warm air from the grotto's vent. [262] The altitude gauge clicked. [266] She watched the needle climb: ten, twenty, thirty meters of imaginary sky. [278] The balloon rose, hovered, then sank, its skin dimpling as the warm air cooled. [292] Mara recorded the numbers. [296] Too little lift. [299] Too much hope placed in a single breath. [307] She adjusted the decorations, tightening the threads until the shells clicked together like small teeth. [322] The new pattern redirected the vent's heat toward the violet balloon. [333] She released a second, then a third. [340] The gauges sang. [343] Two balloons held altitude. [347] One drifted toward the ceiling of the grotto and burst against the limestone, and Mara felt the small catastrophe settle into her like weather. [371] She noted it calmly. [375] Failure, she had learned, was only a coefficient. [383] The grotto, though, held more than her work. [391] Within its echoing walls she had begun to hear other versions of herself. [404] Parallel timelines, she called them, and the term came from the leather journal, which her grandmother had filled with diagrams of crossings and currents. [428] When the tide was at its lowest, the water in the deepest pool went absolutely still, and in that stillness Mara could see, faintly, the reflections of other grottos, other balloons, other women bent over other gauges. [465] She was lucidly watchful in all of them. [473] One of these reflections looked up. [479] It raised a hand. [483] In its other hand it held a trial balloon of a color Mara had never made, a green like the sea before a storm. [507] It mouthed a number. [511] Mara leaned closer, her own gauge pressed to the rock, and she understood: the number was a coefficient for hope, the missing term in her equations. [537] Across the parallel timelines, another Mara had solved it. [546] Mara wrote the number in the wet sand. [554] Then she did something she had never done: she released every balloon at once. [568] The grotto filled with drifting spheres, each tethered by threads of shell and glass, and the light shattered into a hundred moving constellations. [591] She had come to revitalize the old proof, to give its dead variables breath again. [606] It was a serene upheaval, the quietest revolution she could imagine, and it changed the pressure in the room so that every gauge clicked in unison. [632] The reflected Mara smiled and released her own balloon. [641] The two spheres rose together, one violet, one storm-green, and touched at the ceiling without bursting. [657] The shells chimed. [660] The equations in the sand rearranged themselves under the moving water. [671] Mara felt, for the first time in years, that the proof might complete. [684] She stayed until the tide returned and erased the sand, and then she climbed the stairs into the morning, her grandmother's gauge warm against her hip, her notebook full of new variables. [716] Outside, the sea was flat and silver. [723] Somewhere beneath the waves the other grottos waited, and across them all the balloons rose, each one a small experiment in the arithmetic of becoming. [748]</story>
