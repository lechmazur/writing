Rain had passed over the cobalt lagoon an hour before, and the water held the color the way a bell holds its note. [23] Along the tideline the poet walked alone, quietly, a shoreline figure who had learned to speak only when the sand asked. [44] In the wet shine lay a drake's tail feather, broken-backed, its iridescent barbs raked backward by the surf. [62] He tucked it behind an ear first, then thought better of that and tied it to his wrist with a strip of jute. [85]

For ten years he had come to this shore at first light to practice what he called woven dawns. [104] The idea was simple and impossible: to take each sunrise and thread it through the last, until the mornings were one long cloth. [127] The cloth never held. [131] Each dawn tore loose from the one before it, and he was left with a heap of bright unraveled mornings. [151] He had kept them anyway, pressing the failures into a notebook, because his whole art was an argument with the incomplete. [172] What he wanted, he admitted to the water, was to discover beauty in the incomplete, and he had not managed it yet. [194]

Children had been flying kites above the lagoon all morning, and the wind off the rain carried their shouting like chaff. [215] One kite was higher than the rest, a paper drake dyed cobalt to match the water beneath it. [233] He watched it the way he watched everything, warmly attentive, as if the sky had leaned down to tell him something. [254] Then, as the kite climbed into a hard gust, the string snapped. [266] The sound was small and clean, a single plucked note, and the paper drake folded and began to fall. [285] The children cried out and then grew quiet, the way people do at the edge of a burnished hush. [304] The poet stood with his hand still half-closed on the air where a string had been, and felt the wound the feather had cut into his palm begin to sting. [334] He looked at the feather on his wrist and understood that he had been given a bow. [351]

The lagoon, still slack from the rain, had ribs: sandbars and old pilings and the long edge of the jetty, each with its own tone when struck. [378] So he knelt and did the only thing he knew: he began tending the wreck. [393] He swept the sand flat with a driftwood stick, he cleared the broken shells from the tideline, and he set the fallen paper drake where the current could reach it. [423] Then he drew the drake's tail feather across the jetty's ribs, slowly, finding one tone and then the next. [442] They disagreed, the two notes, and he let them disagree, adjusting the pressure of the feather until the discord found a shape. [464] This was his method, the one he had never written down: by harmonizing discord, not by smoothing it away. [483] The sound he made was thin and imperfect, but the current answered it. [496]

The paper drake, cobalt and broken, turned once and came in on the tide. [510] He lifted it out of the shallows and found its tail plume of dyed jute tangled with the snap of its string. [532] He untangled it, tended the tear along its spine with a smear of wet sand, and tied the drake's tail feather to the plume where the string had been. [561] There was no one left to fly it; the children had gone home with their remaining kites. [578] So he let the lagoon have it, wading out until the water was warm as tea against his knees. [597] The paper drake drifted, its broken spine healed by sand, its new feather catching the last of the burnished light. [617] It did not fly again and it did not sink. [627] Both of those seemed to the poet like the same mercy. [638]

He walked the tideline toward the dark, and the whole lagoon lay open like a page he could not finish. [658] He did not try to weave this dawn to the last one. [670] Instead he pressed the wet sand flat with his palm and left it there, a single morning held open on purpose, imperfect and warm. [694] The broken feather had taught him what the cloth never could: that beauty lives in the tear, and that a thing tended well is already complete. [720]
