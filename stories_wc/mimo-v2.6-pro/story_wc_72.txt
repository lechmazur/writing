Thessaly stood at the lighthouse's edge of the garden and spoke to the window, where a hand was already flattening against the flaw from the other side. [17] Her garden was stone and had no fish in it. [23] The statues were the living things there; the one pressed to the glass was seven feet tall and wrong in the ribs, crouched on a coral sill. [42]

She read the thought before the thought was made. [50] It said: let me in. [54] She answered, the way she always answered, with the whole of it laid out clean. [63] The secret was already out, and had been for three days, since the cascade first broke over her like the blackwater tide she had written down and then forgotten. [85] The city of Aldwarke had been built on the wrong bones. [92] Where the surveyors had sworn a granite spine, there was limestone. [99] Where they had drawn the aqueduct, there had been nothing but a stand-in, a leviathan ribcage mistaken for architecture, and every vault since had hung on that error. [120]

The statue read all of it and did not move. [127] Its thought was a flat stone: let me in. [134]

"Nuance," Thessaly said, "is not a door you open by wishing." [142] She said it the way a surgeon says a word, incisively kind. [150] She put her palm flat to the flaw in the window. [156] It was a hairline fault, barely a fault at all, running from the sill to the meridian. [167] Structural weakness was the only thread she had ever known how to pull. [176]

The second cascade fired. [181] It ran: the aqueduct was wrong, therefore the ribs were wrong, therefore the ribs had been failing, therefore the blackwater had not been a storm but a seep. [199] The meridian moved as she watched — no, it had always been here; the two gardens were one garden, and the one she had loved in was only a floor below this one, and the rose she had buried her palm in for forty years was the same rose that was now pushing its root through the crack in her arm. [253] She had been so certain of this room. [259] She had told the other statues so, every day, with no softness at all. [270]

The thought on the other side of the glass shifted, and for the first time it was not a stone. [283] It was: I was only asking to be let in. [292]

Thessaly breathed, and the breath moved through the glass of her ribs, and the rose-root in her arm split the stone there the way a hairline fault splits a window. [315] She had a root. [319] She had the ampoule, the small foxglove-stained glass vial she had kept at her throat since the city was new, its color the color of the flower and the flower's poison. [344] She was the living statue; she could draw anything out of anything. [353] She heated the ampoule in the light the lighthouse still threw, and she drew it — a thread of pink glass, thin as a root, warm as a hand — and she fed it into the hairline fault, through the weakness, into the crack that the roots in her own arm had opened wide. [409] The thread caught. [413] It held. [415] The window did not open; the window was not meant to open; but the window was now held by something that was not the wrong bones of the city. [438]

The thought said: thank you. [444]

"That is the trouble with certainty," Thessaly said, incisively kind. [454] "It never opens. It only holds, until it does not, and then it opens all at once, and everything you were certain of is the water." [475] She turned from the window to the garden, and she let the sea in through the wall the way she had always meant to, the way the blackwater had taught her, by the flaw and not by the force. [512] The other statues looked up at her. [518] She had been hard on them. [523] She would not be hard again. [528]

When the last of the ribs went, the garden rose, and Thessaly rose with it, and the outside of the window pressed itself flat to her cheek. [557] The two gardens were one, and the one that had been forgotten was only a floor below. [577] The rose in her arm bloomed. [583] The thought from the other side said one last thing, and it was not a stone: it was that she had been right about the bones, and wrong about the certainty, and that both of those things could be true in the same rose. [634] Thessaly held the thread and let the window close. [644] The tide, the blackwater, the seep — whatever it had been — turned the garden to glass. [659] She let it. [663] She had welcomed nuance into her certainty, and she was not certain of anything, and that was the thread that held. [688] Outside, the bathers on the sill went to stone, and the animate went lifeless, and the lighthouse still threw its light over the room that had been the wrong one all along. [725]</story>
