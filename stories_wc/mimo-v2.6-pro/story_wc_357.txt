Mara's skiff cut through the whirling silence above the orchard planet, its pistons humming beneath the soundlessness like a heart buried in earth. [23] She was an orchard pilot, the only one who still flew these routes, tracing the currents of water that braided through floating rock arches across the planet's broken surface. [52] Below her, thousands of fruit trees clung to stone ledges and island meadows, their roots threading through cracks in rock that should not have supported anything alive. [79] Mara had spent three seasons searching for the source of resilience behind those impossible orchards, and today the gravity inversion would give her the opening she needed. [106] For one hour, gravity would reverse; the planet would pull everything downward and then, without warning, hurl it all upward into the sky. [129] The arches, those massive curved stones that hung suspended between ground and cloud, were the only landmarks that remained stable through the shift. [152] She climbed out of the skiff and stood at the base of the nearest arch, listening. [168] It was said that when gravity inverted, the planet spoke in echoes, and she had come to hear it. [187] Mara knelt beside a stream that ran along the orchard floor and cupped her hands into the cold water. [206] Water was her compass; she had learned to navigate by its flow, following where it gathered and where it split, trusting it to lead her deeper into the network of rock and root. [239] The stream's current slowed as she moved beneath the arch, pooling into a shallow basin fringed with moss and silver-leafed vines. [260] Something glinted at the bottom. [265] She reached in and drew out a broken arrow tip, its metal dark and warm as if it had been fired only moments before. [289] The shaft was gone, but the tip was intact except for the fracture where it had snapped free, and it thrummed against her palm with a faint vibration. [317] Around the basin, the moss had grown in concentric rings, the vines thickened and bloomed, and she realized the arrow tip had been accelerating plant growth in this hollow for years. [348] She turned it over, searching for a mark, and found none, only a faint inscription she could not read. [367] Then gravity shifted. [370] The world lurched; water tore from the basin and spiraled upward, trees bent skyward, loose stones lifted from the ground like startled birds. [393] Mara gripped the arch's underside and held on as everything around her rose. [406] In the silence that followed, that whirling silence of a planet holding its breath, she heard them: echoes, hundreds of voices layered atop one another, a gathering of echoes that had no single source. [440] They were pilots, she realized, orchard pilots who had flown before her, who had come to this place and left their voices behind in the rock and water and root. [470] The arrow tip burned against her skin and the voices sharpened. [481] "She broke it herself," one echoed. [487] "The shaft would have killed the tree," another replied. [496] "Let the water carry the message forward." [503] Mara understood then. [506] The arrow had been aimed at the heartwood of an ancient tree, the first tree, the mother tree of the orchard planet, and a pilot before her had snapped the arrow mid-flight to spare it. [541] The broken tip, embedded in the water, had fed the tree's resilience outward, accelerating the growth of every root and leaf connected to it, binding the orchards together through a network of living water. [575] The echoes were the pilots who had protected the orchard across centuries, each one adding their voice to the gathering, each one carrying the memory of that single act of mercy. [606] Gravity reversed again. [609] The arches groaned as everything crashed back to the ground, and the echoes faded into the returning silence. [627] Mara stood, breathless, the broken arrow tip cupped in both hands. [638] She had found the source of resilience, not a machine, not a spell, but a choice made long ago by someone who had let the arrow break instead of letting the tree fall. [671] She slipped the tip into her jacket and walked back to the stream, following the water's flow toward her skiff. [691] The orchards swayed above her in the fading wind, their branches thick with fruit, their roots deep in rock, their silence the whirling silence of something that endures because someone once refused to let it die.
