On the morning the auction notice was nailed to the marina post, Marisol Vane woke to the sound of the raft city breathing. [23] Below her window the carousel turned all night and always, its painted horses rising and falling like a tide that never decided to leave. [47] The Wake had been afloat for forty years, a carnival stacked on lashed pontoons and planks, and Marisol had lived on it for nine. [71]

She was the city's culture shock absorber, the woman the Trust hired whenever mainlanders arrived with clipboards and questions. [90] Her job was to translate carnival into paperwork and paperwork into carnival before either could frighten the other. [108] She took the Trust's money and she slept under the Trust's purchase, and that was the shape of her dual allegiance. [129] The purchase, the notice said, would close before the autumn rains, and after privatization the pontoons would be theirs to tow. [150]

Around her neck she wore the chip. [157] It was a chip of the foundation stone, the granite slab the original raftmasters had sunk between the hulls to hold the city steady in the current. [184] Old Tiber had pressed it into her palm the day the guilds wrote her name into the resident ledger, and the stone was still warm from his hand. [212] Its speckles were her cipher. [217]

Because the Trust monitored the radios and pinned its own notices over the message boards, Marisol spoke to the city the way the city spoke to itself. [244] She painted. [246] Across the tent walls and loading doors she laid down graffiti art in carnival colors: three roses for a dawn meeting, a chipped moon for the loan office, arrows for new routes. [278] The order was intuitive, not written down: she read the moods of the crowd the way a musician reads a room, then set the symbols in rows like a ledger. [308] Orderly intuitive, the raftmasters called her, half compliment and half warning. [319]

The day the appraisers came, Marisol met them at the gangplank with coffee and a smile that had cost her years to build. [342] There were four of them, young and quick, carrying tablets that did not care about frying dough or the hurdy-gurdy. [362] She walked them through the lanes and absorbed every small shock on their behalf. [376] When the fire-eater spat a plume that singed an appraiser's sleeve, Marisol laughed first and apologized second, and the man forgave the city on the spot. [402]

That night, in her tidy cabin, she reorganized. [410] She drew the whole city on butcher paper: every guild, every stall, every family with a boat tied to the raft, every debt to the loan office. [437] Then she erased the borders between them and wrote one word across the middle: cooperative. [452] If every guild signed a single title, the Trust could not buy the fire-eaters out from under the ferrymen, or the ferrymen out from under the rope-dancers. [479]

At dawn she painted the plan into the walls. [488] Where the stone's dark speckles clustered, she painted the meeting times; where the pale flecks ran in lines, she painted the routes. [510] Anyone with a chip of the foundation stone could read the city's new order at a glance; the appraisers saw only a carnival painting its own walls. [537]

For three weeks she worked in hopeful desperation, meeting guilds at dawn, absorbing their suspicion and the Trust's impatience, painting through the night. [560] Her dual allegiance frayed and held and frayed again. [569] The Trust's director called her into the floating office and asked which of the murals were, in her expert opinion, agitprop against their gentrification plans. [594] Marisol turned the chip over in her pocket, felt the granite grit, and gave the answer nine years of practice had taught her. [617]

"Carnival people paint when they are happy," she said. [626] "They paint a great deal lately, because they are happy about the purchase." [639] The director wrote that down, and the murals stayed up. [649]

On the last morning before the auction, every guild filed into the registrar's barge with one title, and the Trust's purchase arrived to find nothing it could buy in pieces. [679] There was shouting, and lawyers, and a week of rain in which the carousel turned anyway. [695] In the end the Trust agreed to buy the cooperative whole or not at all, and the cooperative voted to stay afloat. [717] Nothing was solved exactly; the rains came early, the loan office still held its notes, and the pontoons still needed patching. [738]

But that evening Marisol climbed to the Ferris wheel with a pot of red paint and her chip in her teeth. [759] Across the rust she painted a small rose and a wide arrow pointing home, and the city read it before the paint dried. [782]
