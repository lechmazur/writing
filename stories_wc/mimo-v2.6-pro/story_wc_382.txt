I moved into the top floor of a brick building that had been a hotel, a boarding house, and a place where names were forgotten. [25] The walls kept a language I could not read, and I wanted to learn it. [40] My neighbor across the hall taught dead languages for a living, which seemed the right kind of expertise. [58] I began by borrowing sugar. [63] She had a jar of it on a dark oak desk, beside an antique desk seal with a handle of tarnished silver. [85] She pressed the seal into red wax and set the stamp on the corner of each lesson card, so that every word she taught arrived signed. [111] The borrowing of sugar turned into weekly tea sessions. [120] Her name was Mara Vess, and she had a way of handling things that was patiently nimble, as though every object were a word she was about to translate. [149] She taught me to interrogate a wall. [156] Not to tap it and guess, but to ask, and then to wait long enough that the waiting became part of the question. [179] She corrected me only once, and never twice, which is its own kind of grammar. [194] Settling, she said, is the past tense of a house. [204] Cracks are its punctuation. [208] Smoke stains in the chimney breast are marginalia, someone arguing with the record. [221] Her translations carried a mysterious familiarity, as though I had heard the building speak in a dream and forgotten it on waking. [243] We had an understanding, the two of us, built on agreed upon lies. [256] She told me the translations were certain, and I agreed to believe her. [269] I told her the building was listening, and she agreed to believe me. [282] Neither of us mentioned the cough that had begun to write itself in the pauses between her words. [300] That was the third lie, and it held us together longer than the truth would have. [316] In the last spring of her teaching, she moved our sessions to the rooftop garden. [331] There were planters up there and a long bench, and the roof was the one surface of the building that answered her. [353] She brought the seal and the cards and a thermos of tea. [365] We worked while the sky turned, and the vocabulary grew in her handwriting. [378] The night of the meteor shower she was frail enough that I carried the chair up for her and she came on my arm. [402] The sky began to fall in slow bright scratches. [411] She said the meteors were the building's handwriting, quick notes in a margin too large for us. [428] That night she taught me one final word and pressed the seal into the wax so hard the handle bit her palm. [450] The word meant, she said, the sound a house makes when it agrees with you. [465] I have never found it in any dictionary. [473] She died in autumn, and the seal came to me with her cards, still smelling faintly of red wax and tea. [494] The building did not change. [499] That is what I could not forgive. [506] So I went back up to the rooftop garden alone, and I interrogated the brickwork the way she had taught me. [527] I asked about the joists and the chimney breast and the scars where a fire escape had been cut away. [547] I waited until the waiting became part of the question. [557] Nothing answered, of course, and then something did. [565] A draft moved along the parapet, the seal grew warm in my hand, and the whole roof settled one long syllable into the cold. [589] I pressed it into wax and wrote down what I heard. [600] On the card I stamped her seal at the corner, the way she always had. [615] Whether the translation was true was not the point. [624] We had agreed, years ago, to accept the building's account of itself, and I have held up my end. [643] Some nights I still go up to the garden with a jar of sugar and a thermos, in case she climbs the last flight. [667] The walls keep talking in their patient, crumbling grammar. [676] I understand more than I should. [682]</story>
