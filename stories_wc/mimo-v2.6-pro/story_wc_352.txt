Vessa Orle was a prism-minded archivist, who kept the sealed annals of a city that had learned to fear its own memory. [22]
Each vault in her basement carried a lock whose combination was written nowhere, so the record-keepers had buried the numbers in places the eye could not map. [49]

The worst of the locks belonged to the grotto archive, a serpentine grotto in mist beneath the old riverbed. [91]
Twice a year, the masons said, gravity inverts for an hour, and the tunnels drain upward into a ceiling of white vapor. [113]
Only in that hour could a person walk the grotto's throat without drowning, because the water hung above like a slow silver roof. [136]
She had gone down once as a girl and come back with a broken wrist and a fear she would never name. [158]
Now, at thirty-four, she would reenter that throat, older and armed with a doodle. [172]
Vessa had spent nine months with one folio, a hymnal from the drowned quarter, hunting the number that would open the deepest door. [195]

That glow was warning and invitation at once, and it drew her toward the riverbed on the evening of the inversion. [287]
She carried the folio under her coat and a lantern she would not light, because the grotto punished borrowed flame. [307]
