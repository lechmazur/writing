Oren Kestler arrived at the brass table before the last light left the mullioned windows, a magnetic nexus at twilight where twelve chairs waited for a decision. [27] Five years of exile had thinned his coat and sharpened his posture, but his hands still moved as a teacher's do, marking the air with small deliberate signs. [55] The Committee for Recreational Culture had reopened his file at the request of three petitioners, and everything he might say had to be said before the next phase, before the budget hearing went to midnight and the vote closed the room. [96] The ban had a name and a date and a signature, and Oren had spent his exile in a fishing town where the only games were dice, teaching geometry to boatwrights who asked no questions. [131] He was the exiled chess tutor, sentenced for teaching a forbidden opening to children in the southern field, and he set his polished basalt worry stone dent on the table as a clerk might set an exhibit. [168] The stone held a thumb-shaped hollow, worn smooth by five winters of worry, and Oren correlated the depth of that hollow to the number of silent minutes he had spent waiting in corridors outside rooms he was forbidden to enter. [208] His manner was thoughtfully precise, each clause numbered and each step named before he took it. [224] Clause one, he said, the eclipsed covenant of the founding charter promised that every player might sit at the board, and the ban has dimmed that promise rather than ended it. [255] Clause two, I do not ask for reinstatement, compensation, or apology; I ask for one unnamed morning in the southern field where a board may be set out and left for whoever comes. [288] Clause three, and this is the whole of my defense, silence is a language I learned from the grandmaster of the sixth column, and I have brought it here to be read. [320] Then he stopped speaking and did not speak again for nine minutes, recording his testimony by translating silence into something the room could hold. [344] The chairwoman counted on her fingers; the deputy for parks looked at the windows; the youngest member, who had been one of the southern field children, placed both palms flat on the brass. [377] He did not look toward the youngest member's chair, because a former student's kindness would undo what the silence was building. [398] Oren did not fill the silence with argument; he let it work, the way a pawn works a diagonal no one is watching. [421] It is not beauty I came for, he had written in his petition, but to discover beauty in the difficult, which is a different thing and a slower one. [450] Twelve names hung on the wall behind the table, and he knew each chair by its sound, the third from the left scraping, the ninth always rocking. [477] When the ninth minute ended, the chairwoman asked if he had anything further, and he said only that the stone should be returned to him if the answer was no. [507] She nodded, and the clerk entered his addendum as E. Kestler, testimony recorded by translation of silence, nine minutes, object: polished basalt worry stone, one dent. [533] The vote would not come for hours, so Oren walked out to the corridor and stood where the brass doors threw back the sound of the room. [560] He had learned patience the way the southern field taught it, by losing games to children and naming each loss out loud so it could become a lesson rather than a wound. [592] The worry stone sat in his palm, its dent a small honest hollow, and he correlated the shape of it with the shape of the evening, both worn by pressure into something that fit the hand. [628] Outside, the last light went, and the windows of the chamber became mirrors in which the twelve chairs looked almost occupied. [649] Whatever the committee decided, the covenant was only eclipsed, and an eclipse, as he told his students, is a promise the sky keeps by darkening and then by giving everything back. [680] Oren Kestler put the stone in his pocket and waited, hopeful and melancholy at once, for the doors to open. [700]
