<story>Mara hadn't slept in eleven days, and the canvas knew it. [11]
The island of Vennick rose from the salt flats like a knuckle on a drowned hand, accessible only at low tide. [32]
After the water wars, the treaty had declared such places neutral ground—neither nation's grief nor another's was allowed to claim them. [54]
She had come here to paint, though "paint" was generous. [64]
What she did was more like threshing—dragging color across raw surface until something husked out of her that resembled meaning. [85]
She worked by lamplight because sleep wouldn't come and darkness was no reason to stop. [100]
The amber brooch had belonged to her mother, who'd died in the flooding of Kessin before the wars ended. [119]
Mara pinned it to her coat every morning with the wryly earnest habit of someone who knows talismans don't work but suspects they might anyway. [144]
That evening the rain came, not falling so much as migrating across the island in silver ribbons. [161]
Water found the old flagstones and ran along their grooves in bright threads, branching and rejoining like veins. [179]
Mara, driven by some impulse she'd later call a painter's restlessness, followed one of those rain trails out of her shack and across the causeway rocks. [205]
The trail led her to a cove she hadn't seen before, where the retreating tide had uncovered something the sea had kept hidden for a very long time. [233]
Beneath the kelp and silt lay a mosaic of shell and glass—tiles arranged in spirals that must have taken generations to lay. [256]
An unearthed convergence, she thought: every storm, every tide, every drowned village finally delivering their debris to the same point. [276]
She knelt in the wet sand with haggard awe, her lamp casting gold across the spirals. [292]
She touched the mosaic and felt the years in it—smooth glass from war-era bottles, ancient shells, even fragments of blue pottery from the water wars themselves. [319]
It was everything that had ever been lost, rearranged into something like hope. [332]
A figure stood at the far edge of the cove, hooded against the rain. [346]
He introduced himself as Oren, a diver from the mainland, and he too had followed the water to this place. [366]
His eyes were the same exhausted red as hers. [375]
"Are you an artist?" he asked, wryly. [382]
"An insomniac who paints," she corrected, and he laughed in a way that made her feel like she'd told the truth by accident. [405]
They stood together over the mosaic as the rain slowly thinned. [416]
Oren reached into his coat and produced a small clay figure—a woman holding a bowl, her face worn smooth by handling. [438]
"My daughter made it before the flooding of Kessin," he said. [449]
Mara felt the coincidence settle into her bones like cold water. [460]
She reached for the brooch at her collar and unfastened it. [471]
"Let's trade," she said, though the word felt too light for what she meant. [485]
An exchange, then: his daughter's clay for her mother's amber, each carrying a life the other couldn't bear. [503]
She pinned nothing to her coat that night. [511]
Instead she went back to her canvas and painted the mosaic's spirals, but in the center she placed two small objects—an amber stone and a clay bowl—circling each other like planets that had finally stopped running. [549]
The painting took her until dawn, and when it was finished she slept for the first time in eleven days. [569]
Meaning, she decided, was not something you could think into being. [580]
It arrived like the rain—by following trails of water to wherever they converged, and then by giving away the thing you thought you couldn't survive losing. [607]
Outside, the tide began its slow return, and the cove disappeared beneath the sea again. [622]
But the mosaic remained, and so did the painting, and so did the weight of what she'd given and received. [642]</story>
