In the winter of her fifty-third year, Nell Keene trained the courier pigeons of Alderney's air-mail loft by day and sorted the undelivered letters of dead men by night. [29]
She called it paper. [33]
At Owlfeather & Sons, a vintage bookshop whose attic smelled of foxing glue and guano, the letters lay in crates addressed to nobody, and the pigeons roosted in the rafters where the books gave out. [67]
It was her whole estate. [72]
On a Thursday in March, a man who called himself Jonah Vane climbed the shop ladder with a canvas satchel and a sealed envelope, and he set on the counter below the skylight a chip of mars meteorite the size of her thumbnail, dull as a river stone until the light found it. [125]
It was not for him. [130]
The letter was for a woman he would not name, and the chip was her payment, and the fee he laid beside them was three times what the loft paid her in a month. [164]
She did not ask why. [169]
Because Nell had spent four years telling every stranger a different past, she now told Jonah that she had flown birds for the harbour mail in '09, that a storm had taken her best racer and her hearing in one afternoon, that she had come here to write the letters no one else would finish. [224]
None of it was true. [229]
She had never kept birds before the loft, and the only thing the storm had taken was a rented room in Portsmouth she was glad to lose. [256]
At dusk, when the loft crew went home and the chip lay in her coat pocket pulling at her like a tide, Nell climbed the ladder and found her balance on the counter beneath the skylight, setting her palm flat to feel for the cold and to watch the dust. [306]
The motes did the work. [311]
By mapping the swirl of dust motes under that single skylight, tracing each eddy back to the breath or the hand that made it, she could tell which crates had been opened together and which letters had ridden side by side in the same sack for a hundred miles. [360]
It was her only skill that mattered. [367]
By the third night she had the trail: a letter from a flute-maker in Rouen to his sister, then to his widow, then to a pawnshop in Weymouth, then to Jonah's satchel, and finally the chip, which the widow had sent to buy back her husband's last instrument. [415]
Paper remembers what people edit out. [421]
On the second week, when Jonah's sister Marisol came up the ladder to check on the delivery, Nell stood on the slate ledge of the open skylight with a bird under one arm to show her the loft, and she felt the old fear of falling sit down inside her ribs. [472]
Trust is a kind of balance. [478]
She kept her feet, and she let Marisol hold Ember, the young blue chequer who had not yet flown a route, and the bird stayed. [503]
That was when Marisol hummed the melody her brother's widow played on the recovered flute, a tune with no name that opened like a vine and then withered to one held note, and Nell crossed to the answerphone and pressed play. [544]
For two years she had been taping the yard: birdsong at dawn, the flute practice from the flat below, the girl who sang to the pigeons at feeding time, all of it on one grey cassette she labelled LOST SONGS in block letters and never played for anyone. [592]
That was why she took Jonah's job. [599]
On the last night she opened a crate she had not touched in a year and found her own handwriting on the address labels, her own dates in the ledger, a past she had printed and filed and then forgotten she invented. [641]
There was a photograph too, a young trainer with a bird on her fist and a face she did not recognise, and it withered under her gaze into somebody she had only ever pretended to be. [677]
She let it stand. [681]
In the morning she carried the chip and the letter to the address on the envelope and handed them to a woman with a flute case, and she gave Marisol the cassette, which cost her the loft job in a single telephone call but gave the songs a room to live in. [733]
The loft went blooming, withering, empty, all in one afternoon. [743]
She stayed on the rail until the last bird was fed and the light under the skylight turned the colour of a bruise going yellow, and then she climbed down with the ledger and wrote one true sentence in it at last. [785]
She wrote her name. [789]
