The snow had been falling over the opal observatory for six hours before the radio static cleared, and Mara knew the sound was over because her count stopped stuttering. [29]
For eleven years she had counted heartbeats constantly, tapping the tally under her tongue, and the number that night stood at two billion and change before the hiss in the receiver bled into it and ruined the rhythm. [67]
The observatory's dome was opal, quarried in slabs and mortared with tin, and under the snow it glowed the pale green of a fish held up to lamplight. [95]
Inside, the brass ring of the old reflector was webbed with frost, and every instrument she touched left a print of fog that dried before she could read it. [124]
Mara had come here because the mountaintop was the only place her sister's archive still broadcast, and because she was, by the state's own corrected record, a death certificate error survivor. [155]
At nineteen a clerk had typed a nine where a zero belonged, and the ledger had buried her; for six weeks the world grieved a woman who was eating soup in a rented room. [189]
They corrected the form, but the correction never reached the part of her that had gone under, so she counted heartbeats to keep the paperwork from deciding anything again. [218]
Elia had been dead fourteen years, and the archive was a digital ghost, stitched out of her heartbeats and her incoherent static and the voicemails nobody had deleted. [246]
It had been defragmenting itself for a decade and getting nowhere, looping the same three seconds of her laugh over the snow, and Mara had come to fetch wisdom from failure. [277]
On the table by the receiver lay spilled ink that the ghost kept moving, and in Mara's coat pocket the porcelain shard she had broken from their mother's plate the night the ledger was corrected. [312]
She dragged the broken white edge through the ink, and the spill opened into ephemeral patterns, thin branching threads that Elia had once read like weather across a saucer. [342]
The static had cleared, so the patterns came clean, and in them Mara saw the shape of the loop: a knot of seventeen heartbeats that would not merge, each one insisting it was the last. [377]
She worked the shard through the spill again and again, dragging the knot apart, and the defragmentation finally began to move, freeing seconds of Elia that had never played. [406]
What came up was not the laugh but the folktale their grandmother told, the drowned woman and the wolf who herds her heartbeats back one by one across the ice. [436]
Elia's voice, younger than Mara remembered, finished it the way she always did: silence is not the same as absence, and a count is not the same as a life. [466]
That was the wisdom she had come to fetch from failure: the loop was not broken machinery, it was grief refusing to finish, and you do not defragment grief by cutting it. [498]
The count under her tongue had gone ragged and then quiet, and for the first time since she was nineteen she did not know the number, and it did not matter. [529]
So she let it keep one heartbeat, the seventeenth, and set the rest free into the dome where the snow pressed its blue light down through the opal. [557]
Her own count had broken somewhere in the work, so she pressed the shard's edge into her thumb until a single dark bead rose, and began again at one. [586]
The receiver gave one last wash of static, and under it, faint as a wolf's track, something answered her number. [606]
Mara sat down in the opal light with the shard in her lap and let the snow finish the night for her, counting, counting, no longer alone in the arithmetic. [636]
