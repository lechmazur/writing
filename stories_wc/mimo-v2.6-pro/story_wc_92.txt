The lamps of the Meridian Bastion died at the hour Rhivane had told them to die. [16]
Nobody knew why the blackout fell, and nobody would ever learn. [27]
One moment the lamp-corridors ran gold; the next, only the quartz veins in the wall held light, faint and drowned-green, for this was a luminous fort and twilight always left it glowing from within. [61]
Rhivane, vigilant lock sentinel of the west stair, did not move for a long breath. [76]
Her hand had already been on the third ward of the door when the dark came, and she finished the turn anyway. [98]
Locks do not care about the dark. [105]
She went up to the west parapet, where the half-painted sundial stood. [117]
Its gnomon threw a shadow across numerals no mason had ever finished. [129]
That was the beauty of it. [135]
An unfinished face was a face that could hold other words. [146]
Tonight the shadow leaned where it should not have, and the coded angles spelled four names, a stair number, and the word for patience. [170]
The angles were meant to intrigue a reader, not shout at one; she read them twice, calmly inventive, and let the hush between readings do its own winking. [198]
On her sill, where she had left nothing, sat the bronze quill holder. [211]
It had been her mother's, hollow at the base, and she had carried it on her belt since girlhood. [230]
Someone had taken it off her and set it back down full. [242]
Inside, wrapped in a brass-edged page of angles, lay enough flash-stone to light a hundred wicks. [258]
Below, in the lamp-court, the youngest apprentices were already forming the cradle of sparks. [272]
Four to a brace, each child cupped one live wick between two gloved palms and passed it along without a single spoken word. [295]
It was old drill, made for exactly this hour, and Rhivane had helped write it. [310]
She came down the stair with the quill holder in both hands and let them see it. [327]
The eldest apprentice understood first; her mouth shaped no word, only a small round of air. [343]
Rhivane tipped a pinch of flash-stone onto the lead wick to spark it, and the cradle answered. [360]
Flame ran hand to hand down the court, and where it passed, the locked doors of the granary, the armoury, the treasury, and the apprentice dormitory swung open before the sentries on them could even turn a key. [398]
Rhivane had spent two years filing each of those wards so that one right angle of torque, and no more, would free them. [421]
This was the silent revolution she had wanted, and it had no speeches and no signal horn. [438]
It had fire, relayed quietly, and it had angles. [447]
By the time the Commandant reached the lamp-court with his own lantern, the granary rolls were already being copied in the dark by twenty steady hands. [473]
Nothing was burned. [476]
Nothing was shouted. [479]
Every door stood open in the drowned-green glow, and no lock on the parapet had been broken. [496]
Rhivane was already back at her post, turning the third ward of the west door back to its resting place. [516]
The Commandant found her there, hands steady, eyes on the dark. [527]
"Sentinel," he said. "Who opened the doors?" [534]
"The blackout, sir," she said. "Fear does it every time." [544]
It was a lie shaped like the truth, which is the only kind a winking hush will carry. [562]
He left, unsatisfied, and the quartz veins went on holding their drowned light. [575]
Then Rhivane slid the bronze quill holder back into its old place at her belt, empty now, its work done. [595]
When the lamps were relit an hour before dawn, the garrison found the granary open, the armoury counted, and the treasury exactly two coins lighter, left on the Commandant's desk like a receipt. [628]
Nobody could say who had done it. [635]
The sundial on the west parapet had been repainted that same night, straight through to the last numeral, and no mason would admit to the work. [661]
Rhivane stood her watch, vigilant, and waited for the next dark. [672]
The cradle of sparks had been only a rehearsal; the real hour would come again, and the angles were already being painted over in her head, one by one, by the small quiet children who had learned to carry fire without speaking a word. [716]
