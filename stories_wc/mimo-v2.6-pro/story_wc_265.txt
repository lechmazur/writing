The stray philosopher had walked the rusted walkways of the mechanical rainforest for three years with a small gear in his coat. [22]
He had found it under a cluster of urchins the color of dead stars, and he had kept it because its teeth meshed with nothing he had ever seen. [51]
Every part of this biome turned its neighbor; that was the law. [63]
But the small gear turned nothing and nothing turned it, and the philosopher loved it the way you love a sentence no one agrees with. [88]
After the sixth sense awakened, the hum of insight began to arrive at dusk. [102]
It was not music and not tinnitus; it was the sound of wind through different trees, and each timber had a different weight in his skull. [128]
The brass reeds pitched high and thin. [135]
The silverleaf boles pitched low and slow, like a door being closed by someone who forgives you. [152]
The tin-bark columns pitched a third way, a wet metallic dragging he could feel in his teeth. [169]
He learned that the hum told him where he would land. [180]
To unravel the sound was to know the road. [189]
The first time he woke somewhere else, he was lying on a floor of copper litter under a canopy that had no sky beneath it. [214]
He translocates during sleep; he translocates during sleep, always, and the hum is the only warning he gets. [232]
By the third leap he had stopped trying to explain it to the walking parts. [247]
Explanations were his old trade and they had never once stopped a gear. [260]
So he took notes instead: brass reeds at dusk meant a short fall to the urchin shelves. [277]
Silverleaf at dawn meant the lit catwalk near the maintenance towers, where the rain came down in calibrated drops. [296]
He traveled for the reason he had always traveled, which was to rediscover the wild frontier of his mind before the biome mapped it too. [321]
But the tin-bark kept dragging at the edge of every night, and he knew it meant the deep strata, the layers where the cables hummed louder than the wind. [350]
On the seventh dusk he did not fight it. [359]
He slept on the walkway with the small gear in his fist and woke falling. [374]
A hundred cables caught him around the ribs and waist, and they were warm, and they were humming his own name in the third timbre. [399]
He thought he was the one coming apart. [407]
For a whole night he lay in the mesh and believed he was unraveling into the rain, strand by strand, philosophy first. [429]
Then the cables began to leave him. [436]
The humming unraveled as they went, and he understood it was not him at all. [451]
The deep strata had been holding a joint together, badly, for a long time, and his fall had loosened the last tooth. [473]
Something above him had stopped moving. [479]
In the morning he climbed to the maintenance towers and found the great metronome of the upper canopy still at last. [500]
Its pendulum hung dead, and the vineyard of pipes above it groaned and sagged. [514]
The engineers had lost the part that rode the swing; the biome could not replace it because every tooth had a neighbor. [536]
He opened his fist. [540]
The small gear meshed with nothing because it was the one free part, and a free part is the only thing that can join two systems that have stopped speaking. [570]
He seated it with the handle of his knife and gave the pendulum a push. [585]
It began to click, and the rain around the towers came down in time again. [600]
That was a probable miracle: nothing supernatural, only a part that fit because it had never been broken to fit. [620]
The hum of insight arrived at dusk with the third timbre, and for the first time it had no direction. [640]
He lay down on the boards and chose to sleep. [650]
Wherever he translocates during sleep tonight, he will wake listening. [660]
The small gear stays with the metronome, and the philosopher walks on, unraveling nothing, carrying only the wind through different trees in his head. [684]
