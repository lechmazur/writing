Tamsin Vey had been a deckhand on the Larkspur for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once let a crossing end badly. [26] The ferry ran from Port Ashen to the far shore in twelve minutes, a tidy distance she knew by the rhythm of the engines alone. [51] She loved it the way some people love weather: absolutely, and without asking it to change. [67] On the night the Larkspur vanished, the clocks began to run sideways. [79] The second hand and the hour hand traded places in the wheelhouse, and the crossing stretched past every measurement anyone had. [100] One moment the deck was crowded and the next the whole ship was gone out of the world like a held breath. [122] Tamsin was not aboard it. [127] She had gone down for the spare lamp and come back up into silence. [141] Below the route, under the black water where the tide pools keep starlight even at noon, there is a grotto that the Larkspur passes over twice a run. [169] Tamsin found it that night because it was the only place that remembered the ship. [184] The spectral grotto held the Larkspur the way a shell holds the sound of the sea: faintly, and as evidence. [204] In her pocket she carried an empty hourglass, sandless and cold, left to her by her grandfather, who had worked the same run. [227] It had one rule, and she had learned it young: it fills only at the seam between two runs of time. [248] She went to work, because working is what a deckhand does. [259] She radioed Port Ashen and got her own voice answering three seconds late, cheerful as a lark. [276] She radioed the far shore and heard the harbor master ordering breakfast he would never eat. [292] Neither answered her question, so she stopped asking questions and started counting. [304] The way to find a vanished thing is by interconnected patterns, and she had three. [319] First, the lamps she could still see from the grotto wall, which were eleven, then nine, then eleven again. [338] Second, the three seconds her radio took to answer any hail. [349] Third, the nicks her grandfather had cut into the rail to mark crossings, which she counted to two hundred and eight and then found counted themselves. [375] When the three counts agreed, the grotto opened like a mouth and showed her the missing half of the run. [395] There the Larkspur hung in starlight with all hands aboard her, waiting out an interval that had no end to be measured in. [418] She understood then what she wanted, and it was not only rescue. [430] Two runs of the same crossing were standing side by side, one drowned and one saved, and the crew would be half of themselves forever unless the two were made one. [461] Her motive was that plain: she meant to merge parallel timelines. [472] Tamsin keyed the radio and held it steady while the sideways clocks lined up for the second time that night. [492] At the seam, the empty hourglass filled. [499] The sand in it was fine gray ash, and it fell both ways at once. [514] She spoke into the handset a message so ordinary that it could only have been meant for a ship: all clear, come alongside, watch your step. [540] The two runs folded together without a sound, the way two halves of a card fold, and the grotto was suddenly only a wet cave full of tide pools and stars. [571] The Larkspur came home with everybody aboard her, crossing exactly twelve minutes and not one more. [587] They found Tamsin still on the wall with the radio in her hand, and they cheered her as if she had done something difficult. [611] She had, but that was not what stayed with her. [621] What stayed with her was the hourglass, which went empty again the moment the seam closed and stayed empty. [640] There was no passenger manifest that listed her, no payroll that had ever paid her, no name painted on the crew board. [662] The grotto had taken its price at the seam, and the price was the one thing the crossing could not carry across. [684] Tamsin stayed on, because a permanent transient is still crew. [694] She works the deck every night, and she keeps the empty hourglass in her pocket, and when the clocks run sideways she radios the far shore to say that all is well. [726] She is cheerful about it, the way she is cheerful about weather. [738] Every crossing is twelve minutes for everybody else, and for her it is as long as it takes. [756] That is enough. She would not trade it. [764]
