Inez Marrow mapped routes the way other people kept diaries, in pencil so fine the lines resembled hair laid on paper. [21] She was a meticulous route mapper by trade and by nerve, and the city trusted her for that, which is why the transit board hired her to chart the last unfinished spur out to the northern yards. [58] The spur had no timetable and no station, only a legend about a train that stopped once at the hour that divides bad habits and new starts. [85] Inez kept a phantom train ticket punch in her coat pocket, a thing of cold nickel and a jaw that closed on air, and she had spent years pressing it into blank strips to mark the routes the board never filed. [126] Each hole was a stop no one had named. [135] Her father had gone looking for the fortress of unspoken truths and had not come back, and Inez had been mapping ever since, because a route is a question that admits of an answer. [169] On the third night of the fog she found the first arrow in the cracks of the pavement outside the yard gate. [191] It was not damage; the fracture ran true as a drawn line, and it bent where a compass would bend. [211] She was analytically artistic enough to see that a thing could be both broken and composed, and she crouched and traced it with a gloved thumb and felt the geometry hold. [242] The second arrow waited two streets on, and the third in the gravel of the tram cut, and she understood the cracks in the pavement were forming arrows on purpose, a message laid down by whoever built the spur. [281] Here she dared herself. [285] She tore a fresh strip from her pad, pressed the phantom punch through it three times to match the three vertices of the arrow, and dared the fog to prove her wrong. [317] Nothing laughed at her, which she counted as agreement. [326] So she walked, and the mist closed behind her like a hand, and every thirty paces the ground gave up another fracture that bent the way she needed to go. [356] She punched each one onto the strip, and the holes began to read like a route, stop after unnamed stop, and the unwritten spur took shape in her hands. [385] She felt a trembling certainty in her chest, the kind that shakes and does not let go, and she kept walking because turning back would be a bad habit and the hour was nearly spent. [420] At the last arrow the pavement gave way to packed earth, and the earth gave way to steps. [438] The steps ran down into a starlit vault in mist, a chamber where the fog hung thin and the ceiling was all cold pinpricks of light, as if the sky had been brought indoors and pinned there. [475] Under those lights sat the fortress of unspoken truths, and it was not stone at all but a long low platform of poured concrete with a single bench and a single lamp. [507] On the bench lay a ticket, punched clean through with the same three holes Inez carried on her strip. [526] She held the two up together and the light came through both, and the route matched, and the mystery unraveled at last into this: her father had found this place, boarded the train that stopped here, and gone on to wherever the unspoken truths are kept. [572] The ticket was not a claim or a warning; it was a handoff, left for the one meticulous enough to follow the arrows down. [596] So Inez sat on the bench, folded her new map into her coat beside the punch, and dared the hour to end. [618] It did, and somewhere in the vault a mechanism began to turn, and the mist took on the shape of something arriving. [640] She did not reach for her punch, because some routes you are meant to ride rather than to record. [659] She waited with trembling certainty, and the ticket in her hand grew warm. [672]</story>
