The viaduct had been leaking since before anyone remembered, and Hal was the one they sent down to measure it. [20] He was a lukewarm mage, which meant he could not hold fire in his hands long enough to finish any serious work. [42] What he could do was count drops. [49] So he crouched on the sapphire viaduct at dawn with a tin cup and a notebook, catching the water as it wept from the stone ribs. [75] The drips came slow that morning, slower than yesterday, and he wrote that down. [89]

His master, who was dead, had taught him the old accounting: every drop that fell from the viaduct was one fish taken out of the sea. [115] The viaduct was the pipe between sky and ocean, and a leaking pipe draws more than it owes. [133] Hal had measured for two years now, and he knew the rate was falling toward zero. [149] When the last drop fell and the last fish was caught, the debt would stand unpaid and the sea would remember it. [171] That was the cosmic imbalance he meant to right, and it was the only thing he had ever been earnestly naive enough to believe he could fix. [198]

The remedy was a bell. [203] Down at the tide-mark lay the wreck of a ship that had come in wrong, and in its ribs hung what was left of its bell, cracked clean into four pieces and salted green. [237] Hal carried the shards up the viaduct in his coat, holding them against his chest like something warm. [255] A bell rung true over the water would close the pipe's mouth and teach the sea its due. [273] He stood the four shards in a ring of clay and began to rebuild it at first light, because dawn was when the stone gave up its heat willingly. [302]

This is where a warm mage would have been enough, and Hal was not warm. [317] He drew heat out of his own palms and laid it on the bronze in long slow passes, and each pass cooled the metal a little and cooled him a little more. [349] The first pour cracked. [353] The second pour held the seams but left one thin as thread, and he knew it would not survive a real ring. [375] He brazed it anyway, and the work cost him the feeling in his left hand for a week. [393] That seam was his devotion made visible, and it was brittle in the way that things are when they are true and tired at once. [418] He ground the lip true with a stone until it rang clear when he struck it with his knuckle. [437]

By the time he climbed to the crown of the viaduct the tide-mark was bare, and there was no sound from the nets at all. [462] The last fish had been caught an hour before, and he had not been there to see it. [480] The cup sat on the ledge where he had left it, empty, and the stone above it was dry. [499] Whatever the sea was going to do, it had started. [509] Hal hung the bell from the outer span on a rope of his master's chain and waited for the sun to clear the water. [533]

When the light came it came flat and green, and the sea below had stopped moving. [549] He pulled the rope. [553] The bell rang once, and the sound was wrong, thin at the seam, exactly as he had feared, and he rang it again anyway, and again, putting his whole lukewarm body into the pulls. [587] On the ninth ring the seam gave and the bell split along the line of his brazing, and the two halves fell swinging against the stone. [613] But the nine notes had gone out across the flat green water, and they did not stop at the horizon. [633]

What came back was not fish, at first. [641] It was a pressure in the water, and then a slow grinding of the viaduct's ribs, and then the drips began again, one and then another, from the mouths of the stone. [673] Hal held his cup under the first of them and felt the water come warm, which is impossible, and felt it level out at a steady rate, which is not. [703]

His left hand ached for a month and never quite came right, and he thought of the fish exactly once a day after that. [727] He kept the two halves of the bell on the ledge beside his cup, and every dawn he measured the dripping rate and wrote it in the notebook. [755] It held steady at twelve drops a minute, and the sea went on owing, and the world went on holding. [775] That was the whole of it: a broken bell, a broken hand, and a number that stopped falling. [793]
