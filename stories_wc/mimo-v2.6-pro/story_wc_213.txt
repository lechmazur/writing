<story>Aldwin climbed the last stair of the bell tower with his notebook tucked under one arm and the taste of copper in his mouth from the cold. [27] The windblown sanctuary above the clouds held no congregation at this hour, only the spliced rope, the bell, and the faint hiss of the siren housing cooling itself. [55] He was a humble storm siren tester, and he had chosen the solstice hush because the valley keeps its quietest air on the longest night, which makes small errors audible. [85] His credibly curious habit was to press his ear to a thing before he trusted it, so he set the notebook on the parapet and listened to the cathedral bell rope breathe against the stone. [120] It had been spliced that afternoon, and the repair crew had told him to wait until after the glue dries before anyone pulled it, so the fibers sat stiff and new, smelling faintly of resin. [155] In the notebook he had been tracking microclimate variations all week, the way the dew point climbed just before the cloud sea settled, the way a shear layer formed level with the bell's mouth. [189] Tonight the numbers were strange in a way he trusted more than any forecast, because the pressure fell half a millibar in the hour and then held flat, as if the sky were holding its breath with him. [227] Zero hour was the marker he had set for himself, the minute the siren was scheduled to sound a single clean tone over the sleeping towns. [253] He checked the harness the crew had fitted into its frame, the switch sewn to his left strap, the feed cable running from the siren amplifier to the weather-gear terminal at his hip. [286] His motive was simple and hard to explain to a man who ran a loudspeaker yard: he wanted to favor listening over noise, to make the warning a thing the valley heard rather than a thing that drowned the valley out. [327] The test called for a blast at full gain, three seconds long, loud enough to leave an ache in the teeth, and the crew would log the number and call the night a success. [361] But the microclimate said otherwise, because at this hour the cold air pooling in the valley would swallow a straight tone whole and hand it back three times over the ridges, a stutter that would wake people for nothing. [400] He had learned to respect that stutter years ago on a hillside above a dairy town, where a miscalculated blast sent sheep through a fence and a boy to the door in his coat, and no forecast had warned anybody about it. [442] So he made his decision the way he always did, quietly and without ceremony: he reached to the harness and flipped the switch. [465] The feed rerouted from the street loudspeakers to the tower's microphone array, so the siren would sound into instruments instead of into houses, and the tower itself would listen. [494] At zero hour the tone rose anyway, but out here on the platform it was only a vibration in the rope and a soft white bloom behind his sinuses, nothing that would carry far. [528] The arrays caught the ridges answering him, three returns exactly as his notes had predicted, and he wrote them down in the dark with a pencil he had warmed in his glove. [560] He thought of Pell, the old bell-keeper, who had kept this rota for twenty years and told him the tower says more when you let it finish. [587] Down in the cloud sea the towns stayed dark and quiet, and Aldwin stood at the parapet a long time, feeling the cold carry nothing at all toward them. [616] There would be a complaint in the morning, a form to sign, a note that the public test had been rerouted without authorization, but the data would stand and the valley would sleep straight through the hour. [653] He coiled nothing, touched nothing, and left the rope where it hung, still curing, because a warning worth hearing is one that has already practiced at silence. [680] By the time the glue finished drying in the cold, he was already halfway down the stair, listening to the tower settle above him the way an instrument settles after it has been tuned.
