Selia called herself an esoteric realist, meaning she believed in every invisible thing that left a visible dent in the world. [21]
She kept a shard of moth wing pressed between two panes of glass, its gray scales like rain-light, the only proof she had ever once been a child. [28]
For years she had carried it up the mountain to the verdant grotto above the clouds, where the air tasted of wet fern and the white floor of sky drifted beneath her feet like a slow pale river. [38]
Now, on the last warm morning of the migration, she climbed to build her signals again. [103]
The grotto was a hollow of green stone and hanging roots, a room the mountain had grown around one drop of silence. [125]
Selia was transiently rooted there the way a pilgrim is rooted in a doorway: planted, breathing, already half gone. [144]
She had no monument to raise and no state to petition, only the older work of making temporary structures as signals: arches of birch, ladders of vine, little roofs of moss that would stand for a week and mean something to whoever came after. [188]
Today she meant to honor the overlooked histories: the mill girls who swallowed their names, the grandmothers who catalogued moths by lamplight and died before anyone read a single page. [218]
Her grandmother had been one of them, a night-watcher who wrote in pencil so the dark would not notice, and who left behind a box of wings and no explanation. [248]
Into the green mouth of the grotto came the last monarch butterfly, orange as a coal in the shade. [267]
It staggered on the wind and settled on the glass with the moth wing inside it, and Selia held her breath until the whole valley seemed to blush. [295]
That was the tone she wanted from this place, the blushing stillness of a thing being seen for the first time. [316]
She began to work, and the work had a cadence: cut, notch, tie, lift. [330]
Cut, notch, tie, lift. [334]
Then the wind tore the arch from her hands and rolled it to the edge of the white river below, and her rhythm broke mid-syllable. [359]
The interrupted cadence left her standing very still, a bell struck and not allowed to ring. [375]
She did not curse; an esoteric realist knows that accidents are only messages with the address smudged. [392]
Instead she cut a length of vine and tied a knot so deliberate it looked like a word in a language of grasses. [415]
The knot was her punctuation, and around it she raised a small house of branches no taller than her knee, its roof a single flat stone. [441]
Under the stone she laid the moth wing, and beside it a sprig of alpine thyme, and on the stone she scratched nine names she had rescued from old ledgers. [471]
The monarch walked across the flat stone on its stained-glass legs and paused at the ninth name, and Selia felt the strange courtesy of being witnessed. [497]
She had once tried to carve names into permanent stone and found that the stone refused, as if it knew it would outlive the meaning. [522]
She worked on in the blushing stillness, building her signals the way sailors build buoys: to say I was here, the water is deep, mind your course. [549]
By evening the grotto held a crooked village of three arches, two ladders, and the small house of knots, each one a sentence in a history nobody had bothered to keep. [580]
Selia sat down among them, transiently rooted again, and let the wind resume the cadence she had broken. [598]
Cut, notch, tie, lift. [602]
Tomorrow a stranger might read the knot and understand that somebody, somewhere, had loved the things history dropped. [620]
In the morning the monarch would leave, and her structures would soften and fall, and that was the point of them. [641]
A signal that lasts forever is only a wall; Selia's signals lasted just long enough to be read by a stranger who happened to climb above the clouds. [669]
She blew out her lamp and lay still, listening to the last orange traveler breathe on the stone, and the grotto blushed in the dark. [694]
Then the wind took up cut, notch, tie, lift, and carried it out over the clouds like a slow hymn for the unnamed. [717]
