It is the season after luck becomes measurable, and the garden maze wears brass dials like fruit. [17]
Maren, the withered vine dresser, is wisely pragmatic about every number. [28]
Her apron is stitched from shed bark, and her gloves are worn to soft maps of roots. [45]
Her collection of marbles, cracked, waits in a tin by the hedge bench. [58]
Each marble is a mended deception, smoothed by care instead of hidden. [70]
Her habit is commemorating small victories elaborately, with candles, leaves, and whispered praise. [83]
After each tiny success, she writes a date in chalk, pins a ribbon to a leaf, and lets the path remember. [104]
She will polish a marble only when the truth she has broken has been repaired. [119]
Since the brass dials appeared, people judge seeds, marriages, and funerals by a thin red needle. [135]
A boy named Cael stands at the west gate, his dial glowing four. [148]
He has no family plot, no old name, and the dial says the maze will not favor him. [166]
Four is bad luck, and the hedge in front of him stiffens like a closed door. [182]
Can the garden make me lucky, he asks, because the maze is blooming withering around him. [198]
Maren does not answer at once, for her motive is to prove that gardens know their gardeners. [215]
She shows him a withered vine and says, Touch the part that still wants to grow. [231]
He touches it, and one dry tip leans toward his hand like a slow question. [246]
Maren counts that as a small victory and lights a candle in a clay cup. [261]
She places a cracked marble in the path and says, This is for the first thing the garden noticed. [280]
Cael laughs, but his dial blinks from four to five, and he stops laughing. [294]
Maren tells him that the marble holds a mended deception, her own from last spring. [309]
Then she had told visitors that she never lost a vine to frost. [322]
The truth was that a row died, and she mended the lie by naming each name in her log. [341]
Cael waters the base of the vine, and the clay cup flame steadies. [354]
Maren commemorates this victory elaborately, drawing a line of sand before it and bowing twice. [369]
The hedge opens a hand, not much, enough for a shoulder to pass. [382]
His dial becomes seven, and he asks if the number is real luck. [395]
Maren tells him that luck is weather, while gardening is weather plus choice, which is harder and safer. [413]
It is only what the garden can measure, Maren says, and gardens measure care first. [428]
He ties a dead vine to a live stem, and the brass leaf on the gate turns green. [446]
She will polish the second marble before the light fails. [456]
The boy now tends the maze, and his dial climbs to nine, though he forgets the number. [473]
At dusk, the roses bloom and wither in the same minute, a blooming withering that seems kind. [490]
Maren adds a third marble, the one for the deception she told the luck office. [505]
She had claimed the maze answered only to luck, and she mended that claim by this proof. [522]
The final dial on the gate changes from nine to a word: known. [535]
The word is not carved by him, but by the hedges, which have been reading him all morning. [553]
Cael reads it twice, then touches the withered vine, which opens a single pale flower. [568]
Maren sets the third marble beside the first, and the tin is almost empty. [582]
She has one marble left, cracked and bright, for the day she can no longer lie. [598]
The garden maze stands in the dark, blooming withering, patient as a true teacher. [612]
Luck is measurable, but the garden knows more, and its knowing is enough. [625]
