After the water wars, the village had learned to read the sea's anger in the color of its foam. [19]
An insomniac painter named Mara kept a lean studio above the fish market, where salt chewed the corners of every canvas. [40]
Her tidal island was a bruise of black rock and shifting sand, accessible only when the tide allowed a path of wet stone. [63]
When rain came, Mara went out by following rain trails, because old grief, she thought, always left a slicker road. [83]
She spoke of art in a wryly earnest way, as if the universe had made a mistake worth correcting with brushes. [104]
She painted not to beautify the ruin but to thresh meaning from experience, to beat chaff from the memory of drowned streets. [126]
Her mother had left her an amber brooch, a small trapped sunrise sewn into a coat she no longer owned. [146]
The brooch had been found after a flood broke open the chapel floor, part of what the elders called the unearthed convergence. [168]
Mara wore it now in haggard awe, believing it held a map to the buried meeting of rivers and roots. [188]
Each night, she made an exchange: one brushstroke for one remembered voice, one thin line for one name returned. [207]
One storm, a crack in the chapel wall let in a wind that smelled of iron and wet cedar. [226]
Mara traced the drip line with her lamp and found a symbol carved beneath the moss, a spiral with a pinched throat. [248]
The symbol matched the back of her amber brooch, though she had never seen it there before. [265]
She took the brooch, pressed it against the stone, and felt a faint pulse like a second heartbeat. [283]
Rain began to bead along the wall, and the beads moved against the pull of gravity. [299]
By following rain trails, she stepped into a passage that the tide had kept for centuries. [315]
The passage opened under the sea into the unearthed convergence, a dry room of packed sand and old bones, not human, but shaped with care. [340]
At the center stood a basin of black glass, and in it lay a seed the size of a thumb. [360]
Around the basin were carved words: give what the sea has kept, take what the land will grow. [378]
Mara understood the exchange as a test, not of wealth, but of willingness to let one thing go. [396]
The brooch was her last proof that her mother had survived the water wars, and part of her feared to release it. [418]
She thought of her canvases, full of houses standing on water, and of names she had failed to write. [437]
To thresh meaning from experience, she needed a truth that could not be painted alone. [452]
So she put the amber brooch into the black basin and watched the water close over it without a ripple. [472]
The room answered with a sound like a breath, and the seed floated up, pale and warm. [489]
Mara lifted it from the glass and found it pressed into her palm, as if her hand had always held a root. [511]
When dawn came, the tide had withdrawn enough to show the island's true line, a spiral matching the brooch. [530]
She painted it then, not with fear but in haggard awe, with the seed in one hand and her brush in the other. [553]
The canvas showed the island as a body, the chapel as a crown, and the sea as a patient question. [573]

Mara finally slept, though not from ease, but because the island had taken what needed taking. [606]
