<story>Mara had spent twenty years as a cuneiform translator in the low archives, reading the pressed grief of dead kings. [20] She was kindly methodical, the sort of woman who repaired torn tablets with rice paste and left tea for the night cleaners. [42] After the spirit leaves, the old texts said, the clay remembers the shape of breath. [57] She had never understood what that meant until the day her brother Tomas died. [71] His ambition had haunted him all his life, a bright and restless hunger to build a structure that would never fall. [92] Those haunted ambitions had drawn him skyward, to the project the archive called the Aerie. [107] It was a magnetic arbor above the clouds, a lattice of ironwood and lodestone suspended by the planet's quiet field. [127] Tomas had been its chief architect, and Mara now stood beneath its arch for the first time since his funeral. [147] The wind passed through it without resistance, and every leaf on its vines trembled with a faint metallic hum. [166] In her palm she held the only thing he had left her in his workshop, a knowledge fruit seed no larger than a thumbnail. [190] The fruit was said to grow only in places where thought had been held for a long time, and to store what it had witnessed. [215] She had come not to mourn, but to understand the physics of transition, the exact moment when presence becomes absence. [235] The priests said the spirit simply vanished, but Tomas had taught her that nothing vanishes without exchange. [252] She knelt and pressed her hand to the cold plating of the arbor floor, feeling its pull like a second gravity. [273] It was touching distance, close enough that she could smell the iron and hear her own pulse in her ears. [293] Grief, she realized, was not distant or abstract but a pressure against the skin. [307] Through analogical thinking, the way she had always solved broken tablets, she began to work the problem. [324] If a sentence was missing its verb, she looked to the sentence beside it and guessed the shape. [342] If a life was missing its end, perhaps the structure around it could tell her how it broke. [360] She examined the arbor the way she examined clay, tracing stress lines and noting where the magnetic field wavered. [379] Near the eastern strut, the vines had grown thick, but no fruit had ever formed there until now. [397] The lodestone hummed unevenly, and she understood that Tomas had died trying to reestablish the balance. [413] A storm three weeks earlier had tilted the core, and he had climbed alone to reset it. [430] His notes, which she now carried in her satchel, ended mid-syllable, as if interrupted. [444] She took the knowledge fruit seed and found the spot where the field was weakest, a shallow hollow in the ironwood. [465] With kindly methodical care, she cleared the dust, lined the hollow with soft lichen, and set the seed down. [484] She poured a little water from her flask and whispered the old cuneiform word for return, ba-a-da-an-gi4. [501] Nothing happened at first, and the clouds moved slow and indifferent below. [513] Then the seed shivered and sent a pale root outward, not into soil but into the magnetic field itself. [532] It glowed faintly as it drank the imbalance, visibly smoothing the hum of the arbor. [547] Mara felt the floor steady beneath her knees, and for a moment she felt Tomas beside her, not as a spirit but as an intention completed. [573] She understood then that transition was not a tearing but a handoff, like passing a tablet from one scribe to the next. [595] The weight did not disappear, it moved, and the field held it for someone else to carry. [612] Tears came, but they were quiet and warm, and she let them fall onto the ironwood where the seed had taken hold. [634] In time the seed would become a fruit that would hold this moment, and another translator might one day taste it and know her. [658] That was enough. [661] She stayed until sunset, watching the clouds turn amber beneath the arbor, feeling the field hold her as gently as it held the seed. [685]</story>
