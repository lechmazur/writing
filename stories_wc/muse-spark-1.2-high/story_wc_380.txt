<story>Mara had been the dock-side lantern lighter for thirty years before the harbor finally went dark. [16] After the whale beaches, no ships came, and the town left its pier to rust beneath an empty sky. [35] In the gleaming silence that followed, even the gulls stopped crying and the water held perfectly still. [52] Mara kept lighting the lanterns anyway, because someone had to remember how the light used to guide people home. [71] Her secret was that the light no longer worked the way it should, since the air above the bay now held pockets of gravity where nothing behaved normally. [99] A dropped coin might hang suspended for hours, and a lantern flame might burn sideways or not at all. [118] The weight of things fluctuates mysteriously with the tide, and Mara learned to time her rounds to when the air felt heaviest. [140] She was not only keeping the harbor lit, but trying to find where deleted memories go after her sister's illness took whole years from her mind. [166] The doctors said the memories were simply gone, erased like chalk, but Mara knew nothing ever truly disappears in a place that bends physics. [190] Old Jonah at the cannery told her that if she wanted answers, she would have to exhume the town archive buried beneath the salt flats. [215] It was sealed after the whale beaches, when the council decided to forget the year the water turned black and the children dreamed the same dream. [241] Mara waited for low gravity and dug through the crust until she found the metal door, pulling it open with hands that suddenly weighed almost nothing. [267] Inside was not paper but a salt crystal growing chamber, a low humming room where the town had grown its records as living crystals for a century. [294] Each crystal held a year, and each flaw inside was a memory someone had asked to have deleted. [312] Mara found the year her sister was born, and saw it was cloudy with uncut facets, humming faintly in the gleaming silence. [334] She did not know how to read crystal, so she carried one small shard to the woman who read futures through patterns in coffee grounds at the market. [362] The woman, whose name was Eli, ground the crystal into the coffee and poured hot water, watching how the salt and grounds swirled together. [386] Eli said deleted memories do not vanish but settle, like sediment, into the lowest pockets of gravity where the world is heaviest. [408] That was why the harbor felt heavier some nights, because it was full. [421] Mara asked if she could get her sister's memories back, and Eli warned that to return a memory you must trade one of your own, equal in weight. [449] Mara thought of her pressed wildflowers, kept for twenty years in a book, the only thing she had left from the summer her sister taught her to name every bloom on the cliff. [482] That summer was her clearest memory, and therefore her heaviest, the one that anchored her when gravity fluctuated. [500] She decided to trade it, and Eli stirred the cup again until the grounds formed a perfect circle around a single bright fleck of salt. [525] Drink, Eli said, and Mara did, feeling the memory of wildflowers dissolve on her tongue like sugar and salt at once. [546] For a moment she could not recall the cliff or the flowers at all, and the gleaming silence inside her head was absolute. [569] Then the crystal on the table pulsed, and a pocket of gravity behind her opened with a soft sigh, releasing something that had been held for years. [596] Mara turned to see her sister standing in the doorway of the salt crystal growing chamber, eyes wide, remembering the name of every bloom. [620] I remembered, her sister whispered, the sea lavender and the cliff rose, and how you used to press them so carefully you would hold your breath. [646] Mara smiled though she could not remember doing it, and found tears on her cheeks without knowing why they had come. [667] She took her sister's hand, and together they walked back toward the dock, where the lanterns still burned sideways in their pockets of gravity, steady and strange. [694] The harbor was still silent, but it was no longer empty, and Mara the dock-side lantern lighter knew her work was finally finished. [717]</story>
