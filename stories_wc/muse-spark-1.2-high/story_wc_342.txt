<story>Mara had been a face blind portrait artist for twenty years, famous for paintings that captured people she could never recognize on the street. [24] She did not paint from memory of features but from measurements, shadows, and the way light fell across a stranger's cheek. [45] The commission that finally undid her came from the city, which wanted her to craft a final mural inside the old Arboretum before it was torn down. [72] The Arboretum was not a greenhouse but a tree trunk library hollowed by a scholar's guild a century ago, its circular walls lined with shelves carved directly into living wood. [102] The city had condemned the building after the great oak died, and she arrived at the hush just before a condemned building is demolished, when the wrecking crew had stepped back for their last break. [137] Inside, the air held a heightened stillness, as if the tree itself were holding its breath. [153] The guild's last archivist, an old man named Tomas, met her among the empty shelves and explained what they really wanted her to do. [177] They did not want portraits of scholars; they wanted her to mirror the sky inside walls that had never seen open light. [199] For decades the scholars had studied astronomy from within the trunk, charting constellations they could only imagine through narrow slits in the bark. [222] Their dream was fractally precise, a ceiling that reproduced every star, cluster, and nebula at exact scale so that children below would feel they stood under the real heavens. [251] Mara walked the curved wall and felt the familiar panic of discovering limits, the line where her talent for copying faces ended and true invention must begin. [278] She had always hidden her condition, tracing photographs with calipers because she could not hold a face in her mind. [298] Now she was asked to paint something she had never truly seen whole, a sky she could not memorize any better than a smile. [322] Tomas led her to the center of the hollow and showed her a secret book safe, a hollowed folio that had sat unnoticed on the shelf for fifty years. [351] Inside it lay no book at all, but a single centuries-old coin, black with age, and a folded sheet of vellum covered in calculations. [375] The scholars had worked out how to map the heavens through faint indentations on a centuries-old coin, using its worn surface as a stamp for star positions. [402] Each tiny pit and scratch on the coin corresponded to a star, pressed into the metal by generations of careful hammering with a needle. [426] By inking the coin and rolling it across plaster, they could transfer a perfect, repeatable field of stars, larger or smaller depending on the pressure. [451] Mara understood at once; she would not have to imagine the sky, she could craft it mechanically, honestly, without pretending to remember. [473] She set to work as the demolition clock ticked, mixing a pale night-blue plaster and preparing the curved wall. [492] The heightened stillness deepened as she rolled the inked coin in slow arcs, its indentations kissing the wet surface and leaving constellations behind. [515] Every pass revealed more light points, fractally precise in their spacing, spiraling outward from the coin's center like frost. [534] She did not need to recognize the patterns like other artists might; she needed only to trust the method and keep her hand steady. [558] Tomas watched and whispered that the guild had accepted discovering limits as their core philosophy, that knowledge was not about seeing everything but knowing the edge of sight. [586] Mara felt tears for the first time in her studio, not from failure but from relief, because the coin gave her a way to be accurate without being able to remember. [617] The wall began to mirror the sky inside walls, a deep vault of blue flecked with silver that made the dead tree feel alive again. [642] Outside, the foreman called out that the hush was ending and the cables would soon pull. [658] Tomas begged her to leave, but Mara made one final pass, pressing the coin to the highest point of the ceiling to place Polaris. [682] She stepped down, her hands stained, and saw that she had not painted a portrait of the sky but had let the sky paint itself through her. [709] For a face blind portrait artist, that was enough, to be the hand that carries an image without having to hold it. [731] They ran from the tree trunk library hollowed by a scholar's guild as the first cable groaned, dust falling like snow from the starry plaster. [756] The demolition did not feel violent; it felt like a page turning, and the stars she had pressed into the wall cracked open to the real dusk above. [784] Later Tomas would say she had learned to craft not faces, but limitlessness itself. [798]</story>
