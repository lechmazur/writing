<story>In the year when creativity gets automated, Alden was still trying to pass his journeyman trials as an underachieving mage who could barely light a candle without singing the wrong syllable. [31] The Guild had replaced most spellcraft with generative engines that wrote perfect incantations for a fee, and human mages were left to handle only the odd jobs the engines could not parse. [63] Alden did not want to compete with perfection; he wanted to find the sacred in the simple, to know why a boiled kettle or a swept floor could feel more true than a conjured palace. [98] For that reason he accepted the posting no one else wanted, keeper of the icebound spire above the clouds where the old weather observatory had been left to freeze. [127] The spire accommodates impossibilities by design, its foundations anchored in solid stormcloud and its balconies hanging over nothing, and only those who have learned the old ways can even approach it. [158] Alden reached it by mist walking, the slow and outlawed method his grandmother taught him, where you step not through space but between the breaths of fog and let the mountain forget you were ever climbing. [194] He arrived with a pack that was too heavy, his boots crusted with rime, and the caretaker's hut waiting empty except for a single crate marked for the new keeper. [224] Inside the crate was not food or fuel but a garden folly keystone, a carved limestone block the size of a loaf, etched with a laughing sun and the words HERE LIES NOTHING USEFUL. [258] Alden laughed because the spire had no garden, only wind-scoured ice and iron rails, and he thought the previous keeper had left it as a joke. [284] That night the spire settled into a brilliant dimness, the aurora above too bright to look at directly and the corridors too dark to see your hands, yet somehow both at once. [316] In that light he noticed a lingering luminance around the keystone, a faint afterglow that remained for minutes after he shuttered his lantern, as if the stone remembered being in sunlight. [347] He had been taught that lingering luminance was a failed enchantment, light that had nowhere to go, but his grandmother had called it proof that some things refuse to be spent all at once. [381] The next morning he tried to unstrap the keystone from its crate and found it strangely warm, humming faintly against his palms like a quiet beehive. [407] The instructions etched on the crate said to set the stone at the spire's heart, where the wind passed through a hollow column meant to sing when storms came. [436] Alden carried it up the spiraling stairs, his breath short in the thin air, until he reached the open observatory where snow had never been allowed to settle. [464] There was no garden there either, only a shallow basin of black earth that the previous keepers had kept thawed with a low, wasteful heat spell. [490] It accommodates impossibilities, Alden whispered to himself, understanding at last, because the basin should have been frozen solid three thousand feet above the clouds. [514] He placed the garden folly keystone into the basin, not as a keystone for an arch but as a promise, and pressed damp soil around its edges with his cold fingers. [545] Nothing happened at first, and he felt the familiar sting of being an underachieving mage whose best efforts produced no visible result. [567] Then the lingering luminance brightened, not upward like a beacon but downward into the soil, threading light like roots through the dark earth. [590] By midday a single green shoot pushed up beside the stone, impossibly alive in air that could freeze lungs, its leaves edged with that same soft afterglow. [617] Alden understood then that the folly had never been a folly at all, but a quiet rebellion from a time when creativity gets automated and people still wanted to make useless beautiful things by hand. [652] He did not cast a growth spell or call an engine to optimize the sprout; he simply fetched water melted from cloud ice and poured it carefully, watching droplets catch the brilliant dimness. [685] Days passed, and the spire no longer felt like an exile but like a small, necessary task, tending a garden that should not exist and therefore meant everything. [713] When the Guild queried him by signal-mirror, demanding weather data and efficiency reports, he answered with a single pressed leaf that glowed faintly even after the mirror went dark.
