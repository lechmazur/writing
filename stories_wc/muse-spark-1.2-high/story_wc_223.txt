<story>The palace had been empty for thirty seven days during an interregnum, and no one knew who would claim it next. [21] Mara had been hired there twice before, once as a crisis actor real crisis survivor contradiction that no briefing sheet could resolve. [43] Years ago she had recited scripted grief for cameras after a factory fire that never happened, and then a year later she had actually burned her hands pulling her brother from a real fire that no camera saw. [81] That was why they called her back when the ministry needed someone who could cry on cue and also know when not to. [104] She wanted the job for a quieter reason, to become the historian of small gestures when the grand histories had collapsed. [125] The new archivist, an old woman named Selene, was benevolently ruthless, offering tea and then demanding truth without ornament. [144] Selene met her not in an office but in the whispering gallery that ran like a halo around the abandoned council chamber. [166] The gallery had been built so a whisper on one side could be heard clearly on the other, and during an interregnum even the walls seemed to listen for a successor. [197] In the center of the chamber, on a low table, lay a chessboard missing all the pawns, its kings and queens and knights standing in lonely formation. [224] What is this for, Mara asked. [230] Selene said the board had been left by the last council, who in their final session had removed every pawn because they could not decide which people the pawns represented. [260] Mara understood then that the board was not a game but an archive of a luminous absence, the shape left when ordinary lives are taken away. [286] Selene wanted her to unbind the official record, to loosen the knots that tied every citizen's story to either victim or actor. [308] We need someone who knows the difference between performed sorrow and lived sorrow, Selene said, and who will not confuse them. [329] Mara spent three nights in the gallery, learning how sound moved and how silence moved differently, and by the fourth morning she developed a method to record what had never been recorded. [361] She began a pattern of walking that spells words, pacing the curved wall so her footsteps echoed as letters on the opposite side. [384] An archivist on the far end transcribed the taps, not the speeches, but the hesitations between speeches, the way a hand hovered before signing a decree. [410] This was her history of small gestures, a ledger of how people straightened their sleeves before lying and how they touched their thresholds before leaving. [435] Selene listened and corrected her gently and then ruthlessly, insisting that sentiment not blur observation. [450] You are benevolently ruthless too, Mara told her one evening, and Selene smiled because she had been waiting to be seen accurately. [472] On the sixth day, the wind cut through the broken dome and cast a nomadic penumbra that slid slowly across the chessboard, a shadow with no fixed home. [500] Mara watched that penumbra and realized it was the perfect image for the interregnum itself, a half light that belonged to no ruler and to every witness. [527] She stopped performing grief and began documenting it, walking her alphabet of steps until the gallery whispered back not her own grief but dozens of others. [553] A guard remembered adjusting his collar before abandoning his post, a cook remembered leaving a pot of soup on the stove for whoever came next, a child remembered tying a ribbon to a door that would never open again. [592] Each memory was a pawn that had been missing, and Mara placed them not on the board but beside it, honoring their refusal to be played. [618] When the new government finally arrived on the seventh day, they found no grand proclamation in the gallery, only a woman who had walked words into stone and an old woman who had guarded a board without pawns. [656] The officials asked for a record of the transition, and Selene offered them Mara's ledger, warning that it was benevolently ruthless and would not flatter anyone.
