<story>Mara had been a crater inspector for twelve years, long enough to read the moon's scars like braille. [18] During the space age, that meant she was rarely on Earth, and even more rarely trusted to work without a handler. [39] But her handlers had learned she was purposefully curious, not recklessly so, and they let her take the solo assignment on Tranquillitatis Ridge. [62] The mission was simple: to photograph the last of something precious before it vanished for good. [78] At the moonlit summit, the old selenium bloom still clung to the crater's lip, a flower that had mutated to survive vacuum and yet flowered only once every seven years. [108] Its petals opened only in the hour before lunar dawn, and closed forever by the time the sun cleared the horizon. [129] Mara arrived three hours early and set her tripod in the dust, her breath loud inside her helmet. [147] The earth hung low and blue, and the silence had that specific quality she had come to recognize as the dawning hush. [169] It was not absence of sound but the expectation of it, the moment when night holds its breath for light. [189] She had been told the bloom would not outlast this sunrise, poisoned slowly by the increased solar flare activity of the past decade. [212] That was why she had to capture it, not for science alone but for the memory of a world that tried to make beauty in a place that did not ask for it. [245] She checked her chemical timers, small glass vials she had prepared for timed messages back to base. [262] Using chemical reactions for timed messages was old fieldcraft, a way to send a signal even if your radio failed, and hers was already crackling with static from the coming dawn. [293] She had mixed a pale blue solution that would turn clear after sixty minutes, then a second that would flare amber after ninety, bracketing the bloom's brief life. [321] If she missed her window to transmit, the colors would speak for her. [334] She knelt and found, half-buried in the regolith beside her boot, a single nightwatch's hourglass sand grain, impossibly smooth and dark. [355] Someone from the first watch station must have dropped it decades ago, a relic from when men timed their shifts with glass instead of atomic clocks. [381] She slipped it into her specimen pocket, not as data but as a promise that someone had waited here before her. [402] The sky began to pale at the edge of the ridge, though on the moon there was no atmosphere to scatter the light, only a sharp line of white advancing over black. [434] Mara felt the familiar ache of dawnlit melancholy settle over her, the quiet grief of seeing something beautiful precisely because it would not remain. [458] Below her, the selenium bloom shivered, its translucent petals beginning to unfold toward the coming sun. [474] She leaned to her viewfinder, adjusted the focus until each vein in the petal was sharp, and waited for the light to be perfect. [498] A tremor, faint but deep, ran through the summit as the thermal shock of sunrise cracked an old fissure a few meters away. [521] Dust leapt, and one vial tipped, spilling its blue solution into the gray. [534] Mara caught the second vial before it followed, her gloves slick with the cold. [548] She had no time to remix, no time to call for help that would not come before the bloom closed. [568] She did the only thing she could and wedged the amber vial upright where the lost blue one had been, knowing its signal would be late and therefore true. [597] Then she returned to the camera, exhaled slowly, and took the photograph as the first direct light struck the petals and made them glow like paper lanterns. [624] The image would outlast her, outlast the ridge, outlast the agency that sent her, and that was enough. [642] When the sun was fully risen, the bloom curled inward, browned at the edges, and folded into itself for the last time. [664] Mara stood, her knees stiff, and watched the amber vial flare bright against the dark rock, sending its silent message to a base that would soon know she had succeeded. [694] She placed her palm briefly on the empty summit where the flower had been, feeling nothing through the glove but meaning it anyway. [717] In her pocket the nightwatch's hourglass sand grain warmed faintly against her thigh, a tiny weight that had measured time long before rockets ever did. [742] She began the long walk down, carrying the photograph and the hush with her into the brightening day. [760]</story>
