<story>Elias was a grim optimist who polished his failures until they shone like warnings. [14] He believed the world would end in disappointment, and therefore every small kindness surprised him. [29] For thirty years he had tended the Observatory of Saint Joren, a tower that housed an infinite staircase. [47] The staircase had been built by a mad astronomer to reach the stars without ever leaving the building, and it succeeded in a way that felt like boundless confinement. [76] Each flight looked exactly like the last, stone steps spiraling upward through dim light, yet the air grew colder the higher you climbed. [99] Elias lived there alone after his sister died, and he spent his evenings trying to capture the texture of a missed opportunity. [121] The opportunity had been simple: ten years ago, Liora asked him to leave the observatory with her for the coast, and he had said not yet. [147] She left alone, married a cartographer, and never wrote, and the silence became a shape he could feel in his hands. [168] To understand that shape, he began building a machine that promised perpetual motion. [181] If he could make something that moved forever without cause, he thought he could explain why her absence still moved through him. [203] His design was balancedly daring, precise enough to be plausible and wild enough to be beautiful. [219] It used a weighted wheel, a set of magnets, and a pair of dice, one chipped, that he rolled each morning to decide which gear to adjust. [246] The chipped die always landed on its flawed edge just long enough to make him hesitate, and he cherished that hesitation. [267] He worked through mathematical proofs taped to the staircase walls, each sheet fluttering as wind moved through the impossible height. [287] The proofs were supposed to show that energy could be borrowed from the stairs themselves, from the endless fall of weight through endless height. [311] For months the wheel stuttered, caught, and stopped, and each failure made Elias grin with a grim satisfaction. [329] Failure meant the universe still had rules, which meant his choice to stay had also had meaning. [346] Then one damp autumn evening, the wheel did not stop. [356] It accelerated, humming, its magnets blurring, and the dice on the workbench trembled as if recognizing a coming rupture. [375] Elias felt the tower shudder, not with collapse but with possibility, and the staircase seemed to fold slightly inward. [394] He realized through mathematical proofs that he had not created energy but had uncovered a seam where the building cheated space. [415] The infinite staircase was not infinite at all but looped through a hidden curvature, and his machine had learned how to push against that curve. [440] If the wheel kept turning, it would widen that seam until the staircase could no longer hold its own geometry. [460] He understood he had built not an engine but a key, and every turn was turning a lock he could not relock. [482] The grim optimist in him had expected ruin, yet he had secretly hoped for a miracle that would return Liora's footstep to the stairs. [506] Now he had to choose whether to let the machine run and rupture the place that held his grief, or stop it and preserve his careful sorrow. [533] He placed his hand on the warm, vibrating wheel and felt the texture he had sought for a decade: the rough, warm edge of not going. [559] It was not sharp like regret but broad and humming, a feeling that could have gone on forever if he let it. [581] With a steady breath, he wedged the chipped die into the gears, not as a gamble but as a brake. [601] The wheel shrieked, sparked, and seized, and the staircase exhaled a long, low sigh as its impossible height settled back into stone. [623] Silence returned, but it was a different silence, one that had been chosen rather than endured. [639] Elias climbed down, step by step, feeling the boundless confinement finally become merely a building, and for the first time he imagined leaving it. [663] He left the motionless machine on the landing as a monument to the proof that some things should not move forever. [684] At the bottom door, he tucked the remaining good die into his pocket, to be thrown only once, on the road to the coast. [708]</story>
