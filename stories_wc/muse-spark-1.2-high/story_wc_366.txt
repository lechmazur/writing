<story>The supply run to Gray Point had always been Silas's alone. [11] He was a reticent lighthouse supply skipper who spoke to the gulls more than to people, and the harbor folk had learned not to expect more than a nod. [40] Yet he was warmly observant in the way that quiet people often are, noticing the tremor in a keeper's hand or the way the light caught a child's hope at the pier. [72] For three winters he had carried crates to the rock where the old lighthouse stood, and for three winters the lantern had burned without a keeper. [98] The light was automated now, but the stories said the haunted lantern room still held the voices of those who had tended it. [121] Silas did not believe in ghosts, but he believed in duties left unfinished. [134] On a Thursday in late November, after the lamp flickers out during a squall, the radio at the harbor office crackled with a flat, automated alert. [160] The light was dark, and any vessel trying to make the channel would be blind. [175] He loaded oil, wicks, and a spare lens, and pushed his small cutter out into the swell before anyone could tell him not to. [199] The sea was black glass, and halfway to the rock he found a piece of sea glass caught in the scuppers, pale green and smooth as a worn promise. [228] He slipped it into his pocket without thinking, as if to bottle the feeling of safety that such small, softened things always gave him. [252] The lighthouse rose from the reef like a bone, its lantern dark, its door banging. [267] Inside, the spiral stairs were cold and the air smelled of brine and old kerosene. [282] In the haunted lantern room the wind made a low hum through the cracked vent, and Silas felt at once that he was not alone, not in any frightening way but in the way an empty church still feels held. [322] Dust lay thick on the lens housing, and beneath it, tucked against the brass, was a bundle of letters tied with tarred twine. [345] They were not addressed to anyone, each one starting only with For whoever tends the light next, and each one ending with no name at all. [371] Silas, who had not read another person's private words in twenty years, opened the top one because the loneliness in the room asked him to. [396] The handwriting was a keeper's from 1928, then another from 1947, then a supply hand like his own from 1961, an anonymous letter chain carried across decades by strangers who never met. [428] Each letter described the same thing: how to keep the lamp lit when the storm took the cables, and how to leave a sign for the next boat that all was well. [460] At the bottom of the bundle was a newer note, paper still white, that read If the light fails, stack the stones on the south ledge so we know you stood watch. [492] Beside the letters was a small cairn on the window sill, three flat stones balanced one atop the other, left by the last hand to shelter here. [519] Silas understood then that the chain was not about haunting at all, but about a quiet promise to protect whoever came next, even without a name to thank. [547] A sudden draft slammed the lantern hatch, and the spare lens rolled, threatening to shatter on the iron deck. [566] He threw his weight against it, the piece of sea glass pressing sharp then smooth against his thigh, reminding him that tender things survive by being tumbled, not by being hidden. [597] He righted the lens, fitted it, and worked until the wick caught, the light spilling out across the water in a pale, trembling gold that made his chest ache. [626] From the gallery he looked down and, with cold fingers, began a way of stacking stones as messages on the south ledge, one for I was here, one for the light endures, one for you are not alone. [664] The ache that had lived in him, luminescent and quiet since his wife died and left him ferrying supplies for ghosts, lifted just enough to let the light through. [693] He added his own letter to the bundle, anonymous as the rest, saying only that the sea glass was still in his pocket and that he would keep the lamp in sight. [725] When he motored back through the dark, the beam turned steady behind him, and he knew whoever came next would find the stones and the letters and understand they had been held. [757] It was not much, to bottle the feeling of safety for a stranger, but it was everything he had learned to give. [779]</story>
