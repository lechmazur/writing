<story>Ellis had been the firm shore surveyor for Greyport for twenty three years. [13] He knew every stone, every tide mark, and every lie the water told about permanence. [28] He was stoically sensitive, the kind of man who could record a collapse without flinching yet remember the name of the child who once played there. [54] After the drawbridge rises for the night, no one was supposed to remain on the outer bar. [71] That was the rule Ellis had written himself, to keep the curious from the old channel. [87] But on the evening the survey stakes showed the shoreline had retreated another four feet, he stayed. [104] His motivation was no longer simple measurement, for he had come to want something stranger, to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams. [126] For years he had watched the town abandon small hopes along the shore, picnic tables, boat sheds, promises etched in concrete. [147] The submerged pavilion at the mouth of the inlet held them all. [159] Built for summer dances in nineteen twenty, it had sunk after the storm surge of seventy two and now rested ten feet below the low tide line. [186] At low water its domed roof showed like a pale moon just beneath the surface. [201] Ellis anchored his skiff and lowered himself with a rope, holding his breath against the cold. [217] Inside his waterproof satchel was an antique traveling inkwell, brass capped and glass bodied, that had belonged to his grandfather. [237] He carried it not for ink but for preservation, because its screw seal still held air perfectly. [254] His method was quiet and deliberate, placing notes in foundation repairs wherever the shore was crumbling. [270] He wrote on rag paper with iron gall ink, rolled the slips tight, and tucked them into fresh mortar or behind new seawall stones. [294] He wanted future hands to find not just his measurements but the stories he had been told while measuring. [313] Tonight he would place one inside the submerged pavilion itself, if he could wrest an opening in its silt sealed doorway. [334] The water was dark and cold, and the pavilion hovered in a hushed blaze of filtered moonlight that made every particle glow without sound. [358] Ellis had to wrest the warped service door, his shoulders straining against wood swollen tight for fifty years. [376] It gave at last with a soft sigh, releasing a cloud of silt that cleared to reveal the dance floor intact. [397] Chairs still circled the edge, and a bandstand leaned gently, as if waiting for music that never returned. [415] The place was defined not by what remained but by interlaced absence, every present object outlining a missing person, a missing song. [437] Ellis felt the weight of it settle in his chest, familiar and precise as a survey line. [454] He swam to the central pillar where the foundation had cracked, and there he found a hollow already prepared by water and time. [477] From the satchel he took the antique traveling inkwell and unscrewed its brass cap. [491] Inside, dry and safe, was his note for this night, a list of names he had collected from old town records of every couple who had held a wedding dance in the pavilion. [524] He added one final line at the bottom, We remember that you hoped here. [538] He rolled the paper, slid it into the inkwell, and sealed it tight. [551] Then he wedged the inkwell deep into the crack and packed it around with fresh hydraulic mortar from his belt pouch. [572] The mortar would cure even underwater, holding his small archive against the tide for a century. [588] As he worked, the drawbridge bell rang faintly through the water, a reminder that the channel was closed until dawn. [608] He was alone on the far side until morning, alone with the pavilion and its quiet fire. [625] It did not frighten him. [630] He pressed his palm to the pillar, feeling the cold seep in, and thought of all the future surveyors who might one day map a different shore and find his inkwell waiting. [662] They would open it and read, and understand that someone had tried to hold absence together with care. [680] Ellis rose slowly toward the surface, where moonlight still burned hushed and steady above the dark water. [697]</story>
