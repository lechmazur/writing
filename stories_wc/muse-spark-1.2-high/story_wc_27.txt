<story>Mara had been called the barefoot stargazer since she was six, when she refused shoes even on the frosted observatory deck. [21] Now twenty-four, she lived in the abandoned spaceport repair bay at the edge of Kos Station, where gutted shuttles hung like sleeping whales. [44] She stayed in the margin of daylight, that thin amber hour when the station's artificial sun dimmed and the real stars bled through the cracked dome. [70] It was during a lull in rebellion, the weeks after the Orbital Collective's failed strike when no one knew whether to protest or pretend normalcy had returned. [97] The quiet felt heavier than gunfire, a waiting silence that pressed on Mara's chest. [111] She had come to Kos to discover meaning in the mess, sifting wreckage for patterns the way other people read scriptures. [132] Her only inheritance was a pair of vintage opera glasses, brass and mother-of-pearl, that her grandmother had used to watch lunar ballets before the wars. [157] Through them, the stars looked closer, but also inverted, as if she were gazing down into a deep well of light. [178] Beyond the bay doors lay the stone circle, twelve basalt pillars thrumming faintly since before Kos had a name. [197] The old mechanics said it was a navigation anchor, the first settlers said it was a grave, and Mara simply listened to it by the hum of an ancient stone circle. [228] In the last days of the strike, she had watched the rebellion's signal fires die one by one, until only her bay remained lit. [252] Her friend Joss, awkwardly confident even when terrified, had promised to return with parts to fix the transmitter. [270] He had left his boots by her workbench, laughing that a true stargazer should not need them. [287] He never came back. [291] Mara found his signal badge melted near the launch trench, and understood the lull was not peace but grief holding its breath. [313] For three nights she did nothing, letting dust settle on the opera glasses and the silent transmitter. [330] On the fourth night, the stone circle's hum changed, deepening into a note that vibrated through the metal floor and into the soles of her bare feet. [357] It was a sound wrapped in thunderous serenity, violent and calm at once, like a storm heard from orbit. [376] Mara lifted the vintage opera glasses and saw, far beyond the dome, a faint pulse approaching in the dark, a convoy running without lights. [400] They were not Collective, not Corporate, but families from the outer fields trying to slip through Kos while the patrols were still disorganized. [423] She realized the transmitter could guide them, if she could reignite its dead core. [437] The problem was power; the bay's cells were drained, and the storm over the flats had silenced the solar collectors. [457] Joss had once joked that the stones sang when they were hungry, and Mara pressed her palms to the nearest pillar. [478] The basalt was cold, then hot, and a low current leapt from rock to skin to copper wiring she dragged across the bay floor. [502] Sparks coughed, died, then caught, and the ancient hum aligned with the transmitter's whine until they sang as one. [521] Mara was awkwardly confident again, hands shaking as she tuned the frequency, whispering coordinates through chapped lips. [538] She climbed the gutted shuttle for height, bare feet gripping cold rungs, and flashed the signal outward. [555] One by one, lights answered from the dark, not weapons but lanterns on caravan roofs, flickering in acknowledgment. [573] The convoy altered course toward the bay's faint beacon, moving through the margin of daylight that now bled fully into night. [594] Mara sat atop the shuttle, opera glasses in her lap, and let the stone circle's hum travel up through her bones. [615] She did not know if the rebellion would rise again, or fail again, or become something else entirely. [633] She only knew that in this held breath between histories, she had chosen to make a light rather than curse the dark. [655] Below, the bay doors shuddered open, and the families began to arrive, tired, dusty, and alive. [671] And the stars, seen through her grandmother's glasses or with her own unaided eyes, seemed at last to be watching kindly. [692]</story>
