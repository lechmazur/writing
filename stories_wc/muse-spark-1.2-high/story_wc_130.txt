<story>Silas had lived for eleven years as a secretive conjurer in the depths of a cavern orchard nourished by dripping stalactites. [21] He had not chosen the place for its beauty, though the pale trees that grew without sun were beautiful, but because the constant drip gave him a metronome for his work. [52] He was gently inventive in a way that made other conjurers distrust him, preferring small accommodations over grand summonings. [71] For two years he had been trying to understand the mathematics of emotion, charting how grief could be halved and doubled without ever changing its weight. [97] The problem had begun with his sister Mara, who had walked into the orchard one evening and never walked out, though her coat was later found folded neatly by the roots. [128] Her absence was not a death and not a leaving, and the village priest called it ambiguous loss, a phrase Silas hated for how precisely it described nothing. [156] He kept her coat and he kept his calculations, and neither brought her closer. [170] On his worktable lay a taxidermist's glass eye pair that Mara had once bought as a joke, two perfect amber irises that seemed to watch without seeing. [197] Silas believed he could dovetail his conjuring with her lingering presence if he could find the correct pattern to anchor his spell. [219] Spells in the cavern did not hold unless they were tied to something already belonging there, something that the stone remembered. [240] He found his answer not in books but via the chipped patterns on a mosaic stepping stone that Mara had laid as a child. [264] The stone was near the oldest tree, and its blue and white tiles had broken over years of drip and frost into a map of fissures. [290] Mara had always stepped only on the white fragments, claiming they were safe islands, and Silas remembered the exact hop of her game. [313] He began to copy the pattern into his notebooks, assigning numbers to each chip and curve, and a quiet structure emerged. [334] The numbers did not measure distance but feeling, and he saw that grief repeated like the drips, irregular yet bound by a mean. [357] After the orchard grows silent beneath the moon, when even the stalactites pause and the leaves stop turning toward no light, he would work. [381] He placed the taxidermist's glass eye pair at the two widest chips on the stone, as if giving the pattern sight. [402] Then he spoke his gently inventive conjuring, not to bring her back, but to make space for both her absence and her continuance. [425] The spell asked the orchard to hold the contradiction as the stone held its broken tiles, together without being whole. [445] For a long moment nothing happened, and the buried optimism he had hidden even from himself felt foolish. [463] Then the glass eyes caught the moonlight that filtered through a narrow shaft and threw it back onto the mosaic in two soft circles. [487] The light did not restore the stone, but it made the chipped pattern complete, linking white islands that had seemed separate. [508] In that light he felt Mara not as a figure to be recovered but as a ratio that lived within him, grief divided by love retaining its value. [536] He understood at last that to understand the mathematics of emotion was not to solve it, but to learn how to carry its remainder. [560] The orchard made a soft sound as a drop fell, and the sound was neither empty nor full, but both at once. [582] Silas left the glass eyes where they were, guardians of the mosaic, and he finally wept without needing to know where his tears belonged. [606]</story>
