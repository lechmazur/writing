<story>Ellis was a vagrant penny poet who traded verses for loose copper on the corner of Fifth and Mercer. [19] He molts personalities monthly, shedding one skin for another whenever the calendar turned thin. [33] In February he was the Prophet, in March the Jester, and in April he had settled on being the Archivist, a quiet man who remembered everything. [59] The Archivist kept his earnings and his unsold poems in a dented coffee tin with secret false bottom that once held his mother's beans. [83] Under the printed label the lower chamber hid not money but seed packets, each folded tight around marigold and wild rye and clover. [106] His motivation had never been survival alone but to forge beauty from anarchy, to make something ordered and bright out of the city's noise. [130] That idea had not come from the street but from a friendship sparked sorting books at a prison library two winters before. [152] There he had met Mara, the volunteer who taught him how to repair a broken spine with thread and how to catalog hope under Dewey decimal 635. [179] She smuggled him the first seeds, whispering that a garden could be a quiet revolt. [194] Mara called it blooming resistance, a phrase she wrote on the inside cover of a discarded botany manual and slipped into his palm. [217] When he was released, he promised to find the old Carnegie branch the city had left to rust on the edge of the railyard. [241] For decades the building had stood as a rusted library under stars, its iron skeleton showing through peeled brick and its skylight shattered into a map of constellations. [269] Pigeons lived in the stacks and vines choked the checkout desk, but the roof still held against rain. [287] Ellis decided he would rebuild it, not as it was but as it could be. [302] He began by sweeping broken glass while reciting poems for a penny to children who stopped to watch. [320] He traded verses for buckets, for brooms, for the help of neighbors who thought he was mad until they saw the seeds. [342] Each night he pressed seeds into cracks in the concrete floor where rain pooled, and each morning he whispered to them as if they were lines needing revision. [370] Mara returned in May, no longer a volunteer but a woman with a van full of salvaged shelves and a thermos of black tea. [394] She did not ask which personality he wore that month, only whether the tin still kept its secret. [412] He showed her the false bottom and the last packet of moonflowers meant for the central atrium. [429] Together they planned to open the library as the moon clears the skyline on the solstice, when light would fall straight through the broken skylight. [454] The city sent inspectors who saw only trespass and anarchy in their work, and posted condemnation notices on the rusted doors. [475] Ellis answered by writing a new poem on the notice itself, selling it for a penny to the inspector who lingered to read. [498] On solstice eve the neighborhood gathered, carrying lanterns and folding chairs and children sleepy on shoulders. [514] The air inside smelled of paper and soil, and the shelves Mara had rebuilt held donated books beside pots of green that had forced their way through concrete. [542] Every wall was a luminous fracture, light from the lanterns splitting through cracked plaster and blooming flowers and casting patterns that looked like illuminated manuscripts. [567] Ellis stood on a crate and became the Poet again, voice shaking as he read about roots that learn to read. [588] As the moon clears the skyline, its cold light poured through the skylight and struck the moonflowers, which opened all at once as if on cue. [614] A gasp moved through the crowd, not at the flowers alone but at the understanding that the building was alive again. [635] In that moment his monthly molting ceased, and he felt all his borrowed selves settle into one continuous person. [654] Mara touched his arm and said the library needed no more rebuilding, only tending. [668] Later, after the crowd thinned and the lanterns dimmed, he tucked the empty coffee tin onto the highest shelf, its false bottom now holding a single penny for luck. [697]</story>
