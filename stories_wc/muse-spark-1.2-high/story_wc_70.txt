<story>Mara had been the candid toll keeper at the northern causeway for eleven years, waving through commuters, truckers, and wandering tourists without ever learning to small-talk properly. [27] It was her honesty that made drivers trust her, even when she was involuntarily truthful about the delays ahead or the price of crossing going up next month. [55] She liked routine, but she lived to preserve wonder in routine, polishing the brass bell at her booth and humming to the radio static as if the road itself might listen. [86] That changed after the city-wide blackout, when the grid failed for three nights and the sky over the estuary stayed dark enough to see every star. [112] On the fourth morning the power returned in stutters, and the mayor announced that the lightning collection tower on the hill would be reactivated to keep the city charged through winter. [143] Mara was ordered to report there for a new assignment, since the causeway toll was now automated and her post considered obsolete. [165] She climbed the winding service road at nectar dusk, when the light turned thick and golden as honey poured across the fields, and the tower hummed above her like a patient giant. [197] The tower was not what she expected, not a sleek needle but a lattice of copper and glass built to drink storms, its collectors empty and waiting. [224] Inside the control room, an engineer named Jonah waited beside a small lacquer box, its surface dark red and worn smooth at the edges from many hands. [251] He explained that the city still faced a crisis, not of power but of pollination, because the drone beehive failure had left the orchards silent and the harvest uncertain. [280] Without bees, even with electricity, the valley would starve by spring, and the tower's energy was needed to wake the reserve hives sleeping in the cold storage below. [308] Jonah said he did not know the ignition sequence, and because he was involuntarily truthful, Mara believed him instantly when his voice cracked on the last word. [335] He slid the lacquer box toward her and told her it had been left by the old keeper, who believed that wonder could not be programmed and must be earned. [365] Inside the box there was only a matchbox containing a cryptic riddle, rattling faintly when she shook it, as if it held something lighter than matches. [391] She opened it and read aloud: I drink without a mouth, I gather without hands, I die each winter and am born in spring. [415] Jonah frowned and guessed storm or river, but Mara, who had watched the light over the fields, thought of pollen and hives and the tower's purpose. [441] She was also involuntarily truthful, so when she said I think it is a bee, Jonah knew she was not guessing but remembering something true. [466] To test her, Jonah asked her to reveal what the old keeper had told her years ago on the causeway, a story she had never shared. [492] Mara hesitated, then opened the matchbox fully and found a folded slip and a single dried lavender sprig pressed flat as paper. [514] The slip read: Give me to the tower when the sky is amber and the air is sweet, and I will carry lightning to the sleeping queens. [541] Mara understood then that the riddle's answer was not just bee but the whole cycle of gathering light and life, and that the matchbox was a key. [568] She carried the lavender sprig to the central pedestal where the tower's copper roots converged, and the air smelled of ozone and nectar dusk all at once. [595] The pedestal had a shallow depression exactly the size of the lacquer box, as if the tower had been waiting for its offering. [618] She placed the lacquer box into it, set the matchbox containing a cryptic riddle on top, and struck the single match that had been hidden beneath the slip. [646] The flame did not burn the box but lit the lavender, and the smoke rose in a thin coil toward the collectors, sweet and sharp. [671] The tower shivered, then sang, drawing the last amber light from the horizon and converting it into a low pulse that ran down into the earth. [697] Below them, in the vault, the reserve hives warmed, and a soft, drowsy buzzing began to rise through the vents, answering the tower's call. [721] Jonah laughed, a real laugh this time, and admitted he had been afraid the city had forgotten how to ask nicely for help. [744] Mara, still candid, told him she had been afraid that her years of taking tolls had been pointless, but now she saw they had taught her patience. [771]</story>
