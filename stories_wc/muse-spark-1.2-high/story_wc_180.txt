<story>Mara had been a quartz-eye mender for forty years before anyone thought to call her anything else. [17] Her left eye, replaced with rose quartz after the fever, let her see fractures in glass, ceramics, and sometimes in people. [38] It was after the breach of contract that she finally climbed the hill to the abandoned cathedral. [55] The paper mill that had employed her granddaughter had voided its apprenticeship agreement, leaving the girl with idle hands and unanswered questions. [77] Mara wanted to bridge generational wisdom, but she did not know how to speak across the silence that had grown between them. [99] Her granddaughter Lena was endearingly feisty, quick to argue and quicker to forgive, and she had refused to come with Mara that morning. [122] Mara went anyway, carrying her old leather tool bag and a folded blue daughter's hair ribbon that had belonged to Lena's mother. [144] Inside the cathedral, the roof had collapsed decades ago, and in its place grew a lush orchard spanning the rafters of an abandoned cathedral. [168] Sunlight poured through shattered stained glass onto apple and pear trees that rooted impossibly in stone gutters and broken beams. [188] Mara stared and laughed, because it was the kind of accidental wisdom that only nature seemed capable of offering without trying. [209] She had come to mend the cathedral's great rose window, a commission she had accepted years earlier before the town lost interest. [231] Now the window lay in a hundred quartz-veined shards on the nave floor, and the trees dropped petals upon them like blessings. [253] Mara knelt and opened her bag, but her resin and fine brushes had dried up from months of disuse. [272] She would have to persist with what she had, or abandon the work entirely. [286] It was then she remembered her own rule for mending: use what remains, not what you wish you brought. [305] She tipped the bag sideways, using forgotten bag contents she had not inventoried in years. [320] There was a spool of copper wire, a jar of honey that had crystallized, a child's magnifying glass, and a handful of pine resin beads. [345] At the bottom, beneath a folded receipt, lay a small paper packet labeled in her own handwriting: deciduous vows. [364] She had written those words twenty years earlier when she promised her daughter she would let her choose whether to stay in the valley or leave it. [391] Deciduous vows, she had called them then, promises that could shed like leaves when seasons changed and still keep the tree alive. [413] Her daughter had left, and Mara had kept the vow intact by not demanding her return. [429] Now Lena was weighing the same choice, stay or go, and Mara felt the old fear rising again. [447] A noise at the doorway startled her, and Lena stood there, breathless from the climb, holding two cups of cold tea. [468] You said you would be back by lunch, Lena said, trying to sound stern and failing. [484] Mara smiled and patted the stone beside her, and Lena sat, eyeing the shattered window and the trees above. [503] I thought you needed help, Lena said, though it was clear she needed to be needed. [519] Mara handed her the magnifying glass and the daughter's hair ribbon and asked her to tie the ribbon around the thickest branch she could reach. [544] Lena frowned, but she climbed the low scaffolding and looped the bright blue ribbon where green apples were beginning to swell. [565] What does that do, Lena asked, and Mara admitted she did not know, except that it marked that someone had been here and cared. [589] Together they melted the honey and pine beads over a small tin, using the magnifying glass to focus the sun through the broken rose window. [614] The mixture became a warm amber adhesive, imperfect but honest, and they began to fit shards together on the stone slab. [635] Mara's quartz eye caught the hairline cracks before her fingers did, and Lena's young eyes caught the color patterns Mara could no longer distinguish. [659] They worked without speaking, letting the work teach them how to be together after years of polite distance. [677] When the afternoon light slanted sideways, the half-mended window cast a new pattern on the orchard floor, leaves and glass overlapping. [698] That evening they left the ribbon tied in the rafters, a small flag of their deciduous vow to let each other grow without letting go. [723]</story>
