<story>I did not meet the ghost until long after strangers became family on New Aurel. [15] We had arrived as contract pickers from six different worlds, and the orchard planet riddled with floating rock arches had made us neighbors before it made us kin. [43] The arches sang when wind passed through them, and beneath their shadows our apple rows stretched for miles in uneven light. [64] It was Mara who taught us how to prune in that light, and when blight took her during the second harvest, we buried her butterfly garden watering bell with her. [94] We thought that was the end of her work, until the chalk appeared. [107] We received guidance through messages chalked onto deserted sidewalks at dawn, where someone began telling us where the blight would spread next. [129] The handwriting was hers, looping and hurried, strangely trustworthy even though we knew she was dead. [145] Joren said it was a cruel prank, but I walked the eastern row and found the first chalked root exactly where the message promised, already blackening. [171] That was when I understood our Mara had remained as an altruistic ghost, not haunting but helping. [188] She had always given away her rations, her gloves, her sleep, and death had not cured her of generosity. [207] Above us, the nebula of respite hung low that season, a bruised violet cloud that softened the hard radiation and let our orchards breathe. [231] Mara had loved that nebula, saying it was the only sky that let tired things rest without forgetting how to grow. [252] Now her chalk maps asked us to reexamine everything we thought we knew about the blight. [268] We had assumed it moved with insects, but the messages showed it followed the cold shadows cast by the floating arches. [289] The arches were shifting, just a few meters each night, and their moving shade was stressing the trees enough to invite infection. [311] Her true purpose was to orchestrate collective effort, because no single crew could prune and treat the orchard fast enough alone. [332] So she divided the work between us, not in orders but in invitations. [345] By Thursday the north crew found flour at their door with a chalk arrow pointing to the spore sacks they had missed. [367] By Friday the children, who now called each other cousins, were running bell water to every marked root while singing to keep pace. [390] I asked her once, aloud to the empty sidewalk at dawn, why she had chosen chalk, and the next morning the answer looped across the concrete in pale blue. [419] Because chalk washes away, it read, so you must choose again each day to believe. [434] That line made us feel the brambled hope she had always carried, hope tangled with thorns yet stubbornly flowering. [453] It would have been easier to despair, to let the orchard rot and take the company transport home. [471] But after strangers become family, you do not leave the table while others are still hungry. [487] We reexamined our old quarrels in the same spirit, finding that fear had made us territorial about rows that belonged to no one. [510] On the equinox, under the full light of the nebula of respite, we gathered at the arch that had marked Mara's favorite row. [533] We dug up her butterfly garden watering bell, clean and bright despite months in soil, and hung it from the lowest branch. [555] When wind moved through the arch, the bell rang with a clear, thin note that carried across the orchard. [574] One by one we chalked our own messages on the sidewalks, not to her but to each other, promising which rows we would tend. [598] The ghost watched, I think, and then the wind lifted the chalk dust like pollen and scattered it over the blossoms. [619] No one has seen her handwriting since, but the bell still rings whenever the arches shift and the orchard needs turning toward the light. [643] We wake before dawn now without being asked, and we sweep the sidewalks clean for whoever might need to write next. [664] The nebula holds, the trees hold, and we hold each other, which is the only kind of respite that lasts. [684]</story>
