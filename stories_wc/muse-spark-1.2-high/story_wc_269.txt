<story>Mara had not expected to be posted to the sea level monitoring station after the fossil fuel ban, but the assignment felt like an exile she had chosen. [28]
She was a coral bleaching witness, one of the last divers to catalog the Great Barrier before it turned bone white in a single summer. [53]
The station was moored fifty kilometers off the Queensland coast, a cluster of white pylons and solar mirrors that rose and fell with the swell but never drifted. [81]
Its anchoring felt periodically eternal, as if the cables had always been there and always would be, tightening and slackening with the tide. [104]
Mara spent her mornings calibrating the lidar that measured the ocean's slow ascent, millimeter by millimeter. [120]
In the afternoons she tried to discover why some paths disappear. [131]
Old shipping channels, reef passages, and migratory routes were vanishing from the charts faster than the water could rise. [150]
The official explanation was silt and coral collapse, but the data showed clean erasure, as if the sea had simply forgotten them. [172]
She kept an atlas page torn out and taped above her console, a 2032 survey of the Coral Sea where a thin blue line marked the Larkin Passage. [200]
According to every sensor on the station, the passage no longer existed. [212]
According to the atlas, it had been deep enough for container ships to pass without scraping the reef. [230]
Mara had developed a practice of tapping rhythms on surfaces when she was thinking, knuckles against steel railings and tabletops until patterns emerged. [253]
She tapped out the depth soundings of the missing passage from memory, converting numbers into beats, long for deep and short for shallow. [276]
The rhythm always broke halfway, a sudden silence where the water should have continued. [290]
One stormy evening the station's junior tech, Jonah, watched her and asked why she wasted time on a route the satellites said was gone. [314]
She told him she was trying to assemble a history from fragments, and that fragments sometimes remembered more than systems. [334]
Together they began to assemble the missing records, pulling archived sonar logs, diver notebooks, and the station's own acoustic pings. [354]
For three nights they worked, moored above an abyss of data, drifting in thought while their bodies stayed fixed to the station. [376]
Mara noticed that every erased path shared a single characteristic: it had been declared uneconomical after the ban, too shallow or too fuel costly to justify before the sails returned. [406]
The charts had not been corrected; they had been edited. [416]
Someone had removed the inconvenient passages to force the last freight fleets onto fewer, taxable corridors. [432]
The delayed revelation came not as a shock but as a slow settling, the way sediment finally clouds clear water after you stop stirring. [456]
She understood then that she had witnessed two kinds of bleaching, the coral and the maps. [472]
Jonah asked what they should do, and Mara said they would put the paths back. [487]
They began transmitting the old coordinates on the open channel, tapping them out as rhythmic pulses the automated buoys could not censor. [509]
Mara used her tapping again, but now on the station's hull, sending the Larkin depths into the water itself so any passing sailor with a hydrophone might hear. [537]
At dawn the sea was flat and grey, and the station hung perfectly still, drifting moored between what was recorded and what was true. [561]
For the first time since the bleaching, Mara felt that witnessing was not only about loss but about remembering. [580]
She left the atlas page taped where it was, its torn edge fluttering, a reminder that some lines must be drawn again by hand. [604]
Outside, the restored wind sails passed on the horizon, following the lines she had returned to the sea. [622]
Mara listened to her own knuckles against the rail, and the sound carried, steady and clear, across the water. [641]
