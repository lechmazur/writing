<story>Mara was an otherworldly sentimentalist who tended the edge of known space where light went to die slowly. [18] Her form was not flesh but folded memory, and she remembered every seed she had ever planted as if it were still in her hands. [43] The nebula garden stretched before her, a vast cathedral of gas and dust where stars were born like shy blossoms opening in the dark. [67] She had inherited its care from her mothers, and their mothers before them, each keeper bound by generational patterns carved into light itself. [90] Each keeper had tried to find a way to share light without using fire, for fire consumed what it illuminated and left only ash behind. [115] Mara believed she could do it differently. [122] At the garden's heart grew an ancient structure, a lattice of filaments that approximates infinity with every branching spiral it cast outward. [144] The old keepers called it the Loom, and it required constant tending lest its light fade and the garden collapse into night. [166] In its center turned a single calculating machine gear, brass and dark iron, humming against the silence of vacuum. [185] The gear had been placed there by the first keeper, who forged it to count the slow breathing of stars and to keep time when no clocks remained. [213] Now its teeth were worn smooth, and Mara knew she would soon need to transit the filament paths to find its replacement. [235] Transit was forbidden to keepers, for those who left rarely remembered the way back and the garden suffered in their absence. [256] But the gear was failing, and without its rhythm the Loom would stutter and the nebula garden would dim forever. [276] She prepared a vessel woven from fallen starlight, thin as paper and strong as longing, and set her course by sonar pinging through the dust clouds. [302] Each ping returned a map of the unseen, echoing off cold stones and sleeping comets that drifted like seeds in a winter pond. [325] She left across the quiet sigh of falling petals, for in the garden even nebulae shed light like petals when they aged and curled inward. [350] The journey was long, and the silence around her held a troubling serenity that made her heart ache with quiet dread. [371] It was too perfect, too still, as if the universe were holding its breath to see if she would fail. [391] Halfway through the void, her vessel shuddered and the gear in her pocket, the one she hoped to replace, slipped and cut her palm. [415] She watched her luminous blood bead and float, and felt for the first time the weight of being the last keeper in a long line. [440] If she failed, the generational patterns would break and no hands would tend the garden after her, leaving only darkness. [460] She pressed onward, following the final sonar pinging that promised a forge world where new gears were still grown from asteroid iron. [482] The forge world was dead, its furnaces cold, but a single artisan machine remained awake, endlessly stamping gears no one came to claim. [505] Mara knelt and selected the truest gear, one whose teeth caught starlight and threw it back without burning or dimming. [525] She understood then that this was how to share light without using fire, not by flame but by reflection and careful turning. [547] She began her return, the new gear warm against her chest, its weight a promise that the Loom would sing again. [568] The transit home was faster, as if the garden itself pulled her back with threads of gravity and affection. [587] When she arrived, petals of light were already falling faster, and the old gear had stopped turning entirely, silent as a held breath. [610] Mara replaced it with trembling hands, feeling the Loom shudder and then catch, its filaments brightening one by one like lamps being lit. [633] The nebula garden bloomed, and the troubling serenity lifted, replaced by a gentle hum that was not silence but living sound. [654] She sat beneath the Loom as its light washed over her, and for the first time she did not feel alone, but held by all who had sat there before. [684]</story>
