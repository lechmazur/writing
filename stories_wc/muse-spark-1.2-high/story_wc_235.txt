<story>Mara had been the lucid dream chronicler assigned to the mountain ranger cabin for eleven winters, and she liked to say she catalogued other people's subconscious the way rangers catalogued avalanches. [31] Everything changed after seeing your own grave, which she did on a Tuesday when the thaw exposed a small weathered headstone behind the cabin bearing her name and tomorrow's date. [61] She stood there for a long time, jaded enough to be unsurprised and still full of wonder that the moss had spelled her initials perfectly. [86] From that afternoon on she became chronologically confused, waking at midnight convinced it was morning, logging dreams she had not yet dreamed, and remembering breakfasts from next week. [114] To cope she returned to her oldest habit, to distill chaos into narrative, the way she had always turned static and terror into neat entries in ink. [141] On the cabin table lay the only medium that never ran out in her dreams, a pale birch bark scroll that unrolled farther each time she touched it. [169] She began to record there, and the scroll smelled faintly of snow and smoke, like every winter she had ever lived. [190] What rose off the bark was not memory but something wilder, a feral nostalgia for places she had never been, for childhood summers in valleys she had never visited. [219] It made her homesick for lives she had not lived, and that was the beginning of her unraveling. [237] Her method was simple and shameful, getting jealous of past selves who had not yet seen the grave and still believed time moved forward. [261] She envied the Mara of last spring, who had laughed at tourists and taken her coffee black on the porch without checking headstones. [284] She envied the Mara of five years ago, who thought lucid dreaming was a trick of control rather than a window that stayed open. [308] In the cabin at night the wind pressed against the shutters and made the scroll tremble as if breathing. [327] She practiced lucid dreaming deliberately now, lying back on the cot and reciting the ranger protocols for entering snowbound dreams. [347] Inside the dream the cabin inverted, its roof becoming a floor of pine needles, its walls thinning to reveal a vast white sky. [370] In that sky she could finally soar, not as metaphor but as the slow, deliberate lift of a body that remembers being light. [393] She soared above the mountain ridge, and from that height she saw the cabin, the grave, and a second Mara walking toward both. [416] The other Mara looked up, chronologically confused in exactly the same way, and waved with jaded wonder, as if they had both expected this meeting. [441] She understood then that the scroll was not recording dreams but stitching them, making a single narrative from the chaos of overlapping times. [464] The feral nostalgia sharpened, because she realized she missed not a place but a coherence, the simple line from yesterday to tomorrow. [486] Getting jealous of past selves had kept her tethered to grief, but the act of soaring had shown her that perspective could be chosen. [510] She woke on the cot with bark dust on her fingers, the scroll curled around her wrist like a bracelet. [530] Outside, the grave was gone, melted back into thaw, or perhaps it had never been solid ground at all but a dream she had chronicled into being. [557] Still, the date on it remained legible in her mind, and she decided not to fight the confusion but to use it. [579] She brewed coffee, opened the scroll again, and wrote the night's entry without trying to order it correctly. [597] She wrote that she had soared, that she had seen herself, that the feeling of jaded wonder was not cynicism but attention paid long enough to love what remains strange. [627] The mountain ranger cabin held that strangeness well, its logs groaning, its windows holding snowlight even at noon. [645] When the wind rose again she did not close the shutters, because to distill chaos into narrative one must first let the chaos in. [669] She unrolled another length of birch bark scroll, and found at its edge a fresh, empty space exactly the size of a future. [692] She smiled then, chronologically confused and lucid at once, and let the dream take her again. [708]</story>
