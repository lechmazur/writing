<story>After the plague ends, the water finally receded enough to reveal the upper arches of the sunken city ruins. [19] Marcus called himself a philosopher, though everyone else called him the wannabe philosopher living in a sewer beneath the eastern spillway. [40] He maintained his home in the dry pipe with an abrasive calm, politely insulting anyone who came to listen while never raising his voice. [64] He was rationally rebellious, refusing the Council's new doctrine not out of faith but because it failed every test of logic he set for it. [89] The Council claimed the plague had been a cleansing, and that forgetting the old world was the price of survival. [109] Marcus taught a different story, and the competing narratives fought for the ears of the few children who still gathered at the outflow. [132] His motivation was simple and stubborn: to preserve ancient methods that the Council had declared useless and dangerous. [150] Every evening he would agonize over how to make them understand, pacing his concrete trench until his feet ached. [169] He owned one treasure, a small brass seafarer's chronometer cog kept oiled in a tin, the last moving part of a clock that once guided ships. [195] He had found it while digging through sedimentary layers beneath the ruins, where centuries of silt had sealed libraries, streets, and chapels. [217] The Council sent an archivist named Jene to convince him to stop teaching. [230] She arrived with clean boots and a calm voice that matched his own, though hers was official and his was earned. [251] "You are confusing them," she said, crouching at the edge of his pipe. [264] Marcus wiped his hands on his coat and answered without anger, which made his contempt feel sharper. [281] "Confusion is the beginning of thought," he told her, "and certainty without proof is just another plague." [298] Jene sighed and offered a bargain from the Council: he could keep his sewer and his cog if he stopped speaking to children. [321] He turned the cog over in his palm and considered how much silence would cost. [336] The ancient methods he protected were not mystic rites but practical knowledge: how to read tides, how to sterlize water with heat, how to set a broken bone. [364] The Council preferred prayers and forgetting, because forgetting made people easier to govern. [377] "If I stop teaching," he said, "the children will never learn to question the next lie you tell them." [396] Jene looked down at the sunken city ruins visible beyond the drainage mouth, their drowned towers catching the low sun. [416] She had been taught that those towers were tombs best left flooded, but now she wondered who had decided that. [436] Marcus saw the doubt move across her face and pressed his point with the same abrasive calm. [453] "Take the cog," he said, "and see how it fits nothing in your Council building and everything in the world outside it." [475] She held the brass piece to the light and for the first time understood it as proof that navigation had once been possible. [498] "Competing narratives are not equal," Marcus whispered, "but the weaker one is often the one that remembers how the world works." [519] Jene did not report him the next morning, nor the morning after that. [532] Instead she returned with two children and a slate, asking him to show them again how to trace time through sedimentary layers and gears. [556] He felt a brief and unfamiliar relief, as if he had finally won a small argument against the tide itself. [576] In the months that followed, the sewer became a classroom, its damp walls covered in diagrams of clocks, currents, and constellations. [597] The Council pretended not to notice, because even they needed someone who could remember how to keep the pumps from failing. [618] Marcus never left his sewer, but his ideas left it daily, carried out by children who no longer feared the ruins.
