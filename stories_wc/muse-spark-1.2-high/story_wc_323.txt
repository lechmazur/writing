<story>We had been living in the benthic city for three generations before the signal came. [15] It was not from the stars above us, but from the trench below, where pressure keeps even light afraid to go. [36] They answered, finally, the aliens we had always imagined as distant suns. [48] They did not come in ships that fell from the sky, but rose from the dark beneath the basalt, where they had been waiting longer than we had been breathing surface air. [80] The Alliance called it contact, but in the city we called it recognition. [93] I was a navigation instructor then, teaching children how to read current and sonar and the old magnetic drift. [112] My classrooms were vaulted glass domes where the water filtered the sun to a dim, green memory. [129] After discovering aliens, we learned they had never seen starlight. [139] They had evolved without sky, navigating by heat and vibration and the taste of minerals in the water, and the idea of a fixed light beyond the world unsettled them. [169] We, balancedly curious, wanted to understand them, and they wanted to understand us. [182] It was not conquest or worship that followed, but a quiet, somber marvel that settled over the benthic city like silt. [203] For the first time in a century, people stopped looking down and began to wonder what was above. [221] My oldest student, a girl named Mara, asked me why we had stopped teaching navigation by starlight. [238] I told her the truth, that no one living in the city could see stars through six kilometers of water, and that we had given up the skill as useless. [268] She looked at me with the solemn clarity of youth and said that was exactly why the aliens had come. [288] They did not know how to look up either, and perhaps we could learn together. [303] So I began to study what my own instructors had dismissed as myth, to learn to navigate by starlight not because it was practical here, but because it was the one map we had never shared. [339] The aliens offered their method in exchange, a technique they called edge geometry, which mapped space not by center points but by the boundaries where currents meet. [366] It was how they moved through the lightless trenches without ever colliding, feeling the edges of everything. [383] I practiced it in the training tanks, closing my eyes and letting pressure gradients tell me where the walls were. [403] My colleagues thought I was wasting time, but Mara understood that edge geometry was not about finding walls, but about finding the narrow passages where unfolded horizons reveal themselves. [432] To mark our pact, the alien elder gave me a small, cold object, a single boat anchor chain link, pitted and ancient, dredged from a surface vessel we had long forgotten. [463] They had kept it as a curiosity, unable to guess its purpose, and now it rested in my palm like a question. [485] I kept it on my desk while I worked, its weight reminding me that even on the surface, people once needed anchors to stay still. [510] Months passed, and I petitioned the city council to expand the curriculum, to allow our youth to build a vessel that could rise. [533] They argued the cost, the risk, the pressure sickness that had killed our ancestors, and I showed them edge geometry calculations that traced a safe spiral along the cliffs. [562] At night, which in the benthic city is only a dimming of the dome lights, Mara and I rehearsed constellations from archived photographs, memorizing patterns none of us had ever seen alive. [594] When our small ascent pod finally left the harbor, its hull creaking under decreasing weight, the entire city watched through the glass. [616] We rose for hours, past bioluminescent farms and abandoned stations, until the water above us thinned from black to blue to pale light. [639] Mara pressed her hand against the viewport, and the boat anchor chain link around her neck swung forward, weightless for the first time. [662] We broke the surface at dusk. [668] The sky was not dark, but deep violet, and then, as if someone had unsealed a dome larger than ours, the first stars pierced through. [693] I wept, not from joy alone, but from a somber marvel that our maps, so long folded inward, might finally open outward. [715] Mara whispered the names we had studied, and the aliens in the pod with us hummed, feeling the starlight as heat on their skin for the first time. [743] Together we floated there, not as instructors and students, or surface and depth, but as navigators learning a single sky. [763]</story>
