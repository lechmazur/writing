<story>The gloom-hill coachman had not driven a carriage in twenty years, but he still walked the old war road every evening as if checking for ruts. [26] It was after the reef dies that the sea turned white and the lowlands quieted, and his village became a shore for ghosts instead of fishermen. [52] He was the only one left who speaks dead languages, having learned Sumerian and Etruscan from the hill monastery before it burned. [74] The monks had used those tongues to name the birds, and so he carried their lexicon like a second spine. [94] In his coat he kept a collection of dried seed pods, each labeled in a dead script, each saved from the monastery garden before the shelling. [120] He kept them for a single reason, to let a garden reclaim a battlefield that had taken his brother and his horses. [142] Every dusk he went to the blooming jetty at twilight, where salt-tolerant vines had cracked the concrete and opened white flowers to the dim. [166] There he listened, and the air held a narrow vastness, as if the sky were a low roof over an endless sea. [188] He would fertilize the thin soil only by understanding bird calls, waiting for the warblers to tell him when the ground was ready. [211] The birds had returned after the reef dies had starved the shore, and their dialects mapped the recovery of insects and rot. [233] When the reed buntings sang low and broken, he knew the earth was too acidic to hold life. [251] When the swallows stitched their notes high and clean, he knew the nitrogen had returned. [266] That knowledge had become his submerged concord, a silent agreement between his dead languages and the living birds, between loss and renewal. [288] For three seasons he waited on the jetty, scattering ash and crushed shell to coax the pH into balance. [307] The battlefield stretched behind him up the gloom-hill, a plain of rusted wire and cratered clay where no grass had grown for a decade. [331] Generals had called it strategic ground, but the coachman remembered it as the meadow where his brother taught him to braid horse manes. [354] One evening the swallows finally sang that clear pattern, and the buntings answered without breaking. [369] He understood it at once by understanding bird calls, the way a translator hears meaning beneath sound. [386] He carried the collection of dried seed pods to the jetty and opened his palm to the wind. [404] Each pod held a future: vetch to fix the soil, blackthorn to break the wind, and wild rye to hold the hill against rain. [428] He began at the jetty and walked uphill, pressing the seeds into the fertilized furrows the birds had approved. [447] The gloom-hill coachman moved with the patience of a man who had once timed his horses to the breath of the road. [469] Behind him the blooming jetty at twilight glowed faintly, its flowers closing as stars pricked the narrow vastness above. [488] He whispered the old names for each seed as he planted, Sumerian for barley, Etruscan for root, because he speaks dead languages to the dead earth. [514] It was not superstition but promise, a way to keep faith that words could still fertilize what war had sterilized. [534] By midnight he had emptied his pockets, and the hill was a field of shallow, hopeful wounds. [551] The submerged concord held, the birds fell silent in approval, and the wind from the dead reef smelled briefly green. [571] He sat on the overturned hull by the jetty and wept, not from sorrow but from the vast relief of a small, narrow task completed. [596] Spring would come late, but when it did, green would reclaim the wire and soften the craters where his brother had fallen. [618] People from the village would see it and remember that the coachman had never truly stopped driving, he had only changed what he carried. [642] And the garden would keep its own concord, needing no translation, rooted in the battlefield he had finally let go. [662]</story>
