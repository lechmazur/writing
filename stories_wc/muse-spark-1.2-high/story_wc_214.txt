<story>Mara had not been hired as an astronomer but as the cosmic janitor for Outpost Selene, and that suited her. [20] The shooting star tracking station had been built during the optimism of the last century and then forgotten through the hush of an overlooked era. [45] Its domes were still turning, its computers still listening, but no one came to read what they heard. [63] Mara swept dust from abandoned consoles and polished the great retractable roof until it shone like a second sky. [82] She was spiritually curious in a way that made solitude feel like a conversation. [96] Every night she asked the same unspoken question, not of anyone living, but of the dark itself. [113] She wanted to find strength in vulnerability, to learn how something so small could endure such vastness without breaking. [132] The answer, she suspected, was not in the data but in the old yellow lantern glass left behind by the previous crew. [154] It had once filtered the warning lights of the station, casting everything in a warm amber glow that softened steel and glass. [176] She held it to her eye and looked at the sky, and the constellations seemed closer, more patient. [194] Her official task was to empathize with machines, to keep them calm, but she found herself learning to empathize with the light instead. [217] She did this not with calculations but by tracing the path of falling stars with her fingertip across the cold glass of the observation dome. [242] Each meteor left a brief silver wound in the night, and she followed it until it vanished, breathing with it. [262] There was a primal elegance in their fall, a clean and ancient geometry that no human hand had drawn. [281] Over months she catalogued not numbers but stories, giving each streak a name and a last wish. [298] The slow one of November she called the Hesitant Pilgrim, the quick burst in January the Sudden Mercy. [316] In the silence between falls, the station settled into a flourishing stillness that was not emptiness but fullness held in reserve. [337] Dust motes hung in the yellow light of her lantern, and the air itself seemed to listen. [354] One night the arrays caught a storm, a hundred trails in an hour, and the alarms woke with soft chimes she had not heard in years. [380] Instead of logging them, she climbed to the roof with the yellow lantern glass in her palm and let the station track without her. [404] The sky was falling in slow fire, and for once she did not feel small beneath it. [421] She lay back on the cold metal and pressed the glass to her chest, feeling its faint warmth like a second heart. [443] Each star that fell seemed to pass through her, and she finally understood what the night had been trying to teach her. [465] Vulnerability was not the absence of strength but the aperture through which light could enter. [480] To be open, to be breakable, was to be visible to the universe. [493] She thought of all the quiet custodians before her, the other janitors of forgotten places who had kept watch without witness. [514] They too had learned to shine without being seen. [523] When the storm faded, she returned inside and found the printers humming, spilling paper covered in perfect trajectories she had not interfered with. [546] The data was complete without her needing to be essential. [556] She filed the papers in the archive, but she slipped the yellow lantern glass into its old socket above the main door, where it would greet anyone who finally arrived. [586] Its light fell across the empty chairs and the quiet instruments, turning the station from an outpost into a home. [606] She made tea and waited for the next fall, no longer watching to understand, but to belong. [623] Outside, the universe continued its patient erasure and inscription, uncaring and kind in equal measure. [638] She remained, the cosmic janitor, keeping the floor clean for the stars to land upon, if only in reflection. [657]</story>
