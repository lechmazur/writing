<story>After dreams become shareable, Mara learned that people no longer woke alone. [12] She worked as a sharp-eyed dusk noticer in the blooming library above clouds, where every archived dream was catalogued like a pressed flower. [35] The library itself was softly deliberate in its growth, extending new glass wings each spring to hold more light. [54] Mara's job was not to interpret dreams but to watch the edges where they frayed. [69] For decades the archivists had noticed something moving between stored dreams, a shadow unbound that slipped from one sleeper's memory to another's. [91] It was not a nightmare, but the absence left behind when hope was quietly taken. [106] Most noticers dismissed it as compression artifact from the early shareable-dream codecs. [118] Mara did not, to preserve the shape of hope that had once kept her sister alive through a long winter illness. [139] Her sister's final shared dream had been bright, then strangely hollow, as if something had siphoned its center. [157] Mara suspected the shadow had fed there, and she vowed to track its path. [171] The old catalog had no tools sensitive enough to catch it. [182] So she began working at dusk, when the library's light thinned and the dream shelves glowed faintly amber. [200] She isolated two collections of silent video footage from the earliest public dreaming, one from 2047 and one from 2083, and studied them by comparing silent video footage from decades apart. [231] Frame by frame, she traced how certain shared gardens, shared kitchens, shared childhood streets appeared lighter in the later archive. [251] It was as if someone had stolen a fraction of the color from hope itself. [266] On the fifty-seventh night, she found the pattern. [274] The shadow did not appear as darkness but as a sudden lag, a place where light forgot to fall. [293] It lingered longest over dreams of people learning to trust again. [304] Mara realized the shadow was learning, too, trying to metamorphose into something that could be felt while awake. [322] If it succeeded, waking hope would thin the same way dreaming hope had. [335] She carried a river stone in her pocket since childhood, smooth and grey, given to her by her sister for courage. [356] That evening she set the stone on the central viewing table, where the library bloomed outward into cloud and starlight. [376] The stone was an anchor, a thing too simple and too real to be dreamed, and shadows avoided what could not be edited. [399] When she projected both archives simultaneously onto the stone, the shadow recoiled. [411] For the first time Mara saw it clearly: a thin, restless filament that had never belonged to any single dreamer. [431] It hovered above the stone, unbound no longer, caught between two times that refused to let it hide. [449] Mara felt luminous regret wash through her, not sharp grief but the quiet ache of all the small hopes she had not protected sooner. [473] She understood then that the library had not been built to store dreams, but to teach people how to tend them. [494] She closed the projection, lifting the stone, and whispered her sister's name into the archive one last time. [512] The filament trembled, and then, with nowhere left to feed, it dissolved into ordinary dust caught in amber light. [531] The shared dreams on the shelves seemed to breathe deeper, their colors returning in small, brave increments. [548] Mara remained at the table until dawn, softly deliberate in her cataloging, logging each restored dream so no future noticer would miss the warning. [572] Outside the blooming library above clouds, the morning broke wide and blue, and the world below woke with borrowed light it finally owned again. [596] She pressed the river stone once more to her palm, warm now, and knew she would leave it here for the next sharp-eyed dusk noticer to find. [623]</story>
