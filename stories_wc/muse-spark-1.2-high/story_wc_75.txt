<story>Elias had been a cultured museum guide in the upper levels for twenty years before the displacement. [17] He was historically oriented by training and temperament, cataloguing every chisel mark on the sandstone friezes that lined the passage behind the falls. [40] The grotto city behind a monumental waterfall had been carved over centuries, its chambers humming with damp echoes and soft light. [61] After the beekeeper's last visit, no honey had come down the elevator shafts and the lower apiaries fell silent. [80] Without the wax seals the archivists used to preserve scrolls, the old songs and woven patterns began to fade faster than anyone could memorize them. [105] Elias understood his new purpose was to rescue dying arts before they vanished into mist. [120] That purpose felt sweet and sickening at once, a rosewater dread that clung to his throat whenever he inhaled the perfumed damp of the grotto. [145] The city's displaced persons had arrived three winters ago, when the northern plateau was flooded for a reservoir and their villages were declared uninhabitable. [169] They lived now in the limestone galleries below the museum, carving small altars and speaking in dialects Elias only half recognized from his academic texts. [194] Among them was Mara, a weaver in her sixties whose fingers remembered loom patterns no book had ever recorded. [213] There was also Jonah, a potter who could fire clay without a kiln, and a cluster of children who still knew the words to stone cutting chants. [240] Elias approached them not as a curator but as an apprentice, asking if they would teach him what could not be written down. [263] For weeks they hesitated, because their arts had been taken before, catalogued and then forgotten in glass cases. [281] Elias offered no contracts and no recording devices, only his hands and his memory, and a promise that their work would remain alive. [304] Mara finally relented when she saw that his palms were calloused from years of dusting stone, not soft from handling accession papers. [326] She taught him to card wool by feel in the dark, where the waterfall's roar made speech impossible and touch became language. [348] Jonah showed him how to read the heat of clay against his cheek, waiting for the precise moment to lift the pot from the ember bed. [374] In return Elias taught the children how to navigate the museum's upper archive, where the oldest maps of their lost plateau were stored. [397] He used an emerald-tinted opera glass lens left behind by a visiting conductor to examine the faded pigments without exposing them to harsh light. [421] Through that green tint the colors returned briefly, as if the past could still be seen breathing under dust. [440] One evening Mara brought her granddaughter Lene to the gallery, where the child performed a family custom of baking bread shaped like initials for each apprentice accepted. [467] Lene had shaped a careful E for Elias, its crust dark and fragrant, and a trembling M for Mara herself. [487] Eating the bread meant accepting responsibility to carry the knowledge forward, not to archive it behind glass. [504] That night the temperature in the grotto dropped sharply and a thin sheet of ice formed over the inner pools, sealing the lower galleries. [528] The elders said it had not frozen like that since the waterfall was diverted for the first excavation, a warning of stillness. [550] Elias woke to find the museum air sweet with rosewater and cold with fear, and he knew the displaced persons would lose their heat if the water stopped moving. [579] He gathered everyone in the central grotto and began to chant the stone cutting song Jonah had taught him, his voice cracking on the low notes. [605] One by one the others joined, the displaced persons and the archivists together, and the vibration loosened the ice. [624] Slowly the pools began to thaw, first at the edges where hands had pressed warmth into stone, then toward the center with a soft sigh. [649] Behind the thawing water the carving of a forgotten loom appeared on the grotto wall, revealed after decades of mineral veil. [670] Mara traced it with trembling fingers and wept, recognizing the pattern her mother had used before the flood. [688] Elias lifted the emerald-tinted opera glass lens again, not to catalog but to witness how light lived in the restored color. [709] In that moment he understood that his museum would no longer be a tomb of preserved objects but a living workshop where displaced hands remade history. [735] The rosewater dread did not leave him entirely, but it softened into something like reverence as the children laughed and the bread cooled on stone slabs. [761]</story>
