<story>Mara had been called an errant legend seeker long before she learned to care what the name meant. [18] She followed stories that other people abandoned, the drowned towns and the saints who never existed, hoping they would explain something true about the living. [43] Her grandfather left her only an antique pocket sundial, its brass face scratched and its gnomon bent slightly westward. [62] It did not tell time unless the sun cooperated, which made it useless and therefore honest. [78] She carried it to understand the architecture of lies, because every legend she chased was built like a house with false fronts and hidden rooms. [103] The latest lie was the city of Karra, said to have sunk intact beneath the twilight barge canal during the drought of nineteen twelve. [127] Old maps showed a bend where no bend should be, and the canal company denied it had ever diverted the water. [148] Mara arrived at the canal at dusk, when the water turned to dark glass and the towpath was empty except for dragonflies. [170] The air held a serene upheaval, quiet on the surface but churning underneath with the coming change of history being rewritten. [191] She hired a flat-bottomed barge from a man who warned her not to anchor where the old locks had been. [211] By midnight the fog had settled, and by training instincts she learned to read the water instead of the charts. [231] Her hands knew the drag of a submerged roof before her eyes did, and they trembled thoughtfully clear despite the cold. [252] She was not there to prove Karra existed, but to salvage what the canal had tried to erase. [270] Lowering a weighted line, she felt it catch on something solid and unnatural, a straight edge where nature allows none. [290] She dove, the sundial secured in a waxed pouch against her chest, because she needed a truth that did not need batteries. [312] Underwater the canal was another world, where silt hung like weather and the drowned bell tower emerged thoughtfully out of the murk. [334] Its clock had stopped at three fifteen, its hands rusted in place as if time itself had been asked to keep a secret. [357] She pressed her palm to the stone and felt the carved name KARRA still legible under a century of mud. [377] For a moment the old stories made sense, not as facts but as warnings about what people will bury to keep their roads straight. [401] She surfaced with a brick, a child's slate, and a brass canal company seal, proof that the diversion had been deliberate. [422] On the barge again she opened the sundial and caught the last thin sun through the fog, its shadow falling just past noon. [445] The light was too weak to read by, yet it showed her the truth with hindsight clarity, how the company had lied to hide a flooded town and called it progress. [476] She understood then that legends do not start with monsters but with paperwork, with men deciding which map will be remembered. [497] The water had kept the secret better than any archive, because water does not argue or edit. [514] She did not raise the town, she only recorded it, marking coordinates, photographing the slate where a child had written KARRA IS HOME. [537] To salvage a story is not to bring it back, but to let it be seen on its own terms. [557] By dawn she was back at the towpath, her notes damp, her hands cut from the brick, her pouch still heavy with the sundial. [581] She would publish by the silence of morning radio, when the station played static between anthems and only the sleepless listened. [602] Those listeners would hear about Karra and know that their own towns might also be built on a quiet flood. [622] She slipped the sundial back into her pocket, useless for navigation but perfect for remembering that truth needs light to cast a shadow. [645]</story>
