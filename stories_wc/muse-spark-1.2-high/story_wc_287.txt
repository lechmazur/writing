<story>Mara had been a fire escape gardener for seven years, coaxing life from rusted grates above the alley. [18] She was wistfully pragmatic, believing that nothing bloomed without attention but that attention did not guarantee bloom. [35] On her third floor landing the old rosebush had survived three winters in a cracked paint bucket, its thorns sharp as promises. [57] She told herself she would replant it properly after the final petal drops from the old rosebush, when its season finally ended. [79] That night the last petal fell without ceremony, curling brown at the edge before landing on the iron slats. [98] Mara stared at the bare stems and felt a quivering hush settle over the fire escape, as if the plant were holding its breath. [170] Her grandfather had been a lockmaster, and in his dented tobacco tin she found a lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth, worn smooth at the tip. [194] He had used it to cut keys for the whole harbor, each notch measured with a patience Mara had never inherited. [215] Now the boathouse locks were rusted shut, and the town council planned to demolish it by week's end. [233] Inside lay his notebooks, his brass shims, and the small mirrored panels he had once made to catch harbor light for the winter storeroom. [257] Mara wanted to save them, not for money but from a protected vulnerability that tightened in her chest whenever she thought of his hands. [281] She carried the rose bucket's empty soil, the tin, and a borrowed rowboat key down the alley toward the water. [346] Each stroke rippled the moon's reflection and sent it scattering like coins toward the boathouse pilings. [362] The boathouse door was swollen and locked, its padlock crusted with years of spray. [376] Mara knelt, fitted the lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth against the keyway, and used it not as a cutter but as a feeler to map the pins. [402] Her grandfather had taught her as a child to listen for the click, to wait for the lock to confess. [457] Inside, moonlight fell through broken shutters in narrow bars, illuminating dust and old cut keys hung on nails. [475] She lifted one mirrored panel and angled it toward the window, trying to reflect light into dark corners where the notebooks lay stacked. [498] Light jumped, caught another panel she propped against a beam, and suddenly the whole room flickered awake. [515] She wanted to instill that same reflected brightness in the town council, to show them that preservation was practical as well as sentimental. [538] At sunrise she would bring them here, with the rhythm of oars in water still echoing in her arms, and let the harbor do the arguing. [564] She gathered the notebooks, wrapped the lockmaster's bitting gauge tooth in cloth, and tucked the mirrored panels under her arm. [584] Outside, the luminous harbor by moonlight was beginning to pale, but the water still held the moon's last coin of light.
