<story>Mara had spent twelve years chasing earthquakes, floods, and famines, but no one could ever quite pin down where she went between disasters. [23] As an elusive disaster-relief logistician, she existed in manifests and radio chatter, a name that arrived before she did and lingered after she left. [47] When the story changes, it is always on a Tuesday in late October, when the first frost makes the air precise. [68] That Tuesday, Mara was not in a refugee camp or a warehouse, but waiting alone at the disused railway station overgrown with ivy outside Karlovac. [93] The station had been abandoned after the war, its platform cracked, its timetable forever stuck at 14:42, its waiting room home to pigeons and peeling posters. [119] She was there because a boy in Split had given her a fragment of moon rock, no larger than a thumbnail, and told her it was lonely. [146] The boy, Tomas, had received it from his grandfather, who had been given it by an American geologist in 1973, who claimed it was from Apollo 17. [173] Mara did not believe it was real, but she believed Tomas believed it, and that was enough to make her stay. [194] Tomas was desperately jocular, the kind of child who made jokes when the lights went out, who offered to sell her invisible umbrellas during a storm. [220] He had asked her, with a grin that did not reach his eyes, if she knew how to find the cure for loneliness. [243] Mara had laughed, then realized he was not joking, and the question lodged in her chest like a splinter. [262] She told him she did not know, but that she would look for an answer and send it through improbable rumor chains, the only network that still reached everywhere. [291] In her world, official channels failed, but rumors moved like water, from truck drivers to nuns to border guards to bakers. [312] She left Tomas her satellite phone number and took the rock, promising to bring it back with an answer. [331] Now, at the station, she was waiting because the rumor had returned, as she knew it would, bound by reciprocity. [351] She had released a single question into the wild three months earlier, whispering it to a convoy cook in Kosovo: what cures loneliness? [374] By design, she asked for no direct reply, only that anyone who had an answer should pass it along until it found its way back to the ivy station on the last Tuesday of October. [409] It was polite anarchy, her favorite system, where there were no leaders but everyone agreed to keep the bench by the ticket window free for strangers. [435] People left what they could, took what they needed, and trusted that the balance would hold without being enforced. [454] At noon, an old woman arrived pulling a cart of apples, looked at Mara, and without introduction said, my sister says you have to catalyze something that is already there. [484] Mara frowned and asked what she meant. [491] The woman placed an apple on the bench, took the moon rock from Mara's open palm, warmed it in her hands, and placed it back. [516] Loneliness is not cured, the woman said, it is witnessed. [526] My sister lost her husband to the river and she still sets two cups of tea every morning, because the emptiness listens when she does. [551] Mara felt the small weight of the rock grow heavier, not with mass but with all the hands it had passed through. [573] Another traveler came at one, a teenage conductor who no longer had a train, and left a folded note that read, invite someone to watch you do nothing. [601] A third arrived at two, a deminer with a scar like a zipper, who simply sat beside Mara for an hour and shared his bread without speaking. [628] Each visit was an answer, each silence a sentence in the rumor that had circled the continent to return to her. [649] Mara understood then that she had not been sent to find a cure and deliver it to Tomas, but to catalyze the cure by making people admit they needed it. [679] When Tomas arrived at sunset, breathless and clutching his too-big coat, he had followed the same rumors that guided everyone else, trading a joke for a direction. [706] You came, Mara said, surprised at the tightness in her own throat. [718] Tomas grinned, desperately jocular to the end, and said, well you left me a moon rock so I had to bring you an earth rock to keep it company. [747] He held out a smooth river stone, and Mara, bound by reciprocity, traded it for the fragment and then immediately traded it back, so neither owed the other. [775] They sat on the bench together as the ivy shivered in the wind, watching the empty tracks and feeling not alone at all. [798]</story>
