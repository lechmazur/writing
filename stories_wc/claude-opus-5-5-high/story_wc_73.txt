Ottoline Vesk had been climbing the staircase long enough to stop calling it long. [14] There was no time on the stairs, only the next step, and the next, rising forever under a ceiling that rose with her like a patient lid. [41] She could go anywhere upward and nowhere else. [49] It was the largest cell ever built, and she had decided, grimly, that it was a good one. [67] Things will go badly, she liked to tell the steps, and that is how we will learn where they go well. [88]

In her coat pocket she carried a pair of dice that had belonged to her brother, Anselm, who had climbed beside her until the landing. [113] The landing had appeared once, a flat square of oak where every other surface was stone, and on it stood a door with a brass handle warm from someone else's hand. [144] Anselm had rolled the dice to decide. [151] Seven, he said, and opened the door, and went. [160] Ottoline had wanted a proof first, and while she was still reasoning, the door closed, the oak became stone, and the stair resumed its endless ascent as though nothing had been offered. [192] He had left the dice on the last oak board. [202]

She did not want the door back; she knew better than to hope that cleanly. [217] What she wanted was the feel of it, the exact grain of the chance she had not taken, so she could hold it and know its shape instead of carrying a blur. [249] A missed opportunity with no texture was only a wound; one with texture was a map. [265]

So she worked the way she trusted, through mathematical proofs, chalked step by step onto the risers as she climbed. [285] Lemma one: the staircase never tires, never slows, never asks for fuel. [297] Lemma two: she had passed her own chalk marks three times, identical to the stroke, which meant the stair was a loop pretending to be a line. [324] Therefore the staircase was a perpetual motion machine, lifting her forever while returning her to the same place, and a perfect machine of that kind must lose nothing, not heat, not dust, not a single grain. [360]

Lemma three was the chipped die. [366] One of Anselm's dice was whole; the other had a notch bitten from the corner beside the six, and every time she rolled it, it shed a fleck of bone that did not reappear on the next loop. [404] The stair kept everything else. [409] It did not keep the dust. [415] The conclusion was chalked in her smallest hand: something here is losing, and whatever loses has an edge, and whatever has an edge can be opened. [441]

She rolled the chipped die ten thousand times, or perhaps four, there being no time to count against, and tracked where the flecks drifted. [465] They slid, always, toward one riser where the stone was faintly warm. [477]

Anselm would have kicked it. [482] Her younger self would have written another page. [490] Ottoline split the difference, as she had learned to: she wedged the whole die behind her heel so she could not slip backward, kept one hand on the rail, and with the other pressed the chipped die into the warm seam like a key. [534]

The stair ruptured. [537]

It was not loud. [541] The riser split along the line of her proof, and the perpetual hum she had stopped hearing faltered, like a heart missing one beat out of courtesy. [568] Behind the crack was oak. [573] She reached in and touched it: the grain ran crosswise, rough where a thumb had worried it, and the brass handle was still faintly warm, not from Anselm now but from the memory of him standing there, grinning, asking her to trust a number she had not derived. [621]

That was the texture, then. [626] Not regret, which was smooth and endless like the stairs, but this: splinter and warmth and a question that had once had a deadline. [650] She held it until she knew it by heart. [659]

The seam was already closing; the machine repaired itself, as nearly perfect things insist on doing. [675] Ottoline considered the gap, the stair, the door, and judged the odds poor, which to her had always meant possible. [695] She scrawled the last line of the proof on the step, set the unchipped die beside it for whoever climbed next, and kept the broken one, because it was the one that had told the truth. [731]

Then, with one foot still on the stone and the other on the oak, she opened the door. [749] Beyond it was another staircase, going down. [756] Somewhere below, faintly, dice were rattling. [762]

Good, she thought, and stepped through. [768]
