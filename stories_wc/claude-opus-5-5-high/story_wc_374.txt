The forests were holding elections again, which meant the radios hissed with pollen counts and nobody in Harrow's Reach could sleep. [21] Ines Vell climbed the lavender bluff after midnight, her skin glowing the faint blue that the orbital nurseries had stitched into every child born above the clouds. [48] She had been designed to read instruments in the dark of a hull, not to hike through knee-high flowers, and she hated how the lavender brushed her calves and lit up in her light like something sentimental. [85] Above her the aurora unrolled in green and rose, the old sky doing what it had done before anyone learned to live inside it. [109]

On her left hand she wore her grandmother's tin compass ring. [120] It was a cheap thing, a souvenir from the last century, with a needle no longer than an eyelash under a scratched lens. [143] It had never once pointed north. [149]

Her grandmother, Maren, had carried weather. [155] Everyone in the Reach remembered it: the soft private drizzle that followed her to market, the fog that settled on her porch in August, the single snowflake that landed on her coffin while the rest of the valley baked. [194] The station doctors had called it a folk illness. [203] Ines, who trusted sensors and nothing else, had called it coincidence, loudly, for twenty years. [218] But Maren had pressed the ring into her palm at the end and said, Taste it when the trees vote, and then decide what's coincidence. [243] So Ines had come down the elevator for the first time in a decade to learn why some people carried weather with them, or to prove that they did not. [273]

She reached the crest and stopped. [279] There were others on the bluff. [285] Dozens of them, standing in scattered ones and twos among the lavender, and above each head hung a small disobedient climate: a coil of mist, a pocket of sleet, a cloud no bigger than a pillow dripping steadily onto an old man's hat. [328] None of them spoke. [332] They looked like people waiting for a tram that had stopped running years ago. [346]

Ines raised the ring to her lips the way Maren had shown her, a childish trick, and touched her tongue to the tin. [369] It tasted of pennies, as it always had. [377] Then, as she turned toward the old man, it tasted of wet slate. [390] She turned toward a woman wrapped in fog, and the metal went cool and green, like crushed stems. [408] Toward a boy with sleet in his hair: ozone, sharp as a blown fuse. [422]

Her pulse climbed. [425] Tin did not do that. [430] Tongues did not do that. [435] Unless the metal was catching something in the air, some ion or spore, the way the forest networks passed their ballots root to root through miles of fungal thread. [464]

The aurora brightened, and the weather-carriers began to walk. [473] Slowly, without looking at each other, they drifted, each seeming to wander at random, and yet their paths bent the same way, like iron filings finding a magnet nobody could see. [504] They converged on the western edge of the bluff, where the ground dropped toward the dry black spruce country that had not seen rain in three summers. [531] The tiny needle in the ring swung and settled there too. [542]

Ines understood it the way she understood a failing seal, all at once and too late to argue. [560] The needle had never been broken. [566] It had simply been waiting for enough of them. [575] No single carrier knew the direction; each only leaned a little, tasted a little, followed a hunch in the lungs. [595] Together they were the compass, emergent, built from people the way the forest's vote was built from a billion quiet roots. [616] The trees had not asked for rain by speaking. [625] They had chosen messengers, seeded them with something that caught at clouds, and when the solar wind lit the sky and the election closed, the messengers walked the result to where it was needed. [659] It was the most earthbound thing she had ever seen, and it answered to the sun. [675] Her grandmother had been a ballot. [681]

Ines laughed, a strange cracked sound, and the fog-wrapped woman glanced back at her with something like welcome. [699] The ring tasted of rain now, plain and cold and unmistakable, the taste of the porch in August, of being eight years old in Maren's lap. [725] Something damp touched Ines's cheek. [730] She looked up. [733] A small grey cloud had gathered above her, lit from beneath by her own blue skin, so that it glowed like a lantern hung between the earth and the stars. [763]

She thought of the station, of its perfect filtered air, of all the reasons she had to go back. [782] Then she followed the others west, carrying the vote. [791]
