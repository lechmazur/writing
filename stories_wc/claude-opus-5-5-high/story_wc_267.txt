The Cobalt Hold had been a fortress before it was a hotel, and its blue-glazed walls still wore the snow like armor. [22]
Ansel carried luggage up its corridors in gloves two sizes too large, because the nerves in his fingers were growing back and the doctor had said they must be kept warm while they learned to feel again. [59]
Every dawn he joined the other bellhops in the chapel for the silent choir. [73]
Above the Hold hung a cornice of snow the size of a cathedral, and the house rule was older than anyone: no bells, no shouting, no music. [100]
So they stood in rows and mouthed the hymns, which were seraphically mundane, verses of praise for dry socks, banked coals, bread that rose, doors that latched, each ending on the same word: remain. [134]
Mother Ilse, who led them, conducted with two fingers and never once opened her own mouth. [150]

Ansel had nearly lost his hands on that mountain, the winter he crossed the pass in a whiteout and swung his lantern at the fortress gate for an hour while no one answered. [183]
A goatherd had found him at dawn. [190]
He took the bellhop job afterward because he wanted to understand what kind of safety was worth that hour. [209]
Nobody would say. [212]
The guests spoke of the cornice the way sailors speak of reefs, and the staff simply lowered their eyes. [231]

It was the west tower that told him. [239]
Its spiral staircase was closed, roped off, grey with years of dust, and on his rounds Ansel noticed the slightest discoloration on the ninth step: a faint orange bloom, like a thumbprint of rust. [273]
Higher up there was another, then another, always beside a narrow path worn along the wall, as if someone climbed often and paused on each step to set down something small, iron, and wet with snowmelt. [309]
He ducked the rope. [313]
His fingers buzzed inside the gloves, the hot needling the doctor called a good sign. [328]

At the top was a round room with a black piano under a sheet, and Mother Ilse sitting on the floor beside it. [351]
The piano bench stood crooked, one leg sagging where a bolt was missing. [364]
The bolt was in her palm, rusted orange, and she was turning it the way other women turned rosary beads. [384]
She spoke, the first time he had heard her voice, low as a draft under a door. [401]
"I was the one on duty the night of your lantern," she said. [414]
"I saw it from this window." [420]
"The director said the cornice was loaded, that one chord could bury forty guests." [434]
"So I took the bolt out of the bench, so no one could sit, so I would not be tempted, and I watched your light until it went out." [463]
She had carried the bolt up these stairs every storm night since, she said, to prove to herself she could still choose not to put it back. [490]
"Some silences keep people alive," she said. [497]
"Mine was not pardonable." [501]
"It only happened to be safe." [507]

Beyond the glass, down in the blue dark below the gate, a light was swinging. [522]
Ilse's hand closed over the bolt. [528]
Ansel understood then that she had not climbed here to confess, but to see whether she would do it again. [548]

He pulled off his gloves. [553]
The air bit his raw fingers and the nerves shrieked awake, every whorl of skin suddenly alive, and he held out his hand. [576]
She looked at it for a long time. [584]
Then she gave him the bolt. [590]
He threaded it through the bench leg by feel, and for the first time in a year feeling was something he could trust. [613]

He did not play a chord. [619]
He played a single low note, then another, slow and steady as a heartbeat, the softest sound a lost person could still follow. [642]
Above them the cornice groaned, and somewhere a shelf of snow let go and hissed past the tower. [660]
Ilse stood behind him and put her hand on his shoulder. [671]
"Remain," she whispered, which was the last word of every hymn they had ever mouthed. [686]
So he remained, fingers burning on the keys, while the cornice held and the light below turned toward the gate. [706]

By dawn the silent choir stood in its rows again, and a stranger with frost-white eyebrows sat among the guests, and Mother Ilse, for the first time, sang the last word aloud. [738]
It was barely a breath, and the mountain let it pass. [749]
