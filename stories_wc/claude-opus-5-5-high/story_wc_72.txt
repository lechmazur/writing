The garden had been drowned for sixty years, but nothing in it had agreed to die. [16] Kelp leaned over the hedges like gardeners bent at their work, and the stone benches seemed to shift their weight whenever Mara's lamp swept across them. [42] She kicked past a fountain that still held water inside water, past trellises whose iron roses opened and closed in the current, until she reached the statue at the garden's heart. [73] It was a woman carved from white marble, kneeling with a trowel, and a crack ran from her collarbone down to her ribs. [96]

"You told them," the statue said, not aloud but inside Mara's skull, in a voice like sand settling. [114]

Mara's breath roared in her regulator. [120] Three days ago she had stood in the harbor hall at Esk and read her grandmother's confession aloud: that Ilse Varn, last keeper of the sunken garden, had opened the sluice that drowned the old city and the forty people still sleeping in it. [164] The hall had gone silent, then furious, and by evening the town had its certainty: Ilse was a murderer, and Mara was a murderer's blood. [189] Mara had agreed with them. [194] That was why she had come down. [201]

She unclipped the ampoule from her belt, a finger of old glass freckled purple from the foxglove tincture it had once held, and pushed it toward the statue. [229] "I found this in her apron after she died," Mara thought, as loudly as she could. [245] "Foxglove stops a heart." [248] "The warden who guarded the sluice died the night before the flood, and now I know why." [265]

The statue's cracked chest seemed to rise, though marble cannot breathe. [276] "Foxglove also steadies a heart," it said. [283] "In small doses it keeps a failing one beating." [292] "The warden was named Tomas, Ilse loved him, and she dosed him every night for a year because his heart was giving out." [315] "He died anyway, of the sickness, not of her." [324]

Mara hung motionless while a shoal of silver fish drifted between them like a thought neither wanted to finish. [343]

"Then she still opened the sluice," she thought. [351]

"She did." [353] The statue did not soften it; its kindness was the kind that cut cleanly, so the wound could close. [372] "But the seawall was already splitting along its old seam, and a wall that bursts does not drown forty people." [392] "It drowns four thousand, all at once, in the dark." [402] "Ilse opened the sluice beside the weakest seam and let the sea thread through it slowly, a rising tide instead of a wave, so the city had half a night to climb the hill to Esk." [438] "Forty would not leave their houses." [444] "She rang the bell until the water reached her mouth." [454]

Mara's lamp trembled, and every shadow in the garden trembled with it, the hedges shivering, the iron roses clenching shut. [474] "Why did she write only that she opened it?" [483]

"Because she believed she should have found another way, and guilt is a very certain thing." [499] The statue paused, and for the first time its voice faltered, grains slipping. [512] "And because she was protecting me." [518]

"You?" [519]

"Whole stone is too dense for thought to pass," the statue said. [531] "The same tremor that split the seawall split me, and through that crack I heard the city for the first time." [552] "I felt the seam giving before anyone else did, and I was the one who told Ilse to open the sluice." [573] "Forty people are in my choice as much as hers." [583]

The revelations kept arriving, each one loosening the last, until Mara understood that the truth was not a single stone but a wall of them, and her reading had shown the town only one. [617]

"Why tell me now?" [621]

"Because you came to bury the ampoule and your grandmother with it," the statue said. [636] "I would rather you carried it back up and let Esk hold two things at once: that she drowned forty people, and that she saved everyone else." [663] "Certainty is a whole stone." [668] "Nothing gets through it." [672]

Mara looked at the crack in the statue's chest, the only reason it could speak, and then at the ampoule in her glove. [695] She unwound a length of line from her reel, threaded it through the wire loop on the ampoule's neck, and tied it at her throat where she would feel it knock against her heart on the long ascent. [733] In Esk, she would read the rest aloud. [741] Behind her, the garden stirred, every lifeless thing leaning after her light, and the statue lowered its trowel as if, at last, it could rest. [766]
