The first message appeared the spring after we stopped calling ourselves survivors and started calling ourselves the Vale family. [19] Eleven strangers had crawled out of the wreck of the Perpetua, and twelve years of shared blight, shared soup, and shared graves had braided us together tighter than rope. [48] Now the orchard was dying anyway. [54]

Kessa is an orchard planet, or was meant to be, seeded by an earlier colony that abandoned it for reasons no archive explained. [77] They left us their pear groves, their empty town with its neat stone sidewalks, and the arches. [94] The arches float above the rows like broken halos, great curves of rock that drift on the magnetic winds, trailing brambles from their undersides. [118] Some days their shadows slid over the trees so fast the leaves shivered. [131]

The pears had stopped setting fruit three seasons ago. [140] No pollinators, Tomas said, spitting into the dirt, and no way to make any. [154]

Then I walked into the empty town at dawn to gather roof slate and found words chalked across the sidewalk in a looping, patient hand. [179]

FROST TONIGHT. [181] COVER THE NORTH ROWS. [185]

Nobody in our family had written it. [192] Nobody else lived on Kessa. [197] We covered the north rows anyway, and the frost came, and we lost nothing. [211]

After that the messages came every few mornings, always on deserted streets, always gone by noon as if rain had licked them away when no rain had fallen. [239] Tell Oni the well rope is fraying. [246] Tomas is lonely; sit with him. [252] The handwriting was strangely trustworthy, the way a grandmother's voice is trustworthy even when she tells you to eat the bitter greens. [274] It never asked for anything for itself. [281]

Tomas called it a trap. [286] Oni called it a ghost. [291] I called it the Gardener, because I had a suspicion I wanted to test. [305]

At the edge of town lay the old butterfly garden, a glass house long since shattered, thorned over with wild roses. [326] Inside, hanging from a rusted hook, I had once found a copper watering bell: a vessel shaped like a church bell, pierced with holes, with a clapper that chimed as water poured through. [359] Beside it, on a plant label, someone had written ARCH BUTTERFLIES, SUMMER ROOST in that same looping hand, signed M. Vey, Keeper. [381]

I carried the label into town, knelt beside that morning's chalk, and made myself reexamine every loop and crossbar until there was no doubt left. [406] The keeper of the butterflies had never left Kessa. [415]

The next dawn, the message ran the whole length of Main Street, the letters thinner than before, as if each one cost her something. [439]

WHEN THE HUSH RISES, THE ARCHES STOP. [446] ALL OF YOU, TOGETHER, CLIMB THE GREAT ARCH. [454] RING THE BELL INTO THE HOLLOWS WHERE MY BUTTERFLIES SLEEP. [464] ONE PAIR OF HANDS WAS NEVER ENOUGH. [471]

The Hush is what we named the nebula that crosses our sky each autumn, a violet smear of gas that dims the sun's harsh light and stills the magnetic winds for three nights. [504] It is our nebula of respite, the only time anyone sleeps well. [516] I understood then why the first colony failed: they had quarreled and scattered, and the keeper had stayed alone with a bell she could not lift high enough, and died waiting for help that never came. [552]

Tomas refused until the second night of the Hush, when he saw the rest of us roping ladders together and swore and took the heaviest coil himself. [579] Nine of us climbed the great arch in violet light, passing water buckets hand to hand up a chain of bodies, the brambles tearing our sleeves and our palms. [608] Oni bled; Tomas bled; I bled. [614]

At the summit I held the bell over the dark hollows and poured, and it rang, a thin sweet note that went down into the stone. [640] For a long time, nothing. [645] Then the hollows breathed out wings, copper and ash-gray, hundreds of them, then thousands, spiraling down toward the starving pear trees like embers returning to the fire. [672]

Not every tree will recover. [677] The east rows are too far gone, and our hands will scar. [689] But this spring the blossoms came back, pale and stubborn among the brambles. [702]

The last message was so faint I had to kneel with my nose nearly to the stone. [719]

THANK YOU, FAMILY. [722] NOW I CAN REST. [726]

I left a stick of chalk there, and the watering bell, filled, beside it, in case she ever wants to garden again. [748]
