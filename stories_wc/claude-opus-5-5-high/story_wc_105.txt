The tide went out of the orchard at four in the afternoon, and Ilse Varga walked in after it. [19] The greenhouse had sunk forty years ago, when the storm that drowned her grandfather's apples also threw six boats against the glass, and now the hulls lay around it in a broken ring, ribs furred with kelp. [56] Salt had frosted the panes that remained. [63] Inside, the trees still stood, black and leafless, their branches strung with barnacles like hard fruit. [79]

She was eleven weeks along, by the clinic's count, and she thought of it as a clock: somewhere under her coat, cells were dividing, two to four to thousands, whether she was ready or not. [114]

She designed hearing aids for a living, small shells of resin and wire that taught damaged ears to notice what they were missing. [137] Her own ears worked too well, or too strangely. [146] Since childhood she had heard colors faintly, a thin hum beneath the world: rust sang low, the aurora above the island buzzed a cold green, her mother's voice had always been amber. [178]

Her mother had been buried nine days ago. [186] In the desk drawer, beneath the funeral bills, Ilse had found the letters. [199] Her father had not gone down with the trawler Solveig, whose split hull lay nearest the greenhouse door. [217] He had lived in Tromso for thirty years, writing every spring to ask whether his daughter might visit, and every spring her mother had written back no. [244] He had died in March. [249]

Reading the letters, Ilse had waited for the break to sound, for some crack of color, and heard nothing at all. [270] That frightened her more than the lie. [277] Because she had already begun rehearsing one of her own: that the child's father, a clinic number on a form, had been a good man lost at sea. [305] It had come to her so easily, and in her own voice she had heard amber. [321] If trust breaking made a sound, she wanted to learn it before she made it herself. [337]

So she did what she did for clients who could not describe their silence: she collected absences. [354] She set a calibrated microphone inside the Solveig's hull, where no body had ever lain, and recorded the hollow for ten minutes. [376] She recorded the air above the memorial stone her mother had set among the drowned trees, a stone with nothing beneath it. [398] She recorded the gap on the answering machine tape bundled with the letters, the four seconds after a man's voice said her name, where someone had pressed erase. [426]

Then, on her laptop in the dripping greenhouse, she subtracted the room tone from each recording, the wind, the tide, the gulls, until only the shape of what was missing remained. [457] The three shapes matched. [461] Layered, they hummed a color she had never heard before, bruise-violet shading into grey, like light remembered rather than seen. [481] A waveform on a screen would not hold it; screens forgot. [492] She needed metal. [495]

Her grandfather's forge stood on the ridge above the wrecks, roofless now, its hearth a drift of pale ash under the aurora. [517] The green light rippled across the anvil, and the buzz filled her teeth. [530] She dug coal from the lean-to, fed the bellows until the ash woke orange, and heated a disc of brass no wider than a thumbnail, the kind she used for diaphragms. [561] Then she laid the cooling metal over the speaker of her phone and played the absences into it, again and again, tapping with the small hammer in rhythm, until the waveform was imprinted in the brass as ridges fine as fingerprints. [602]

When it cooled, she struck it. [608] It did not crack. [612] It sighed, a long falling tone, exactly the sound of the tide leaving the orchard: slow, patient, repeated every day for thirty years until nothing green could live there. [641]

Ilse sat on the anvil's edge and understood. [649] Trust did not break in a moment she could have caught. [660] It drained, one kind omission at a time, and each omission became a memory someone else had to live inside, the way she had lived inside a drowned father. [689] Her mother had not been cruel, only afraid, and the fear had become the shape of Ilse's whole childhood. [708]

Below, the tide turned and began to fill the greenhouse, silver climbing the black trunks. [723] Ilse punched the brass disc, threaded it onto a cord, and hung it at her throat, where it would hum whenever she spoke. [746] Somewhere beneath it, cells went on dividing, building a child who would one day ask about its father. [764] She would tell the truth, and if her voice ever turned amber, the brass would sigh against her skin. [783]
