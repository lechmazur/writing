The Electra was found in April, nose-down in forty meters of coral off Nikumaroro, and by June the cameras had gone home. [22] Ines Calder stayed. [25] She told the foundation she was mapping debris fields, which was true in the way that most of her sentences were true: precisely, and not completely. [51]

Two kilometers north, under the reef shelf, lay the Corriveau, a French liner that had gone down in the typhoon of 1937, three weeks after a woman's voice stopped coming through the static. [84] Nobody remembered the Corriveau. [88] Its library had settled intact on the port side, shelves bolted to what was now the floor, and Ines had been swimming through it every morning for eleven days. [117]

Hal was usually there before her. [123] She had known him since she was nine, face-down in a quarry in Ohio, when he had sat beside her on the cold stone bottom and asked, politely, whether she was finished. [155] She had said no. [159] He had told her his name, as though that were a courtesy owed to anyone who refused him. [177] Since then he had turned up at the edges of things: her mother's hospice room, a flooded cave in Yucatan, and now the satellite calls from a hospital in Suva, where a ventilator breathed for her husband with the patience of a machine that did not know what it was waiting for. [229] The doctors wanted her answer by Friday. [236]

Ines was a physicist before she was a diver, and she believed that anything could be understood if you measured it patiently enough. [259] Letting go was a problem of forces: tension held, tension released, a mass surrendered to gravity or current. [277] She had come to the library because she could not find the equation for it anywhere else. [294]

The Corriveau's wireless room had shared a wall with the library, and the sinking had pushed the operator's desk through it, so that on the second day Ines found it wedged among the reading tables, a logbook and a snow globe sealed in its drawer. [339] The globe held a tiny silver airplane above a painted sea. [350] Its glass had survived eighty-seven years of pressure, and when she turned it, the flakes rose around the little plane and drifted down so slowly that she timed them out of habit: forty-one seconds from storm to stillness. [388]

The logbook was worse. [392] Its pages had fused into a single brown brick, and the binding thread had swollen and snarled into the paper like roots through soil. [416] She could not surface it without losing it, so she worked where it lay, hovering over the reading table with dental picks and a lamp, untangling the threads one fiber at a time. [449]

Hal watched from the stacks, his outline wavering like a reflection that had lost its surface. [465] "You could just ask me," he said, the words arriving without bubbles. [477]

She wrote on her slate: NOT YOUR DEPARTMENT. [485]

"Everything that ends is my department." [491]

She did not answer, and he did not insist; in thirty-one years he had never once hurried her. [509]

On the eleventh morning the last knot gave, and the pages separated like a held breath let out. [527] The operator's hand was small and exact. [534] July 2, 1937, 0843 local: KHAQQ, faint, "We are on the line 157 337... running on line north and south." [554] Below it, every hour for nineteen days, the same note: Listening. Nothing. [566]

The final entry was not in log form. [574] "I have kept her aloft by listening," it read. [583] "But a signal is not a person, and holding a frequency open is not the same as holding a hand. [603] Tonight I switch off the set, and let her land wherever she has already landed." [618]

Ines read it three times, measuring nothing. [625]

Then she turned the snow globe once more, set it on the table, and took her hands away. [643] As long as she had held it, the small tremors of her hands had kept the storm alive; released, it began at once to fall. [668] That was the whole of the physics, she noted, and it had never been about force at all. [686]

She did not stay to count the seconds. [694]

At the library door she turned to Hal, who was looking at the little silver plane with something like tenderness. [714] She wrote on her slate, FRIDAY, and underlined it, and held it up so he could read. [731]

"Friday," he agreed, and for once she let him rise beside her toward the bright, ordinary surface. [748]
