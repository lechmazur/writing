On Sessily, where the tide sometimes came in upward, Oma Reyt was paid in salt to decide what was true. [20] She carried calipers, a brass thermometer, and a ledger ruled into two columns: Happened and Dreamed. [36] When the fishwife swore a heron had recited her grandmother's recipe, Oma measured the heron, tasted the soup, and wrote it under Happened, because it was good soup and the heron's beak was exactly the length of a ladle. [75] Her methods were empirically whimsical, the islanders said, and they meant it kindly. [88]

Now she stood on the ancient ridge above the village, where the old terraces had gone wild with bloom, and she did not know which column she belonged in. [117] Everything flowered at once up here before the monsoon arrived: plum-colored lilies, bee orchids, a white vine that opened only when someone nearby told the truth. [143] At the center stood the stump of the Remembering Tree, split by lightning in the dry months. [160] Every year, on the last clear evening, the whole island climbed here to eat its fruit. [176] The monsoon on Sessily did not only drown the fields; its rain rinsed minds, and for three weeks people forgot the names of reefs, the steps of the harvest dance, which cousins had feuded and why. [212] The fruit held all of it, sweet and heavy, and when the rains cleared, the island remembered itself. [230]

This year there was no fruit. [236]

There was one seed, black and ridged like a knuckle, which Oma had pried from the scorched heartwood. [254] Old Pell, who had tended the tree for sixty years, said a knowledge fruit seed would not waken for water or sun. [276] "It grew from us," he said. [282] "Feed it what it's made of." [288] What it was made of, Pell insisted, was dreams, the ones the island shared without noticing: the same drowned bell rung in a hundred sleeps, the same staircase into the sea. [319]

A reality checker had no business believing this. [327] But Oma had seen the clouds stacking on the horizon like wet wool, and she remembered the thin-harvest year when her grandmother, given only half a fruit, had forgotten Oma's name for an entire wet month. [363]

So she went door to door with her ledger and asked people what they had dreamed. [379] The net-mender had dreamed of a woman braiding rope from eel shadows. [391] The schoolteacher had dreamed of counting boats that turned into her uncles. [403] A boy had dreamed only of a door painted green. [413]

Oma interpreted them the way she checked everything, with tools and patience. [425] The eel-shadow rope matched the old knot the island used for mooring in storms, a knot nobody under forty could tie. [446] The boats-into-uncles was the Drowning Year, when eleven men were lost and the village stopped saying their names aloud. [465] The green door took her two days, until Pell told her the first shrine on the ridge had had one, before the fire. [488]

Each evening she climbed the ridge, knelt beside the seed in its bed of ash and petals, and read her interpretations aloud: the knot, step by step; the eleven names; the color of the vanished door. [524] She measured the seed each time. [530] It did not change. [534]

She began to suspect she was inventing the meanings, stitching coincidence into history because she needed it to hold. [553] A reality checker who made things true by wanting them was the worst kind of liar. [569]

On the last clear evening, with thunder walking the far reef, the villagers followed her up the ridge without being asked. [590] They had heard what she was doing. [597] They brought more dreams, and she stopped interpreting and simply let them speak: an old man tying the storm knot with shaking hands, the schoolteacher saying eleven names into the wind, the boy describing a green he had never seen awake. [638]

Beside Oma, the white truth-vine opened, one blossom after another, until the ridge glowed. [652]

Then the first drop of monsoon struck the ash. [661]

When Oma looked down, the seed had split, and a green thread, the exact green of the boy's door, was lifting toward the rain. [685] She would never know whether the dreams had wakened it, or the water, or simply time. [701] Rain filled the lilies, and around her the neighbors kept talking, repeating names and knots to one another so the rain would have to wash through all of them at once. [732]

Oma opened her ledger, shielded the page with her body, and beneath neither column wrote: Unverified. [748] Then, because the ink was already running, she wrote one more word, larger: Keep. [762]
