The arbor began to tilt on the third morning after the spirit left. [13] Iset Varga noticed it first in the leaves. [21] For eleven years every leaf in the grove had pointed north, thin iron-veined blades turning like compass needles above the white floor of cloud, and now a few near the eastern rim had begun to wander. [57] She wrote the observation in her notebook before she let herself feel anything about it. [72] That was her habit, learned over thirty years of translating cuneiform: first the mark, then the meaning, then the grief. [92]

The arbor floated because its roots were lodestone, woven into a knot that held itself against the earth's field the way two magnets refuse each other. [118] But the knot had never been enough on its own. [128] Something had kept the domains aligned, something the clay tablets buried in the root-hollow called the Keeper of the Turning, and Iset had spent a decade learning to speak to it in the old wedge-script it seemed to prefer. [167] It had answered her in pressure and warmth, a presence always at touching distance, near enough that the hair on her arms lifted, never near enough to hold. [195]

She had wanted to hold it. [201] That was the plain truth, and she made herself write it down too. [214] She had wanted to know what the Keeper was, how a thing could pass from living to not living, from here to elsewhere, and what law governed the crossing. [243] Her husband had died in a hospital bed while she held his hand, and she had felt the moment he went and understood nothing about it. [269] The ambition had followed her up the ladder of cloud ever since, patient as a ghost. [285]

So when the tablets described the single fruit at the arbor's heart, the fruit whose seed held the knowledge of turning, she had climbed to it. [311] She had picked it gently, apologizing aloud. [318] The warmth had withdrawn from the grove like a tide going out, and it had not come back. [336]

Now the seed sat in a tin on her worktable, black and glossy as a drop of ink, and the arbor was sinking half an inch an hour toward the clouds. [367] She could have eaten it. [372] The tablets promised that whoever did would understand the turning completely. [383]

Instead she laid out her fired copies of the root-hollow texts and read them again, the way she always read, slowly, charitably, assuming the scribe had meant something sensible. [412] One line had always troubled her: As the lodestone to the needle, so the seed to the Keeper. [430] She had read it as poetry. [436] Now she read it as instruction. [442]

A needle does not lose its magnetism because the magnetism flies away, she thought. [456] Nothing leaves. [458] Heat or a hard blow simply scrambles the tiny domains inside, each still a magnet, all pointing different ways, cancelling one another into silence. [482] And you restore it the way a child learns to, by stroking the needle with a lodestone, again and again, in one direction. [505]

If the Keeper was a kind of alignment, then it had not departed at all. [520] It had been disordered by her taking, scattered through every leaf and root, still present, pointing everywhere, and therefore nowhere. [540] Perhaps her husband, too, had not gone anywhere she could name, only turned in a direction she had no needle for. [561]

The seed was the lodestone. [566] She understood then that eating it would keep the knowledge in her and leave the grove scrambled forever. [584] She did not let herself mourn the answer she would never have. [596] Not yet. [598]

She worked the way she translated, one sign at a time. [609] Holding the seed between thumb and finger, she walked the grove from south to north, drawing it along each wandering branch in a single steady stroke, murmuring to every tree as she went, the way she used to thank a broken tablet for surviving. [653] It took until dusk. [657] Leaf by leaf the needles swung back. [664]

At the heart of the arbor she knelt, pressed the seed into the soft lodestone loam where the fruit had hung, and covered it with her palm. [691] The sinking stopped. [694] Then the warmth returned, not rushing, but gathering, the way a crowd of strangers slowly learns to face the same way. [715] It hovered beside her, at touching distance, as it always had. [726] Iset lifted her hand from the soil and did not reach for it. [739]

In her notebook, beneath the day's observations, she wrote one more line: Transition is not departure but orientation. [757] Then, because she was honest, she added: I think. [766] Above the clouds, every leaf pointed north, and somewhere she could not see, she hoped, something else was pointing too. [786]
