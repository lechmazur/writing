The jetty bloomed only at twilight, and only for the dead. [11] Ines Varga had known this since she was nine, when her mother first walked her past the pilings and named each pale flower as it opened: the Halloran boy, the cook from the Sorrel, two sisters who had gone out for mussels and never come back. [57] Now she was sixty-one, the last walnut-ink archivist of Keel Harbor, and the flowers were closing. [73]

The county had absorbed the harbor in March. [81] Clerks with laminated badges had scanned her ledgers, praised her handwriting, and sent the originals to pulp, because paper was a fire risk and the drowned were, after all, already recorded. [112] Names and dates survived in a database somewhere inland. [121] What did not survive were the lines Ines and every archivist before her had added beneath each name in brown ink: how the man had whistled while mending nets, which song, which bad tooth made the note go flat. [160] Within a week of the pulping, the first blossoms had failed to open. [173]

Ines did not trust legends, so she worked through proximate causes, the way her mother had taught her. [191] Not why the sea kept flowers, which no one could answer, but why this flower, on this piling, had browned on this night. [214] She kept a list in pencil, gently thorough, matching each dying bloom to the page that had been destroyed the day before. [236] The correspondence was exact. [240]

The covenant had never been about names. [247] It had been about weight. [252] The old charter, which she had only ever seen quoted, said the harbor would keep the small moments of its lost, and the water would keep the harbor in bloom. [282] Everyone had assumed it meant tourism. [288]

On the last evening of April, a locket washed against the lowest step of the jetty and caught in the vines. [309] Ines knelt, knees complaining, and worked it free. [317] The chain had snapped at a single link, bent sideways the way links bend when they catch on a cleat. [337] The clasp was brass painted gold, the kind sold at the ferry kiosk forty years ago. [353] Inside, under a warped disc of celluloid, was a locket photo of a young woman squinting into sun, a gull blurred at her shoulder, her mouth open mid-laugh as if the bird had just said something rude. [390]

Ines knew the face. [394] Marta Lisk, lost off the breakwater in 1984, entry four hundred and twelve. [407] She knew, too, what her own hand had written beneath that name, because she had been twenty and in love with Marta and had written it badly, weeping: She laughed at gulls as though they were cousins. [444] That page had gone to pulp on Tuesday. [452] Marta's flower, the one at the ninth piling, had not opened since. [464]

Ines carried the locket home and set a pot of walnut hulls to steep, the way she had done every autumn of her working life. [489] The kitchen filled with that bitter, green-black smell, like rain on a cellar door. [503] She let it darken through the night, adding rusted nails at midnight to deepen the brown, and at dawn she strained it through muslin into a jar. [530]

She had no ledger left, so she used what was near: the back of the county's own transfer notice. [549] She wrote Marta's name, and the gull line, and then, because the covenant had to be regenerated by someone who meant it, she wrote her own small moment beneath it: I held her hand on the ferry and never told her why, and the not-telling has weighed more than anything I carried after. [602]

Then she walked to the jetty at twilight and nailed the page to the ninth piling. [618] For a long moment the water only breathed. [626] Then the bud unclenched, pale as a palm turning upward, and the scent came off it like salt and warm bread. [647] Down the length of the jetty, others stirred and opened, one after another, as though a promise shouted into an empty room had finally been answered. [673]

Ines laughed aloud, and the laugh frightened her, because it sounded like Marta's. [686] She understood the terms now. [691] The flowers would last exactly as long as she wrote, and when she stopped, someone else would have to steep the hulls and remember. [715] Tomorrow she would begin again, page by page, from the nearest name outward. [728] Tonight she sat on the wet boards with the locket open in her palm, while a gull landed beside her and said something rude, and the dead bloomed all around her in the dark. [762]
