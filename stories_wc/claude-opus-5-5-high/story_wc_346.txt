The lighthouse had been hidden so long that even the sea forgot where it stood, and Ines Varro liked it that way. [22] At dusk she climbed the one hundred and nine steps to the lamp room, where no lamp had burned in a century, and where the Clock waited instead. [50] It was not a large thing. [56] A brass face, one black hand, a single mark near the top labeled in her grandmother's cramped script: Last. [75] Ines was its adjuster, as her mother had been, and hers before that. [88] Every evening she read the tides and the wars and the fevers in the newspapers the supply boat left, and every evening she moved the hand by the width of an eyelash toward the mark. [123] The supply boat had stopped coming in spring. [131] Tonight the hand touched the mark, and did not need her. [142]

She felt it before she saw it: a tremor passing outward through the air, through the stone, through her own wrists, as if the world were a bowl of water and someone had set the last pebble down. [180] Time's final ripple. [183] It would take a while to cross everything, her grandmother had written, but it would not come back. [201] Ines had always imagined she would feel terror at this moment. [212] Instead she felt something closer to the ache of a bruise pressed on purpose, the wonder of finding it still there. [233]

She turned from the Clock to the window that had replaced the great lens. [247] The keepers called it the pane of hush. [255] It was glass poured, the story went, from the silence between two heartbeats, and it faced not the sea but every direction at once. [279] Look through it and you saw lives: a fisherman mending nets in some year that had no number, a girl asleep in a cart, a soldier's widow counting coins. [308] A thousand lifetimes, layered like pages. [314] Nothing passed through it, not light, not sound, not a single word; the pane had never vouchsafed its watchers anything but the watching. [337]

Except for the crack. [341]

Ines knelt and took the puppet head from its shelf beneath the sill. [354] She had carved it herself, thirty years ago, for her son Tomas: a round-cheeked sailor with painted eyes. [372] Tomas had been four and furious about bedtime, and he had thrown it with all his small strength. [390] The head split along the jaw. [396] The pane took a star-shaped fracture in its lower corner, the only flaw in glass that had been perfect for four hundred years. [419] Her mother had wept over it. [425] Ines, holding her sobbing boy, had put her ear to the fracture and heard, faintly, a stranger's child humming on the other side. [448]

Tomas grew up and sailed away and did not come back, the way people didn't, and the pane stayed beautifully flawed, and Ines spent her evenings studying the crack. [477] It was not random. [481] Each line of the star branched into smaller lines, and those into smaller still, the same shape repeating inward until it vanished. [503] Sound could slip through it, she learned, only if the sound was built the same way. [519] So she had spent twenty years rebuilding the lullaby her mother sang, the one she had sung to Tomas, through nested patterns: a verse folded inside each verse, a melody that held a smaller copy of itself in every bar, the way the crack held smaller cracks. [566] She had never dared to sing it through. [574] A song like that did not end; it would go on crossing lifetimes as long as there were lifetimes to cross. [595]

Now there was only the ripple left, and it was coming. [606]

Outside, the dusk had turned the color of an old plum. [617] In the pane, the thousand lives flickered as the tremor reached them, one by one, candles in a draft. [636] Ines fitted the broken puppet head against the fracture, its split jaw lined up with the crack it had made, so the wood would carry her voice like a cupped hand. [667] Then she put her lips to the painted sailor's ear and began to whisper. [681]

Hush now, the tide is turning, and inside that line, hush, and inside that, hush. [696]

The song went down into the glass and branched. [705] She watched it arrive: the fisherman lifted his head, the girl in the cart smiled in her sleep, the widow's hands went still over her coins. [731] Far down among the layers, a grown man on a ship's deck paused, frowning at a tune he had not heard since he was four. [756]

The ripple reached the lighthouse. [761]

Ines kept whispering, and at the very end the pane vouchsafed her one thing more: a small voice, somewhere, humming back. [782]
