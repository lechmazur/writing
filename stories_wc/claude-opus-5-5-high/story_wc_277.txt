After the journalist called, Clementine Vane rode the funicular up through the clouds, because a woman who had inherited forty newspapers ought to check at least one story herself. [29] Pell from the Courier had been polite and hungry. [38] He had found, in her own family's archive, an anonymous letter from 1962 accusing the astronomer Ilse Marr of inventing a comet, and he had found the Vane Gazette front page that printed it. [72] He believed her father had written both. [79] "Tell me I'm wrong," he'd said, and Clementine, who had read every edition of every Vane paper since she was nine, circling errors in red the way other children solved riddles, said she would find out. [115]

The observatory sat on the mountain like a stone egg left above the weather. [129] Inside, every footstep came back twice, as if the building were fact-checking her. [142] Ilse Marr was ninety-one and polishing a brass eyepiece she no longer used. [155]

"Augustus's girl," she said, not turning. [161] "You have his habit of staring at things until they confess." [172]

Clementine laid the photocopied letter on the chart table. [181] Beside it she set the one thing her father had left her personally: a dip pen whose nib was carved from rosewood, hardened in oil, split so finely it wrote a double hairline on every downstroke, like a road with its shadow. [223] The anonymous letter was full of those doubled strokes. [232] Pell had noticed that too; every editorial Augustus Vane ever signed carried the same shadowed line. [248]

"Let's assume he wrote it," Clementine said, because her father had taught her to argue like a geometer, starting from the claim she most feared. [273] "Then he owned this nib in March of 1962, when the letter was postmarked." [287] She turned the pen so the lamplight caught the tiny letters burned into its shoulder: I.M. to A.V., for printing the truth, 1963. [310] "But you carved it, and you gave it to him a year later." [323] "So he couldn't have written with it in 1962." [332] "Contradiction." [333]

The dome repeated the word softly, diction, diction, like a schoolroom that had caught someone cheating. [349]

Ilse laughed, a dry small sound. [355] "He said you'd get there." [360] "He said Vanes always find the buried paragraph." [368]

"So you wrote the letter yourself." [374]

"I predicted my comet would return in sixty-one years, and then I found an error in my own arithmetic, a perturbation from Jupiter I'd botched." [399] "Better to be called a fraud by a stranger than a fool by my colleagues, I thought." [416] "Your father recognized my hand, printed it because I begged, and never once ran a correction." [432] "Imagine what that cost a newspaperman." [438]

Clementine could imagine it exactly. [443] She remembered him at breakfast, reading the corrections column first, always, his lips moving as though in prayer. [461]

On the wall hung Ilse's old chart, a long ellipse inked around the sun, and pinned beside it, dozens of later sheets recalculating the same orbit in shakier and shakier hands. [492]

"Regret moves in ellipses," Ilse said. [498] "It goes out to the cold edge and you think it's gone, and then it swings back and burns." [517]

Clementine studied the sheets the way she studied galley proofs, devotedly, line by line, and on the fourth page from the bottom she found it. [542] A minus sign had been copied as a plus. [551] The botched Jupiter correction was not in the original prediction; it was in the recalculation. [566] Ilse had been right in 1962 and wrong only about being wrong. [578]

"When is it due?" Clementine asked. [584]

Ilse glanced at the dome's closed shutter and did not answer, which was an answer. [599]

"Tonight," she admitted at last. [604] "I wasn't going to look." [609] "There's a limit to how many times one woman can be embarrassed by the same sky." [625]

Clementine had spent her life reading other people's stories; she had never once walked into one to change its ending. [645] She decided to intervene anyway. [650] She found the hand crank and hauled until the shutter groaned open and the clouds lay beneath them like an unprinted page. [672] Then she guided Ilse's trembling hands to the eyepiece. [681]

For a long moment there was only the echo of their breathing. [693] Then Ilse made a sound Clementine would later try, and fail, to set in type. [708]

Near dawn, Clementine called Pell back. [714] "You were wrong," she told him, dipping the rosewood nib to begin, in doubled hairlines, the correction her father had never run. [736] "Get a pencil; the real story came back on schedule." [746]
