Ned Harrow had run supplies to Gannet Rock for nineteen years, and in that time he had said perhaps a hundred words to Moll Pierce, the keeper. [27] She had not needed more. [32] She had spoken in stones. [37]

On the landing rock she kept a flat ledge, and on it she stacked what he needed: two grey stones for diesel, a white one on top for flour, a split stone when the radio was down. [74] Ned would read the stack from the wheel of the Petrel, nod, and restack it before leaving: all delivered, all well. [95]

Moll died in March. [99] The automated lamp the Coast Guard installed after her lasted until October, when it flickered three nights running and then went dark for good. [123] A letter came saying a lighted buoy would do, and that supply runs were cancelled. [138] Ned went out anyway, on the first Tuesday, because his hands did not know how to stop. [155]

There was a stack on the ledge. [162] Three smooth stones, and on top, where Moll had never put anything, a piece of sea glass the color of a starboard light. [185] Three stones meant come up. [190]

The town said the lantern room was haunted now. [199] Crab men swore they saw a pale shape moving behind the dead prism, a small light that was not the lamp. [220] Ned climbed the ninety-one steps with the sea glass in his fist and found no ghost, only a jam jar on the lamp pedestal, stuffed with folded paper. [248]

The letters were unsigned. [252] Each began the same way: I felt safe when. [261] I felt safe when the beam crossed my ceiling and I could count to nine before it came again. [280] I felt safe when my father's boat rounded the Rock and the light found him. [295] I felt safe when Moll waved from the gallery, even though she was cross about my kite. [312] At the bottom of each page, in the same careful print: Leave yours. [325] Pass the jar. [328]

Ned read them twice, the wind fingering the cracked panes, the brass fittings ticking in the cold. [345] He noticed things; it was the one gift he had in place of talk. [359] He noticed the handwriting changed from letter to letter but the ink did not, blue ballpoint, the cheap kind sold at Farrow's store. [382] He noticed a child's rubber boot print in the dust by the pedestal, and a flake of green dinghy paint caught on the door latch. [407] Only one dinghy in the harbor was that green. [416] It belonged to Moll's granddaughter, Tess, who was eleven and had not spoken at the funeral either. [433]

So the ghost collected letters from a town that missed its light, rowed a mile of open water after dark, and climbed up here to keep them where the light had been. [465] Ned understood the wanting. [469] She was trying to bottle it, the feeling of being watched over, before everyone forgot what it had been like. [489] He also understood the tide tables, and the November swell that had flipped better boats than a plywood dinghy. [508]

He could tell her mother. [513] He could tell the harbormaster, who would padlock the door and end the haunting in an afternoon. [530] Either way the jar would stop filling, and she would be left alone with whatever had made her start it. [550]

Instead he took a pencil stub from his coat and wrote, slowly, the most words he had put together in years. [571] I felt safe when the beam swept the Petrel's deck at two in the morning and I knew someone awake was counting me home. [595] He folded it around the sea glass and pushed it into the jar. [608]

Back in the harbor, on the float where the green dinghy was tied, he built a stack from ballast stones, the one Moll used when the weather turned: two stones, a gap, one stone. [642] Wait for the boat. [646]

Tuesday evening she was sitting on his bow when he came down with the fuel cans, the jar in her lap, fuller now. [669] Neither of them said anything. [674] He handed her a life jacket, and she put it on. [685]

They ran out under a sky going violet, the Rock black ahead, its lantern a dead eye. [702] Up in the lantern room she set the jar on the pedestal, and Ned switched on the small torch he had brought and laid it inside, among the letters. [731] The prism caught it. [735] Thin gold bands fanned out across the water, faint as breath, nothing a ship could steer by. [752] Onshore, a kitchen light blinked, then another. [759]

Tess pressed the green glass into his palm and closed his fingers over it. [773] "Your turn to keep it," she said. [780]

He nodded, and it was enough. [786]
