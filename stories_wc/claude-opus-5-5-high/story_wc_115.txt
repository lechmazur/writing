The confession came out of the radio at 3:07, and then nothing did. [13] Dell Harrow stood knee-deep in the black water of the Carvel tunnel with the transistor hooked to his pack strap, listening to dead air hiss where Madame Orla's voice should have been. [45] Even the psychic had nothing to say. [52]

The caller had been an old man, breathless, the kind of voice that rattled like a loose bolt. [70] "I turned the wheel on the ghost shift," he had said. [81] "I shut it and let them drown, and I have carried it in my belly for fifty years." [99] Then he had hung up, and Orla had not cut to commercial, and the hush spread out of the speaker and into the tunnel like cold. [125]

Dell knew the voice. [129] He had heard it every Sunday of his childhood, refusing grace at the head of the table. [146] His grandfather, Amos, had been night foreman at Carvel Mill before the flood of 1971. [161] Now Amos lived in a care home across the river and called psychics at three in the morning, which made two Harrows who did not believe in anything and could not stay away from it. [196]

Dell had climbed every rotten floor of every dead mill in the valley. [209] He had fallen through two of them, and he had a pin in his ankle to prove it. [227] He did not believe in God, luck, or ghosts, but he believed in patterns, and for three weeks the frantic callers to Orla's show had been giving him one. [256] A trucker heard a shift whistle on Route 9 where no mill stood. [269] A nurse swore her ceiling dripped river water at 3:10 every night. [281] A girl sobbed that she dreamed of women counting beads in the dark, forty paces past the old rail signal. [301] Orla told each of them the dead wanted peace. [310] Dell wrote down the times and places, drew lines on a county map, and watched them bend toward the mouth of this tunnel, where the mill's night shift once walked to the loading platform. [344]

Nobody had called them the ghost shift until they stopped coming home. [356] Twelve spinners and a sweeper had sheltered in the tunnel when the river rose, and the official report said the spillway gate failed. [379] Gates did not fail, Amos used to say, people did. [389] Dell had always thought he was talking about engineering. [398]

Now, in the hush, he was so tired his eyelids ached, and yet every drip in the tunnel landed in his skull like a dropped wrench. [424] He counted forty paces past the rusted signal. [432] His headlamp found a brick alcove and, set into it, a valve wheel the size of a hubcap, its spokes wound thick with rosary beads. [457] Dozens of them, glass and wood and cheap plastic, knotted around the iron until it looked less like machinery than a shrine. [479] Families, he guessed, had come down here for years to pray over the spot, never knowing what the wheel did. [499]

Dell did what he always did with salvage: he appraised it. [510] Cast iron, maybe sixty pounds. [515] The stem was stamped CLOSE with an arrow, and the wheel had been cranked hard to the end of its travel. [536] The valve controlled the tunnel's drain into the mill culvert, and someone had shut it so the flood would pool here instead of in the mill's turbine room. [564] Sixty pounds of iron against thirteen lives and a set of looms. [576]

He had come here to learn what that truth weighed, the one his grandfather had swallowed and eaten around for half a century. [599] He had expected to feel something tip. [606] Instead he stood swaying with sleep and certainty, and the radio still hissed. [619] He did not believe the dead were listening. [627] He believed an old man across the river was sitting by a phone, waiting to hear if anyone had heard him. [648]

Dell unwound one strand of beads, cheap plastic, weightless in his palm, and put it in his pocket. [666] Then he set both hands on the wheel and leaned into it, and the iron groaned, and the stem turned toward OPEN. [688] Somewhere below, the culvert coughed, and the black water around his knees began, very slowly, to move. [705]

On the radio, Orla finally drew a breath. [713] Dell was already wading toward the tunnel mouth, thumbing the station's number, because someone should answer the old man, and it should not be a psychic. [739]
