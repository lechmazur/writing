The sun was already sliding off the rim of the world when Wren slipped into the stationmaster's office, and she had perhaps four minutes of gold left. [27] Emerald Station was built of green glass, and at this hour the light poured through its vaulted roof like syrup, blinding the guards and softening every edge. [54] That was when she worked. [59]

People called her the golden-hour portrait thief, though never to her face, because nobody had ever seen her face. [78] She took only paintings made in the last light of day, since everyone knew those canvases held something the rest did not: the sitter's illuminated moment, the single instant when a life tipped toward what it would become. [116] Collectors paid fortunes to hang other people's destinies above their fireplaces. [127] Wren stole them back, one at a time, and returned them, for a modest fee, to the people who had sat for them. [150]

Tonight's canvas hung behind the stationmaster's desk, small and dusty: a girl of about six standing on Platform Nine, her hand lifted against the glare. [175] Wren cut it from its frame in eleven strokes and was back among the commuters before the sun finished drowning. [195]

The job had come, as all her jobs came, through Mrs. Abernathy's flower kiosk beneath the departures board. [213] Every morning the old florist set a single arrangement in the window, and every morning Wren read it on her way to nowhere. [236] Marigolds meant sunset. [239] Blue iris meant an office. [244] The number of fern fronds gave the platform, and a sprig of rosemary meant the client would collect in person. [264] Today's bouquet had carried all of that, plus a single forget-me-not, planted upside down. [278] She had assumed it was a mistake. [285] Mrs. Abernathy did not make mistakes. [291]

Now, clutching the rolled canvas under her coat while the last gold thinned to rose, Wren began to reinterpret it. [311] She was quietly savvy in the way of people who had grown up unwatched; she noticed what others let blur. [331] An inverted flower in the old language meant a reversal, a thing turned back on its sender. [348] Forget-me-not, reversed, might mean remember yourself. [354] And rosemary, the client in person, might mean the client was the thief. [367]

Her whole life she had wanted just once to press her ear against destiny's door, to hear what waited on the far side instead of stealing glimpses of strangers' futures. [397] No one had ever painted her at golden hour, or so she believed; the city had taken her from this very station at six, with nothing but a name stitched into her collar. [430]

Night came down fast, and the station changed. [438] By moonlight the green glass went deep and cool as a pond, and the empty platforms shimmered like the floor of something submerged. [461] Wren sat on the bench beneath the kiosk's shuttered window and unscrewed the jar of captured moonbeams she kept for appraisals. [482] Gold hid things; moonlight told the truth underneath. [490] She tipped the jar, and silver spilled across the canvas like water finding cracks. [504]

The girl's face surfaced first, and Wren's breath stopped, because it was her own face, rounder, freckled, squinting. [522] Then a second figure bloomed beside the child, a woman kneeling, pinning a forget-me-not to the girl's collar. [540] In the corner of the painting, half hidden, stood a flower kiosk, and in its window sat an arrangement Wren had never been taught. [564] She read it anyway. [568] White poppies for a promise, lavender for waiting, and ivy wound through all of it, which meant only one thing: home. [589]

Behind her, the shutter rattled up. [595] Mrs. Abernathy stood in the lit window, older than the painted woman by twenty years but unmistakably the same, holding a forget-me-not right side up. [620]

"I painted it the evening before the city took you," she said. [632] "Your mother was gone, and they decided a florist with a bad hip was no fit guardian. [649] The records were sealed. [653] When a young thief began reading my flowers three years ago, I prayed she was you, and I set her jobs until I was sure. [678] Then I sent her for the only painting I ever made at golden hour." [692]

Wren stared, dazzled past believing, the moonbeams still pooling silver in her lap. [705] She had stolen a hundred illuminated moments, and never once imagined one might be stealing her back. [722]

Mrs. Abernathy opened the kiosk's narrow side door, painted green like everything else in the station. [738] Wren rose, crossed the moonlit platform, and before stepping through, pressed her ear to the wood. [754] Inside, a kettle was beginning to sing. [761]
