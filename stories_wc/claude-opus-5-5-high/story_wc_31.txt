The lamplighter of Gull Pier worked the late shift, which meant he lit nothing at dusk and everything after midnight, when the gas lamps sputtered and needed a second hand. [30] Tonight the town was releasing its sky lanterns, and the harbor had gone quiet in that particular way, a meteor hush, everyone holding breath as paper lights climbed like slow falling stars in reverse. [64] Each lantern carried a vow inked on its side. [73] Tomas Reyl had written one once, forty years ago, standing beside Mara. [85] This year he carried no lantern, only a brass tag on a ring, stamped HOTEL MERIDIAN LIBRARY MASTER, with a skeleton key hanging from it like a small black bone. [115] Mara had left it in his coat pocket before she died, wrapped in a note that said only: The color of your voice is where I kept it. [143]

She had heard sounds as colors all her life. [152] Gulls were orange, rain was a pale green, and church bells, she swore, were the violet of a bruise healing. [172] But whenever he asked about his own voice, she smiled and told him he would have to discover it himself. [192]

The Meridian had been a floating hotel moored off the pier until a winter storm opened her hull in 1981. [212] She settled on the sandbar with her upper decks still breathing air, and the town, embarrassed, simply stopped looking at her. [233] Mara had been her librarian. [238]

Tomas lit the last lamp at the end of the pier, then climbed down the service ladder into his skiff. [258] Above him the lanterns drifted seaward, and their reflections laid a road of trembling gold across the black water, straight to the wreck. [281] He rowed it without a sound. [287]

The library door sat half a flight above the tideline, swollen in its frame. [301] The key turned as if it had been waiting. [310] Inside, the room tilted eleven degrees toward the sea, and the shelves leaned with it, thousands of spines furred with salt. [331] Water lapped somewhere below the floor like a slow pulse. [341]

Tomas raised his taper and did not search. [349] Mara had always said you never find a book by hunting it; you find it by wandering purposefully, letting your feet follow the question instead of the answer. [377] So he walked the way she used to walk the pier with him on her nights off, slow, touching things, stopping where the light stopped. [402] He passed poetry, maritime law, a shelf of guest registers. [412] At the librarian's desk, wedged against the downhill wall, a ledger lay dry under an oilcloth, as though someone had come back more than once to keep it that way. [442]

Its cover read SOUNDS AND THEIR COLORS, in her hand. [452] He turned pages of foghorns and kettles and a child laughing on the promenade, each one given a hue, until he reached his own name. [477]

Tomas, reporting the weather, the tide, the price of oil: the grey of wet rope. [492] He never lies to me, and he never says anything else. [503] He hides behind the truth the way other men hide behind a wall. [516]

Below that, in older ink: Tomas at the lantern release, 1979, saying he would light my way home every night of his life: the gold of a lamp in the first second after the match. [551] I have heard it only once. [557]

Tomas sat down on the slanted floor. [564] He had kept that vow exactly, lamp by lamp, for forty years, and had let the lamps speak for him, as if lighting them could signify what his mouth refused to say. [596] She had understood the lamps. [601] She had still wanted the gold. [607]

Through the porthole, the last lanterns were rising, and the whole harbor had gone silent enough to hear paper breathe. [627] He could not see colors in sound; he never would. [637] But he understood now that the color was not something to be seen, only something to be given. [655]

He took the pen from the desk, found the blank line beneath her last entry, and did not write. [674] Instead he spoke, aloud, into the leaning room she had tended alone, and the words were not the weather or the tide. [696] They were a vow, the old one and a new one, that he loved her, that he was sorry he had made her wait, that he would keep lighting her way even now. [729] His voice cracked on the last word. [736]

Outside, a real meteor slid down behind the lanterns, silent and brief, and for one second the porthole filled with the gold of a lamp just lit. [763] Then he rowed home along the road of gold, leaving the library door open behind him. [779]
