The circus train had died on a siding below the Serpentine Pass, and Tomas Varga had lived in it for eleven years. [22] He kept the calliope car swept, the brass pipes rubbed to a soft shine, and on the rusted doors he chalked his boundaries the way his father had taught him: a circle, a line, a date. [58] His father had been Bela Varga, the Tollman, who stopped tour coaches on the switchbacks and relieved foreign passengers of their wallets with such courtesy that some of them wrote him letters afterward. [91] What Bela left behind was a biscuit tin of banknotes: drachmas, lire, schillings, French francs, all of them dead now, swallowed by the euro before he could spend them. [120] The police had taken him from this same train, in the lion car, where he had come back one time too many. [142] Tomas wanted to know why. [147] A clever man with a whole mountain of escape routes had kept circling back to one rotting carriage, and Tomas turned the question over the way he turned the brass, patiently, until it shone. [181]

Ines Achterberg arrived one autumn with a laptop, three engineers, and a pitch deck that called the train "authentically disruptive." [201] Her company, Waypoint, built an app that steered travelers off crowded routes and onto hidden roads, and she wanted the cars for offices because the rent was nothing. [229] Tomas did not argue; he had his father's polished calm, which made argument unnecessary. [243] He drew a chalk line across the coupling behind the clown car and said, "Past here is yours." [261] She respected the line, which surprised him. [268]

Waypoint grew. [270] Investors came down the gravel road in rented cars, and each time the headcount rose, Tomas wiped away a line and redrew it one car closer to his own, ceding the clown car, then the dining car, then the lion car itself. [312] Under every line he erased, he found his father's older marks surfacing through the grime, pale circles each with a date inside. [334] Ines noticed him studying them. [339] "Hobo signs?" she asked. [343] "Receipts," he said. [346] "Every time he came back." [351]

She laughed, but she watched him with a new attention, and he watched her too, perceptively calm, reading the tightening of her voice on phone calls, the investors whose cars stopped coming, the engineers who left one by one through the doors he had given her. [397] A rival had copied Waypoint and undercut it; the funding round collapsed in spring. [411] By the second autumn, Ines worked alone in the lion car under a single bulb, and the dusk came through the barred windows striped like an animal's coat. [439]

One evening Tomas found her suitcase by the door, her keys in her hand, and on her laptop a transfer form moving the last escrow, the severance owed her departed engineers, into an account in her own name. [477] "The pass is quiet at night," she said, not quite to him. [489]

He did not tell her to stop. [496] Instead he took the rag from his pocket and wiped the door beside her, where his last line still stood, until his father's circles came up beneath it, crowded together like rings in a tree. [531] "Nineteen," he said. [534] "He had every road in the mountains, and he came back here nineteen times to count money nobody would take." [554] He set the biscuit tin on her desk and opened it: faded kings, composers, bridges, all of it worthless. [573] "I used to think he came back because he was stupid, or sentimental." [586] "Now I think a path circles back when you carry something you cannot spend anywhere else." [602]

Ines looked at the circles a long time. [610] She was, he saw, doing the arithmetic his father never had: how many nights on the pass, how many returns, how much of herself she would have to count alone in a car like this one. [646] Then she cancelled the transfer and sent the severance where it belonged, one name at a time. [663]

Waypoint dissolved that winter with a short, honest letter. [672] Ines left by the gravel road in daylight. [680] In spring she came back, as he had half expected, but carrying nothing, and asked if he needed help with the brass. [702] Tomas looked at the chalk line on the calliope door, the last of his boundaries, and wiped it clean. [721] That evening they polished the pipes together until the brass held the dusk like still water, and when the calliope breathed its first note in twenty years, it sounded less like a return than an arrival. [757]
