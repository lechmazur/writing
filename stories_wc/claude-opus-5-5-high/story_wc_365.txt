Ines Varo made maps that no one could see. [9] Her clients were blind freight pilots and deaf welders, and her charts recorded where the air turned bitter with coolant, where a floor plate hummed underfoot, where the cold pooled like water in a low corner. [45] She had never mapped Bay Nine of the Castile Spaceport, because her father had died there. [61] Now the port was abandoned, its repair gantries furred with rust, and her mother was dying in a hospice three levels below the surface. [85] Every time Ines asked about the accident, her mother turned her face to the wall. [100] The only thing she had ever said was, 'It was heavier than you think,' and then she had asked for water. [121] Ines had decided to weigh it herself. [128]

She came at the fourth hour, when the old dock crews used to sleep between shifts, because the scavengers said that was when the bay slipped. [154] The fold engine that had failed twenty years ago was still bleeding faintly into the walls, and its leaks were cyclically spontaneous: no one could predict the exact minute, but every night, in the murmur of half-remembered dreams, the bay drifted sideways into some neighboring version of itself. [202] Scavengers who stayed too long came back speaking of wives they had never married. [216]

Ines carried a green glass shard her mother had given her as a child, the only piece left of the bottle her father kept on his workbench for luck. [245] Glass was stubborn; it did not fold well, and she hoped it would hold her to her own world the way a knot holds a thread. [271]

The first slip came as a smell of oranges. [280] The rust vanished, the lamps burned amber, and a crew in grey coveralls moved around a ship cradled in the clamps. [301] Ines pressed her palm to the floor and noted the vibration, the heat, the sour tang of an overworked core, and wrote them into her ledger in raised dots. [330] Her father stood at the core housing, younger than any photograph. [341] Before she could speak, the world shivered and she was elsewhere. [352]

She learned to steer by sensation. [358] Each universe differed in small textures, a warmer draft, a louder alarm, and by holding the shard against the pulse points of the walls she could redirect her drift, weaving between worlds toward the moment the core began to fail. [398] She mapped it again and again, through parallel universes, until the pattern rose out of her ledger like a coastline. [418]

In every version, the core cracked at the same minute. [428] In every version, her father shouted for the crew to run, and stayed behind to vent it by hand. [447] And in every version, a woman at the blast door console, her mother, young and shaking, waited for his signal. [467]

In one world she did not seal the door. [476] Ines felt that one through her boots: a pressure wave, a heat that blistered the paint, and then a silence so complete it had no texture at all. [504] The whole port was gone, and eleven thousand people with it. [515] She fled that world with her hands burning. [523]

In another, her father reached the door in time, and the core followed him out. [538] There was no thread that led anywhere kinder. [546]

She threaded her way back to her own world's night and stood beside her mother at the console, unseen, a draft at the shoulder of a girl of twenty-three. [575] Through the viewport, her father raised his hand. [583] Her mother's palm came down on the seal, and the green glass bottle on his bench shattered in the heat. [603] Ines understood at last why her mother had once gone back into that bay alone to find a piece of it. [624]

It was not an accident. [629] It was a choice made in four seconds and carried for twenty years in silence, so that a daughter would never look at her mother's hands and see the door. [659]

The slip released her at dawn, back among the rust. [669] Ines weighed the shard in her palm; it was light, a few grams at most, and yet she finally knew what her mother had meant. [694]

She took the lift down to the hospice and sat by the bed. [707] She did not ask anything. [712] She opened her ledger to the last page and read aloud what she had mapped: the warmth of the console, the tremor of the seal, the hand lifted beyond the glass, steady, asking. [745] Then she set the shard in her mother's palm and closed both their hands around it, so that for once it would not be carried alone. [771]
