Ines Varro wrote her poems on the backs of road signs, in grease pencil, where only the lost would read them. [21] Since the energy beings arrived, there had been more lost people than signs. [34]

They had come down three autumns ago like struck matches that never went out, and wherever they drifted, time went smooth. [55] Hours slid past without catching. [60] Grief did not stick, and neither did promises. [68] People forgot birthdays, debts, the faces of the dead, and mostly they seemed grateful. [82] The few who kept calendars, who scratched tallies into walls, were gathered into Stillwater Prison for their own peace. [101]

Ines had been one of them. [107] She still carried the proof over her shoulder: a coil of rope braided from bedsheets, frayed at one end where it had torn against the window grate. [134] Her cellmate, Oren, had braided half of it and held it while she climbed. [148] He had meant to follow. [153] The searchlight found him first. [158]

She did not know if he remembered her now. [167] That question had walked her south to the grotto the road-crews whispered about, where the clay was said to remember everything. [188]

It opened in a hillside like a split pomegranate. [197] The walls were terra-cotta, banded red and umber and rust, and the ceiling had collapsed long ago so that starlight fell straight in and lay on the floor like spilled salt. [228] Ines set down her pack and put her palm to the stone. [240] It was the first rough thing she had felt in months. [251]

She began to scrape with her thumbnail, uncovering layers. [260] Under the dust was a band of pale clay, fine as flour, and under that something darker and gritty, and under that a seam with a fossil leaf pressed into it, veins still sharp. [294] Each layer was a season that had happened and could not unhappen. [306] She had wanted, for three years, to discover the texture of time, and here it was: not a river but a stack of decisions, each one lying on the last and pressing it flat. [340]

Light gathered behind her. [344] The beings came the way they always did, without footsteps, a warmth at the edge of the eye. [362] Three of them hovered near the fossil seam, and on the grotto wall they began to pattern the starlight, bending it into shapes, which was the only way they spoke. [392] The shapes were her rope. [397] Then Oren's hands. [400] Then a window, open. [404]

She understood the offer slowly, the way one understands cold water. [415] They could fold the smoothness back to that night and let her stand again at the grate. [432] She could climb, and Oren would be caught, as he had been. [444] Or she could stay, and they would both remain in Stillwater, together, remembered by no one else. [461] Whichever she chose, the other path would never have been possible at all. [474] They had never made a choice, she realized, because for them everything happened at once, and they wanted to watch one being made. [497]

"That is not a gift," she told them. [505] "That is a trap wearing a bow." [512]

Ines had lied to wardens, stolen apples, and once sold a sheriff a sonnet she swore was his dead mother's; she believed a rule was only a rhyme scheme, meant to be bent until it sang. [548] So she sat on the warm floor, uncoiled the rope, and ran it through her fingers until she reached the frayed end, where the threads splayed like a river delta. [578]

"Here is my choice," she said. [584] "I choose the one I already made." [591]

She wet the clay beneath the fossil seam with water from her canteen and pressed the frayed end into it, hard, until every broken thread left its mark. [619] It was the newest layer in the grotto, lying on all the others. [632]

"You wanted to see a choice," she said. [640] "This is what it looks like afterward. [647] It does not go away. [652] It frays. [654] That is the texture." [658]

The beings drifted close to the imprint. [665] One touched it, and for an instant its light stuttered, brightened, dimmed, brightened, as if it were learning to count. [685] The patterns on the wall rearranged into something new: her rope, then Oren, then Oren again, older, a line of grey in his beard, looking up at a window. [714]

She did not know whether it was a promise or a guess. [726] She decided, playfully and on purpose, to treat it as a promise. [738]

When she walked out at dawn, her legs ached, and the hour it took to reach the road felt like an hour, grainy and slow and wonderful. [765] On the back of the first sign she passed, she wrote: Time is a rope; hold on by the fray. [785]
