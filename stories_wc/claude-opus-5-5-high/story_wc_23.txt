After the hydraulics failed at Veldmark, the sea came home in a single night, and nobody at the starlit signal station on the dike could stop it. [27] Ines Varga did not try. [32] She was the station's navigator, a patchwork of three dissolved maritime authorities whose stamps still crowded her license, and she knew the difference between a problem and a verdict. [61] Her coat was patchwork too, oilcloth sewn over wool sewn over her father's old canvas, and her charts were stitched from surveys that had never agreed. [87]

The polders drowned. [90] The village of Hessel went under to the height of its church roof, and her mother, who had refused every evacuation boat, went with it. [115] That was what the registry said. [121]

Then, eleven nights later, the station's brass telephone rang at midnight. [132] Nobody had called that line in years; it ran under the old dike road to the Hessel exchange, which was now four fathoms down. [156] Ines lifted the receiver and heard water, then breath, then her mother's voice saying, "Bakery. Two fathoms. Past the linden, the paw points east." [180] The line went dead. [184] She sat until dawn with her hand on the cradle, shaking. [195]

The calls came again, always at midnight, never longer than a minute. [207] Their clarity fluctuated with the moon phases, and Ines worked out why: her mother had no lamp, and only a full moon gave her enough light to climb to the belfry junction and speak into the handset properly. [245] Under a thin crescent she got only fragments. [253] "School steps, three fathoms." [257] "Your father's bench." [260] "The paw." [262]

Ines understood the paw. [266] On her desk, holding down the stitched chart, sat the patina bronze censer foot her mother had pressed into her hand on the last evacuation day, a lion's paw gone green, snapped from the church censer when the vestry flooded. [306] "So you can find your way back," her mother had said, which Ines had taken as nonsense. [323] It was not nonsense. [327] It was an instruction. [331]

So she began to build. [336] Every midnight she wrote down what the voice gave her, and every morning she took the station skiff out over the drowned fields, tied the censer foot to her sounding line, and lowered it until it touched a roof, a wall, a gatepost. [379] She inked each one onto a new chart, not of depths but of what had been: here the bakery, here the school steps, here the bench where her father had fed the cranes before they stopped coming. [416] Her colleagues called it grief with a pencil; Ines called it the only survey that mattered, a way to navigate the geography of loss one sounding at a time. [445]

Then the cranes returned. [449] Ornithologists on the radio called it a reverse migration, flocks abandoning the southern reservoirs to come back to marshland their great-grandparents had lost when the pumps were first built. [478] They settled on Hessel in thousands, and at the next full moon her mother's voice came through clear as glass. [498]

"They came back for the same reason I stayed," she said. [509] "Bring the chart." [512] "Follow the paw." [515]

Ines rowed out under that moon, the censer foot swinging below the skiff like a pendulum, touching bakery, school, bench, each one a name she said aloud. [542] The cranes lifted and resettled around her, grey and enormous, calling. [553] The church tower stood out of the water like a raised hand, and in the belfry window a figure sat wrapped in altar cloth, thinner than Ines remembered, the three-footed censer beside her. [586]

Ines climbed the knotted bell rope and did not say any of the things she had rehearsed. [603] She unrolled the chart across the belfry floor. [611] Her mother traced it with one finger, the bakery, the school, the bench, and wept, and laughed, and wept. [630]

"I couldn't leave him down there alone," she said. [639] "And I couldn't find my way to you without you finding your way to me." [654]

Ines did not ask her to come ashore. [662] The hydraulics would not be rebuilt; the sea had its verdict, and so did her mother. [678] Instead she fitted the bronze paw back beneath the censer, wedging it with a strip torn from her patchwork coat, and the old thing stood level for the first time since the flood. [711]

At midnight, out of habit, Ines listened for a phone, and heard only her mother breathing beside her. [729] They sat together until the moon went down, naming the drowned streets below them while the cranes slept on the rooftops of the world they had both come back to. [759]
