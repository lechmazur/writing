The library on Scaler Street had been lined with velvet before the war, so that nothing inside it would echo. [20] Its founders had wanted a room where the old songs could be recorded clean, the three-part laments that river families and hill families once sang together at funerals. [48] Now the velvet was balding and wine-dark, and every October the leaves from the plane trees slid under the door and lay on the carpet like dropped programs. [76]

Ilse Marek set out twelve chairs anyway. [83] She was the choir teacher, the last one in Vesk who still knew the laments, and she had learned to be expectantly resigned: she hoped for twelve singers and prepared herself for two. [116]

The first autumn, two came. [121] Dara was from the river side, where a hill shell had taken her uncle. [135] Pavel was from the hill side, where river men had burned his grandfather's orchard. [149] They sat at opposite ends of the row and sang as if trying to drown each other out. [167]

Ilse did not scold them; she was harmonious by habit, her speaking voice settling into whatever key the room held. [187] The velvet ate their shouting, and in the dead air each of them heard how thin and alone a single voice is. [209] That was the room's first lesson, and Ilse's method was simply to keep taking things away. [225] She taught by subtracting noise. [230] First she removed the piano, then the names of the villages from the verses, then the words themselves, until only vowels were left, open and unclaimed. [256]

"Nobody can hate a vowel," she told them. [264]

Pavel said he could manage it. [270]

The second autumn, five came, and Ilse brought her grandmother's zither down from the reading gallery. [286] It had traveled with the family through two evacuations and a flood, and its strings were gone, but the soundhole rosette was intact: a disc of carved pearwood no bigger than a palm, cut into three interlocking bands. [324] One band was the reed pattern of the river weavers. [334] One was the thorn pattern the hill families stitched into wedding shawls. [346] The third, plain and looping, belonged to no one. [355]

Dara turned it under the lamp and frowned. [363] The bands crossed at irregular intervals, some knots tight, some loose, and after a long silence she began to hum, rising where the reed rose over the thorn, falling where it dipped beneath. [396]

Ilse felt the hair on her arms lift. [404] She had spent forty years assuming the carving was decoration. [414] It was notation. [417] The old makers had used the rosette to denote the braid itself, which voice crossed which and when, so that anyone holding the instrument held the whole lament. [445] Vanishing arts rarely vanish all at once, Ilse thought; mostly they are left in plain sight, waiting for someone quiet enough to read them. [469]

They spent that winter learning the third band, the plain one, the middle voice that had no village. [487] It could not be sung alone. [493] It existed only in the places where the reed and the thorn touched, so whoever sang it had to listen to both sides at once. [518] Pavel asked to sing it. [523]

Ilse understood then that unlearning hatred was not a single decision but a slow subtraction, one grudge set down each week like a coat on the back of a chair. [553] Some weeks Dara picked hers back up. [560] Some weeks Pavel arrived with a bruise and would not say from whom. [573] Ilse kept setting out chairs, still expecting little, still hoping for more. [585]

The third autumn, all twelve were filled, and three people stood at the back among the shelves. [602] One was Dara's mother. [606] One was Pavel's grandfather, who had not entered a river-side building in thirty years and kept his hat on as if he might need to leave quickly. [633]

Ilse placed the rosette on the music stand where everyone could see it and lowered her hands. [650] She did not conduct. [654] The reed voices began, low as water, and the thorn voices entered above them, sharp and bright, and then Pavel's middle line came in between, touching each, belonging to neither, binding them. [686] It was what she had wanted since the ceasefire: not unison, which erased people, but a braid, every voice kept distinct and held. [709] The velvet swallowed every echo, so there was nothing in the room but the song itself, and the leaves, and the old man slowly taking off his hat. [737]

When it ended, no one applauded. [743] The stillness felt like arrival after a very long road, and Ilse, who had hoped for twelve and prepared for two, let it last. [767]
