Every night at the third turning of the stars, Mara Quell climbed the emerald lighthouse and laughed. [17] She did not choose to. [22] The laugh arrived the way weather arrives, from somewhere past her ribs, and the fishing families of Harrow Cove had learned to read it better than any barometer. [50] A chuckle meant squalls by morning; a howl meant hull-breakers. [60] They called her the storm herald, and she hated how often she was right. [74]

The routine never changed, though it had never felt entirely of this world. [87] She wiped the salt from the green glass walls, built by the first keepers from ten thousand melted bottles. [106] She trimmed the glass lantern wick, a braid of spun crystal that did not burn but carried, drinking the glow of the phosphor well below and pouring it upward without losing a drop. [139] Her grandmother had explained it once: light inside the strands struck their walls at such a shallow angle that it could never escape, only bounce onward, trapped and faithful, total internal reflection. [171]

Then she slid the shadow plates into their brass tracks. [181] Those were the oldest things in the tower: iron stencils cut before anyone remembered, each throwing a shape onto the clouds. [202] A fish for good catch, a broken mast for loss, a cradle for birth. [216] Since the last print newspaper had come ashore, its final edition yellowing now on the lantern-room wall, the shadows were the only news the coast still received. [243] Its headline read WE WILL FIND ANOTHER WAY TO TELL YOU. [254] Nobody had, except Mara, with her old shapes and her treacherous laugh. [266]

She wanted more than that. [271] For months she had been arranging the plates in new combinations, overlapping cradle and fish and the sun-wheel no one used, trying to spell something the ancient cutters had never imagined: not warning, but beginning. [306]

Tonight, a small boat was crossing from Saltmere carrying exactly that. [317] Her brother Tobin had rebuilt a hand press from salvaged type, and the whole cove had pooled its coins for ink. [338] If it arrived, Harrow Cove would print again. [346]

The stars were sharp and still when the laugh came. [356] It was not a chuckle. [361] It tore out of her so hard she gripped the rail, a howl of delight with no joke inside it, and far to the west the stars began to go out one by one beneath a rolling black wall. [400]

Mara stopped laughing and started working. [406] The beam was fixed; it had always pointed the same seaward arc. [418] But the wick was glass, and glass could bend. [427] She unclamped the braid from its mount and curved it gently in her gloved hands, feeling the heat of the trapped light running through it like blood in a vein. [457] Too sharp a bend and the light would spill out and die. [469] Just gentle enough, and it stayed inside, reflecting wall to wall, following wherever she led. [484]

She steered the tip past the fixed lens, through a crack in the emerald pane, and aimed it low across the water. [506] Then she held up the sun-wheel plate before it. [515] The ancient shadow leapt onto the storm's underside, huge and green-rimmed, a ring of spokes turning above the swells. [534] Tobin would know it; they had played with the plates as children, and the sun-wheel had always meant home. [553] She moved it inch by inch, drawing a road of shadow between the reefs, her arms shaking as the rain hit the tower like thrown gravel. [579] Somewhere out there, a lantern answered. [585]

It took until the stars returned, pale and thinning, for the boat to scrape onto the cove's shingle. [603] Mara watched from the gallery as figures ran down to meet it, as a crate was lifted ashore. [621] The wick lay across her lap, bent now for good, its light still circling faithfully inside a shape it had never been meant to hold. [646] The old beam would never sweep the same arc again. [656] She found she did not mind. [662]

Then the laugh came again, softer, and she felt the cold of it before she understood. [678] There was no cloud anywhere, only the east going gray. [688] For the first time, her laughter was not warning of weather. [699] It was warning of what words would do once they were loose again, the quarrels and reckonings and hard truths that news had always carried in with the tide. [728]

Mara unpinned the final edition from the wall, folded it into her coat, and kept laughing anyway, into the first green light of dawn. [752]
