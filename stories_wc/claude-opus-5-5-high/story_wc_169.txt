The tide had polished the grotto until its walls looked wet even when they were dry, and Odile Marr slept there every night at slack water. [26] She was a surveyor by trade and a dreamer by accident, and she had decided, the week after the surge took half of Lathe Cove, that the accident deserved method. [56] In the grotto her dreams did not wander. [64] They crossed. [66] She would close her eyes on the salt-dark stone and open them in another Lathe Cove, one where the seawall had held, or one where it had never been built, or one where everyone had simply packed their kettles and gone inland. [108] Most dreamers would have called it grief. [115] Odile called it a data set. [121]

The trick was staying lucidly watchful, and for that she carried her grandfather's balloonist's altitude gauge, a brass aneroid the size of a pocket watch that he had flown over the Channel in 1911. [155] It was the only thing that never changed between timelines. [165] In every Cove its needle rested at the same scratched mark, the height where Grandfather Aurel had once said the air went thin and quiet and you could finally think. [195] If the needle moved, she was only dreaming. [203] If it held, she was somewhere true. [210]

In waking life she had been floating trial balloons at the council for a month. [225] A higher wall, she suggested, and they flinched at the cost. [236] Relocation, and old Mrs. Penhale wept. [242] Salt marsh buffers, and the fishermen laughed. [249] Each idea rose a little way and sank, and the town went on sitting in its damp kitchens, waiting for someone to tell it what hope was supposed to weigh. [279] So she went looking for the answer across the parallel Coves, trying to puzzle out new equations for hope, since the old ones clearly did not balance. [306]

Her notebook swelled, then swamped, then failed her. [314] Numbers in columns told her nothing; the Coves were too various. [325] What finally worked was the salvage from the drowned Lantern Fair. [336] Crates of decorations had washed into the grotto after the surge: glass fishing floats, tin bells, ribbon spools gone stiff with brine. [358] Odile began hanging them on the smooth walls, one row for each timeline she visited. [373] A bell meant the wall stood. [379] A float meant families stayed. [384] A red ribbon meant people were still laughing in the square a year on. [398] She was creating patterns with decorations the way her grandfather had plotted winds, patiently, by feel, trusting that shape would emerge before meaning did. [422]

By the ninth row, it had. [428] The bells were scattered at random. [434] Walls held in dying towns and failed in thriving ones. [444] But every row thick with red ribbon shared one small mark she had nearly left off: in those Coves, someone had held the Lantern Fair the very first autumn, in the mud, before anything was fixed. [480]

Odile sat back on the cold stone while the tide breathed in and out of the grotto mouth, unhurried, rearranging the pebbles as it had for ten thousand years. [509] Everything she believed about engineering had just tipped over, and she felt oddly calm, the way Aurel said the basket went still at the top of a climb. [537] Hope was not the wall. [542] Hope was the proof, offered early, that the town still wanted to be a town. [557]

The council did not approve the fair; she did not ask them to. [570] She dried the ribbons, mended the tin bells, and spent three nights folding tissue lanterns with the fishermen's children, who asked why. [592] "They're trial balloons," she told them. [598] "We send them up to see which way the air is moving." [610]

On the first cold evening of October, forty people came down to the flooded square in their boots. [628] Mrs. Penhale brought soup. [632] The fishermen strung the glass floats between broken lampposts, and this time their laughter was aimed at no one. [651] When the lanterns lifted, Odile climbed the cliff path after them with the gauge in her palm and watched the needle creep toward Aurel's scratched mark, closer than it had ever come while she was awake. [687] Below her the tide slid into the square, calm and relentless, and forty people stood in it, looking up. [706] It stopped just short, and held. [712] Held meant somewhere true. [716]

That night she did not go down to the grotto. [726] Revitalizing a town, it turned out, was not a problem you solved in a dream; it was a pattern you hung where everyone could see it, then waited, lucid and watchful, for the air to answer. [762]
