The final petal dropped from the old rosebush a little after eleven, and Ada Voss watched it fall four stories through the iron slats of her fire escape. [28] She did not cry. [32] Her father had planted the bush in a paint bucket the summer she was born, and he had told her plainly that roses were a lease, not a deed. [61] Everything on the fire escape was a lease: the basil in coffee tins, the beans climbing the ladder, the tomatoes she hauled inside before every storm. [87] Below her, the harbor lay luminous by moonlight, every mast and mooring line silvered, the water holding the moon like a coin it meant to spend slowly. [114] But the courtyard behind the building got none of it. [124] It was a brick well, three walls and a padlocked gate, and at its bottom was the basement window of Theo Marsh, who was nine and had not slept with the lights off since his mother's boat failed to come back in March. [167] His grandmother could not afford to leave the lamp burning, and the landlord had never replaced the courtyard bulb. [186] Ada had heard him some nights through the airshaft, not crying exactly, but counting, the way children count to keep something at a distance. [210]

She had waited for the rose to finish because she needed its bucket, and because it seemed wrong to take anything from it while it was still trying. [238] Now she cut the canes back, lifted the root ball onto newspaper, and set the empty bucket on the railing. [258] Into it went sand for weight, and into the sand went a broom handle, and onto the handle she bolted the round shaving mirror from her late father's bathroom. [287] He had been lockmaster for the whole waterfront, the man who cut keys for every warehouse, chandlery, and widow's door from the fish market to the breakwater. [314] When he died, the shop sold for debts, and all Ada kept was one steel tooth from his bitting gauge, the little stepped notch he had used to measure how deep each cut must go. [349] A lock does not care how much you want in, he used to say; it cares whether you are exact. [369]

She wedged the tooth between the mirror's bracket and its bolt, and its stepped edge held the glass at a series of fixed angles, one click per step. [397] Then she tilted the mirror toward the moon, and a pale disc slid across the courtyard bricks, too high, missing the window by a yard. [422] The moon would move; the light would wander; exactness would have to be kept, not found once. [439]

Out on the water, old Benedetto was rowing his night skiff home from the lobster pots, and his oars came down with the patience of a clock: dip, pull, lift, drip. [470] Ada began to move the mirror with the rhythm of oars in water, one notch of the tooth on every fourth stroke, until the disc crept down the wall and settled against Theo's glass. [504]

The counting from the basement stopped. [510] What followed was a quivering hush, the kind that happens when a frightened thing decides not to run yet. [529] Then the window scraped open, and a small face tipped upward, blinking into the borrowed moon. [545]

"Is that yours?" Theo whispered. [550]

"It's the harbor's," Ada said. [555] "I'm only aiming it." [559]

She did not pretend it would fix anything. [567] Light did not bring boats back; she knew that the way she knew roses ended. [582] But she could instill a habit, and a habit could be a railing to hold. [597] So she leaned over the iron and taught him the count: listen for the oars, and every fourth stroke, call up to her, and she would click the tooth one step. [628] When the skiff had tied up, Theo kept the rhythm alone, whispering dip, pull, lift, drip, and his voice came up the airshaft steadier each time. [654] It was no longer counting to keep something away; it was counting to bring something closer. [670]

Near two, the moon slid behind the grain elevator, and the disc thinned and went out. [686] Ada waited for the counting to start again, the old frightened kind. [698] Instead Theo called, "Same time tomorrow?" [704]

"Same time," she said, and she sat among the bare tins and the resting root ball, the iron cage of the fire escape around her like cupped hands, and let herself be, for once, a little glad. [741] In the morning she would repot the rose in her washtub; a lease, after all, could be renewed. [759]
