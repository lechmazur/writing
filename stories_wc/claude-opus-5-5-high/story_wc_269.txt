The station swung on its four chains like a thought that could not finish itself. [15] Nadia Ferrer had lived on it again for nine years, since the fossil fuel ban emptied the shipping lanes and the sea went quiet enough to hear itself rise. [44] Every morning she read the gauges, logged the tide, and tapped. [55] It was a habit from childhood, a practice her father had taught her on the reef flats: knuckles on a hull, on a railing, on the bone of her own wrist, a rhythm of long and short that he said was how the water counted. [100] Tap, tap, pause, tap, the long rest, then again. [109] She had never known what it meant. [116]

She had been nineteen during the last great bleaching, one of the divers the tribunal called to testify. [134] She had described, under oath, how the coral went white the way a face does before fainting, how the fish stayed for a week and then did not. [162] Her testimony helped pass the ban. [168] It did not bring back the path. [175]

The path was on a single page she kept pinned above the chart table, torn from her father's old atlas and left on her bunk the night he drowned. [204] It showed the archipelago as it had been drawn a century before, and running from the shoal where the station now stood to Isla Pequena was a dotted line labeled in faded ink: foot passage. [239] He had gone out in a squall to free a fouled gauge, and the sea had closed over him. [258] Everyone said the path was simply gone, drowned like the reefs, one more thing the burning century had taken. [277] Nadia did not believe in simply. [283] She wanted to know why some paths disappear and others only seem to, because the difference was the whole of her grief. [305]

So that autumn she began to assemble the record. [314] She pulled every tide log the station had kept since before her birth, the paper ones in salt-stiff binders, the digital ones recovered from drives that hummed like tired insects. [344] She laid them across the floor in sequence, weighted with shackles and mugs, until the room became a long gray shoreline of numbers. [367] The station drifted on its chains while she worked, never going anywhere, always moving. [381] For weeks the numbers said only what everyone said: higher, higher, higher. [393]

Then, one night when the wind had dropped and she could not sleep, she sat among the binders and tapped her father's rhythm on the floor without thinking. [421] Tap, tap, pause, tap, the long rest. [428] Her hand was resting on a year when the lowest spring tides had dipped unusually far. [444] She counted forward along the floor, binder by binder, and found the next deep dip eighteen years and some months later, then another, then another. [469] Small beats, then a long rest. [475] It was the moon's slow wobble, the nodal cycle, the one every tide table accounted for and no one thought about. [496] Her father had not been tapping nonsense. [503] He had been tapping the interval, the way his own father had taught him, so that a child on the flats could carry the calendar of the path in her hands. [534]

She turned the atlas page over for the first time in years. [546] On the back, in pencil she had always taken for a smudge, were dates stretching back a century, each one a low spring tide when the path had surfaced. [575] Beneath them he had written a single line: the sea may swallow a beat, but the song is still the song. [596] The cycles since his death had bottomed out underwater, during the worst of the rising, and the town had decided the path was dead. [619]

Nadia went to the newest binder, the one from after the ban, and read the line she had logged a hundred times without seeing it. [644] The rise was slowing. [648] Not reversing, not yet, but flattening, the way a held breath eases before it lets go. [664] She ran the numbers twice, then a third time with her knuckles on the table, tap, tap, pause, tap. [683] The next low would come in two springs, and for perhaps an hour the shoal might show its spine again. [703]

Some paths disappear because the world ends them, she thought, and some because the world has not finished its sentence. [723] She had watched the reef go white and had learned to mourn at once; her father had known how to wait. [744]

Outside, the chains groaned and held. [750] She pinned the page back above the table, face down this time, so the dates showed, and wrote the next one beneath his in her own hand. [777] Then she tapped the long rest, and let the station drift. [788]
