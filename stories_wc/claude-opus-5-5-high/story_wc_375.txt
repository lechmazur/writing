The tourists always laughed at the same place, where the guide's lantern caught the great selenite blade and threw their shadows huge against the ceiling. [25] Ines Varga waited on her folding stool until the last echo of that laughter faded up the stairwell, then capped her fixative and wiped her charcoal fingers on her apron, which did nothing, because nothing ever did. [62] Eleven years of portraits had worked the black into the whorls of her skin. [76] She could draw a face in four minutes, and she never forgot one. [89] That was the trouble. [93] Her memory kept every jaw and eyelid she had ever sold, eleven thousand strangers stacked behind her eyes, and whenever she tried to draw something new, one of them rose up and took the page. [128] Her mother had called it the family gift, and then, more quietly, the family leash. [143] Every Varga for four generations had ended up in this cavern with a sketchpad, no matter how far they ran. [163] Her great-grandfather had come to mine gypsum and stayed to draw the miners. [176] Her mother had gone north to paint the sky and come home within a year, pregnant and silent, to draw tourists by the crystal pool. [201]

Ines took the service lantern and walked past the pool to the old miners' niche. [216] The only painting her mother had kept from that northern year hung there, bolted into limestone: an aurora over black water, green ribbons tearing into violet, so bright it seemed to hum. [248] When the lantern struck it, the crystals around the frame caught the colors and scattered them across the cave until the whole chamber wavered, grieving and gorgeous, like the sky had come underground to mourn. [283]

Behind the painting was a steel box. [290] Her mother had shown it to her once, when Ines was nine, and had spoken the combination aloud a single time: thirty-one, four, seventeen. [314] "For when you want to stop remembering," she had said. [324] Ines had never forgotten it, of course. [331] She had simply been afraid. [336]

Tonight, three weeks after the funeral, she swung the painting out on its hinge and dialed. [352] The lock did not catch. [357] She tried again, slower, and felt the dial spin loose as a coin in water. [372] Twenty-four years of cave damp had rusted the spindle and slipped the wheels inside, so the numbers on the face no longer meant anything to the numbers underneath. [400] The combination was perfect and useless. [406] She almost laughed, and the sound died in the crystal before it became an echo. [421]

Then she did what she had always done. [429] She remembered. [431] Not the numbers but her mother's hands, the one time she had watched them open this box: the dial turned right past zero twice, the ear pressed to steel, the small pause at each click, the way her mother had counted under her breath not in numbers but in heartbeats. [481] Ines pressed her own ear to the cold door and turned, listening for the wheels themselves rather than the painted marks. [502] Each time a gate brushed the fence she marked the dial with a smudge of charcoal, and slowly, black thumbprint by black thumbprint, she rectified the drift, finding each true number hiding a quarter turn from its name. [540]

The bolt gave. [543]

Inside were sketchbooks, dozens, swollen with damp, and every page held a face Ines had never seen. [560] Not a single tourist. [564] An old woman with auroras in her hair. [572] A boy made of ice fractures. [578] A man whose mouth was a door. [585] Invented people, drawn at night, year after year, while by day her mother sold perfect likenesses and never once admitted she had kept going north in the dark. [613]

On the last page was a note in her mother's hand. [624] "The cave always calls us back, and I stopped fighting it." [635] "But nobody said we must only draw what we remember." [645]

Ines sat down on the wet stone beneath the painting. [655] The colors moved over her like slow water. [663] She understood now that she would never leave this place, that the calling had her the way it had them all, and she felt that sorrow settle into her ribs, bright and permanent. [696] But beneath it something else loosened, like a cork easing from a bottle kept too long. [712]

She opened a fresh page, and for the first time in eleven years she did not reach for a face she knew. [734] The strangers rose behind her eyes, and she let them pass. [745] Her charcoal fingers moved, and a woman appeared who had never existed, laughing, mouth open, in a cave where the echo had not yet begun. [770]
