When the geese came down on Vell, Ines Marrow was the only person in the city who refused to call it a sign. [23] They arrived every autumn at the same hour, a gray rope of wings unraveling over the rooftops, and they settled in the dry basins of the forgotten fountains as if water still stood there. [57] For twenty-one nights they would rest, and then they would go. [68] Ines worked for the Bureau of Correction, which existed to explain away weeping statues and haunted wells, and her colleagues called her the Lamp because nothing false survived long in her presence. [100] She was pale-haired and quick, and she had a habit of holding her lantern high even in daylight, as though the truth might be hiding in a corner. [128]

Her grandmother had been the last fountain-keeper of Vell. [137] Before the Council capped the fountains forty years ago, mourners wrote a memory of their dead on a slip of paper, sealed it in a glass vial, and dropped it into the nearest basin, where the current carried it underground. [177] Once a year the keeper drew them up and read them aloud in the square. [192] The Council called it superstition and turned the great valve shut, and the vials stayed wherever the water had left them. [213]

When her grandmother died, Ines inherited a stained glass box no larger than a loaf of bread, its panes leaded in blue and amber, its lid fused shut. [241] A card tied to it read: Follow the crumbs, and be as patient as the stars. [257] Ines had spent her life debunking riddles like that. [266] But her skepticism was realistically imaginative: she never dismissed a strange thing, she only asked what ordinary cause could make it. [287]

So on the first night of the geese, she noticed what everyone else had stopped seeing: a thin line of breadcrumbs running from the dry basin in Tanner's Row toward the old river quarter. [321] The geese were eating them, one waddling step at a time. [332] She followed, lantern hooded, past fountains choked with leaves and a stone dolphin whose mouth had been plugged with mortar. [352] The trail ended at a bakery shutter, where an old man knelt with a sack of stale rolls. [370]

"Forty autumns," he whispered, not looking up. [377] "Your grandmother asked me to lay the path each time the geese pause, so the way to the cistern would not be forgotten." [400]

He lifted a grate in his cellar floor, and cold air rose smelling of stone and old rain. [418] Below, in the cistern chamber, the great valve wheel stood rusted solid, and beside it hung an iron plate with a keyhole shaped like a star. [444]

Ines did not pry at the box. [451] She held it near her lantern until the old sealing wax softened, then worked a thread of wire around the lid, slowly, to unstick it without cracking a single pane. [481] Inside lay a star-shaped key and a page in her grandmother's hand. [493] Force the valve and the pipes will burst, it said. [503] Turn it one notch each night, when Vega crosses the grate, the way the stars slip one degree ahead of the sun each day. [527] Twenty notches; the geese will lend you just enough nights. [537] Their racket will hide the groaning of the pipes from the Council's watch. [550]

Ines thought of the Bureau, and of the forms she would have to sign against herself, and she put the key in the plate. [574]

Every night after that she came down the baker's ladder and waited, lantern dimmed, watching the blue star creep across the bars of the street grate overhead. [601] She wanted to hurry; everything in her that argued and proved wanted to hurry. [615] Instead she learned the old patience of things that move too slowly to be seen, and each night she turned the wheel one notch while the geese shouted above her like a crowd keeping a secret. [651]

On the twentieth night the wheel gave with a sigh. [661] Water moved through the dark like a held breath let go. [672]

By dawn, the forgotten fountains of Vell were murmuring again, and in every basin small glass vials bobbed to the surface, pale as seeds. [696] People came out in their nightclothes, defying the ordinance in silence. [707] They unrolled the papers and read, lips moving, a whole city remembering under its breath. [722] Ines found one in Tanner's Row, its wax newer than the others, with her own name inside in her grandmother's hand: I trusted you to doubt me until you saw. [752]

Overhead, the geese lifted and turned south, and the Lamp of the Bureau of Correction stood among the whispering crowd and, for once, let a wonder stand. [779]
