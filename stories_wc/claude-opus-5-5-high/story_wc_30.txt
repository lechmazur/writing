After tomorrow disappeared, the tide still came into the atrium every morning, which was the first contradiction Orrin learned to shelve. [21] He was the titan-gear librarian of Saltmere, forty feet of salvaged turbine wheels and brass escapements, built to reach the highest stacks and never to hurry. [47] The atrium had once been a reading hall with a glass dome; now the sea breathed through its lower doors, and low winter sun lay across the water like a pale page. [79]

With the tide came the identities. [85] Since the calendar stopped, nobody in Saltmere woke as quite the same person twice. [99] Without a tomorrow to carry them forward, names and habits and small loyalties slipped loose in the night and drifted seaward, silver and quick, schooling like smelt. [126] The flood tide swept them back through the atrium, and Orrin caught them in a fisherman's bamboo creel. [144]

The creel had belonged to old Tamsin Hool, whose own identity had been the first Orrin ever netted: a patient, weathered thing that smelled of tar and still hummed sea shanties when he opened the lid. [180] Every morning he waded in, dipped the creel, sorted the catch, and shelved each identity in a labeled jar until its owner came asking. [204] It was a routine, and Orrin loved it the way one loves a well-cut key. [219]

But his gears fluctuated with the moon. [226] At full moon every tooth meshed and he remembered every jar, every shanty, every face. [241] As the moon thinned, his memory wheel slipped a notch each night, and at new moon he woke knowing only his hands and the smell of salt. [268] He considered this a failure of engineering. [275]

So, as the thirteenth new moon approached, he gathered the red ironstone the tide deposited along the atrium steps, stoked the old furnace beneath the map room, and began to smelt. [306] He meant to cast a locking pin for his memory wheel, a spike of iron that would hold every tooth fast, whatever the sky did. [331]

Mira found him there, slag glowing orange on the stone. [341] She was nine, or had been nine yesterday, and she came most mornings to reclaim whatever she had lost overnight. [361] Today she stood very still, which was not like her. [371]

"I woke up patient," she said. [377] "I don't think it's mine." [382]

Orrin lifted the creel from its hook. [389] The old fisherman's hum inside the bamboo had grown thin; half of him was gone. [404]

He knew the proper procedure. [409] He could draw the patience out of her, return it to the creel, and restore both owners to their correct shapes. [430] But Mira was humming a shanty under her breath, and between verses she laughed at herself, quick and impatient, entirely her own. [452] She was both at once. [457] Nothing in the catalog allowed for that. [464]

"Does it hurt?" Orrin asked. [469]

"No," Mira said. [472] "It's strange, but it's nice. [477] Like finding a shell in your coat pocket." [485]

Orrin looked at the cooling iron in its mold, and then at the creel, and he understood something his builders had never engraved on any plate. [511] Identities migrated because the world no longer held them still, and they came back changed, and the changes were not errors. [532] A girl could be impatient and patient. [539] A fisherman could be in his creel and in a child's song. [551] A librarian could forget and still be faithful. [559]

If he locked his wheel, he would keep every morning, but he would never again meet one. [576] The routine he loved would harden into mere repetition, a tide that only came and went. [592]

So he broke the pin mold and poured the iron again, smelting it down to a thin, bright band. [611] With Mira holding the lamp, he engraved one line along its curve: You have done this before, and it is new. [632] He fixed the band to the creel's handle, where his hand would find it no matter what his gears remembered. [652] Then he let Mira keep her borrowed patience, and let Tamsin keep humming through her. [667]

The new moon came. [671]

Orrin woke knowing only his hands and the smell of salt, and his hands were holding a bamboo creel. [690] He read the band. [694] He did not know the words, and he believed them anyway. [705]

The winter sun rose low and gold across the water, the tide breathed in through the lower doors, and something silver flickered beneath the surface, trailing a child's laughter and an old man's song. [739] Orrin waded in, as if for the first time, and dipped the creel. [752]
