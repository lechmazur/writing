The signal came in at 2:14 every morning, and Ines Varga was the only one awake enough to hear it breathing. [21] She worked the graveyard shift at WKRU, the university radio station wedged beneath the physics building, where the ceiling pipes sweated and the old transmitter hummed like a sleeping animal. [51] Officially she was a graduate student in radio astronomy. [60] Unofficially, since October, she had become the town's celestial signal interpreter, the woman who translated the pulses from the sky into words people could fight over. [86]

And they did fight. [90] Provost Hallam read her transcripts at faculty meetings as proof that the heavens endorsed his expansion plan, the new observatory that would swallow the east side of town. [118] Mayor Dunleavy read the same transcripts from the courthouse steps as a warning against it. [133] Neighbors who had shared snowblowers for twenty years now stood on opposite curbs holding printouts of her work, and every Sunday the crowds grew louder. [158]

Ines had begun to suspect the signal wanted exactly that. [168]

Tonight she spread the astronomical charts across the mixing board, the brittle 1970s sheets the station had inherited from a dead professor, their constellations inked by hand and annotated in a cramped, furious script. [202] Professor Aldous Rein had heard the signal too, in 1978. [212] His margin notes said so. [217] They also said: It learns what you want to hear and says it back to both sides. [234] Underneath, underlined three times, was a single word: tricksters. [243]

She had laughed at that in September. [250] She did not laugh now. [255]

That afternoon she had finally run the raw recordings through a spectrum analyzer instead of her headphones, and found what lay beneath the audible pulses: a subsonic layer, eleven hertz, pulsing too low for any ear. [291] The station rebroadcast the signal every morning at six, on Hallam's orders, so the whole town had been soaking in it for weeks. [314] Eleven hertz was the frequency of unease, the tremor felt in old churches before storms, the hum that made dogs pace. [335] Whatever lived out there was not speaking to the town; it was tuning it. [349]

The door banged open at 1:50. [355] Hallam stood there in a camel coat, snow melting on his shoulders. [367] "Tonight's transcript goes straight to the regents," he said. [376] "Dunleavy has his own astronomer now, some hobbyist from the library, and I need you to be clearer than he is." [397]

"Clearer about what?" [400]

"About whose side the sky is on." [407]

Ines looked at the charts, at Rein's furious handwriting, and understood that Hallam and Dunleavy were only the loudest instruments in the room. [430] The real player was something that fed on their certainty and paid them back in more of it. [448]

"Give me until morning," she said, and he left satisfied, which frightened her more than anything. [464]

Rein's notes had an answer, half-finished. [470] The tricksters listened as much as they spoke; that was how they learned what each side craved. [487] If someone interfered through subsonic frequencies, answering their eleven hertz with its exact inverse, the town would feel nothing, and the tricksters would hear only silence where an audience should be. [518] Rein had never gotten the transmitter time to try it. [528] Ines had the keys. [532]

She built the cancelling tone by hand, phase-inverting the raw signal on the old reel deck, checking its timing against the charts where Rein had plotted the source: a patch of sky near Bootes, drifting a little each night. [571] Then she did one thing Rein had not thought of. [581] Beneath the silence she laid a faint pulse of her own, the counterfeit heartbeat of an eager crowd, and sent it up through the rooftop dish toward the charted emptiness of the Bootes Void, where anything hungry for an audience would find none. [624] Let them chase applause into nothing. [630]

At six, WKRU rebroadcast the signal as ordered. [638] Under it rode her inverse, and eleven hertz met its twin and vanished. [651]

She walked outside into gray light, frost on the dish, her breath climbing toward stars already fading. [668] By noon the crowds would gather on the courthouse steps with their printouts. [681] Maybe they would still shout. [686] But they would be shouting at each other, not for something listening in the dark, and people who shouted without being tuned could sometimes be persuaded to stop. [714]

Hallam would want his transcript. [719] She would give him the truth instead, Rein's truth: that the sky had been telling each of them what they wanted, and that she had stopped letting it. [747] He would try to bury it, of course; power always tried. [758] Ines folded the charts under her arm and waited, cautiously hopeful, for 2:14, to learn whether anything out there still breathed in her direction. [782]
