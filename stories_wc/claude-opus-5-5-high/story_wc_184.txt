The great clock of Harrow College stopped at 11:52 on a Tuesday night, and Ines Marrow was the only person who thought to bring tea. [25] The engineers had halted the mechanism for its first restoration in four centuries, then gone to argue about bearings in the pub, leaving the archivist alone with the gears, the silence, and the rope. [59] She had come for the rope. [65] It hung from the bell housing in three braided strands, red for the town, black for the college, and a faded white that nobody could explain. [91]

Harrow kept a whole shelf of stories about things that were not there: the empty chair set out each commencement, the bell that was never rung for peace, the envoy Aurel Vasse, who climbed this tower in 1612 to seal the boundary accord between town and college and was never seen again. [143] Students called them the fables of absence. [150] Ines called them unfinished paperwork. [155]

Four hundred years of lawsuits had grown from Vasse's disappearance. [165] The town's ledgers insisted the accord was signed and the meadow below the tower was theirs; the college's minutes insisted it was never signed and the meadow was not. [194] Both sets of records were confident, contradictory, and, Ines suspected, equally sincere. [206]

Her evidence was small enough to fit in her cardigan pocket: a diplomat's wax seal impression, the size of a coin, found last spring loose in a folder labeled only Absences. [237] It bore Vasse's device, a closed door, and around the door ran a border that she had first taken for decoration. [258] It was a braid. [262]

She set down her thermos, pulled on cotton gloves, and compared the two by lamplight, the way she compared handwriting: patiently, one stroke at a time, murmuring to herself like someone coaxing a shy cat. [297] The rope was not simply twisted. [303] Its strands crossed in irregular intervals, three overs and a skip, two unders and a knot, the way a merchant's knotted cord recorded debts. [327] It was a document, and like every document at Harrow, it had been edited. [341] Someone had rebraided the red and the black so they wound around each other without ever touching the white, two accounts talking past a silence. [366]

Ines had trained on vellum and microfilm, not cordage, but a record was a record. [381] She photographed each crossing, numbered it in pencil, and began, gently, to undo the edits. [396]

The work took two hours. [401] She reconfigured the braids of the centuries-old ceremonial rope strand by strand, following the pattern on the seal, until red, black, and white merged into a single interlocking plait, each color passing through the others as the border promised. [440] When she tied the final knot, the rope tugged once, as if something far above had leaned on the bell. [460] The bell did not ring. [465]

Instead, in the brick wall between the escapement and the winding drum, where the architectural plans showed solid masonry, there was a door. [488] Ines understood, with the calm of someone reading a footnote, why no one had found it: for four hundred years the clock had never stopped, and this was a door that could exist only when the tower was not counting. [528] She checked her watch. [532] The engineers would return by one. [538]

Behind the door was a room no larger than a confessional, with a desk, a stool, and a folded sheet under a paperweight shaped like a bell. [565] There was no skeleton, no ghost, no envoy. [573] There was only the accord, signed, and beneath the signature a note in Vasse's cramped Latin. [589] Ines translated it aloud, because translation was a kind of company. [600]

"I have signed that the meadow belongs to neither party but to both in common, which each will call betrayal. [620] So I leave it where it cannot be burned, my seal where only a patient reader will look, and myself out of the story, so that my absence may argue for me until someone braids it back." [657]

Ines sat on the stool and laughed, softly, the way one laughs at a clever filing system. [674] The white strand had never been unexplained; it had been the answer, the common ground, deliberately kept from touching either side. [695] The fables of absence had not been hiding a tragedy. [705] They had been holding a place. [711]

She slid the accord into an acid-free sleeve, labeled it in her neat hand, and tucked the wax impression beside it, a key returned to its lock. [738] Then she stepped out and sat with her tea until the engineers came back, bickering, and set the pendulum swinging. [758] Behind her, the door folded quietly into brick. [766] The clock resumed its counting, and for the first time in four centuries, Harrow had one story instead of two. [786]
