At seventy-one, Ines Varga had finally reached the age when no one remembers you, and she found it restful. [19]
For thirty years she had been the whispering advisor to the mayors of Lantern Bay, the small gray woman who leaned toward an ear at council meetings and changed the vote without raising her voice. [54]
Now the new mayor held doors for her the way one holds doors for weather. [69]
She did not mind. [73]
Her voice had gone soft in the marriage, because her husband Eli was deaf, and for forty years the loudest things in her life had been said with their hands. [103]

Eli had been gone two winters. [109]
What remained was his camera, a brass-cornered Leica older than both of them, which had not held film since 1979. [129]
He never needed it to. [134]
Eli used it to listen. [139]
He would stand on the pier with the camera raised and press the shutter each time the planks beneath his boots stopped trembling, when boats and bells and gulls all paused at once. [172]
Silence, he signed to her once, is the only music I get to keep. [186]
He had wanted to write it down, to translate silence into music that hearing people could play and deaf people could feel, and he had died with the notebook half full. [217]
So Ines finished it. [221]

Every night that summer she walked the harbor by moonlight, when the water was luminous with the tiny creatures that bloomed each July, so that every oar stroke left a green-blue comet behind it. [255]
She was mapping silence. [259]
At the fuel dock the quiet lasted four seconds between bell buoys; under the fish market's pilings, nine; beside the sunken ferry, where Eli had proposed in clumsy, radiant signs, almost twelve. [291]
She clicked the empty camera at each pause and marked the lengths on his harbor chart, until the chart looked less like a map than a score, its rests scattered like islands. [323]

The children from the harbor school for the deaf began following her after the third week. [339]
She never called them; she simply sat on the pier, set her palm flat on the wood, and waited, and something in her patience drew them the way lamplight draws moths. [370]
She was quietly charismatic in the way of lighthouses, never chasing anyone, only staying lit. [385]
A girl named Pilar asked, with quick impatient fingers, what she was photographing. [398]
The spaces, Ines signed back. [403]
The places where the world stops talking over you. [412]

The Regatta was the problem. [417]
Each August the town band played on the pier for three hours straight, trumpets and drums shaking the boards so hard that the deaf children felt only a single blurred roar, and every year they went home early. [455]
Music with no gaps in it, Eli used to say, is just noise wearing a uniform. [471]

On Regatta night Ines climbed the bandstand steps during the second march and did the one thing a whispering advisor had never done in thirty years: she disrupted everything, in public. [502]
She did not shout. [506]
She lifted Eli's camera, pressed the shutter once, and held up her open hand. [520]
The conductor, who could not have told you her name, lowered his baton anyway. [534]
In the hush she unrolled the chart across his music stand and whispered her instructions, and the old habit of listening to her flickered through the players like a half-remembered tune. [565]

They played it then: a slow harbor song whose phrases broke off into silences of four seconds, then nine, then twelve, exactly where the water had gone still. [593]
On the pier, the deaf children lay with their cheeks against the planks. [606]
Now they could feel the edges of each phrase, the rise, the fall, and then the glorious emptiness, and in every emptiness Ines stood at the rail and signed the words Eli had written beside it. [642]
Here, her hands said. [646]
Still here. [648]
Listen. [649]
Pilar laughed, a sound she could not hear, and signed the words back. [662]
Then twenty children were signing them, and the glowing water shivered green beneath the pier each time the drums returned. [682]

Afterward the newspaper called it the Silent Serenade and credited the band. [694]
No one printed her name, and by September the mayor was holding doors for her again, politely, the way one holds doors for weather. [718]
Ines found she did not mind this either. [726]
That autumn, and every night after, the children gathered on the pier, palms flat, waiting for the harbor to go quiet so they could sing it with their hands. [755]
She raised the empty camera, clicked, and missed him, and smiled. [766]
