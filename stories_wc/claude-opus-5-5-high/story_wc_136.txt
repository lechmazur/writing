Mara Pell had mapped every street in Harrow Cross twice, once for the transit authority and once for herself. [19] The official maps were grids of measured lines. [27] Her private ones were something else: inked in six weights, shaded with graphite, every pothole and storm drain drawn with the care other people gave to faces. [54] Her father used to say she measured like an engineer and saw like a painter, and that the two halves would someday argue her into trouble. [80] He had been a conductor on the Lantern Line until it closed, thirty years ago, and he had died in October without explaining why he stopped speaking to anyone the winter it shut. [113]

He left her his ticket punch. [119] It was brass, worn smooth at the grip, and it clicked on its own at night. [135] Mara would wake to the sound and find tiny star-shaped holes in whatever paper lay nearest, though the jaws never touched anything. [157] She logged each punch the way she logged everything: time, location, count. [169] The holes always appeared on her street maps, and always over cracks in the pavement. [184]

So she went out and drew the cracks. [192] Individually they meant nothing, splits in concrete from frost and roots. [203] But when she traced them onto vellum and laid the sheets over one another, the fractures resolved into arrows, dozens of them, chiseled too cleanly to be weather. [231] Only someone who looked at a city as both data and drawing would ever have seen them. [248] Someone had built them for exactly that kind of eye. [258]

They pointed toward the old Terminus on Wick Hill. [267] Her father had called it the fortress. [274] "Where conductors keep what they can't say," he told her once, and then laughed as if he had been joking. [294]

She chose New Year's Eve deliberately. [300] For eleven years she had spent that night the same way, alone with her maps, rehearsing the call to her sister she never made, and she had promised herself that midnight would divide that habit from whatever came next. [339]

At eleven forty she followed the last arrow up Wick Hill into fog so thick the streetlamps looked like drowned moons. [360] The Terminus doors were chained, but a service hatch beside them had a slot the width of a ticket. [379] Above it, scratched into the steel in her father's cramped capitals: I DARE YOU TO SAY IT FIRST. [397]

Mara understood with a certainty that made her hands shake. [407] It was the game he played when she and Nell were small, the dare that ended every argument: whoever confessed first won. [429] She tore a strip from her newest map, the one with every arrow inked in red, and wrote on the back what she had never told anyone: I defended him because I was afraid Nell was right. [466] She punched it once and fed it through the slot. [476]

The hatch swung inward. [480]

Beyond lay the old concourse, a vaulted hall whose domed roof had been perforated with thousands of star-shaped holes, each the exact shape of her father's punch. [507] Mist drifted through them and real starlight fell in thin silver needles to the tiles. [522] Along the walls stood conductors' lockers, and every locker was full of unsent letters. [536] Her father's was labeled PELL. [541] Inside, one envelope bore her name. [547]

He wrote that on the final run of the Lantern Line he had waved a late boy aboard after the closure order, punched his ticket out of kindness, and watched him be injured when the flooded tunnel collapsed. [585] The inquiry blamed the signals, and he let it. [594] The silence had cost him Nell, who sensed the lie, and it had cost Mara the sister she chose him over. [615] "I carved the arrows because I could never walk you here myself," he wrote. [629] "You were always the one who would notice." [637]

The punch clicked in her pocket, and across the vault a single new star opened in the ceiling. [655] Somewhere in the fog, the city clocks began striking twelve. [665]

Mara took out her phone, fingers trembling, and dialed the number she had memorized and never used. [682] On the third stroke, Nell answered. [688]

"It's me," Mara said, before either of them could retreat. [698] "I found where Dad kept the truth, and I'm going to say it first." [712]

Overhead, the stars held steady through the mist, and for once she did not need a map to know the way. [733]
