Wen had not heard a bird in eleven days, and she knew what that meant. [15] The silence was raveling again, loosening out of the bamboo like a pulled thread, and wherever it went the world grew thin. [37] First the cicadas stopped, then the wind, then the creek that had run past her door for sixty years simply forgot how to make a sound. [63] She sat at her loom in the forgotten orchard, where plum trees older than any map stood in a clearing the bamboo had closed over long ago, and she did not weave. [95] Her hands were waiting for a reason. [102]

People in the valley had once called her the loom spinner, because she spun her own thread and wove it the same day, and because she seemed always to be spinning something else behind her eyes, some year she could not return to. [145] That year had a name: Lian, her granddaughter, who had walked out through the bamboo at nineteen and taken a train to the coast. [169] They had quarreled about the orchard, about staying, about everything that did not matter. [183]

Now the roads were gone. [188] Through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry, whole valleys had slipped loose from their hours; letters came back unopened from places that no longer occurred. [213] The coast was not far in miles, but it was impossibly far in every other way. [229]

The reason arrived in a cardboard box. [236] She had bought it weeks before at the last rummage sale the valley school ever held, a jumble of chipped cups and a puzzle whose lid showed a harbor at dawn. [267] Inside were only the edge pieces. [273] She recognized the harbor, and then the pencil marks on the lid, and then her own handwriting: Lian, age seven, finished with Grandmother. [296] The middle had gone with Lian's things when she left; Wen remembered her pocketing a handful, laughing, saying she would keep the heart of it. [321]

A frame here, a heart there. [327] Two halves of one picture, separated by a distance nothing could cross. [339]

Except, perhaps, the tapestry itself. [344] Wen had always suspected that her weaving and time's weaving were the same craft at different scales. [361] If she wove the edges into a cloth strong enough, the border might hold its shape across the fraying, and a border that held would go looking for its middle. [391]

But no ordinary thread survived the silence. [398] She had tried; it went gray and soft and fell apart in her fingers. [412] The only thread that held was spun from something the silence could not touch, and Wen knew of one such thing left in the orchard. [437]

Her own remaining hours. [441]

She took the amber bead from the drawer where she kept it, a drop of plum sap that had hardened around a bee the summer Lian was born. [469] Everything around it had faded, but the bead sat in her palm stoically sparkling, holding its one caught moment against the dark. [491] It was the only weight heavy enough to anchor the warp. [502] She tied it on, set her spindle turning, and began to spin. [514]

The thread came out pale gold and warm, and with each arm's length she felt an evening leave her, a winter she would not see, a spring she had been saving. [545] She did not stop. [549]

Then she recommenced the tapestry she had abandoned the day Lian left, threading the puzzle edges into the border one by one, their knobs and hollows locking into the weft like small stubborn promises. [583] Inside the frame she wove the orchard in bloom, the plum petals, the loom by the door, and words in the old careful script: Nothing we said was the heart of it. [615] You were. [617] Do not try to come back; the road is gone. [627] Live facing the dawn. [631]

By the time she tied the last knot, her hair had gone white as the petals and the silence had reached the edge of the clearing. [657] It came in through the plum trees, and the trees went quiet one by one, and then it reached the loom. [678]

The border held. [681] Within its jagged frame, the woven orchard trembled, thinned, and slipped away, not unraveled but sent, pulled toward the missing middle like water toward the sea. [707] Far off, in a room above a harbor, a young woman opening a tin of old puzzle pieces would find them warm, and would find, fitted among them, a single thread of gold. [740]

Wen sat back with the amber bead untied and cool in her hand, its bee still bright, and listened to the silence settle around her like snow. [766] She had enough hours left, she thought, to watch the dawn. [777]
