Ines had not slept a full night since the water wars ended, and she had stopped expecting to. [18] Instead she painted, because a brush held still is a kind of prayer and a brush moving is a kind of argument, and she needed both. [44] Her studio was a salt shed on Carrow, a tidal island that belonged to the mainland twice a day and to the sea the rest of the time. [72] She found the arrangement honest. [77] Nobody on Carrow pretended to own anything permanently anymore. [86]

During the wars she had been a surveyor, the one who walked the dry country with a divining map and marked every living well in red. [112] Later, other people had come with the poison. [120] She had told herself she only drew circles. [128] Now she painted the circles over and over, trying to thresh meaning from what she had done, beating the memory until the husk came loose from whatever grain might be inside. [159] So far there had only been husk. [166]

Rain came to Carrow perhaps four times a year, and when it did, Ines went out into it. [184] Not to drink, though she did, mouth open like a fool, but to watch where it went. [201] Water always knew something the land was hiding. [209] That was the first thing a surveyor learned and the last thing she could forget. [224]

Tonight the storm arrived after midnight, when she was already awake and already failing at a gray seascape. [242] She took the lantern and a trowel and found her way by following rain trails, the thin bright threads that ran down the dunes and braided across the old sheep pasture. [273] Her eyes burned the way they always burned, as if she had been staring into a kiln. [290]

The trails did not run to the sea. [298] They ran inland, uphill, which seemed impossible, and then she saw the ground was not a hill but a lip, the rim of a hollow she had walked past a hundred times. [330] Seven trails converged there and vanished into the sand as though swallowed. [342] She knelt and dug. [346] Two feet down, the trowel rang against stone. [354]

It was a capstone, round, carved with two sets of marks she knew too well: the wheat sheaf of the Upland towns, who had paid her, and the bee of the Low Villages, whose wells she had circled. [392] Beneath the carvings someone had cut a date from before the wars, when the two peoples had apparently built a cistern together and then forgotten they had. [419]

Pinned to a rag of wool at the stone's edge was an amber brooch. [433] Inside the amber, a real bee hung in golden stillness, wings spread, as if it had simply decided to stop and look around. [456] Ines laughed, which hurt. [460] "Well," she told the bee, "at least one of us got some rest." [473]

Every low tide an eel-seller named Hesper crossed the causeway from the Low Villages, and at her throat she wore a clasp of the same honeyed stone, cut to fit a missing twin. [506] Ines had noticed it the way she noticed everything, sleeplessly, without permission. [518]

At dawn she waited at the causeway with the brooch in one hand and a painting of the capstone, still wet, in the other. [542] Hesper came through the shallows with her basket and stopped when she saw the amber. [557]

"My sister's," she said. [561] "She came here to find the old spring when the wells went bad." [574]

"They went bad because of me," Ines said. [582] It was the first time she had said it aloud, and it came out smaller than she had imagined, and heavier. [603] "I marked them, and I am trying to be useful about it, which is harder than being sorry." [621]

She held out the brooch and the painting, the cistern's place marked not in red but in blue. [639]

Hesper studied her for a long time, long enough for the tide to turn and begin to close the road behind her. [661] Hesper took the brooch, fitted it to the clasp at her throat until the two halves became one small sun, and in exchange she gave Ines a name: Tamsin, her sister, who had reached this stone and been too weak to lift it. [704] "Now you have to carry her," Hesper said, "the way I do." [716]

The tide had taken the causeway, so they walked together to the hollow, two tired women and a trowel, and lifted the stone. [739] Below, black and sweet and patient, lay more water than either village had seen in nine years. [756] Ines stared until her eyes stung, and not from sleeplessness. [766]

That night she slept four hours and woke with a name to paint instead of a circle, which, wryly and earnestly, she counted as grain. [791]
