The Halvard Arcade turned gold a few minutes before anything else in the city did. [15] Dawn came through the old glass roof and caught the gilded ribs of the ceiling, and for a while the shuttered shops looked like a cathedral someone had forgotten to close. [46] Wren Achebe liked it best at this hour, when the advertising holograms had not yet woken and the only light was the kind that could not sell her anything. [75]

She sat on the lip of the dry fountain with an ebony clipboard across her knees. [91] The clipboard had belonged to her grandfather, a postal clerk, back when the city still had postal clerks. [109] Now it held one sheet of cream paper and a fountain pen, and it was the tool of her trade. [129]

Wren made names. [132] Everything that moved through the network was read, indexed, and remembered forever, so people who wanted to say something private had returned to surface mail, the slow, unlogged kind carried by hand. [164] But a real signature on an envelope was still a thread the machines could pull. [179] So they came to Wren for pseudonyms: names the recipient would recognize and no algorithm could trace. [196] She was carefully inventive about it. [202] She never conjured a name from nothing, because nothing was exactly what a stranger would guess. [218] She built them from what was already hidden in a person's life. [230] She had worn so many borrowed names herself that her own had started to feel like one. [247]

The letter on her clipboard belonged to Mrs. Ines Varga, who ran the button shop at the arcade's east end and had pressed it into Wren's hands the night before. [277] It was addressed to her son, Tomas, who had left the grid eleven years ago for a farm collective up north where screens were not allowed. [303]

"Give me a name he'll know," Ines had said. [312] "Not Mother. [314] He stopped answering to Mother a long time ago." [323]

The collection drone would come at ten past six, a brass-bellied relic that hummed down from the roof and swallowed whatever waited in the last mailbox in the district. [352] Wren had forty minutes. [356]

The letter itself was plain: weather, prices, a cough that had gone away. [369] The meaning, Wren suspected, was in the margins. [377] Down both edges of the page Ines had drawn tiny marks, the kind a nervous hand makes while it thinks: a small sun, a triangle, three short dashes, a spiral, then the same four again, over and over. [415] Wren had stared at them half the night and found nothing. [426]

Now, as the gold light slid across the tiled floor, she saw the chalk. [440]

The children of the arcade had brought sidewalk chalk back the way children always brought things back, without asking permission. [460] Chalk was the one writing the cameras never bothered to save, since the night cleaners erased it anyway. [478] But the cleaners had skipped the fountain corner, and there, beside a pink hopscotch grid, ran a line of symbols in blue: sun, triangle, dashes, spiral. [504]

Wren knelt and set the clipboard on the tiles to juxtapose the two, ink beside chalk. [520] Under the chalk line, in a child's careful capitals, someone had written the key so younger players could learn it. [540] Sun was S. [543] Triangle was K. [546] Three dashes were Y. [550] The spiral was not a letter at all but the sign for the top square of the grid, the one where you stood and waited for the others to finish. [580]

Sky, waiting. [582] Sky, waiting, down both margins, over and over. [590]

Wren remembered Ines saying, months ago over a tray of mother-of-pearl buttons, that Tomas had played hopscotch at this very fountain every morning before school, and that she used to stand at the far end of the grid to catch him. [631] He must have named her for the square she stood in. [642] The code had outlived his childhood, handed down from one set of chalky fingers to the next, and his mother had kept drawing it without knowing she was still calling to him. [674]

Wren uncapped the pen. [678] At the bottom of the letter, where a signature belonged, she did not write a name the machines could read. [698] She drew a sun, a triangle, three dashes, and a spiral, and on the envelope's corner she did the same. [718] To any scanner it was a doodle. [725] To Tomas it would be his mother at the end of the grid, arms open. [740]

She slid the letter into the mailbox at four minutes past six. [752] Then, while the drone's hum grew somewhere above the gold, Wren picked up a stub of blue chalk and, for the first time in years, wrote her own real name on the tiles in the children's code, small, where only a child would find it.
