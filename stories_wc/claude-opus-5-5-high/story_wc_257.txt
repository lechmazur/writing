Mara Vell made mourning jewelry for forty years, braiding the hair of the dead into brooches no thicker than a moth's wing. [22] She could weave anyone's grief into something wearable, except her own. [33] When Tomas died, she stopped braiding and started drinking, and by the following winter she had sold her tweezers, her loupe, and the little gold findings she had hoarded like teeth. [64]

Rock bottom, when it came, was the kitchen floor, and her daughter's face above her, not frightened anymore, only tired. [84] Wren was seventeen. [87] After that night she stopped eating at the table, then stopped leaving her room, and the geraniums on her sill browned one leaf at a time, as if keeping her company. [118]

Sober for ninety days, Mara took the only job that would have her: night custodian at the Hallam Observatory, a domed relic on the ridge where the lobby clock had stopped at 9:14 decades ago and no one had ever bothered to fix it. [162] Inside, time pooled rather than passed. [168] The telescope had been dismantled long ago; what remained was the old star projector, a black iron insect squatting in the center of the dome, and the curtains. [196]

They hung in heavy maroon folds around the base of the dome, and Mr. Oduya, the retired keeper who still visited on Thursdays, called them the curtains of intention. [225] "You don't open them to look," he told her. [234] "You open them because you've decided what you want someone to see." [246]

Mara thought about that for a week. [253] Then she went home and opened the drawer she had not touched since the funeral. [268] Tomas had been a tattoo artist, and inside lay his last stencil, drawn on vellum for Wren's eighteenth birthday: a rose, half its petals curled and falling, the other half opening. [299] He had never finished explaining it. [305] Mara thought she understood now. [310]

She did not knock on Wren's door; knocking had become a kind of pleading. [324] Instead she folded a paper crane, the way she once folded tissue around finished lockets, and penciled inside its wings the latitude and longitude of the observatory's side door. [353] She left it on the hallway floor. [360] The next morning it was gone, but Wren did not come. [371]

Mara folded another, a frog this time, then a fox, then a lily, each carrying the same numbers in her small, precise jeweler's hand. [395] She had spent her life making objects for people who could not yet speak about what they had lost; she knew how long some messages needed to sit in a pocket before they were read. [430]

On the eleventh night, the side door sighed open behind her. [441] Wren stood in the dark lobby beneath the dead clock, a crumpled paper fox in her fist. [458] "I'm not staying," she said. [463] "You don't have to," Mara said, and led her into the dome. [475]

She had spent the afternoon with Mr. Oduya, fitting the vellum into a slide frame they had cut from brass shim, her old hands remembering their steadiness. [502] Now she switched on the projector, and a thousand stars pricked open overhead. [515] "They're fake," Wren said. [519] "Genuinely artificial," Mara agreed. [523] "Somebody built every one of them on purpose, because they wanted people to have a sky when the real one was clouded over." [546] "That doesn't make it less of a sky." [554]

She walked the rim of the dome and drew the maroon curtains back, one panel at a time, slowly, deliberately, until the whole curved wall was bare. [581] Then she slid the brass frame into the auxiliary lamp. [591]

Her father's rose bloomed across the dome, forty feet wide, its lines of ink turned to lines of light. [610] Mara turned the projector's slow crank, and as the artificial sky wheeled, the stencil wheeled with it, so that the falling petals swept down toward the horizon and the opening ones climbed into the stars. [645] "He drew it for you," Mara said. [652] "I think he knew we'd need to see it from far enough away to understand it isn't two flowers." [671] "It's one flower, turning." [675]

Wren said nothing for a long time. [682] The light moved over her face, petal by petal, and Mara watched her the way she had once watched a braid of hair settle into its setting, waiting to see if it would hold. [716] "Can I come back tomorrow?" Wren finally asked. [724]

Mara unfolded the fox from her daughter's fist, smoothed it flat, and wrote a new set of numbers inside: the latitude and longitude of the kitchen table. [751] "Breakfast first," she said, and pressed it into Wren's hand like a locket. [764]
