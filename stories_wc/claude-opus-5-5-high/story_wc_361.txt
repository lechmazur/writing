Pell had carried relics along the ridgeway for eleven winters, and his hands still shook every time he unbuckled the satchel. [21] Above him the aurora hung in green sheets, crackling faintly, lighting the frozen spine of stone that ran between the valley towns like a seam holding the world together. [50] Tonight the satchel held a saint's knucklebone for Harrow, a cracked bell-clapper for Mire, and a clay jar of orchard seeds for Tallis, the last town on the ridge. [79] The seeds were a century dead, the manifest said, kept only for reverence. [92]

Around his neck, beneath three scarves, hung the dragonfly-wing pendant. [102] It had belonged to Oda, who had taught him the route, and who had walked north into the lights one night six years ago and left it swinging on the hook by the door. [136] The wing was real, or had been, a single veined pane set in silver, thinner than breath. [153] When something nearby sang at its pitch, the wing trembled with it, vibrating sympathetically, and time around the wearer went slow and generous. [176] A nod across a market became a conversation; a handoff at a gate became an afternoon. [192]

Oda had used it constantly. [197] She would stop a baker mid-sentence and let the moment stretch until the man remembered his mother's bread, or stand with a widow for what the clocks called a breath and the widow called an hour. [233] A delivery is routine, she used to say, and the routine is where wonder hides; you only have to coax it out. [255]

Pell never let it hum. [260] Long moments frightened him; people looked at you too closely in them. [272] He kept the pendant muffled in wool and made his handoffs quick, eyes down, satchel shut before anyone could ask him where she had gone. [297]

At first they had asked constantly. [303] Then less. [305] In Harrow this year the gatekeeper called her the Wing Woman, and swore she had once carried a sunrise over the ridge in a lantern. [330] In Mire the children sang a counting rhyme about her, and none of them believed she had ever been a person who sneezed or burned porridge. [356] Pell had seen her do both. [362] He felt her sliding away from him across the fade from memory to myth, and every retelling rubbed out another true thing. [384]

The last stretch to Tallis was the highest, where the aurora came down so low it hissed against the stones. [404] Halfway along, the pendant began to buzz through its wool, a thin insect whine against his breastbone. [421] He clamped a hand over it. [427] It only sang louder, answering the crackle overhead, and the whole ridge seemed to lean into one held instant. [446]

Inside the satchel, something ticked. [451] He opened the clay jar with shaking fingers. [459] The dead seeds had split. [464] Pale threads uncurled from each husk, reaching toward the light, and as he watched, stunned, they began to germinate in earnest, rooting in the dust of the jar faster than any spring could manage. [498] The stretched instant had given them a whole season, and they had not wasted it. [513]

Pell sat down hard on the cold stone. [521] He understood, suddenly, why Oda had walked north. [529] She had not been fleeing the route; she had gone looking for the place where the lights sang loudest, to see how long a moment could be made to last. [559] Perhaps she was still standing in it somewhere, one breath long and endless. [572] And she had left him the wing not as a keepsake but as a job. [587]

At the Tallis gate a girl of about seven waited with the reliquary box, stamping her feet against the cold. [607] Pell's instinct was to hand over the jar and run. [617] Instead he unwound the scarves and let the pendant hum. [627] The moment opened around them like a door. [635]

"They're alive," the girl whispered, staring at the green threads. [645] "Is it true the Wing Woman made things grow?" [654]

Pell's hands were still shaking, but he found he did not mind. [666] "She burned porridge," he said. [671] "Every morning, the whole kitchen black with it." [679]

The girl laughed, delighted, and he kept going, giving her the sneezes and the bad knee and the terrible singing, all the true small things, inside a moment the clocks would swear lasted three seconds. [714] Let the myth have her lanterns and sunrises, he thought. [724] He would plant the porridge underneath, where it could take root. [735] Overhead the aurora rippled, and somewhere far to the north, he was almost sure, someone laughed along. [752]
